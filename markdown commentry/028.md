# Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ (Chapter 28) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Narrative of the Oppressed and the Mirage of Tyranny**

Al-Qaṣaṣ, "The Stories," is the twenty-eighth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 88 verses of late Makkan revelation. It is the final and most detailed of the three *Ṭā-Sīn-Mīm* chapters, taking its title from verse 25, where Moses arrives exhausted at the wells of Midian and recounts *al-qaṣaṣ* (the narrative of his life) to the venerable elder (traditionally identified with Shuʿayb or a righteous Midianite patriarch), who consoles him with the words: *“Do not fear; you have escaped from the wrongdoing people.”* Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ is particularly renowned in Islamic history for verse 85, traditionally revealed at the station of Juḥfah during the Prophet's dangerous, sorrowful emigration (*Hijrah*) from Makkah to Madinah: *“Indeed, He who has mandated upon you the Qur'an will surely return you to an appointment / place of return (*maʿād*)”*—a divine guarantee that he would return victorious to the city from which he was being driven. The sūrah provides the most emotionally intimate, biographical account of the infancy, youth, exile, and prophetic commissioning of Moses found in the entire sacred text, framed as an explicit, living mirror for the persecuted Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ and his companions.

**The Epic Biography of Moses and the Fall of Qārūn**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ is built around two monumental historical portraits: the political tyranny of Pharaoh versus the divine empowerment of Moses, and the financial hubris of Korah (*Qārūn*) versus the spiritual wealth of the righteous. The first movement (1–43) delivers the gripping, cinematic narrative of Moses: Pharaoh's murderous decree, the desperate mother of Moses inspired to cast her infant into the Nile, the baby fetched by the family of Pharaoh, the intervention of Moses' sister watching from afar, Moses refusing all wet-nurses until his own mother's breast is restored to him *“so that her eye might be comforted and she might not grieve and that she might know that the promise of Allah is true”* (28:13). It traces his youth: the accidental manslaughter of the Egyptian while defending an Israelite, the secret warning from a man running from the furthest end of the city that the council was plotting his execution, Moses fleeing in terror across the desert to Midian, watering the flocks for the two modest daughters of the elderly sage, being hired for eight or ten years of service, traveling with his family in the cold, seeing the divine fire at the side of Mount Ṭūr, receiving the staff and radiant hand, returning to confront Pharaoh. Pharaoh's minister Hāmān ordered to build a lofty brick tower to view the God of Moses, culminating in the drowning of the tyrant and his armies cast into the sea like refuse. The second movement (44–75) turns to the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ, confirming that he was not present on the western slope of Sinai when the decree was given, nor among the people of Midian, but this revelation is a direct mercy from his Lord. It rebukes the pagan excuses that following guidance would cause them to be snatched from their land, and presents the tragic narrative of Qārūn (76–84): an Israelite had of such vast treasures that the mere keys to his vaults weighed down a band of strong men, who boasted that his riches were acquired through his own scientific intellect (*“I was only given it because of knowledge I have,”* 28:78). The sūrah describes the catastrophic climax: the earth splitting open and swallowing Qārūn, his palatial estate, and his gold into the subterranean void, leaving the materialistic onlookers who had earlier envied him to reflect with horror on their folly. The epilogue (85–88) delivers the promise of return to Makkah, commands the Prophet never to be an assistant to the disbelievers, and concludes with the immortal metaphysical declaration of verse 88: *“Everything will perish except His Face. To Him belongs the judgment, and to Him you will be returned.”*

## Verse 28:1

> Ṭâ-Sĩn-Mĩm

**Three Letters That Halt the Reading**

The chapter opens with three letters pronounced one by one: Ṭā, Sīn, Mīm. Twenty-nine sūrahs of the Qur'an begin with such letter combinations — the ḥurūf muqaṭṭaʿāt, the "disjointed" or separated letters, so called because each letter is recited in isolation, name by name, rather than blended into a word. The reciter who reaches this verse does not read a word; he spells. The pause built into that spelling is part of the text's rhetoric, because every human book a seventh-century Arab knew began by introducing its author or its subject, and this Book begins by asking to be sounded letter by letter before it says anything at all. Whatever the letters mean, their effect is immediate: the listener is told at the threshold that what follows is not arranged the way human speech is arranged, and that its authority will not rest on the conventions of oratory. Fourteen letters of the Arabic alphabet appear across these openings. The commentators observed that a Book revealed in plain Arabic (26:195) should begin with bare letters of that same Arabic — a sign, several of them argued, that the challenge of the Qur'an was issued from inside the language, not from outside it.

**The Same Three Letters Open Two Moses Chapters**

Ṭā-Sīn-Mīm opens this sūrah and also Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ (26:1), and the pairing deserves attention because the two chapters are a matched pair by theme. Chapter 26 carries the longest sustained account of Moses' confrontation with Pharaoh before the assembled court, complete with the contest with the sorcerers and the exodus. Chapter 28 carries the arc that chapter 26 leaves off — the birth in a genocidal Egypt, the killing in the street, the flight to Madyan, and the commission at the burning bush. The shared letters function like a series title. Sūrah al-Naml, which falls between them, opens with Ṭā-Sīn alone and the words “Ṭâ-Sĩn. These are the verses of the Quran; the clear Book.” (27:1). The letters therefore do not float free of context: they recur where the Qur'an is about to unfold its grandest continuous narratives, and the earliest listeners could have learned to hear them as a signal that a prophetic story was about to begin.

## Verse 28:2

> These are the verses of the clear Book

**Verses That Are Also Signs**

The word translated "verses" is āyāt, and the Qur'an deliberately stretches that word across two registers. An āyah is a sign — the alternation of night and day is called a āyah, so is the creation of the heavens, so is a miracle worked through a prophet — and the recited units of the Book itself carry the same name. By calling its own verses āyāt, the Qur'an puts the words a believer recites in the same category as the cosmos: both are evidence, both demand a verdict, and neither can be treated as decoration. A reader who accepts the rising of the sun as meaningful but the recited verse as literature has already failed the category. This chapter will go on to use āyah in the narrow sense as well, for the proofs Moses will carry into Egypt; the word's range is the argument. Revelation and creation issue from one Author, so their tokens bear one name.

**A Formula That Opens the Narrative Chapters**

The sentence is a formula, and its placements are the point. Sūrah Yūsuf opens: “Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the clear Book.” (12:1). Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ opens its second verse with the same words (26:2). Both chapters are narrative giants — the first tells Joseph from the well to the throne of Egypt, the second unfurls prophet after prophet before ending in the longest account of Moses before Pharaoh. The formula does for these chapters what a seal does for a decree: it authenticates the narrator before the narration starts. Stories are exactly the genre where a community expects embellishment, where the listener assumes the teller is shaping events for effect. The Qur'an plants its credential at the gate — this telling is not folklore polished by retelling but revelation accountable to truth — and then permits itself a storyteller's art with a clear conscience.

## Verse 28:3

> We narrate to you ˹O Prophet˺ part of the story of Moses and Pharaoh in truth for people who believe

**A Part Deliberately Told**

"Part of the story" is the second claim, and it is the more striking one. The verse concedes selection openly: not the whole account, but a portion chosen for this audience at this moment. The Qur'an returns to Moses in more than twenty chapters, and the episodes shift — his infancy in this sūrah, his mission speeches in chapter 26, the wonders in chapter 20, his prayer for expansion of his chest in chapter 20 as well. Selection of this kind is a form of argument. The portion chosen here runs from a mother's despair to a fugitive's flight, and that arc is precisely the shape of the Makkan believers' own circumstances: hemmed in by a power that kills their sons, awaiting a deliverance they cannot see. A comprehensive chronicle would have buried the application; a selected episode aims it. The concession of partiality in the verse is what guards its truthfulness — no one claims the part is the whole, and the part is true as told.

**What Belief Reads in the Same Text**

Two readers can hold the same chapter and leave with different possessions. One leaves with an episode of ancient politics — an infant adrift, a palace intrigue, an extradition flight. The believer leaves with the machinery laid bare: that God's will ran beneath every scene, choosing the oppressed, turning the river into a corridor, feeding His enemy's household with the child who would end it. The Qur'an itself marks the difference later in this very sūrah, in the confession that most people do not know (28:13) — knowledge of the pattern is granted, not automatic. This verse therefore functions as a set of instructions appended to the story: read as a believer, which means read for the handling of God inside events, and the "part" told will turn out to be the part that matters.

## Verse 28:4

> Indeed, Pharaoh ˹arrogantly˺ elevated himself in the land and divided its people into ˹subservient˺ groups, one of which he persecuted, slaughtering their sons and keeping their women. He was truly one of the corruptors

**Tyranny Organized Like a Census**

The verse sketches a system, not a mood. Pharaoh "elevated himself in the land" — the classical commentators read this as rule exercised through tyranny and oppression within his own kingdom, and some connect it to his claim, recorded elsewhere in the Qur'an, to be the highest lord of his people. Then comes the machinery: the population is divided into factions, one faction is marked down for persecution, and the persecution has a scheduled procedure — sons slain, women kept alive. Elevation, classification, and culling form one sequence, and the sequence is the essence of the verse. Tyranny in the Qur'an's portrait of Egypt is not the caprice of a hot-tempered king; it is administration.

**Why the Sons and Not the Daughters**

The text gives no reason for the asymmetry, and one classical commentator drew the right conclusion from that silence: the Qur'an records the practice without explaining it because the practice requires no defense to be recognizable — it is simply what domination does. The commentators did preserve explanations from the wider tradition, the commonest being that soothsayers or a dream warned Pharaoh of an Israelite boy who would end his kingdom. Two observations weigh against that as the driving motive. First, the same decree is reissued long after Moses stands before Pharaoh: his chiefs protest the threat Moses poses and Pharaoh answers, “We will kill their sons and keep their women. We will completely dominate them.” (7:127), and again later, “Kill the sons of those who believe with him and keep their women.” (40:25). Second, the Qur'an earlier summarized the whole Egyptian period as torment that included “slaughtering your sons and keeping your women” (2:49). The repetition across decades points to collective punishment as standing policy — a tool for keeping a subject people weak — rather than a single prophecy-panic. The Bible's own account of Pharaoh's order to the midwives (Exodus 1) records the same policy shape.

## Verse 28:5

> But it was Our Will to favour those who were oppressed in the land, making them models ˹of faith˺ as well as successors

**Leadership Conferred on the Enslaved**

Two offices are bestowed, and both are startling in context. They are to be made "models ˹of faith˺" — leaders, in the classical understanding: people set foremost in faith and in commanding what is right — and they are to be made successors, heirs who inherit standing in the land. The Qur'an applies this leadership language elsewhere to prophets and to the righteous: it speaks of making guides who call to God by His permission, and of Abraham being raised to leadership for mankind. The pattern underneath the applications is consistent. Leadership in this Book is conferred on the lowly as a trust, never seized by the mighty as a prize, and the men and women who emerge from servitude carrying it testify by their origin to where authority comes from. Egypt's Israelites had spent a generation being told their lives were negotiable; the verse opens by handing them the one title Pharaoh's court could neither grant nor revoke.

**Heirs of a Land They Never Owned**

"Successors" names the inheritance, and the sūrah will not here specify its terms — but the Qur'an settles the question elsewhere, at the sea's far shore: “And ˹so˺ We made the oppressed people successors of the eastern and western lands, which We had showered with blessings.” (7:137). The inheritance was Egypt itself, its cultivated abundance and its standing — not seized as plunder by conquest's own logic, but transferred as a heir's portion after the previous holder forfeited it. The legal imagination of the verse is worth marking: a heir succeeds because the predecessor has died, and the verse has already confined Pharaoh's regime to corruption. What follows in the sūrah is the reading of a will.

## Verse 28:6

> and to establish them in the land; and through them show Pharaoh, Hamân, and their soldiers ˹the fulfilment of˺ what they feared

**Established Where They Had Been Property**

The decree now names its second term: establishment in the land. The verb behind "establish" carries the sense of being given firm footing, empowerment, and settled authority — the very things the Israelites had been denied under a system that kept them landless and expendable. The geography sharpens the point: the land in question is Egypt itself, and the Qur'an elsewhere states the outcome without disguise, that the oppressed were made successors in its eastern and western parts (7:137). A people who could not lawfully keep their own sons are promised secure standing in the capital of the man destroying them. The verse therefore completes what the previous verse began: favor decided, leadership conferred, and now permanence granted. Nothing in the sequence depends on Israelite power, because at this stage of the story they have none — which is exactly the point, since the promise is issued as pure decree while the knives are still working.

**Hāmān Beside the Throne**

The verse names Pharaoh's chief officer, and the naming is worth pausing over because Hāmān appears six times across the Qur'an — here and in verses 8 and 38 of this chapter, then in chapter 29, and twice in chapter 40, where his most famous commission is issued: “O Hamân! Build me a high tower so I may reach the pathways” (40:36) — the tower by which Pharaoh proposed to climb toward the God of Moses. The figure who can be ordered to mobilize the state's builders and materials for such a project is plainly the second man in the kingdom. That functional picture — deputy in authority, director of great public works — is what Muslim scholars who examined the identification question closely concluded the name should carry, treating Hāmān as a title of office on the pattern of "Pharaoh" itself, associated with the high priesthood of Amun and the supervision of construction. The office explains the texts; the texts are what the officeholder would have controlled.

**Six Mentions, One Assistant**

Trace Hāmān through the Qur'an and a single portrait emerges. He is named here at the politics of persecution (28:6), again when the basket reaches the palace (28:8), and at the tower project, where Pharaoh orders him to mobilize the builders (28:38). Chapter 29 lists him among the punished councils of the past, and chapter 40 names him twice in the last act — at the court's vote to kill Moses (40:24) and at the tower's commission (40:36). Six mentions, and in every one he stands at Pharaoh's shoulder. The pattern is the office: the Qur'an never shows him acting alone, because his function in the story is to be the machinery of another man's will — the executor without whom the tyrant's decrees would remain rhetoric. That is why his presence at the persecution in this verse matters as much as his presence at the tower. Persecution on the Egyptian scale required administration, and administration required a Hāmān; the verse includes "their soldiers" in the same clause for the same reason. Guilt in this Book is not confined to the man on the throne, and the sūrah's later scenes of judgment will name the assistant among the ruined precisely because the record kept him beside his principal at every stage of the crime.

## Verse 28:7

> We inspired the mother of Moses: “Nurse him, but when you fear for him, put him then into the river, and do not fear or grieve. We will certainly return him to you, and make him one of the messengers.”

**A Mother Told to Do the Unthinkable**

The command sequence is ruthless on its face: nurse him, and at the moment fear arrives, place him in the river. Every maternal instinct runs the other way, and the verse asks its first reader to feel the contradiction before resolving it. A mother's arms are the one place Egypt's decree cannot reach, and the instruction schedules their surrender for the very hour the danger peaks. Obedience here precedes explanation — she is not told how the river will help, only that she must not fear and must not grieve, and that the outcome is guaranteed twice over: he will be returned, and he will be made one of the messengers.

**Inspiration Without Prophethood**

The verb is the Qur'an's revelation verb — We inspired — yet the recipient is a woman the tradition insists was not a prophet. The word's range in the Qur'an makes room for her: the same verb describes what God cast into the hearts of Jesus' disciples, and what He communicated to the bee, teaching it where to build its home (16:68) and how to draw its provision (5:111). Classical theology sorted such matters into prophetic revelation on one side and inspiration — a casting into the heart without office — on the other. One commentator argued pointedly that even had Gabriel conveyed this message, its arrival would not have made her a prophet, since the tradition holds by consensus that she was not one. The sorting matters for the verse's tenderness. God spoke to this mother directly enough that she could act on it, without conferring rank on her, because the situation required guidance and she was the one who needed it. Revelation's economy in the Qur'an is never wasteful of rank and never stingy with help.

**Two Prohibitions, One Peace**

"Do not fear or grieve" names two different wounds, and the commentators drew the line between them: fear suffers over what may come, grief over what has gone. She is forbidden both halves of maternal agony — the anticipated loss of the child she is about to release and the mourning for him once he is gone. The pairing belongs to a refrain the Qur'an reserves for the people of the Garden, who are told of themselves that “There will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.” (3:170). Placing that grammar in a mother's ear, on the riverbank, at the hour of surrender, amounts to handing her the peace of the saved in advance of the world to come.

**The River as a Road**

The river that carries death decrees for her people becomes, by command, the corridor of rescue. Chapter 20 tells the same moment with one more piece of equipment — “Put him into a chest, then put it into the river.” (20:39) — and the commentaries treat the two accounts as one narrative told at different resolutions, the chest named there and the river here.

## Verse 28:8

> And ˹it so happened that˺ Pharaoh’s people picked him up, only to become their enemy and source of grief. Surely Pharaoh, Hamân, and their soldiers were sinful

**The Rescue Delivered to the Enemy's Door**

Of all the houses on the river, the basket drifts to the one under whose seal the killing decree went out. The verse does not flinch from the fact that the rescue was carried out by the enemy's own household — the people who pulled him up were the people whose policy had condemned him. Providence in this sūrah works by capture: the hunters are made the couriers, the wolf den is made the nursery, and the closer the child is brought to the center of Egyptian power, the more completely that power is penetrated. Any other deliverance — a fisherman's family, a village upstream — would have saved the boy and accomplished nothing more. This deliverance saves the boy and installs him, at state expense, at the exact point where Egypt's decisions are made. The verse's opening phrase, rendered "˹it so happened that˺", concedes the human view: from the riverbank it looked like chance, a floating box, a palace servant's idle curiosity. The verse then states the other view in the grammar of purpose.

**Lovability as Strategy**

How does a condemned Israelite infant survive intake into the household that kills his kind? Chapter 20 supplies the mechanism, spoken by God to Moses long afterward: “And I blessed you with lovability from Me ˹O Moses˺ so that you would be brought up under My ˹watchful˺ Eye.” (20:39). The charm that disarmed the palace was not the baby's luck; it was a deployed attribute, a sweetness assigned for the purpose. The detail repays attention because it shows the plan operating at the level of feelings. Pharaoh's household was not overpowered into mercy — no plague had yet fallen, no sign had been shown. They were moved, and the moving was managed.

## Verse 28:9

> Pharaoh’s wife said ˹to him˺, “˹This baby is˺ a source of joy for me and you. Do not kill him. Perhaps he may be useful to us or we may adopt him as a son.” They were unaware ˹of what was to come˺

**One Speech Against a Royal Mood**

The verse catches the moment of decision, and it is a woman who turns it. The household's default procedure for an Israelite boy was the knife; into that procedure the wife inserts a sentence that reframes the entire object: “˹This baby is˺ a source of joy for me and you” (28:9). Her grammar does the work — the pronouns fold Pharaoh into the blessing, making the child's survival a shared interest rather than her private sentiment, and the closing plea is a command to the king himself: do not kill him. One classical report imagines his reply — a comfort for you, but for me, no — and whether or not he said it, the scene it imagines is exactly the one the verse shows: the family's men see a threat, the wife sees a son. [UNVERIFIED: the exchange and the report that Pharaoh would have been guided had he echoed her hope are transmitted in the commentary tradition without a checkable collection number.] The political fact underneath the dialogue is that extermination policies run on unanimity. One voice inside the palace, placed to speak at the moment of intake, was enough to break it.

**Āsiyah in the Company of Four**

The Qur'an does not name Pharaoh's wife, but the tradition does, and it ranks her without embarrassment. The Prophet himself named her among the four women who stand at the head of the women of the worlds — Maryam, Khadījah, Fāṭimah, and Āsiyah — in a narration carried by Muslim (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2430). The Qur'an elsewhere records her own prayer, made under torture in her husband's house: “My Lord! Build me a house in Paradise near You, deliver me from Pharaoh and his ˹evil˺ doing, and save me from the wrongdoing people.” (66:11). Read together, the two texts give her a biography in outline: a believer living inside the tyranny, dissenting at the decisive moment in this verse, and finally asking for delivery from her own household. Her sainthood was not a withdrawal from that household into safety; it was exercised precisely at the point of intake, when her word was the last wall between one Israelite child and the state's blade.

## Verse 28:10

> And the heart of Moses’ mother ached so much that she almost gave away his identity, had We not reassured her heart in order for her to have faith ˹in Allah’s promise˺

**A Heart Emptied to Its Walls**

The Arabic says the mother's heart became empty, and the translators reach for "ached" to carry the weight of a word that pictures a vessel scraped bare. The classical commentators read the emptiness precisely: a heart emptied of everything except the remembrance of her son and the grief of separation — no room left for patience, strategy, or self-command. Some noted that the verb can carry a morning sense, so that the verse would say morning found her heart already emptied, the sleepless night compressed into two words. The state described is the far edge of maternal endurance, and the verse does not treat it as failure. A mother who felt nothing at the river would have been the failure; the text's honesty about the emptying is what makes the next clause — the almost-disclosure — comprehensible. She was not weak. She was a woman holding the two heaviest things a heart can hold, the command of God and the body of her child, and the verse reports what that weighed.

---

The emptiness of the mother's heart is filled in the next verses: God restores him to her so that her eye may be comforted and she not grieve, which is stated as the purpose of the whole arrangement (28:13). The Book gives the same account in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā, where the child is returned and the sister follows him (20:39–40), and the mother's state before the restoration is described in the same language of a heart that has become empty. The verse's mention of her near-disclosure is what makes the secrecy of the whole plan visible: she was held back by the strengthening, not by her own resolve.

## Verse 28:11

> And she said to his sister, “Keep track of him!” So she watched him from a distance, while they were unaware

**The Sūrah of the Tracking**

This chapter bears the name al-Qaṣaṣ — the twenty-eighth sūrah, Makkan, eighty-eight verses, titled in English "The Story." The Arabic root behind the name carries a double sense that this verse happens to stage: qaṣṣ is to follow a thing closely, to trace it. It is also to narrate what one has traced. The sūrah is both a following and a telling, and its eleventh verse hands the following to a little girl. The mother who cannot walk to the river herself sends the next best thing, a daughter unremarkable enough to move through a slave society's streets unexamined, and the girl's assignment — keep track of him — is the first act of narration's raw material in the chapter. Before the story can be told, someone must be able to see it. The Qur'an's title so points back to a child on a surveillance errand, which is as good a statement as any of the book's habit of loading its greatest weights onto the figures the world's stories use for scenery.

**The Root That Names the Chapter and Runs Through Its Story**

The verb on the girl's assignment belongs to the root that gives this sūrah its name. Qaṣṣ in Arabic is to follow a thing closely, to pursue its trail — and, from that, to relate what the pursuit found. The chapter is called al-Qaṣaṣ, "the following, the stories," and this verse is where the root's two senses meet in one command: the sister is told to keep track of him, and what she tracks becomes the story. The root returns at the other end of the episode, when the fugitive stands at a well in Madyan and "told him his whole story" (28:25) — the trail followed in Egypt becomes the account given in a stranger's house, and the chapter's title brackets the whole arc.

## Verse 28:12

> And We had caused him to refuse all wet-nurses at first, so his sister suggested, “Shall I direct you to a family who will bring him up for you and take good care of him?”

**A Problem Manufactured for a Purpose**

The design inside the difficulty is the verse's theology. The refusal does not merely save Moses from an unknown nurse; it manufactures the one vacancy in Egypt that only his mother can fill, and it manufactures it inside Pharaoh's own household, on Pharaoh's own initiative. Had the baby nursed contentedly from the first woman offered, he would have been raised to weaning by strangers, and the promise made to his mother — We will certainly return him to you (28:7) — would have needed another path. The refusal is that path. The pattern deserves naming because the sūrah will repeat it: providence in this chapter usually advances by arranging a problem, not by removing one. The mother's emptied heart, the sister's watching, and now the baby's hunger are all weights, and each weight is a hinge. The Qur'an's God does not wait for convenient openings; He schedules the inconvenience and then stands in it.

**The Sister's Proposal**

Into the manufactured vacancy walks the only person who knows the solution: a girl the court has never seen, suggesting a family — hers — in words that promise competence and discretion. Her brother's own account in chapter 20 compresses the moment: “when your sister came along and proposed” (20:40) is how the Qur'an later describes her entrance to Moses himself.

**The First Sign Was a Refusal**

Moses' life will fill with signs — a staff that becomes a serpent, a hand that shines, a sea that opens — but the first of his signs is recorded here, and it is completely negative: a baby who will not eat. God's power is displayed as an inability, placed in the smallest body in the story, and the palace feels it as frustration without ever learning its name. The pattern is one the sūrah will keep: the instrument Egypt despises carries the blow Egypt cannot answer. A slave nation's son will judge its king; a stammering tongue (20:27) will out-argue its priesthood; a shepherd's staff will swallow its sorcery. Strength and significance run on different tracks in this Book, and the verse marks the crossing point early, in a hunger that no wealth could relieve. The wet-nurses of Pharaoh's capital — the finest milk Egypt could buy — were defeated by an infant's turned face, and the defeat was total: not one succeeded, not once. What the court read as illness was obedience, and what the mother read as God's doing (28:13) was already, in the plan's own order, the opening sign of a prophetic career. The refusal also protected the secret at both ends. Had any nurse succeeded, the palace would have raised a child with no tie to Israel; because none succeeded, the one woman who could feed him was sent for, and the search for a nurse became the search that ended in his mother's arms.

## Verse 28:13

> This is how We returned him to his mother so that her heart would be put at ease, and not grieve, and that she would know that Allah’s promise is ˹always˺ true. But most people do not know

**The Promise Kept in a Feeding**

"so We returned him" — the verse announces its own key: the return promised at the riverbank (28:7) has been carried out, and the verse's opening word, so, invites the reader to study the mechanism. The promise was kept not by an angel striding into the palace with a verdict, but by a nursing arrangement that every participant understood as ordinary commerce. The mother is reunited with her son lawfully, publicly, and at Pharaoh's expense; the tyrant who could not tolerate one Israelite child now cannot function without him. The classical commentators identify God's Promise here as precisely this return, foretold when she was told to release him. The shape of the fulfillment is the lesson: no part of the promise was scaled down to fit the method. She received the child whole, alive, and restored — but the delivery route ran through the enemy's kitchen, which is where heaven's postal service apparently keeps its doors.

**Comfort as a Stated Purpose**

The verse does something unusual with its grammar: it lists the mother's healing as an outcome in its own terms. Later in the Qur'an, the mother of Jesus receives the same solicitude under the palm tree, told to eat, drink, and be comforted when her labor is upon her (19:23–26).

**The Line of Knowledge Between Two Houses**

The verse splits its own cast into two kinds of knowers. On one side stands the mother, whose knowledge was purchased — a promise believed in terror, kept in silence, and then confirmed in her arms until she knew it was true. On the other side stands the palace, which handled the same events and remained exactly where it started: unaware (28:9), unseeing, certain that its household had acquired a foundling. Nothing about the events themselves distinguished the two houses; the basket, the hunger, and the nursing contract were visible to both. What differed was the capacity to read them — the mother read because she had been given the promise, and the palace could not read because its whole way of seeing had no category for God's action in the world. The closing verdict — most people do not know — therefore draws a line through the story that no observer standing inside it could see. The sūrah itself is the instrument that moves its reader across that line: by telling what the palace could not see, it trains the believing audience in the reading skill the verse says most people lack.

---

## Verse 28:14

> And when he reached full strength and maturity, We gave him wisdom and knowledge. This is how We reward the good-doers

**Forty, the Prophets' Hour**

The tradition fixed the numbers anyway. The commentators report thirty-three years for the first term and forty for the second, and forty is the age the Qur'an itself ties elsewhere to spiritual adulthood, in the verse commanding gratitude to parents: “when the child reaches their prime at the age of forty” (46:15). Forty is also, in Muslim tradition, the age at which Muhammad received the first revelation, and the pattern was read backward into the prophets generally — the age at which a man's judgment can carry a message. The precision should be held honestly: the commentators themselves disagreed on the split between the two terms, and the verse does not state a number. What the number-40 tradition preserves is the pattern — a long, unremarkable maturation before commission, prophethood arriving not to teenagers but to settled men. Moses at the burning bush will be a grown man with decades of formation behind him, and this verse is where the Qur'an notes the formation's completion.

**The Same Payment Joseph Received**

The closing formula — This is how We reward the good-doers — is one of the Qur'an's refrain-verbs, sounded at the close of several prophetic stories, and its most striking repetition is in the previous sūrah's account of Joseph: “And when he reached maturity, We gave him wisdom and knowledge. This is how We reward the good-doers.” (12:22). The near-identity is deliberate. Joseph — sold, enslaved, imprisoned — and Moses — hunted at birth, exiled by a killing — arrive at the same station by the same road: trials borne without corruption, followed by wisdom and knowledge as the recompense. The refrain also folds the mother back into the reward, since the commentators heard in the good-doers her endurance by the river; her son's formation is the verse's payment to her. The template the two verses share is the Qur'an's standing answer to the question of what virtue gets: not immunity from the trial, but a self equal to what comes after it.

---

## Verse 28:15

> ˹One day˺ he entered the city unnoticed by its people. There he found two men fighting: one of his own people, and the other of his enemies. The man from his people called to him for help against his foe. So Moses punched him, causing his death. Moses cried, “This is from Satan’s handiwork. He is certainly a sworn, misleading enemy.”

**The Hour the Streets Emptied**

The verse times its scene with care: he entered the city at a moment of heedlessness, which the commentators most often read as midday, when the streets sleep in hot countries. The hour mattered because of who Moses was by then — a man raised inside Pharaoh's own house, identified with the court that owned the city, yet walking into its heart unrecognized. The commentators divided over how the non-recognition worked: some held that he was still understood to belong to Pharaoh's retinue, even called the son of Pharaoh. The heedlessness lay in the city's stupor at that hour; others held that he had already broken with the court and the people no longer remembered him.

**"This Is from Satan's Handiwork"**

His verdict on the event names a third combatant: “This is from Satan's handiwork. He is certainly a sworn, misleading enemy.” (28:15). Moses does not blame the Egyptian's oppression, his own anger, or Israel's misery — he locates the fight's true author, the one who had engineered a quarrel so that the palace's favorite would become a killer in an ungoverned hour. The diagnosis carries the lesson the scene was built to teach. Partisanship — the raw summons of my man against their man — is the door Satan uses, because it recruits good people into escalated violence without ever presenting them a principle. Moses, the most powerful young man in Egypt, was managed in one afternoon into blood on his hands by his own side's emergency. He saw it instantly, and named it instantly, and the accuracy of his self-indictment is the first evidence in the sūrah of the wisdom verse 14 had just reported given to him.

---

## Verse 28:16

> He pleaded, “My Lord! I have definitely wronged my soul, so forgive me.” So He forgave him, ˹for˺ He is indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Answered Before the Plea Cooled**

The verse's hinge is its smallest clause: So He forgave him. No interval is recorded, no second request, no penance scheduled — the answer is carried in the same breath as the plea, and the closing names carry the reason: He is indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. The Qur'an states the principle behind the scene as a standing rule: “Whoever commits evil or wrongs themselves then seeks Allah's forgiveness will certainly find Allah All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” (4:110). The Prophet's own words carry the same rule into the details of a life: the one who repents from sin is like one who has no sin (Sunan Ibn Mājah 4250, graded ḥasan by al-Albānī). What the verse adds to the principle is the speed. Forgiveness, in this scene, is not a bureaucratic outcome that follows processed remorse; it is the immediate posture of the One whose name the plea invoked. The future prophet learned at the scene of his worst act that his Lord's mercy did not need to be persuaded.

**The Same Confession Decades Later**

The Qur'an has Moses retell this event once, face to face with Pharaoh, and the retelling is faithful to the confession. Asked about the killing, the older Moses answers: “I did it then, lacking guidance.” (26:20) — an admission that names the same event, claims the act without excuse, and locates the failing where the original confession located it. He adds in the next breath that he fled from them in fear and that his Lord then gave him wisdom and made him a messenger (26:21), which is this sūrah's sequence told in the first person. The consistency across the two tellings is itself evidence for the unity of the account: a fabricated tradition would have tidied the hero's worst hour. Instead, the Qur'an lets the man who will carry God's message to the greatest court on earth stand forever on the record as a man who killed, confessed in one sentence, and was forgiven in half of one.

---

## Verse 28:17

> Moses pledged, “My Lord! For all Your favours upon me, I will never side with the wicked.”

**The Vow's Echo at the Summit**

Gratitude returns at the highest point of Moses' story, and God Himself raises it. At the commissioning, when Moses is elevated above all others by messages and direct speech, the command that seals the honor is: “So hold firmly to what I have given you and be grateful.” (7:144). The circle closes on the same duty this verse records him volunteering before he was ever commissioned. The beginning of his prophetic career and its exalted middle are bracketed by one instruction — be grateful — first self-imposed, then divinely commanded. The arrangement teaches what the Qur'an consistently holds about the relationship of the two: the gratitude God commands at the summit is not a new demand but the ratification of what the grateful do unprompted at the bottom. Moses' pledge in this verse, made by a fugitive with blood on his conscience, is the same act God will later command as a honor — which is as plain a statement as the Qur'an gives of where obedience and thankfulness meet.

**Allegiance Declared Before Any Office**

The pledge is remarkable for its timing: Moses declares his side before he holds any commission, before a single miracle, before God has addressed him even once. Identity in this Book precedes office — the two parties, the people of God and the party of the wicked, are joined by choice long before the choice carries authority or rank. Moses enlists in the first while holding the second's citizenship, and the enlistment costs him everything the palace offered. The vow also prefigures, with almost schematic precision, the role he will grow into: the man who promises never to support the wrongdoers will spend his prophetic career as the wrongdoers' named opponent, confronting the most complete wrongdoer his age produced. And the next morning shows the vow's fragility in his own hands — summoned again into the same quarrel, he moves to strike, and stops only because the enemy's tongue stops him (28:19).

---

## Verse 28:18

> And so Moses became fearful, watching out in the city, when suddenly the one who sought his help the day before cried out to him again for help. Moses rebuked him, “Indeed, you are clearly a trouble-maker.”

**Morning After a Killing**

The verse opens on the state the killing left behind: fear, and vigilance — he watched out in the city. The commentators read the vigilance precisely: he was looking to see what consequences the death would produce, whether it had been seen, what the state would do. The pairing of the two words is the psychology of a man with a secret: fear supplies the dread, vigilance the scanning. Nothing in the verse suggests his fear was cowardice; a man afraid of nothing after killing someone in Pharaoh's capital would be a fool, since the state's response to an Egyptian's death at an Israelite's hands would fall on Israel's whole quarter. The verse also lets a day pass without narration — a day in which the death must have been discovered — and the silence prices the interval: Moses spent it inside the city he could no longer trust, at the center of the power he had now injured, waiting for the knock that had not yet come.

**A Rebuke Aimed at the Wrong Target**

Moses' answer is a sentence of blame: “Indeed, you are clearly a trouble-maker.” (28:18). The accuracy of the rebuke deserves to be tested, because the verse quietly complicates it. The man was a trouble in the sense that his quarrels kept summoning Moses into the open; but the death that made yesterday dangerous was delivered by Moses' own fist, and the verse has already — in Moses' own mouth — assigned that act to Satan's handiwork (28:15). The rebuke therefore tells only half the truth, and the half it omits is the one about the speaker. The Bible's parallel scene puts a gentler question in the same encounter — why would you strike your fellow? — aimed at the fighter, with Moses as bystander; the Qur'an makes Moses the accused rescuer and lets his irritation land on the client instead. The commentators were careful to note what the verse does not say: that the rebuke settles the guilt. Its function in the story is to show the friendship between the two men fraying under pressure, and pressure, in this sūrah, is the appointed instrument of revelation.

---

## Verse 28:19

> Then when Moses was about to lay his hands on their foe, the enemy said, “O Moses! Do you intend to kill me as you killed a man yesterday? You only want to be a tyrant in the land. You do not intend to make peace!”

**Yesterday Spoken Aloud**

The sentence that breaks Moses' cover is a question: “Do you intend to kill me as you killed a man yesterday?” (28:19). Its power lies in its tense. The killing had been private, unwitnessed as far as Moses knew, buried in a hour of urban sleep — and here it stands in the open street, packaged as an accusation, knowledge moving faster than any report Moses could imagine. The commentators traced the leak to the quarrel itself: the argument in verse 18 was loud enough to be heard, and suspicion did the rest. The scene teaches the physics of secrets: a deed done in heedlessness does not stay in the hour of heedlessness; it ripens in daylight until it speaks. The man's phrasing also does something crueler than exposure — it frames the killing as Moses' established character, "as you killed," a precedent rather than an incident. An accidental death, once spoken of this way, becomes a biography, and in a state like Pharaoh's Egypt, biography is enough to sentence.

---

The sentence names what Moses believed to be buried: the killing of the Egyptian the day before, which the sūrah has described as done in a moment of anger and followed by his own plea for forgiveness (28:15–16). The report reaching the town is the reason for the flight that follows: the man who warned him came running from the far end of the city with the news that the chiefs were deliberating on his execution (28:20). The Book's account keeps the consequence attached to the act — the first killing did not go unnoticed, and the second was prevented by it — which is why Moses leaves Egypt as a man on the run.

## Verse 28:20

> And there came a man, rushing from the farthest end of the city. He said, “O Moses! The chiefs are actually conspiring against you to put you to death, so leave ˹the city˺. I really advise you ˹to do so˺.”

**A Runner Outpaces the Council**

The verse opens with motion: a man comes rushing from the city's farthest end. The distance is the point — the warning travels faster than the process it warns about, because councils move at the speed of procedure while urgency moves at the speed of legs. The men the runner names are the malaʾ, the notables — the word the Qur'an uses for the leadership class that convenes against its prophets with grim regularity: the council around the Queen of Sheba, the chiefs of Moses' own later opponents, the assembly around Hūd and Ṣāliḥ, the notables of Shuʿayb's people, the courtiers plotting around Joseph's king. The class is consistent across the Book: not the mob, which can be moved, but the committee, which decides — landholders, priests, courtiers whose position depends on the arrangement a prophet has come to disturb.

**Who Was the Running Man**

The verse leaves him unnamed, and the commentators filled the silence in two directions: some said a believer from Pharaoh's own people, others an Israelite. The first suggestion recalls a later figure — the believing man of Pharaoh's household who, when the court votes to kill Moses at the height of his mission, stands up to argue the case (40:28), introduced there as “A believing man from Pharaoh's people, who was hiding his faith” (40:28). Most commentators kept the two apart, and rightly: the believer of chapter 40 speaks openly in council at the crisis of the plagues, years after this scene, while this runner's act is covert and urgent. What the two figures share is the lesson of the verse: God's protection of His servants travels through people whose names history never asked for. The man is defined completely by function — he runs, he warns, he advises — and the sūrah grants him four clauses of direct speech and no identity, which is its own commentary on how deliverance is staffed.

---

## Verse 28:21

> So Moses left the city in a state of fear and caution, praying, “My Lord! Deliver me from the wrongdoing people.”

**Exile From the City That Raised Him**

The man who owned no city leaves the only city he has ever known. Moses had been Egypt's adopted prince — housed, schooled, and identified with the court — and the verse records him walking out of it in fear, watching over his shoulder, a fugitive from the administration that considered him one of its own. The classical commentators connected the two descriptions — fearful here and fearful in the street scene before — as the same condition carried to its conclusion: he is concerned that Pharaoh's forces will catch and kill him. The completeness of the loss is easy to pass over. In one week Moses lost his standing, his cover, his client, and his country, and the sūrah spends no words mourning any of it.

The Book explains the crime that caused the flight: Moses struck an Egyptian and killed him while defending a man of his own people, and the next day the same man asked him for help again, and the plot against Moses formed when it became known (28:15–20). The sūrah's account is careful about Moses' responsibility, describing his own words of regret and his plea for forgiveness in the same passage (28:16), so that the exile that begins here is a consequence of an act he had already repented. The Book records his departure at the same time in Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ, where he leaves fearing that they will call him a liar (26:21).

---

## Verse 28:22

> And as he made his way towards Midian, he said, “I trust my Lord will guide me to the right way.”

**The Right Way Running Through Strange Country**

The prayer's last phrase — the right way — will be answered within verses by a well, two women, an old man, and a marriage contract, which is the verse's own commentary: God's answer to prayers for guidance is usually a scene, not a syllable. Moses asked to be guided, and guidance arrived as a sequence of ordinary events that happened to be perfectly aimed — each one visible only as it occurred, none of them announced. The pattern is the sūrah's standing doctrine of providence, already displayed at the river and the palace, now operating in open desert.

The Book reports what the journey produced: he arrives at the well of Midian and defends two women with their flock (28:23–24), and he is taken into the household of their father, who is identified by the commentators as the prophet Shuʿayb. The prayer of this verse is answered in a way the reader can trace, but the answer is not announced as a miracle: it comes as a well, a crowd, two women, and a man who offers a daughter in marriage and a term of years of work (28:25–28). The sūrah's providence is expressed in the ordinary sequence of events, and the verse records the prophet's trust before any of them had happened.

---

## Verse 28:23

> When he arrived at the well of Midian, he found a group of people watering ˹their herds˺. Apart from them, he noticed two women holding back ˹their herd˺. He asked ˹them˺, “What is the problem?” They replied, “We cannot water ˹our animals˺ until the ˹other˺ shepherds are done, for our father is a very old man.”

**The Well as a Crossroads of Society**

The scene at the well is drawn from life as every desert society knew it: a water point, a queue of shepherds, animals pressing forward, and the day's whole economy arranged around the hours when the water is drawn. Wells in this world were more than infrastructure — they were meeting places, marketplaces, and the setting where strangers became guests, which is exactly what this one is about to become. The Qur'an stages the moment with documentary economy: a community of people watering, and apart from them, two women standing back with their flock. The camera angle is the fugitive's own — a man entering a strange country registers the water first, the crowd second, and the anomaly third. The anomaly is the two women waiting, and the verse's whole drama will grow out of that single observed exception. Providence, in this sūrah's method, rarely interrupts a scene; it hides inside one until the right question is asked.

---

The meeting at the well is the turning point of Moses' exile, and the sūrah gives the whole sequence in the verses that follow: the watering of the flocks for the two women, the invitation to their father's house, the offer of marriage, and the contract of service (28:24–28). The place is the country of Midian, where the same sūrah will later place the well of the messengers in the story of Shuʿayb's people, and the Book elsewhere tells of Moses' marriage to the daughter of the man of Midian and the years he served (20:40 and the tradition's accounts). The scene is built on ordinary labour before it becomes the beginning of prophethood.

## Verse 28:24

> So he watered ˹their herd˺ for them, then withdrew to the shade and prayed, “My Lord! I am truly in ˹desperate˺ need of whatever provision You may have in store for me.”

**FaQīr: The Grammar of Need**

The key word is faqīr, the state of dependence and poverty — used in the Qur'an both for material want and for the soul's standing before God, and Moses applies it to himself in the first person. The prayer's structure repays attention: he does not name what he needs. No bread, no shelter, no protection, no destination — need of whatever good You send down, addressed to a God who knows the inventory better than the beggar does. The commentators heard in the open clause the full condition of the creature: the man is not reporting a shortage; he is declaring a relationship — dependent, on watch, dependent on the Sender for the definition of "good" as much as for its delivery. The prayer is therefore the exact opposite of Egypt's theology, where Pharaoh proclaimed himself the source of his people's goods.

**The Shepherd's Curriculum**

The Prophet said: Allah did not send any prophet but that he herded sheep — and when his companions asked whether it applied to him too, he answered that he had tended the sheep of the people of Makkah for a few qīrāṭs, a small wage (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2262). Moses is the hadith's standing illustration: the man who will shepherd a nation out of an empire learns the trade on an old man's flock in Madyan, for wages of room and bread. The curriculum is not accidental in this Book. Shepherding trains precisely the muscles a prophet needs — patience with the slow, protection of the weak from predators, endurance of solitude, and authority exercised without wealth or force.

---

## Verse 28:25

> Then one of the two women came to him, walking bashfully. She said, “My father is inviting you so he may reward you for watering ˹our animals˺ for us.” When Moses came to him and told him his whole story, the old man said, “Have no fear! You are ˹now˺ safe from the wrongdoing people.”

**An Invitation That Arrives Walking Bashfully**

The verse follows the messenger before it delivers the message: one of the two women came to him, walking bashfully. The adverb is doing real work — it describes a gait that keeps its distance from every impropriety, and the tradition read it as the walk of a free woman managing a public approach to a strange man with modesty intact. The wording joins a hadith the Prophet himself spoke about this very scene: he described Moses as an extremely shy man who covered himself completely. Told how God once cleared him of slander when a stone ran off with his clothes and he chased it unclad before his people (reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim from Abū Hurayrah). The Prophet's Moses is the same man this verse shows — shyness as a constitution, not a mood — and the daughter who approaches him carries the matching quality. The invitation is issued by the shy to the shy, at arm's length, in daylight, for a stated and lawful purpose: wages owed for work done.

---

## Verse 28:26

> One of the two daughters suggested, “O my dear father! Hire him. The best man for employment is definitely the strong and trustworthy ˹one˺.”

**The Daughter Who Makes the Case**

For the second time in the scene, one of the two women takes the initiative — and this time the initiative is a hiring recommendation made to her own father. The verse records no embarrassment in the structure: the household's employment decision is argued, out loud, by a daughter, and the father proceeds to act on her analysis. In the sūrah's ledger of who acts and who is acted upon, the pattern is now unmistakable: the women of this story move its plot — the mother who released her son to the river, the sister who tracked him and spoke up in the palace, the queen who stayed the first decree. Now the daughter who turns a guest into an employee. The men, by contrast, are mostly institutions — a court, a council, a decree. The Qur'an's casting here was countercultural enough to stay startling, and it is framed not as exception but as competence: her argument is simply better than anyone else's in the scene.

---

## Verse 28:27

> The old man proposed, “I wish to marry one of these two daughters of mine to you, provided that you stay in my service for eight years. If you complete ten, it will be ˹a favour˺ from you, but I do not wish to make it difficult for you. Allah willing, you will find me an agreeable man.”

**A Dowry Paid in Years**

The old man moves from hiring to marriage in a single speech. The contract's architecture is one of the Qur'an's most instructive legal sketches: a daughter's hand offered against eight years of service, extendable to ten at the groom's own free choice, with the extra two expressly labeled a favor rather than an obligation. The structure answers a practical problem — Moses owns nothing with which to pay a dower — and solves it in the currency he does own: labor. The father converts his own wages claim into a marriage gift, taking what Moses owes him as an employee and gifting its worth to his daughter as her settlement. Islamic law would later name such arrangements precisely, and the Prophet himself ratified the principle in a reported case when a penniless man asked to marry: he asked what Qur'an the man knew. Married the couple for the sake of what he had memorized (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5087) — the dower measured in what the groom truly has. Moses has strength and trustworthiness; the old man prices the first and banks the second.

---

## Verse 28:28

> Moses responded, “˹Then˺ it is ˹settled˺ between you and I. Whichever term I fulfill, there will be no ˹further˺ obligation on me. And Allah is a Witness to what we say.”

**A Fugitive Who Negotiates**

The answer is a contract clause, and its first lesson is the man delivering it: the refugee with nothing negotiates anyway. Moses accepts the offer — but he adds a term of his own, and the term is the whole speech: whichever of the two terms I complete, let there be no blame and no injustice toward me. The commentators noted the key word's range — ʿudwān names both aggression and injustice — so the clause protects him from either party's overreach: no guilt for taking the shorter term, no penalty for declining the longer. The man who has lived under a system where the strong rewrite terms at will builds the protection into his own contract's text. Slavery in Egypt was, at bottom, the impossibility of saying no to a term; Moses' first act of freedom is to say, in the plainest words available: the contract binds only as written. The fugitive who learned law in Pharaoh's courts writes his liberation's grammar in one sentence at a well in Madyan.

---

## Verse 28:29

> When Moses had completed the term and was travelling with his family, he spotted a fire on the side of Mount Ṭûr. He said to his family, “Stay here, ˹for˺ I have spotted a fire. Perhaps from there I can bring you some directions or a torch from the fire so you may warm yourselves.”

**The Term Completed**

The contract runs its course in half a sentence: when Moses had completed the term. The commentators differed on which term — some held he served the eight and chose to complete the full ten; others counted ten and then additional years; the sūrah itself records only completion, and its silence lets the point stand: the man who insisted on bounded terms served them out whole. What the decade produced is visible in the clause that follows — he was travelling with his family: a wife from the household of a prophet, children, flocks, and the settled identity of a householder. The stateless fugitive of verse 22 owns a family now, and the road he travels is the road back toward Egypt, which he left in fear and approaches without yet knowing why. The sūrah's method of compression — three decades in half a verse, a decade in a clause — teaches its reader to measure formation, not duration: the term's worth is in what it built, and the building is done.

---

The sūrah has already recorded the two terms of the agreement with Moses' father-in-law — eight years, and ten if he wished to complete the term — which is why the verse says he completed the term without specifying its length (28:27–28). The Book records the years of service in the same story in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā, where the mother is told that God has arranged the matter (20:37–40), and the fire on the mountain then opens the call to prophethood (28:30). The chapter's method is to pass over the ten years in half a sentence and to dwell on the night of the fire, since the account's purpose is the mission rather than the biography.

## Verse 28:30

> But when he came to it, he was called from the bush in the sacred ground to the right side of the valley: “O Moses! It is truly I. I am Allah—the Lord of all worlds

**Called From the Tree**

The fire he climbed toward answers him by name: he was called from the bush — the tree of the blessed spot, at the valley's right side. The geography is recorded with the care of an eyewitness: a specific slope, a specific side, a specific tree, and a voice issuing from it. The commentators held fast to the plain reading — God addressed His servant from the tree, as the verse says — and the earliest tradition wrapped the site in the vocabulary of sanctuary: the ground is holy because the address happened there, not the address because the ground was special. A bush burning without being consumed is the scene as later scripture frames it; the Qur'an's own frame is the call itself, and its first word is a man's name.

**God Spoke to Moses Directly**

The Qur'an states elsewhere, in one unadorned clause, what this verse stages: to Moses, Allah spoke directly (4:164). The directness is the event's uniqueness — among all the messengers, Moses is singled out for speech without intermediary, and the later tradition enshrined him as the one God addressed face to face. The theologians of Islam would fight for centuries over the nature of that speech — whether God's word is created or uncreated, an argument that flared two centuries after the Prophet over the Qur'an's own status — and this verse stood near the center of the storm: if the words from the tree are God's speech, what exactly did Moses' ears receive?

---

## Verse 28:31

> Now, throw down your staff!” But when he saw it slithering like a snake, he ran away without looking back. ˹Allah reassured him,˺ “O Moses! Draw near, and have no fear. You are perfectly secure

**The Staff's Career Ahead**

What Moses sees as terror, the sūrah's later verses will display as instrument. The same staff, cast at the same command in Pharaoh's court, will devour the sorcerers' illusions (7:117), and God's instruction in that hour tells the whole design: cast what is in your right hand, and it will swallow what they have fabricated (20:69). The trembling serpent on the mountain is therefore the audition of the court scene — Moses must first be mastered by the instrument he will master, because a messenger who has not feared his own sign cannot wield it before the mighty. The staff's biography in this Book runs from a shepherd's prop, to a serpent on holy ground, to the wonder that breaks Egypt's magicians, to the rod that splits the sea; and it begins here, in the gap between a man's running heels and his Lord's recall. The verse teaches what every subsequent sign will confirm: in God's hand, the ordinary is a deposited weapon, waiting for the command that arms it.

**The Shepherd Meets the Shepherd**

Beneath the drama, the scene completes an identification that the sūrah has been assembling for two verses: the Lord who calls Himself the Lord of the worlds is addressing a man whose decade has been spent keeping sheep — and the first exercise of the call is handling a stray, of sorts, that will not stay put. The Prophet's words about the prophets' common trade — every one of them herded sheep (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2262) — find their deepest illustration here: the skills of the shepherd are exactly the skills the mission will demand, patience with the stubborn, protection of the scattered. The habit of putting the flock's life above one's own comfort. The serpent that spooked him and the recall that steadied him are the first lesson in that second shepherding: from this night forward, the flock will be a nation, the wolves will be Pharaoh's soldiers, and the staff will be everything it turned out to be. The scene's arithmetic of fear deserves the closing word: the object that terrified him and the object he will carry into court are the same length of wood. What changed was neither the staff nor the shepherd; it was the command's author. A thing in Moses' hand is a stick; a thing thrown at God's word is a sign — and the whole distance between superstition and revelation is measured in whose order set the thing moving.

## Verse 28:32

> Now put your hand through ˹the opening of˺ your collar, it will come out ˹shining˺ white, unblemished. And cross your arms tightly to calm your fears. These are two proofs from your Lord to Pharaoh and his chiefs. They have truly been a rebellious people.”

**The Hand in the Bosom**

The second sign is administered to Moses' own body: put your hand inside your collar and draw it out white, without blemish. The commentators identified the garment's opening — the jayb, the collar slit of the shirt — and noted the parallel account in chapter 20, where the hand is drawn toward the side; the two wordings describe the same gesture, the hand entering at the chest's opening and emerging transformed. The sign's design is exact: not a wound healed, not a limb restored, but purity produced where no defect was visible — skin shining white without the blight that whiteness usually means in affliction. The sign spoke the language of Egypt's own obsessions: a court that read omens in bodies was handed a body that showed the Giver's command over its substance. And it was given first to Moses in private — the messenger sees the proof on his own arm before the audience sees it on the mountain of their pride, because signs are entrusted before they are displayed.

---

The two signs given to Moses are the staff and the hand, and the sūrah of Ṭā Hā lists them together when Moses is asked what is in his right hand and is answered with the command to throw it down (20:17–21). The same signs are worked before Pharaoh in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf, where the staff becomes a serpent and the hand turns white (7:107–108), and the verse here adds the instruction to press the arm to the body — the commentators explain the gesture as the way a startled man holds his arm to himself, since the whiteness of the hand could be mistaken for a disease. The signs given to one prophet belong to the same purpose as the sign asked of Muhammad in this sūrah's opening: the proof is granted by God alone.

## Verse 28:33

> Moses appealed, “My Lord! I have indeed killed a man from them, so I fear they may kill me

**The Old Ledger, Opened at the Summit**

Moses stands at the summit of favor — addressed directly by the Lord of the worlds, equipped with two signs — and the first thing he places before his Lord is the street in Egypt: I killed a man among them. The confession he made once in private (28:16) is now restated at the moment of commission, unprompted, as the objection that seems to him to disqualify the mission. The parallel account in chapter 26 records the same disclosure in the same position — they have a charge against me. I fear they will kill me — which shows how the Qur'an consistently frames the event: not as a sin re-hidden but as a fact re-declared whenever the mission approaches its danger. The commentators read the candor as the mark of the prophets' scrupulousness: a man who could have let the signs speak and stayed silent chooses instead to put his worst file on the table before the mission starts. The servant who confesses without being asked is the servant whose record needs no investigation.

**The Fear, Stated in Its Exact Weight**

The fear he names is specific: they will kill me. This is not anxiety about failure or stage fright before the court — it is the plain arithmetic of a wanted man walking into his victim's nation. Egypt's machinery has a decade-old killing of one of its own on file, with the perpetrator identified in public speech as a tyrant with ambitions (28:19); the mission requires him to stand in front of that machinery and announce the overthrow of its god. The Qur'an's honesty about this fear is part of its proof: a manufactured hero would arrive at Pharaoh's court already fearless, but the Book's chosen messenger arrives with his danger priced in his own words. The commentators noted the sequence in the prayer — the fact first, then the fear — as the anatomy of honest speech to God: state what you did, state what you dread, and let the two sit in the same sentence without excuses. Moses does not ask to be excused from the mission; he simply refuses to pretend the danger away.

## Verse 28:34

> And my brother Aaron is more eloquent than I, so send him with me as a helper to support what I say, for I truly fear they may reject me.”

**The Strength Admitted**

Moses' second objection turns from his record to his tongue: my brother Aaron is more eloquent than I. The admission is precise — not that he cannot speak, but that his brother's speech is stronger where the mission needs it most, before the most formidable orator-class of the age. The parallel in chapter 20 shows the same confession in fuller anatomy: uplift my heart for me, ease my task for me, untie the knot from my tongue so they may understand my words (20:25–28). Moses speaks of a physical impediment in that account — a knot to be loosened — and the commentators treated the stammer as the plain sense of his request. The courage of the admission should be marked: before the Lord who equips him, the messenger does not perform confidence; he itemizes limits. The man who will carry God's word to Pharaoh begins by telling God exactly what the word will pass through — a heart needing strengthening and a tongue needing help.

**Send Him With Me as a Helper**

The request is carefully framed: not send him instead of me — send him with me, as a support who confirms me. The commentators caught the distinction and its theology: prophethood is not transferable, and Moses is not negotiating his office away; he is asking for reinforcement inside it. The word for helper — one who buttresses from behind, in the root's own picture — names the role Aaron will hold for the whole mission: the elder brother, the first believer, the voice that strengthens the arm when the arm shakes. The structure also teaches what delegations are for in this Book: not the dilution of responsibility but the completion of it — one messenger carries the call, the second shores the carry. Moses' specific request — that Aaron support what I say — keeps the order of the mission intact: the speech remains Moses', the confirmation is Aaron's. God will answer by strengthening Moses' arm through his brother (28:35) — the reinforcement lands on the original messenger.

**The Fear of Being Rejected**

The stated ground of the request is the last clause: I fear they will deny me. The distinction matters because it shows what Moses thought eloquence was for: not persuasion by performance, but clarity enough to remove every excuse. A people whose rebellion is habitual (28:32) will deny a weak voice and deny a strong one; the strong one at least denies them their plea of never hearing it clearly.

## Verse 28:35

> Allah responded, “We will assist you with your brother and grant you both authority, so they cannot harm you. With Our signs, you and those who follow you will ˹certainly˺ prevail.”

**Authority They Cannot Reach**

The second grant names the protection: an authority given to both, so that they will not be reached. The key word, sulṭān, is the Qur'an's term for binding authority — proof, warrant, dominion — and the same root describes Satan's admitted limit in his last-day confession: I had no authority over you, except that I called you and you answered me (14:22). Authority, in this Book, is always borrowed and always bounded — the question is whose warrant a power carries. The grant to the brothers is the counter-warrant to Egypt's: the state's whole apparatus of compulsion is bounded by the reach of the court's writ, and the verse sets God's given authority against it with a promise of non-reachability. The promise's fulfillment will be literal — Pharaoh's armies pursue to the sea and touch neither brother — and the commentators folded its meaning wider: no harm reaches the one under God's warrant except what He appoints, and what He appoints is never loss. The mission's protection is not the absence of danger; it is the ceiling on danger's reach.

**Two Men Against a State**

The asymmetry deserves its arithmetic. Egypt in this sūrah is the complete power: a ruler styling himself god, a machine of administration (Hāmān), standing armies, and a demography of the knife. Against it, the verse deploys two brothers and a bundle of signs — and declares the outcome in advance. The world has never mistaken which side of that ledger counts its soldiers, and the Qur'an wants the ledger read precisely so: the verse's promises are not confidence-building rhetoric but the announcement of a different arithmetic, in which the Lord of the worlds' warrant outweighs conscription. The commentators' earlier notes gather here — the authority is sent down (11:96), the proof is manifest (23:45) — and the pattern holds across the Book: God's victories are consistently staffed by the few and the weak, because the staffing is the argument. When two men prevail over the age's superpower by signs, no reader can credit the arm that won.

## Verse 28:36

> But when Moses came to them with Our clear signs, they said ˹arrogantly˺, “This is nothing but conjured magic ˹tricks˺. We have never heard of this in ˹the history of˺ our forefathers.”

**The Court's Double Defense**

The reception is delivered in two sentences, and together they are the complete defensive architecture of a court cornered by evidence. The first sentence discredits the signs: nothing but invented sorcery — an explanation that accounts for the phenomena while refusing their source. The second sentence discredits the message: we never heard this from our forefathers — an appeal to inheritance as the measure of truth. The commentators treated the two answers as two registers of one refusal: the court cannot deny that something happened, so it denies the classification — magic, not miracle; novelty, not revelation. The design deserves naming because it has never gone out of use: when evidence cannot be denied, its source is relabeled; when doctrine cannot be answered, its novelty is itself made the charge. Moses' signs were clear — the verse says so — and their clarity is exactly what forced the court into explanations this flimsy.

**"We Never Heard This From Our Forefathers"**

The second defense is the verse's lasting exhibit, and the Qur'an hunts it across the Book: when it is said to them, follow what God has sent down, they answer, we follow only what we found our fathers doing — even if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided? (2:170). One commentator made the verse's function explicit: it is a standing condemnation of blind conformity to ancestral belief, the argument that truth expires at the horizon of inherited memory. The reasoning's flaw is arithmetic: every doctrine now old was new at its beginning; every forefather honored by this court received his rites from someone who first introduced them. The Qur'an's own word for the load the ancestors carried is burden — the imagery of men carrying their loads on their backs (6:25 area) — inherited wrong honored precisely because it was inherited. Against it the verse sets Moses' claim: guidance is judged by its source and its proof, not by its age in a family's memory. The court's forefathers-argument measures truth with a clock; the verse's answer is that clocks do not think.

**The Same Court in Every Age**

The verse's two defenses became the Qur'an's permanent inventory of rejection, and the sūrah's own audience had heard both recently in Makkah: the recitation called sorcery, and its message dismissed as what the ancients said or did not say. The commentators noted the pattern's reach — Abraham's people claiming they found their fathers doing so (21:53), the general verdict that none believes a warning unless he follows the way of the ancients.

## Verse 28:37

> Moses responded, “My Lord knows best who has come with ˹true˺ guidance from Him and will fare best in the end. Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed.”

**The Case Handed Up**

Moses' answer to the court's double defense is not a debate but a referral: my Lord knows best. The refusal to argue on the court's terms is the sentence's doctrine. The court has offered two classifications — sorcery and novelty — and Moses answers by relocating the question to the only bench with jurisdiction: who came with guidance from Him, and whose is the outcome of the Home. The commentators noted what the reply refuses: no entanglement in the court's categories, no defense of the signs' mechanics, no genealogical counter-argument about whose forefathers said what. The referral is not evasion; it is precision — the question the court is actually deciding is whose claim is true, and that question has exactly one competent Judge. A messenger's authority is never strengthened by accepting the opposition's framing; Moses answers the frame by dissolving it.

**Whose Is the End of the Home**

The second half of the referral moves the timeline: the true outcome is not the court's verdict today but the end of the Home — and the end, the commentators explained, carries both meanings the Arabic holds: the final outcome of the matter and the final abode, the Hereafter in its beatitude. The phrase relocates victory and loss to a horizon the court's power cannot touch: Pharaoh can rule the outcome of an afternoon; he cannot rule outcomes. The same standard appears on Moses' own lips to his people in chapter 7 — seek help through patience and prayer. The earth is God's, He leaves it to whom He wills, and the outcome belongs to the reverent (7:128) — and the sūrah itself will reserve the eternal Home for those who seek neither tyranny nor corruption on the earth (28:83). The referral's timeline is so the mission's deepest comfort: the court's verdicts are provisional by definition, and the only verdict that stands is scheduled.

## Verse 28:38

> Pharaoh declared, “O chiefs! I know of no other god for you but myself. So bake bricks out of clay for me, O Hamân, and build a high tower so I may look at the God of Moses, although I am sure he is a liar.”

**Bricks, Fire, and a Tower**

The practical command follows the creed: bake bricks for me, O Hāmān, and build a tower. The order lands where the sūrah's readers expect it — with the officer named at the persecution's beginning (28:6) and again at the basket's landing (28:8), the permanent second man of the regime, here commissioning construction. The detail of the bricks carries its own history: fired clay and mortar belong to the building tradition of Egypt's Nile valley — where wood is scarce and kiln-baked brick is the old technique — and the Qur'an's choice of material fits the setting with the precision its critics spent a century denying. The tower's stated purpose is the creed's architecture: a stairway to the skies, to reach the God of Moses — or in this verse's wording, to look at Him. The commentators noted whether the structure was ever begun; the credible readings treat the command as theater — a demonstration for the chiefs, staging the state's answer to heaven's evidence. The bricks are the regime's reply to the signs: proof answered with public works.

**"So I May Look at the God of Moses"**

The tower's declared aim is an account of knowledge: Pharaoh will climb until he sees Moses' God, and sight will settle the dispute. The commentators heard the irony the story was built to deliver: the God of Moses is not located upward in creation's attic, to be observed from a taller building — He is the Lord of the worlds, nearer than the climber's own jugular. The tower is theology's category error built in baked clay. The Qur'an's account of Moses himself makes the point from the believer's side: even Moses, God's spoken-to prophet, could not bear the theophany when he asked for it, and the mountain crashed to dust (7:143). Pharaoh's project so answers the wrong question with the wrong instrument: God is not found by ascending, but received by submission — and the tower, even finished, could only deliver its builder to a longer fall.

**The Council That Cannot Say No**

The scene's staging completes its meaning: the declaration is addressed to the chiefs, the commission to Hāmān — the regime's whole machinery mobilized around one man's self-deification. No voice in the council registers the absurdity, because the system that produces the absurdity is the council: verse 4's factions, the notables class the Qur'an convicts across its pages, the administrators of the sons' murder. The commentators' political reading is exact: tyranny's final stage is not the tyrant's madness but the court's silence — the point at which no instrument of the state can cost the creed anything. The sūrah has already produced its contrast in the believing man of Pharaoh's house who will argue alone in this same council (40:28), and his loneliness is the measure of the court's capture. Pharaoh says there is no god for you but me, and the silence that answers is the kingdom's true confession: it has one god, and he is speaking.

The claim is not new in his mouth — the sūrah's parallel accounts record him proclaiming to Egypt, I am your lord, the most high (79:24). Claiming not to know any lord besides himself — but here it is spoken in council, as state doctrine, with the chiefs convened to hear it.

## Verse 28:39

> And so he and his soldiers behaved arrogantly in the land with no right, thinking they would never be returned to Us

**Arrogance in the Land, Without Right**

The verse reads like a coroner's summary: he and his hosts exalted in the land without right. The word for their self-exaltation is the same root the sūrah used of Pharaoh at his introduction — he exalted himself in the land (28:4) — and the verse's placement closes the biography with the same verb it opened: the man's whole career was one long rising in his own estimate. The phrase without right carries the legal weight: arrogance is not attitude but trespass — authority exercised where none was granted. The Qur'an's accounting of Pharaoh is consistent on this point from beginning to end: his crime is not strength but the claim that his strength owes nothing, the usurpation of a lordship that belongs, as his victim-nation's prayer will put it, to God alone. The soldiers are included in the verdict because they are the trespass's instruments: every hierarchy of violence that marched behind the claim shares its ledger.

**The Creed Measured Against the Verse**

The verse's two clauses are one syllogism in the sūrah's argument: arrogance in the land is enabled by the assumption of no return. The pairing recurs across the Qur'an — those who exult in the land and scheme against the blessing of God, and God's answer: My plan is flawless while they are give respite (7:183 area); the ones who thought they would not return, and the opening of the gates of heaven's punishment. The commentators' refrain fits here: the certainty of the return is the load-bearing wall of all public morality. Remove it, and the strong do exactly what this verse records — exalt, and trespass, and price the weak at nothing. The sūrah's own closing verses will hang the moral directly: that Home of the Hereafter is given to those who seek no exaltation in the earth and no corruption, and the outcome belongs to the reverent (28:83). Pharaoh's biography and the believers' hope are set on the same scale: one creed of no return, one creed of the Home — and the sea will grade the exam.

---

## Verse 28:40

> So We seized him and his soldiers, casting them into the sea. See then what was the end of the wrongdoers

**The Sea That Opened Both Ways**

The instrument deserves its own reading, because the sea is the story's oldest character. It carried the condemned infant to the palace in verse 8; it carries the condemners to the bottom in verse 40. The same water that served the decree's authors at their height executes the decree's judgment at their fall, and the symmetry is the sūrah's signature of providence: the plans of the powerful are written in a medium that belongs to Someone else. The Qur'an's fuller accounts add the detail the symmetry needs: the sea opened for Israel and closed over Egypt — the identical water serving as corridor and coffin within one night, selection exercised not by the tide but by the Judge. The drowning is recorded across the Book in seven separate chapters, and the repetition is doctrine: the event functions as the standing exhibit of how the return Pharaoh denied actually operates — suddenly, terminally, and by the very nature he ruled.

**The Believer's Water, the Tyrant's**

The Qur'an preserves one scene from inside the drowning that this sūrah's brevity omits: as the water closed, Pharaoh cried that he believed in what the Children of Israel believed (10:90) — and the answer he received fixed the scene's lesson for every reader: faith at the moment of the seizure is not the return, it is the return's arrival. The body of the man who claimed godhood was displayed as a sign for those after him, preserved where his army was not, a monument not of honor but of evidence.

**"Behold How the Wrongdoers Ended"**

The closing command — behold how the wrongdoers fared in the end — converts the event into an assignment. The refrain is one of the Qur'an's standing instruments: travel through the land and see how the deniers ended, addressed to every generation with roads and ruins (12:109, 40:21). The seeing commanded is not tourism; it is induction — the reader is told to treat history as evidence, to walk the earth's wrecked empires and read the pattern the sea just displayed. The commentators heard in the refrain the verse's whole argument: God's judgments on the arrogant are not hidden events but public records, open to any walker with eyes. The command's plural address includes the sūrah's own audience — the persecuted Makkans, whose city's chiefs held the same creed as Pharaoh's — and its logic was meant to be acted on: caravans passed the dead towns of ʿĀd and Thamūd on every northbound journey, and the Qur'an kept asking what they saw there.

## Verse 28:41

> We made them leaders inviting ˹others˺ to the Fire. And on the Day of Judgment they will not be helped

**Leadership Turned Inside Out**

The verse hands Pharaoh and his party the sūrah's oldest title — and reverses it. Verse 5 promised the oppressed they would be made models of faith; verse 41 announces the opposite appointment: We made them leaders inviting to the Fire. The same divine governance that raises the crushed to imamate raises the corrupt to infamy, and the parallel is the argument: leadership in this Book is always functional — a position from which others are led somewhere. Pharaoh led Egypt's institutions, its soldiers, its cult; the road he pointed down ended at the Fire, and the verse says so in the same grammar verse 5 used for the covenant. No reader can miss the symmetry or its harshness: God does not merely punish tyrants; He converts their rank into the measure of their ruin, because the higher the post, the wider the gate it opens for those who follow.

**The Anti-Image of Moses' Party**

Set beside the promise to the followers of the signs — you and those who follow you will prevail (28:35) — the verse completes a matched pair of processions. Two leaders, two invitations, two destinations: Moses calls to the Lord of the worlds through evidence; Pharaoh calls to himself through decree; and each party is defined by whose voice it walks toward. The Qur'an builds its whole social vision out of this figure — the word for party itself means a group distinguished by whom it follows — and the verse fixes the fire-bound procession's final state: leadership without help, followers without excuse. The sūrah's readers belong, by choice, to one procession or the other; there is no third road between the two imams.

## Verse 28:42

> We caused a curse to follow them in this world. And on the Day of Judgment they will be among the outcasts

**A Curse With a Calendar**

The curse of this verse was not left as abstraction — the commentators identified its schedule: the plagues that struck Egypt in succession, the humiliations that stripped the regime's prestige decade by decade, and the final doom at the sea. Pharaoh's Egypt experienced its sentence in installments, each one public, each one aimed at the pretension named in verse 38: water turned to blood, the river that raised their foundling turned hostile, the sky and the granary alternately weaponized. A curse that follows is a curse with dates attached, and the dates teach as much as the blows: between the first sign and the last, the court was given every interval a heart could need to turn. The verse so records not a thunderbolt but a pursuit — measured, patient, and inescapable at its own pace.

**The Ledger Closes on Both Sides of Death**

Read whole, the verse divides the account cleanly: the curse belongs to this world, the outcasting to the next — and neither cancels the other. The commentators' larger point stands in the structure: worldly punishment is not the full sentence, and worldly success is not acquittal. Egypt's plagues were real; so was the Fire's invitation. The pairing corrects two errors at once — the believer who thinks the wicked always prosper until the last day, and the cynic who thinks the plagues settled the account. The sūrah's audience, watching the mighty of Makkah dine at leisure on confiscated reputations, needed the pairing exact: some sentences run in history, some run after it, and the Qur'an's God issues both against the same files.

## Verse 28:43

> Indeed, We gave Moses the Scripture—after destroying earlier nations—as an insight for the people, a guide, and mercy so perhaps they would be mindful

**Scripture After the Wreckage**

The verse dates the giving of the Book with unusual care: after We had destroyed the former generations. The commentators named them — the people of Noah, of Lot, of Ṣāliḥ — the civilizations whose ruins studded the caravan routes of Arabia. The timing is doctrine, not chronology: the Torah entered history trailing the evidence of what refusal costs. Israel received its Book knowing that the world's earlier experiments in denial had test results — drowned, overturned, swallowed. Scripture's first readers were, in this sense, survivors' children, and the Book opened with an argument their eyes could still check: the tell-tale ruins. The Qur'an's own audience, hearing this verse in Makkah, stood in the same seat — their trade caravans passed ʿĀd and Thamūd's empty houses on every northern journey — and the verse's placement here, just after Pharaoh's drowning, hands the same inheritance forward: revelation always arrives trailing evidence.

## Verse 28:44

> You were not there ˹O Prophet˺ on the western side of the mountain when We entrusted the Commandments to Moses, nor were you present ˹in his time˺

**The Prophet Who Was Not There**

The verse states a plain fact about Muhammad with total seriousness: you were not on the western side when the Commandment reached Moses, and you were not among the people of that age. The commentators read it as the Qur'an's own account of its sources — the Prophet reports an event he did not witness, delivered by the One who did. The statement stands inside a genre the Qur'an maintains across its pages: news of the unseen, narrated to a man whose contemporaries knew exactly where he had been all his life. The claim's boldness is easy to miss from a distance: an accused storyteller is being told to tell a story about a mountain he never climbed, and told to do it in the first person of an eyewitness — because the Eyewitness is the Speaker. No human compiler of ancient lore needs this verse; every compiler of divine speech does.

## Verse 28:45

> But We ˹later˺ raised ˹several˺ generations, and the ages took their toll on them. Nor were you living among the people of Midian, rehearsing Our revelations with them. But it is We Who have sent ˹this revelation to you˺

**Ages That Dissolve a Message**

The verse fills the centuries between Moses and Muhammad with one image: generations raised, and ages wearing them down. The commentators read the wearing-down morally — over the long stretch, people came to abandon the remembrance of God — and the image is exact: doctrines do not usually die by refutation. They die by erosion, each generation rubbing a little more off the stone, until what remains of a prophet's teaching is its calendar and its vocabulary. The verse so states, in one clause, the reason the Qur'an exists as a renewal: revelation is not defeated by time, but it is buried by it, and someone must dig. The same clause explains why every prophet's audience thinks its inheritance is original: the ages are long, and memory is short, and the erosion happens below the level at which anyone notices.

**Not in Midian Either**

The second denial completes the audit: you did not dwell among the people of Midian, rehearsing Our revelations with them. The verse closes the other road by which the Prophet could have "collected" the Moses material — the tradition of Shuʿayb's land, the storied well, the shepherd's decade. The commentators noted the completeness of the series: not at the mountain's western side, not in Midian, not in Egypt's story's other stations — the Prophet's biography contains no gap in which the study could have occurred. He was a boy who herded for Makkah's families, then a merchant on the Syrian run, then a husband, then a messenger; no desert decade, no monastery archive. The verse's dryness is its force: the hypothesis of borrowing is not answered by argument but by itinerary.

## Verse 28:46

> And you were not at the side of Mount Ṭûr when We called out ˹to Moses˺. But ˹you have been sent˺ as a mercy from your Lord to warn a people to whom no warner has come before you, so perhaps they may be mindful

**The Mountain Named, the Absence Repeated**

The third denial in the series closes on the most storied location of all: the side of al-Ṭūr, the mountain where We called. The naming is pointed — of all the scenes the Prophet did not witness, this is the one his opponents' arguments leaned on, because the call at the mountain is where revelation as such happened: the direct address, the commission, the beginning of speech between God and man. The verse concedes the Prophet was not there and pivots in the same breath: but you are sent as a mercy. The pivot is the verse's whole logic — the Prophet's mission is not weakened by missing the mountain, because his office and Moses' office share one Sender; the absence of the experience is the presence of the commission. Arabia never heard a warner before him, which makes his arrival the region's first — the one claim no borrowed manuscript could confer.

**Mercy as the Mission's Job Description**

The verse names the Prophet's sending as a mercy from your Lord — the phrase the Qur'an elsewhere magnifies into his whole biography: We have sent you only as a mercy for the whole world (21:107). The wording deserves the weight: the warning he carries is itself classified as mercy, because a warning is the data a soul needs to turn before the turn becomes impossible. The commentators paired the two clauses deliberately — a mercy to warn — so that neither softens into error: the mercy is not indulgence, and the warning is not wrath. For a man being stoned by the very people he was sent to, the verse supplied the identity that outlasted their verdict: his mission's category was fixed in heaven's vocabulary before his first audience, and no rejection on earth could amend it.

## Verse 28:47

> Also so they would not say, if struck by an affliction for what their hands have done: “Our Lord! If only You had sent us a messenger, we would have followed Your revelations and become believers.”

**The Excuse God Retires in Advance**

The verse names the alibi it was sent to destroy: if punishment ever overtakes them for what their hands have done, they will plead that no messenger came. The commentators explained the grammar's elision that carries the logic: were it not that the deniers, when affliction strikes, will plead that no warner reached them, the punishment might have hastened — the Prophet's arrival is, in practice, the reason the sentence is still pending. The teaching is exact and severe: God does not destroy in haste, and the presence of a messenger among a people is the one document every other defense depends on. With the Prophet among them, the plea is pre-empted — no people on earth could say heaven's notice never arrived, because the man reciting in their marketplace was the notice.

**Lest the Punishment Come Early**

The verse implies a doctrine about the world's patience: the affliction their hands have earned is being withheld for the sake of the excuse's removal — mercy running ahead of justice. The commentators connected the teaching to the standing principle elsewhere in the Book: God does not destroy a people for their wrongdoing while its people are unaware (6:131), and never punishes until a messenger has warned (17:15). The sequence is legal: hands commit, warning documents, then sentence falls. The verse so reveals how much of the world's apparent stability is forensic — generations standing in the dock's anteroom while the paperwork of their own defense is being delivered to their doors. The Quraysh heard this verse as living defendants: the punishment they mocked was late precisely because the messenger they mocked was present.

## Verse 28:48

> But when the truth came to them from Us, they said, “If only he was given the like of what Moses had been given.” Did they not deny what had been given to Moses earlier? They claimed, “Both ˹Scriptures˺ are works of magic, supporting each other!” Adding, “We truly deny both.”

**Two Sorceries, Backing Each Other**

The charge that finally surfaces is the conspiracy theory of refusal: two sorceries supporting one another — the Quraysh claiming the Torah and the Qur'an were co-conspirators, each lending the other false credit. The commentators preserved the variant readings: some heard sorcerers — Moses and Muhammad performing a two-man deception across the centuries. The absurdity is structural: to make the charge work, a book revealed across twenty-three years in a city of idolaters must have been coordinated, a millennium in advance, with a scripture preserved by a people the Arabs barely knew. But the charge's absurdity is its function: it explains the Qur'an's evident knowledge of Moses without conceding the source — the same maneuver as verse 36's invented magic, now upgraded to an alliance. Denial's last stage is always the order of the universe: the truth is too well informed to be false, so it must be organized.

**The Sourcing They Hid**

The commentators transmitted a detail that completes the verse's indictment: the challenge itself was coached. The Quraysh, consulting the Jewish communities of their region about matters of prophecy, were told to test Muhammad by the Moses standard — the demand in this verse was proposed by people who professed Moses' book. The coaching proves the demand's insincerity twice over: the standard was suggested by outsiders precisely because it sounded unanswerable, and the suggesters themselves had not accepted the standard they recommended. The verse's audience is by this convicted by their own sources: the community that guaranteed Moses' signs supplied the measure, and the Quraysh wielded it without believing in the measure-maker. Refusal, the verse shows, will borrow from anyone — even the people whose book it intends to reject.

---

## Verse 28:49

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Bring then a scripture from Allah which is a better guide than these two so I may follow it, if your claim is true.”

**The Challenge Turned Around**

The command is a reversal: the men who demanded a Moses-standard from Muhammad are handed a standard of their own — produce a scripture from God that guides better than these two, and the Prophet will follow it. The commentators placed the verse in the Qur'an's family of standing challenges: produce a sūrah like it, calling your witnesses besides God, if you are truthful (2:23). Bring ten forged sūrahs (11:13); bring a single sūrah (10:38); and the widest form — if all humans and jinn banded together to match this Qur'an, they could not, no matter how they supported each other (17:88). Each challenge shares the anatomy: the burden shifts to the accuser, the criterion is public, and the stakes are total — the accuser's own claim requires the production. Verse 49's version is the family's most strategic member: it does not ask for eloquence but for guidance, the one quality the objectors had spent three verses conceding by accident.

**"Better Guided Than These Two"**

The criterion deserves its precision: a book from God that is better guided — more leading, more directing of human life toward truth — than the Torah and the Qur'an together. The commentators noted what the framing concedes and what it forecloses: it concedes that guidance is the proper test of scripture — not antiquity, not formatting, not miracles on demand — and it forecloses every answer except production. A critic cannot win by arguing that the Qur'an is beautiful, or ancient, or influential; the challenge asks only for a book that guides better, and the field of candidates is empty. The criterion also quietly restates the Qur'an's self-description from verse 43: the Book's function is insight, guidance, mercy — and the challenger is invited to beat the function, not the packaging.

---

## Verse 28:50

> So if they fail to respond to you, then know that they only follow their desires. And who could be more astray than those who follow their desires with no guidance from Allah? Surely Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people

**Desire as a Competing God**

The Qur'an's word for the engine is desire in the plural — the caprices, the inclinations of the self — and its most famous usage makes the religious stakes plain: have you seen the one who takes his own desire as his god? (25:43). The phrase is exact: desire-worship is not metaphor but substitution — a will other than God's holds final authority in the person's decisions. The commentators traced the theme across the Book: prophets warned not to bend to the people's whims, rulers commanded to judge by what God sent down and not to follow their appetite. In this verse the plural matters: no single lust rules the deniers — a whole economy of preferences does, each negotiation between pride, habit, profit, and fear producing the day's refusal. Worship of desire is so polytheism of the moods, and the Quraysh's marketplace of idols had its exact double inside their chests.

**Guidance Withdrawn From the Wrongdoing**

The closing sentence states the settled rule: God does not guide the wrongdoing people. The commentators paired it with the verse of the sealed faculties — those who take desire as their god are left by God to stray knowingly, their hearing and hearts sealed, a cover on their sight (45:23). The pairing defines the withdrawal precisely: it is not the removal of the Book — the Word has been steadily delivered (28:51) — but the removal of the Book's effect, guidance as inner traction. A person can hold a lamp and walk into walls; the sentence describes the second half of that process, the walking, which is the self's own doing. The rule's severity is the rule's mercy in the long run: a will that could be overridden would not be a will, and a guidance that compelled would produce the obedience of furniture. God's refusal to force the desire-enslaved is the price of having made them free.

---

## Verse 28:51

> Indeed, We have steadily delivered the Word ˹of Allah˺ to the people so they may be mindful

**The Word, Delivered to Completion**

The verb is the verse's substance: We have steadily delivered the Word — carried it through, connected it piece to piece, brought it to its destination. The commentators gave the two classical readings: We elaborated the Word for them, and We completed the Word for them — both circling the same fact: revelation here is presented as a finished conveyance, not a fragmentary rumor. Them, the commentators noted, may mean the Quraysh, the Jews, or both — the Word's delivery is wide enough to name every party in the dispute the sūrah has been conducting.

**Stage by Stage, on Purpose**

The verse's adverb — steadily — carries the teaching about gradual revelation. The Qur'an defends the method elsewhere in its own words: the disbelievers asked why the Qur'an was not sent down all at once, and the answer was that staged delivery steadies the Prophet's heart and lets each portion land on its living moment (25:32). The commentators read the method's wisdom across the twenty-three years of the mission: a law about orphans arrived when orphans were being wronged; a verse about treaties arrived when treaties were being tested. The Word, in other words, was not delivered to the abstract but to the actual — and the steadiness was its style: no century of silence between Moses and the Qur'an's own arrival, no lost interval for the ages to erode. The delivery the verse announces is the shortest, best-audited chain the message has ever had.

---

## Verse 28:52

> ˹As for˺ those ˹faithful˺ to whom We had given the Scripture before this ˹Quran˺, they do believe in it

**The Believers Already Inside the Books**

Against the refusers of the previous verses, the verse sets a company the dispute forgot: people who held scripture before the Qur'an and believe in it on arrival. The commentators identified them precisely — the People of the Book who recognized Muhammad's prophethood and entered: the rabbi ʿAbdallāh ibn Salām, whose first question on hearing of the Prophet's arrival was whether his prophethood could be true, then declared his belief at once. Salmān al-Fārsī, the seeker who had traveled through faiths before finding the final one; the Christian monks of Syria and Abyssinia; Waraqa ibn Nawfal, who recognized the revelation at its very beginning. The verse's function in the sūrah's argument is evidentiary: the people best qualified to test the new Book — its own claimed predecessors' custodians — were the first to certify it, while the men with no scripture demanded it surpass two books they refused.

**Recognition, Not Conversion Alone**

The wording deserves its exactness: they believe in it — the it being this Qur'an. The commentators noted the mechanism: recognition. Those who knew what revelation was — its weight, its signature, its fingerprint on the conscience — needed no committee to authenticate the Qur'an; they heard their own books speaking in it. The sūrah has already quoted the testimony: whose description they find in their Torah and the Gospel (7:157). The verse so constructs a chain of attestation that runs through the objectors' own authorities: the Quraysh rejected the Qur'an citing Moses; the people of Moses accepted the Qur'an citing Moses. One of the two parties is misreading its evidence, and the verse's placement — immediately after the refusers' confession — lets the reader decide which party had the experts.

**"Before It" — The Priority That Prepared Them**

The phrase before it carries the verse's quiet doctrine of preparation: their earlier scripture was not superseded stock but training. The verse by this answers the sūrah's deeper anxiety: the ages eroded the message between the prophets (28:45), but not uniformly — here and there, someone had kept the original's sound in his ear, and those ears were the first to ring true.

---

## Verse 28:53

> When it is recited to them, they declare, “We believe in it. This is definitely the truth from our Lord. We had already submitted ˹even˺ before this.”

**"This Is the Truth From Our Lord"**

The declaration's middle beat is forensic: this is the truth from our Lord — the hearers identify the recitation's source by its family resemblance to what their Lord had already sent them. The commentators' note deserves its weight: these were the people whose lives had been shaped by Torah and Gospel — their prayer, their law, their stories — and their verdict is that the Qur'an is from the same Sender, not the same compiler. The identification is the precise refutation of verse 48's conspiracy charge: the two books cannot be co-conspirators, because one of them certifies the other as its colleague's genuine issue. The experts of the older document examined the newer and signed it — and the verse records the signature inside the newer document itself, a testimony that could not be forged without forging the whole.

**The Priority of Hearing**

That the verse builds recognition on recitation deserves its own reflection: the Qur'an's self-presentation as heard truth — in their own tongue, clear — is not a concession to an illiterate marketplace but a religious claim about how revelation travels: by voice, from lungs to ear to conscience, the oldest channel there is. The commentators paired the verse with the standing pattern of the faithful in every age — the Book heard, the heart answering before the committee convenes. The scene's economy is instructive against the Quraysh's procedures: they demanded delegations, standards, likenesses; the believing hearers of the Book performed the recognition in a sentence. What was missing in Makkah was never evidence; it was the ear that its evidence requires.

## Verse 28:54

> These ˹believers˺ will be given a double reward for their perseverance, responding to evil with good, and for donating from what We have provided for them

**Repelling With the Better**

The second virtue is stated as a policy: they repel evil with the better — the Qur'an's named alternative to reprisal, whose mechanics it describes elsewhere: good and evil cannot be equal. Respond to evil with what is best, and the one between whom and you is enmity will become as a close friend (41:34). The commentators gathered the virtue's occurrences across the Book — it belongs to the servants of the Most Compassionate, to the household of Abraham's manners — and noted what it predicts: the repelled evil, answered well, converts. For the converts of verse 52, the instruction was living practice: men entering Islam against their communities' fury would spend their first years as believers repelling exactly that fury — with the better. The verse does not counsel passivity; it prescribes a weapon: the response that outclasses the attack.

**Spending From What Was Provided**

The third virtue completes the portrait: they spend from what We have provided them. The wording's possessive — what We provided — assigns all wealth to its Source before commanding its circulation, and the commentators connected the phrase to the Qur'an's standing rule: spending is not generosity from one's own but redistribution of a trust. For the mixed community the verse describes — merchants of Makkah, farmers of Madinah, refugees from both — the command was structural: the young ummah ran on this transfer, the helpers sheltering the emigrants from their own provision. The virtue's placement beside patience and repelling is the portrait's balance: these believers are not passive saints but transactors — enduring, answering, and funding, a complete citizenry of faith.

---

## Verse 28:55

> When they hear slanderous talk, they turn away from it, saying, “We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. Peace ˹is our only response˺ to you! We want nothing to do with those who act ignorantly.”

**The Ear's Discipline**

The verse opens on the battlefield of the ear: when they hear idle talk — the frivolity, the slander, the noise a hostile city produces daily — they turn away from it. The commentators read the turning as trained conduct, not temperament: the servants of the Most Compassionate are described elsewhere in exactly this posture — when the foolish address them, they answer with peace (25:63). The discipline matters because the ear is where most wars start: an insult is an invitation, and most of history's quarrels are two invitations accepted. These believers decline the venue: the talk passes their hearing and dies there. The verse's realism should be noted — it does not say they never hear; it says what they do once they have. Turning away is a skill, and the verse presents it as part of the double-rewarded character the previous verse began.

**Peace as a Complete Reply**

The reply they give is one word of surrender that is not surrender: peace be upon you. The commentators preserved the incident the tradition attached to the verse: certain People of the Book, entering Islam before Abū Jahl, were followed from the gathering by his abuse — and answered only with peace. The reply's lineage runs deep in the Qur'an: Abraham says it to his idolatrous father — peace be upon you. I will pray to my Lord for your forgiveness (19:47) — and its final form is the parting of ways spoken to deniers: you have your way, and I have my way (109:6). Peace, in this lineage, is not appeasement; it is the refusal to accept the quarrel's premise — that worth is contested in conversation. The word hands the speaker's dignity back to its Owner and closes the exchange without a casualty.

**Forbearance Without Surrender**

The verse completes the portrait that verse 54 began — patience, beautiful response, spending — and its placement is the lesson: the slander-response follows the double wage because forbearance is the wage's hardest installment. The commentators' summary deserves the close: these believers have truth without needing the last word, which is the test of possession. The quarrelsome are still bargaining; the secure have left the market. And the sūrah's own history sharpens the point: the same chapter that recorded Moses' restrained referral before Pharaoh's court (28:37) now records the converts' restrained peace before Abū Jahl's abuse — the two scenes are one discipline at two scales. The Qur'an pays both with the same currency: the Hereafter's weights, where the last word is worth nothing and the kept soul is worth everything.

---

## Verse 28:56

> You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills, and He knows best who are ˹fit to be˺ guided

**The Verse of the Beloved Uncle**

The tradition attached this verse to the hardest scene of the Prophet's life: the deathbed of Abū Ṭālib, the uncle who had raised him, shielded him through the boycott and the years of mockery, and refused the faith to the end. The Prophet urged him toward the word by which he could plead for him before God — and the old man's answer, recorded in the soundest collections, was that he would not say it lest the Quraysh mock that he said it only in fear of death (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1360). The verse descended on that grief: you do not guide whom you love. No verse in the Qur'an carries more consolation per word for the earnest, because it addresses the exact wound of every guide — that love is not a passport into hearts, and the best-beloved may die outside the door the guide stood knocking on for forty years.

**Love Is Not a Lever**

The verse's teaching generalizes through the Qur'an's own gallery: Adam's son murdered his brother though their father was the first prophet; Noah's son refused the ark and drowned in sight of his father's call; Abraham pleaded for his idolatrous father until revelation closed the file — Abraham was tender-hearted and forbearing (9:114). The commentators gathered the cases into the verse's doctrine: guidance is not a fluid that flows along affection, and prophethood itself confers no jurisdiction over the will. The teaching's mercy is its severity: it forbids the guide to measure his effort by its yield, since the yield was never his to command. The Prophet, who could persuade an empire's worth of strangers, could not move the man whose shoulder he had slept against — and the verse tells him, and every guide after him, that this asymmetry is the design, not the failure.

## Verse 28:57

> They say ˹to the Prophet˺, “If we were to follow ˹true˺ guidance with you, we would certainly be snatched away from our land.” Have We not established for them a safe haven ˹in Mecca˺ to which fruits of all kinds are brought as a provision from Us? But most of them do not know ˹this favour˺

**The Fear They Confessed**

The verse quotes the Quraysh's true reason in their own words: if we follow the guidance with you, we will be snatched from our land. The commentators read the admission with care: the speaker concedes the guidance is real — the objection is not truth but consequence; they feared being ostracized by the surrounding Arabs, whose idols their sanctuary's economy served. The confession is the verse's exhibit: disbelief, in Makkah, was an economic position. The men who demanded miracles in verse 48 are revealed here as men who believed enough to calculate the price of believing — and calculated against it. The Qur'an's honesty about its opponents is again its mark: they are not quoted as fools but as men with interests, which is the more damning file.

**A Sanctuary With a Supply Chain**

The verse describes the haven with a provisioner's precision: to which the fruits of everything are brought — a safe harbor whose supplies arrive from every horizon. The commentators located the geography: Makkah, barren ground that grows almost nothing, fed by the caravans its sanctuary attracted — the Quraysh's own winter and summer journeys, the trading runs to Yemen and Syria, secured by the House's peace. The sūrah of the Quraysh names the same economy in its four verses: their security in the journeys and the feeding against hunger and the security against fear — all of it from the Lord of the House (106:4). The verse's point is the ledger's first line: the land they feared to lose was never their achievement; it was provision from His presence, delivered through the very sanctity they were trading away.

**"But Most of Them Do Not Know"**

The closing clause returns the sūrah's oldest refrain — most people do not know — now aimed at the benefactors themselves: most of them know not that it is God who established them and kept them safe. The commentators' reading is exact: the ignorance is not of facts but of authorship. The Quraysh knew their market days and their alliance networks; what they did not know — or refused to file — was whose gift the arrangement was. The verse so completes the chapter's account of knowledge in miniature: the palace never knew its nurse was the mother (28:13), and the sanctuary never knew its Keeper was the Giver (28:57). The same blindness, at two scales — and the cure is the same: a messenger, reciting, until the gift is traced to its Giver.

## Verse 28:58

> ˹Imagine˺ how many societies We have destroyed that had been spoiled by their ˹comfortable˺ living! Those are their residences, never inhabited after them except passingly. And We ˹alone˺ were the Successor

**The Question With a Catalogue Behind It**

The verse opens with the Qur'an's standing rhetorical survey: how many a town have We destroyed — a question the Book asks in a dozen registers, each one pointing at ruins its audience could visit. The commentators listed the companion verses: the towns destroyed for their wrongdoing in chapter 7, the overturned in chapter 21, the mightier towns of chapter 47 — how many societies We destroyed that were far superior in might (47:13). The question's force is experiential: Arabia's caravans camped in these dead cities; the ruined wells and fallen towers were the landscape's own museums. The verse is not speculation — it is a guided tour with an itinerary the listeners had walked, and the guide's question has one purpose: make them count what comfort bought the previous tenants.

**"Exulting in Their Ease"**

The destroyed are identified by their signature: they exulted in their life of ease — the word the commentators glossed as prosperity without gratitude, plenty enjoyed as if self-made. The term links the verse directly to Pharaoh's biography, which is the sūrah's own illustration: his hosts exulted in the land without right (28:39), and the verse's placement shows the same file being read across history — every ruined town was a smaller Pharaoh's Egypt. The commentators' note is precise: the ease was not the crime; the crime was the ease's interpretation — wealth read as endorsement, comfort read as permanence. The Qur'an's doctrine of prosperity, established in its earliest pages, runs through here: ease is a test's furniture, and the spoiled are those who thought the furniture was the house.

**Dwellings That Outlive Their Owners**

The verse records the ruins' afterlife with a traveler's eye: there are their dwellings, not inhabited after them except a little — and the commentators corrected the obvious misreading: it is not that a few people survived. It is that the structures survived, sheltering caravans, herders, and the occasional passerby while the nations that built them were gone. The image is the verse's coldest statistic: a civilization's total output, reduced to windbreaks. The commentators connected the motif to the Qur'an's funeral verse for the drowned of Pharaoh's party: neither heaven nor earth wept over them, nor were they reprieved (44:29) — extinction without eulogy. The dwellings standing over the absence are the verse's own epitaph: the world kept the buildings and lost the builders, because the buildings were never the civilization.

## Verse 28:59

> Your Lord would never destroy a society until He had sent to its capital a messenger, reciting Our revelations to them. Nor would We ever destroy a society unless its people persisted in wrongdoing

**The Mother City Named**

The verse fixes the venue of divine notice: your Lord does not destroy a town until He sends to its mother-city a messenger. The commentators identified Makkah behind the phrase — the city whose ancient title, Mother of Towns, the tradition preserved — while noting the broader readings: the mother-city as the greatest town of any people, the metropolis whose fall takes the region with it. The precision of the address matters legally: notice is served at the capital, in the hearing of the center, so that the periphery cannot plead distance and the center cannot plead ignorance.

**Due Process for Nations**

The verse states the Qur'an's standing rule of collective judgment: never destroy without a messenger — the same law recorded elsewhere in its own words: We would never punish until We have sent a messenger (17:15), and never destroy a society for its wrongdoing while its people are unaware (6:131). The commentators gathered the parallel verses into a single doctrine: destruction without notice is not in God's government. The rule's severity lies in its completeness: no civilization is condemned on averages, no generation punished for its ancestors' files. Before any town falls, a man stands in it, reciting signs, in its own tongue, at its own center — and the verse's placement in the sūrah, after the Prophet's commission to the never-warned Arabs (28:46), fixes Makkah's position on the timeline: notice has been served.

## Verse 28:60

> Whatever ˹pleasure˺ you have been given is no more than ˹a fleeting˺ enjoyment and adornment of this worldly life. But what is with Allah is far better and more lasting. Will you not then understand

**"Better and More Lasting"**

The comparison's two adjectives do the verse's work: what is with God is better and more lasting — and the commentators carried the tradition's arithmetic: this world against the Hereafter is a speck against the sea. The Hereafter's goods are lasting because they do not end, while everything here has an end by its very nature. The Qur'an's short sūrah at the Book's end states the comparison in its compressed form: the Hereafter is better and more lasting (87:17) — the same two adjectives, the same verdict. The word better matters as much as lasting: the next life is not only longer; it is superior in kind — the appetite that the world's pleasures fatigue is met there without fatigue. The verse's economy should be noted: it does not say the world is worthless; it says the world is the lesser asset, and the believer is being directed to the greater one while holding the lesser.

**Adornment: The Word's Own Precision**

Why adornment? The commentators connected the term to its sibling verse — the world's goods named as an ornament (33:28) — and the choice of word carries the teaching: adornment is for display, and display is for others. Much of what wealth purchases is not use but appearance — the watched house, the heard reputation, the envied ease — and the verse files all of it under decoration: beautiful, temporal, and inert. The word also explains the world's seduction mechanism: adornment works on the eye from a distance and loses detail on approach — which is why the Qur'an insists the Hereafter's goods are better, not merely later: seen up close, the world's shine is thinner than its invoice. The verse so trains a habit of appraisal: ask of every possession whether it is being enjoyed or merely displayed, and note that neither verb survives the grave.

## Verse 28:61

> Can those to whom We have made a fine promise—which they will see fulfilled—be like those who We have allowed to enjoy the pleasures of this worldly life, but on the Day of Judgment will be brought ˹for punishment˺

**The Verse That Prices Two Lives**

The question sets two men side by side and asks whether they can be the same. The first is the one given a fine promise — the Garden, pledged in revelation — and who will see the pledge paid. The second is the one handed the world's enjoyment and nothing else, summoned on the Day of Resurrection among the arraigned. The commentators explained the comparison's target: the poor and afflicted believers of Makkah were being told, against every visible measure, that they outranked the comfortable deniers — and the verse's own tradition named the two archetypes, the Prophet and Abū Jahl, standing at the two ends of the same city. The comparison's power is its refusal of appearances: by every observable index — food, security, followers — the denier lived better. The verse removes the visible index from the scales and weighs what remains: a promise with a Guarantor against an enjoyment with a foreclosure date.

---

## Verse 28:62

> ˹Watch for˺ the Day He will call to them, “Where are those you claimed were My associate-gods?”

**The Question That Ends All Questions**

The verse stages the Day's opening interrogation as a single question: where are those you claimed were My associate-gods? The call is addressed to the misleaders — the commentators tied the pronoun to the leaders whose disavowal fills the next verse — and the question's genius is its smallness. Not a lecture on theophysics, not a recital of their sins: one question about location. Where are they? The objects of a lifetime's worship are summoned to appear, and their absence is the whole verdict. The commentators listed the claimed partners' kinds — idols, jinn, angels, the righteous dead — and the question works identically on each: worship's premise is presence with power to respond, and on that Day the premise is tested by a single roll call.

**The Roll Call's Echo Through the Sūrah**

The question is a refrain: verse 74 will ask it again, word for word, before the sūrah's final testimony scene — the repetition drumming the scenario into the listener's reflexes. The commentators connected the doubling to the Qur'an's oral pedagogy: a scene of such consequence is shown twice so that no reciter's audience can claim it was mentioned once. The scene also closes a circle the sūrah opened: Pharaoh's court legislated worship of the king (28:38) — the claim itself — and here is the claim's day in court, with the king and his theology both absent. The tyrant who demanded to be the nation's god will stand in a crowd of claimants, all equally discoverable by the same question, none of them locatable.

## Verse 28:63

> Those ˹misleaders˺ against whom the decree ˹of torment˺ is justified will cry, “Our Lord! These ˹followers˺ are the ones we caused to deviate. We led them into deviance, for we ourselves were deviant. We disassociate ourselves ˹from them˺ before You. It was not us that they used to worship.”

**The Confession's Anatomy**

The speech proceeds in four moves, and the commentators anatomized each: they led these people astray; they themselves were astray first; they disavow their followers before God; and — the stunning close — it was not us they worshipped. The first two clauses establish the chain of corruption: deviation propagates like infection, and the carrier was infected before he infected — which is why verse 42's curse was earned. The third clause is the severance: disavowal, the same scene the Qur'an stages in chapter 2 — those who were followed will disown those who followed, when they see the punishment, and the bonds between them will be cut. The fourth clause is the scene's coldest fact: the followers did not, in the end, worship their leaders; they worshipped their own attachments, using the leaders as furniture.

**The Followers Left Holding Nothing**

The scene's asymmetry completes its warning: the misleaders keep their confession, and the misled keep the bill. The Qur'an returns to this severance repeatedly — the followers will wish they had a means of return when they see the punishment — and the commentators read the pattern as the standing fate of secondhand religion: whoever lets another carry his accountability has handed his deed to a man with his own sentence to serve. The bond that united them, chapter 2 says, will be cut; chapter 23 adds that no kinship will remain between them on that Day. The misleaders' speech is so the last thing their followers ever receive from them — a public disavowal — and the Qur'an shows it to the living precisely so that no one buys dependence twice: once with attention now, and once with damnation then.

---

The confession is one of a family of scenes the Book gives at the reckoning, and the followers' complaint is recorded in the sūrah of the Combined Forces: "We only followed you; will you relieve us of any of the punishment?" (33:67–68). The sūrah of the Bee gives the answer of the leaders, who repudiate the responsibility and say they were led astray as their followers were (16:86), and the sūrah of Abraham records the weaker ones asking why they did not help them when they had followed them (14:21). The verse's own account adds the leaders' admission that they were themselves astray first, which is the fact the rest of the scene turns on.

## Verse 28:64

> It will be said ˹to the disbelievers˺, “Call upon your associate-gods ˹for help˺.” So they will call them, but will receive no response. And they will face the punishment, wishing they had been ˹rightly˺ guided

**The Crowd That Will Watch the Call Fail**

The verse's scene has spectators, and the commentators' wider reading of the Qur'an's Hereafter crowd-scapes includes them: the audience of the failed call is every soul whose worship was directed correctly. The design is instructional — the destruction of the fabrication is displayed, not merely recorded, because its end is part of the lesson for those who never built one. The scene's geometry completes the sūrah's doctrine of testimony: the fabricated gods fail in public so that the Truth's monopoly is public too; the verse 75 witness stands in the same courtroom where this silence is performed. For the living, the scene assigns a vantage: the believer is being seated, in advance, in the section of the court where answered prayer is the norm — and the seating is earned in the present tense, one directed call at a time.

The Book stages the same call a second time later in the sūrah, where the disbelievers are told on that Day to call on the partners they claimed, and they call and are not answered, and the punishment comes into view between them (28:74). The scene closes with the confession recorded in the next verse, where the disbelievers are asked what answer they gave the messengers and they are left with nothing to say (28:65–66). The point stands in the sūrah's own frame: the gods that were partners in this world turn out to have no part in the next, and the call is recorded so that the abandonment is heard.

## Verse 28:65

> And ˹watch for˺ the Day He will call to them, asking, “What response did you give to the messengers?”

**The Response That Was Given**

The question's phrasing carries its own reminder: a response was given — every audience answered, in practice if never in words. The commentators noted the Qur'an's realism here: disbelief is itself a reply, and so is delay, so is selective obedience, so is the applause that changes nothing. The audit does not ask whether you answered — it asks what your answer was. The scene's doctrine is the completion of verse 59's rule: a messenger was sent to every mother-city that was judged, reciting the signs; therefore the file for every people contains both the recitation and the response, matched and dated. On the Day, the reading of the file is a formality — which is why the verse's question sounds less like inquiry than like sentencing's preamble.

**Mercy's Mirror in the Audit**

The verse's hard procedure sits inside a mercy that the sūrah has documented for eighty verses: the audit exists because the deliveries existed. No community is questioned about a message it never received — the rule verse 59 legislated — and no question is asked here except the one whose answer the hearers already have, having lived it. The Day's questions, in the Qur'an's portraits, never solicit new information; they transfer the file from the hidden register to the public one. The mercy is in the timing: the same question — what did you do with the message? — can be asked by the reader's own conscience tonight, at no cost, with the full means of changing the answer still on the table. The verse's scene is so a preview sold at the price of attention: the cheapest audit ever offered, and the only one whose failure can still be cured.

## Verse 28:66

> They will be too dumbstruck on that Day to ask one another ˹for answers˺

**The Silence After the Questions**

The verse records the crowd's condition rather than any answer: the tidings will be obscured for them on that Day, and they will not question one another. The commentators gave the two classical readings — the arguments they might have raised are blotted out, or the news of one another is blotted out — and both converge on the same portrait: a humanity reduced to its own file, with no collateral left to consult. The scene follows the audit question of verse 65: asked what they answered to the messengers, the assembled have nothing to offer — no plea to trade, no neighbor to poll, no precedent to cite. Speech itself is the faculty being sentenced, in a day where speech was the currency of every refusal they ever made.

**The End of the Excuse Economy**

The first reading carries the verse's legal weight: the tidings — the pleas, the arguments — are obscured, meaning no valid argument remains available. The commentators' summary is precise: the Hereafter is the one venue where advocacy runs out, because its cases were all adjudicable in life and every extension has expired. The Qur'an has documented the excuse economy across this sūrah — no messenger came (retired by statute in verse 59), the message was sorcery (refuted by its own craftsmen's surrender), the standard was Moses (answered by his people's own converts) — and verse 66 shows the economy's final closure: not one surviving plea, not even the mutual reassurance that wrong is normal. The silence is not imposed from outside; it is the natural condition of a market whose goods have all expired.

## Verse 28:67

> As for those who repent, believe, and do good ˹in this world˺, it is right to hope that they will be among the successful

**The Door the Verse Restores**

After four verses of arraignment, silence, and unanswered calls, the verse swings open a door: but as for one who repents — the particle of exception doing the work of a whole gospel. The commentators placed the verse's mercy precisely: it stands between the scenes of the condemned, so that no listener leaves the recitation trapped in the courtroom. The sequence is the Qur'an's standard mercy-map: show the end of refusal in full — the roll call, the disavowal, the unanswered prayer — and then, while the fear is still warm, name the exit: repentance, faith, righteous work. The door's placement is its eloquence: it is announced at the exact moment the listener can feel what it is worth.

**"It Is Right to Hope"**

The promise's phrasing is among the gentlest in the Book: perhaps he will be among the successful — and the commentators delivered the classical rule about such particles: when God speaks a perhaps, it is not uncertainty but mercy of address — a promise made to sound like hope so that the striving it commands feels like pursuit rather than compliance. The rule's consequence is exact: the verse is a guarantee dressed as a possibility, and the dress is for the wearer's dignity — success that arrives as pure decree would produce no love. The commentators also heard the word's echo of verse 61's fine promise: the same receiver of the pledge, now described by his verbs. The hope is so the currency of the relationship: God speaks in hope's grammar so that the servant may live in hope's energy.

## Verse 28:68

> Your Lord creates and chooses whatever He wills—the choice is not theirs. Glorified and Exalted is Allah above what they associate ˹with Him˺

**Creation and Choice as One Act**

The verse pairs the two verbs — creates and chooses — and the pairing is its theology: the same hand that makes the material selects the instrument. The commentators transmitted al-Ṭabarī's preferred reading — Your Lord creates what He wills and chooses the choicest of what they have — God selecting the best available from among the very people objecting to the selection. The reading turns the objection inside out: the objectors are, in the same breath, the inventory being drawn from — the choice ran through their own households and passed over their own criteria. The doctrine lands on every generation's merited discomfort: God's choices are made from among us, which means our exclusion from any office is never scarcity of candidates but the election's freedom — and freedom is the Sender's, as the verse says twice.

**Submission as the Trained Outcome**

The commentators transmitted, from the Sufi master al-Shādhilī, the verse's inward fruit: forsake the directing of things, and if you must direct, direct yourself not to direct — the art of aligning one's will with the Will that chooses. The teaching is not passivity: the creature's sphere of choice — effort, repentance, work — is real and commanded; what is surrendered is the directorship of outcomes and offices. The verse so heals the believer's two chronic inflammations: envy of others' appointments (the choice was not yours to give) and anxiety over one's own (the choice was not yours to secure). Between those two releases lives the actual spiritual life: work assigned, results owned by the Chooser, and the heart's constant refrain matching the verse's close — glory to Him above what we would have arranged instead.

---

## Verse 28:69

> And your Lord knows what their hearts conceal and what they reveal

**Conceal and Disclose: The Full Spectrum**

The verse's two verbs bracket the whole range of the inner life: what is hidden and what is shown. The commentators' note on the breadth matters: God's knowledge is not limited to the secrets people successfully keep; it covers the full spectrum, including the disclosed — the things worn openly — and the concealments that failed. The pairing corrects two opposite errors: the exhibitionist's belief that the shown self is the whole self, and the conspirator's belief that the hidden self is safe. Neither register is unknown to the Reader. For the believer, the verse converts hypocrisy into a technical impossibility: the discrepancy between breast and speech is recorded as a discrepancy, not discovered by accident. The only strategy the verse leaves standing is congruence — to become, by repentance and practice, what one appears.

The verse's placement after your Lord creates and chooses is surgical: the objectors' resistance to the Prophet was conducted, like most resistance, in a double register — public mockery over private fear, printed scorn over secret conviction. The commentators' historical reading supplies the texture: some of the Prophet's fiercest opponents moved their lips at the recitation when no one watched, and some of his quietest listeners were the ones whose chests concealed the most. The verse tells the Messenger — and every messenger's successor in daʿwah — that the reception he observes is incomplete data: the contempt is seen from above, and so is the crack in the contempt. The verse by this forbids the preacher both despair at visible rejection and confidence in visible acceptance: the file being written is written in a room he does not see.

## Verse 28:70

> He is Allah. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. All praise belongs to Him in this life and the next. All authority is His. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned

**The Creed Stated Plain**

The verse sets down the whole creed in four clauses, without argument: He is Allah — there is no god but He; His is the praise, in the first and the last; His is the judgment; and to Him you will be returned. The commentators read the placement as the sūrah's religious seal: after eighty verses of narrative — baskets, courts, plagues, seas, treasures, trials — the chapter's argument arrives at the identity of its Narrator, stated in the naked formula of worship. The position is deliberate: the creed lands with the stories' weight behind it, because the listener has just watched this Allah act — choose, deliver, drown, provide, forgive, choose again. The verse does not ask for the formula's acceptance; it presents the formula as the summary of everything the listener has already been shown.

**His Is the Judgment — and Then the Return**

The third clause names the running sovereignty: His is the judgment — the word carrying decision, rule, and wisdom together. The commentators paired its two registers: in this world, all creaturely authority functions only within God's delegated judgment; in the next, He judges directly between His creatures with no intermediary left standing. The clause's placement after the praise clause is doctrine: the One praised is the One ruling — worship and governance are not separate departments. And the fourth clause closes the verse on return: to Him you will be brought back — the verb of permanent arrival, the same return Pharaoh denied (28:39) and the verse's whole argument has now showed at scale. The sequence — praise, judgment, return — is the believer's whole orientation compressed into a single breath: live praised, governed, and homeward.

## Verse 28:71

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Imagine if Allah were to make the night perpetual for you until the Day of Judgment, which god other than Allah could bring you sunlight? Will you not then listen?”

**"Will You Not Then Listen"**

The verse closes on an odd verb for an argument about light: will you not hear? The commentators caught the pairing's design: the ear is the night's organ — in darkness, hearing is the sense that carries a person — so the question of the perpetual night is addressed to the faculty that the night proves. The correspondence is exact: verse 72's day-question ends with will you not see?, the day's organ. The two questions are so tailored to their hemispheres, and the tailoring teaches the verse's deeper method: God's signs are audited by whichever sense each sign serves — and each of the reader's own faculties is being recruited as its own witness. The listener who will not hear a question about hearing has exhausted the verse's remedies; the question's repetition in the next verse shows the Reader willing to try the other sense.

**The Mercy Inside the Hypothesis**

The perpetual night is hypothetical — and the hypothesis's non-fulfillment is the daily evidence. Every morning in the audience's lifetime had arrived on schedule: the sun that Pharaoh claimed lordship over, the light the Quraysh traded under, the dawn Moses' mother rose to — the same event, relaunched without a single failure across the species' whole memory. The commentators' reading of the verse as an invitation to gratitude follows: the counterfactual's horror is the gratitude's ledger — the switching of night and day is not a natural right but a standing mercy, withheld from no generation by the One who could withhold it by a single command. The verse so converts an astronomical routine into a religious audit: what would you pay, in that locked night, for one hour of this morning you walked through untouched?

---

## Verse 28:72

> Ask ˹them also˺, “Imagine if Allah were to make the day perpetual for you until the Day of Judgment, which god other than Allah could bring you night to rest in? Will you not then see?”

**The Mirror Challenge: The Locked Day**

The counterfactual flips: if God made the day permanent until the Day of Resurrection — which god besides Allah could bring you night to rest in? The pairing with verse 71 is deliberate architecture: the two hypotheses together cover the whole cycle, proving that whichever half is withdrawn, the other half's restoration is beyond every associate-god's reach. The commentators explained the day's gift that the hypothesis removes: rest — the night as the creature's appointed shelter from exposure and labor. A perpetual day is not a blessing multiplied; it is a blessing weaponized: work without end, sight without mercy, heat without shadow. The verse's insight is psychological as much as astronomical: the human being does not only need light — he needs the ending of light, and no power he worships can supply an ending.

**"Will You Not Then See"**

The closing question switches organs: will you not see? — the day's faculty, as the commentators noted, matching verse 71's ear. The design completes the two-verse apparatus: each hemisphere's question is addressed to the sense that hemisphere serves. That the reader's own body convicts him — the eyes that enjoy the day's light cannot fail to see the day's Argument, and the ears that carry him through the night cannot fail to hear the night's. The rhetoric leaves no neutral faculty: every sense is deputized. The commentators' wider point follows: denial is not an information failure — the evidence is piped through the deny-er's own senses, daily, at full brightness; refusal is the only word left for it, and the verse's question makes the refusal conscious by asking it to look at what it is refusing.

## Verse 28:73

> It is out of His mercy that He has made for you the day and night so that you may rest ˹in the latter˺ and seek His bounty ˹in the former˺, and perhaps you will be grateful

**"Made for You"**

The verse's middle phrase carries its warmth: He made for you — the design addressed to its beneficiaries. The commentators heard in the construction the personal register of all provision: the cycle's scale is planetary, but its addressee is singular — you. The teaching dignifies the ordinary: the reader's last night's sleep was a designed delivery, and this morning's light is addressed mail. The phrase also grounds the verse's argument from mercy: provision that is for you implies a Giver who knows you — and the knowledge theme of verse 69 stands behind this one: the One who reads the chest designed the schedule the chest rests by. The believer's gratitude, in this light, is not a mood but an accurate reading of the arrangement's address label.

**The Seeker's License in the Day's Design**

The verse's clause about the day — that you may seek of His bounty — deserves its own hearing as permission. Labor, in the verse's design, is not the curse of a fallen world but the assigned channel of provision: the day is made for the seeking, and the seeking is directed at His bounty — which makes every honest occupation a form of prayer conducted with the hands. The commentators' reading dignifies the whole spectrum of work: the farmer's field, the merchant's caravan, the craftman's bench are all doors of the same bounty, and the verse removes the false ranking between "spiritual" and "worldly" effort — both are the day's business, and both are addressed to the Giver. The clause also carries the seeking's ethics in miniature: bounty sought of Him is bounty sought lawfully, because the address defines the channel.

## Verse 28:74

> And ˹watch for˺ the Day He will call to them, “Where are those you claimed were My associate-gods?”

**The Question, Repeated**

The Day's question returns word for word: where are those you claimed were My associate-gods? The commentators noted the sūrah now carries the scene twice — verse 62 and verse 74 — and the repetition is deliberate pedagogy: the single most devastating fact of the Hereafter, the non-appearance of the claimed gods, is recited into the community's memory as a refrain, until no listener could ever claim the scenario was unfamiliar. Between the two askings stands the whole anatomy of the reckoning — the misleaders' disavowal, the unanswered calls, the silenced crowd, the open door of repentance, the Lord who creates and chooses, and the sky's nightly mercy — so that the second asking arrives with its evidence stack complete. The question has not changed; the listener has.

**The Refrain's Placement**

The verse's placement between the mercy verses is the second asking's sharpest feature: verse 73 has just displayed the daily mercy of night and day — and the question follows immediately, addressed to those who credited the mercy elsewhere. The commentators' logic: the associates are asked for twice because they were claimed everywhere — in the creed and in the provision's bookkeeping; the same partners were named as gods and as providers of safety and harvest. The Day's audit therefore re-opens the ledger at the provision section: the Quraysh who thanked their idols for the sanctuary's security and the Qurashi rains are summoned to produce the beneficiaries of their gratitude — and the products, as verse 57 proved, were always from God's presence. The second asking is so narrower and deeper: where are the gods you thanked?

## Verse 28:75

> And We will bring forth a witness from every faith-community and ask ˹the polytheists˺, “Show ˹Us˺ your proof.” Then they will ˹come to˺ know that the truth is with Allah ˹alone˺. And whatever ˹gods˺ they fabricated will fail them

**They Will Know the Truth Is Allah's**

The verse records the discovery rather than the sentence: then they will know that the truth belongs to God — knowledge arriving as the case closes. The commentators noted the tense's precision: they will know — the verb of the unveiled, the same knowledge-by-necessity verse 62 described, arriving after the window in which knowing could have been chosen. The scene so completes the Qur'an's standing pattern: faith's value lies in its voluntariness before evidence becomes unavoidable; the Day supplies certainty to everyone and credit to no one. The phrase's content matters equally: the truth is God's — He held it throughout the dispute; the communities' versions were borrowings against it, and the borrowing is now called due. Every argument the deniers ever filed was, in the end, a try to relocate the truth's ownership — and the verse announces the repossession.

**Their Fabrications Forsake Them**

The verse's closing clause ends the partnership: what they used to fabricate will fail them — and the commentators listed the fabricated: idols invented, lies told, the whole constructed religion of convenience. The word fails them — the phrase of an ally deserting the field — completes the sūrah's longest arc: the associates who could not be found (62), could not answer (64), and now formally defect. The abandonment is the fabrication's own nature coming due: things that were made hold only as long as their makers hold, and the maker's death dissolves the make. The scene's teaching for the living is inventory doctrine: audit what you lean on, and ask what it is made of — everything fabricated holds in this world's weather and fails in the next world's. Only what was never fabricated — the Truth, which is God's — survives the transfer of venue.

---

The demand for proof is the same challenge the Book puts to the claimants of special standing: bring your proof if you are truthful (2:111; 27:64), and the closing discovery — that the truth belongs to God — is the counterpart of the confession of the misleaders. The witness from every community is the prophet of that community, as the sūrah of the Women states (4:41), and the sūrah of the Bee describes the same proceeding (16:84). The verse's phrase about the destruction of their claims is the fitting end of the dispute: the case is not lost for want of argument but for want of the thing argued about.

## Verse 28:76

> Indeed, Korah was from the people of Moses, but he behaved arrogantly towards them. We had granted him such treasures that even their keys would burden a group of strong men. ˹Some of˺ his people advised him, “Do not be prideful! Surely Allah does not like the prideful

**The Wealthiest Man in the Sūrah's World**

The chapter introduces its second superpower: Korah — Qārūn — of the people of Moses, whose treasures required a relay of strong men just to carry the keys. The commentators placed him in the Bible's record: Korah of Numbers 16, the prominent Israelite who rebelled against Moses — but the Qur'an's portrait adds the dimension the story needs: he was one of the oppressed nation's own sons, raised from slavery's ranks into wealth that embarrassed empires. The introduction's purpose is stated by the commentators directly: his case exemplifies verse 60's appraisal — that whatever is given is the world's enjoyment and adornment — because Korah is what maximum acquisition looks like when the appraisal is refused. Pharaoh held power; Korah held gold; the sūrah files both under the same verdict by the chapter's end.

**God Loves Not the Exultant**

The counsel's closing clause is the verse's doctrine: God does not love the exultant — the ones who rejoice in what they hold as if holding were deserving. The commentators tied the clause to the chapter's running theme: exulting in the land without right (28:39), the spoiled towns exulting in their ease (28:58) — exultation is the signature sin of every prosperous refusal in the Book. The doctrine's precision matters: the crime is not wealth but the self-worship wealth induces — the conversion of gift into merit, possession into identity. The verse's placement at Korah's introduction hands the reader the verdict before the biography finishes: the man is being introduced by the clause that will bury him. Every line between — the council's advice, the show of his adornment, the earth's command — is the working out of a sentence the reader has already read.

## Verse 28:77

> Rather, seek the ˹reward˺ of the Hereafter by means of what Allah has granted you, without forgetting your share of this world. And be good ˹to others˺ as Allah has been good to you. Do not seek to spread corruption in the land, for Allah certainly does not like the corruptors.”

**"And Do Not Forget Your Portion of the World"**

The middle clause is the verse's most quoted balance: do not forget your portion of the world. The commentators gave the readings: do not waste your life neglecting good works and preparation; and do not fall into extremes of self-denial — enjoy what God has made permissible. The tradition sealed the balance with the saying the commentators transmitted from Ibn ʿUmar — work for your world as though you would live forever, and work for the Hereafter as though you were to die tomorrow. The clause's placement in counsel to a rich man matters: the Qur'an does not counsel Korah to abandon the fortune but to hold it correctly — his portion is real, designed, and lawful; what was missing was the portion's subordination. Some in the tradition even read the portion as the grave itself — the one acquisition no wealth escapes — which turns the clause's counsel from balance to urgency: take your share, but know which share is taken last.

---

The balance the verse draws between the world and its Lord is stated again in the sūrah of the Night Journey, where God gives to whom He wills of this life and the next, and in the sūrah of al-Shūrā, where those who desire the harvest of the Hereafter are given increase (42:20). The commentators gave several readings to "your portion of this world": food and clothing, a lawful livelihood, or the grave that will receive the body, and they agreed that the clause forbids both the monkish refusal of provision and the pursuit of it against the command. The verse then attaches the duty that frames the balance — do good as God has done good to you, and do not seek corruption in the land.

## Verse 28:78

> He replied, “I have been granted all this because of some knowledge I have.” Did he not know that Allah had already destroyed some from the generations before him who were far superior to him in power and greater in accumulating ˹wealth˺? There will be no need for the wicked to be asked about their sins

**The Self-Made Man's Creed**

Korah's reply is the creed of the self-made in one clause: this came to me on account of a knowledge I have. The commentators parsed the claim's layers: some took the knowledge as commercial skill — the crafts and competencies of a fortune; some transmitted the reports that he was an alchemist, reports the tradition itself dismissed; others read the phrase as opinion — in my view, I earned this. Every reading converges on the doctrine beneath: the attribution of provision to the self. The claim is the mirror-image of verse 57's error: the Quraysh knew not that God established them; Korah actively asserted the opposite credit. Between passive ignorance and active self-crediting, the Qur'an files both under the same verse — most of them know not — because the knowledge owed is the same: that what the hands gather was handed.

**The Question That Answers Him**

The verse answers the creed with a question: did he not know that God destroyed generations before him, stronger in might and greater in gathering? The commentators listed the parallel challenges — have they not seen how many generations We destroyed before them, more established in the land, with rivers flowing at their feet? (6:6) — the standing argument from history's actuarial tables. The question's logic is exact: if wealth followed from knowledge and power, the greater accumulators would still be standing; the ruins of the greater are the falsification of the merit-creed — and the falsification was standing in plain view of any man with Korah's education. The question's opening — did he not know — indicts by prior access: the information that refutes his creed was not hidden; it was unexamined, because the creed was doing too much work for its owner to audit it.

**No Interrogation Needed**

The verse closes on the sentence's procedure: the guilty will not be questioned about their sins. The reason is in the clause: their sins — the deeds are already known, itemized by the Knower of what breasts conceal (28:69); the Day's questions exist for the record's completion, not for the Judge's information.

## Verse 28:79

> Then he came out before his people in all his glamour. Those who desired the life of this world wished, “If only we could have something like what Korah has been given. He is truly a man of great fortune!”

**Power as a Public Argument**

The scene teaches the political function of display: the parade is an argument in the language of things, and it works on exactly the audience it targets. The commentators' realism holds across ages: no tyrant's court and no magnate's lobby ever relied on reason alone; the procession, the palace, the launched product are arguments addressed to the eyes — because sight is the belief-channel of the undisciplined. The Qur'an's counter is the next verse's knowing cohort, but this verse's honesty comes first: the display works. A treasury paraded through a public square recruits more believers than a sermon, and the recruits' creed is stated in their wish — fortune belongs to the displayed. The verse's gift to the reader is the parade's secret, told in advance: what converts the crowd is the lexis of things, and things are the world's own adornment (28:60) — rented, and priced to fail.

**Adornment as the Display's Whole Truth**

The verse's chosen word for what Korah wore is adornment — the same word verse 60 used to price the world — and the choice is the display's sentence before the earthquake: he came out in his decoration. The commentators' eye catches what the word concedes and denies at once: the display was genuinely beautiful — the silks shone, the procession gleamed — and it was genuinely surface, the world's own category, priced as enjoyment and ornament. The parade so shows verse 60 in motion: maximum adornment, zero duration. The verse's diagnostic for the reader is wearable too: every presentation a life makes — the clothes, the credentials, the curated feed — falls under the same file label. Decoration is lawful, even lovely; the error is the crowd's: reading the decoration as a verdict about the man.

## Verse 28:80

> But those gifted with knowledge said, “Shame on you! Allah’s reward is far better for those who believe and do good. But none will attain this except the steadfast.”

**The Reply Between the Counsel and the Earth**

The verse's placement completes the sequence the sūrah has been assembling: counsel (77), creed refused (78), parade (79), and now the public refutation (80) — the debate conducted in the square, in front of the exhibit, on the record. The commentators' narrative instinct sees the design: the knowing men's rebuke is Korah's last unmuzzled hearing — the wisdom he silenced in private now answered him in public — and the earth's command, one verse away, will ratify the rebuke in front of the same audience. The verse so leaves the reader standing in the square with the knowers: the parade is passing, the wish-cohort is murmuring. The question the scene presses on every generation is cohort assignment — not which procession to join, but which cohort to stand in when the next one passes: the wishing, or the knowing, whose rebuke time always vindicates.

**"Woe to You": Rebuke as the Knowing Men's Duty**

The knowing men's answer opens with a word the comfortable skip: woe to you — a public rebuke, addressed to the wishing crowd, in the parade's own venue. The commentators' care with the scene shows the rebuke's license and its limits: the knowing spoke because the error was doctrinal and the speakers were qualified — this is correction, not abuse; and the rebuke's content, once delivered, is the verse's real payload: God's reward is better, and patience is its gate. The scene so teaches the community's second immune response: after private counsel to the straying magnate (77) fails, the qualified must correct the audience his display recruits — because the parade's real damage is the wishes it manufactures, and wishes are corrected in public or not at all.

## Verse 28:81

> Then We caused the earth to swallow him up, along with his home. There was no one to help him against Allah, nor could he even help himself

**The Earth Answers**

The verse carries out the sentence the counsel verses delayed: We caused the earth to swallow him and his home. The instrument is the point — no invading army, no rival fortune, no plague; the ground itself, the most stable thing a treasure-owner knows, receives the command. The commentators related the reports that Moses was granted the power to call upon the earth for this — the prophet's staff, his hand, and now the ground beneath the fortune all conscripted as evidence. The display of verse 79 had been built to say this cannot end; the earth's command said otherwise in one motion, and the verse reports the motion without embellishment, because the fact needed none.

**No Party, No Self**

The closing clause prices the fortune's protection at zero twice over: he had no party to help him against God, and he could not help himself. The word for party, the commentators noted, is the Arabic of a battle-support company — the allies a man musters in war. Korah, who could fund any company, discovered the one battlefield where money conscripts nothing: against God's command, no retinue marches. The second failure is starker: the man of verse 78's self-credit — all this by my knowledge — could not devise one application of that knowledge to save his own body. The verse's arithmetic is the chapter's verdict on the self-made creed: the knowledge that could not gather what God withheld could not retain what God recalled. Wealth's two promised protections — patronage and competence — are shown failing in the same breath, on the record, before the same audience the parade had recruited.

## Verse 28:82

> And those who had craved his position the previous day began to say, “Ah! It is certainly Allah Who gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills of His servants. Had it not been for the grace of Allah, He could have surely caused the earth to swallow us up! Oh, indeed! The disbelievers will never succeed.”

**The Overnight Conversion of the Wishing Crowd**

The verse returns to the parade's audience, and the scene's irony is complete: those who wished for his position yesterday — the desire-cohort of verse 79 — are standing at the crater today, reciting theology. The commentators noted the shift's completeness: the same crowd that read the display as fortune now reads the disappearance as doctrine — it is certainly Allah who outspreads and straitens provision for whom He wills of His servants. The conversion's speed is the lesson, not a comedy: the crowd was never far from the truth; it was near-sighted, not blind. One event re-calibrated the whole faculty. The Qur'an's realism about audiences includes this encouraging fact — the parade's recruits are recoverable, sometimes overnight, because what recruited them was evidence, and evidence moves.

**The Creed They Recite**

The confession they produce is the Qur'an's own doctrine of provision: God outspreads and straitens for whom He wills — the formula repeated across the Book, in the thirteenth chapter: Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills (13:26). The seventeenth: Surely your Lord gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills (17:30). The commentators gave the particles their due: the alas at the sentence's head is read as emphasis or as a cry against the audience's own yesterday — know that God outspreads — so the recitation is both creed and self-reproach. The doctrine's content repairs exactly the error the parade had planted: provision is not earned by the displayed, nor denied to the hidden; it is administered, by will, across a population the watcher cannot audit. The crowd learned in one morning what Korah refused across a fortune's lifetime.

## Verse 28:83

> That ˹eternal˺ Home in the Hereafter We reserve ˹only˺ for those who seek neither tyranny nor corruption on the earth. The ultimate outcome belongs ˹only˺ to the righteous

**The Outcome Belongs to the Righteous**

The closing clause states the standing law the sūrah has now showed three times over: the outcome belongs to the reverent — the same verdict Moses pronounced to his people by the sea's shore: the ultimate outcome belongs ˹only˺ to the righteous (7:128). The same refrain the eleventh chapter closes its stories with. The word outcome carries both senses the Arabic holds: the end of the matter and the abode at the end — whichever reading runs, the ownership is fixed. The clause's proof in this chapter is documentary: Pharaoh took the sea, Korah took the ground, and the mother of Moses — holding nothing but a promise — took the outcome. The law's comfort for the readers of every age is its record: outcomes are not distributed by the evening news; they are held in trust by the reverent, and the trust pays on a schedule the world's markets do not post.

## Verse 28:84

> Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded with what is better. And whoever comes with an evil deed, then the evildoers will only be rewarded for what they used to do

**The Verse of the Two Handings-Over**

The Day's transaction is stated as an exchange: whoever comes with a good deed receives better than it; whoever comes with an evil deed is repaid only for what he earned. The verb is the same in both clauses — coming, bringing, the posture of a worker presenting his output — and the symmetry is the doctrine: everyone arrives with something; the difference is the exchange rate applied at the counter. The commentators paired the verse with the multiplication verse of the sixth chapter: Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold (6:160) — the better-than-it being quantified elsewhere, while here it is left vast on purpose. The evil deed's rate is stated with equal care: only for what they used to do — the word only doing the work of justice's ceiling: no compounding, no interest on wrong, repayment capped at the principal. The verse's two rates are the Qur'an's mercy and justice in a single ledger: good is under-paid forever, wrong is never over-charged.

## Verse 28:85

> Most certainly, the One Who has ordained the Quran for you will ˹ultimately˺ bring you back home ˹to Mecca˺. Say, “My Lord knows best who has come with ˹true˺ guidance and who is clearly astray.”

**The Promise of Return**

The verse addresses a grieving exile with a guarantee: the One who ordained the Qur'an for you will bring you back to the place of return. The commentators transmitted the tradition's setting: the verse, some sources say, was revealed at Juḥfah — the waypoint between Makkah and Madinah — as the Prophet made the hijrah, sorrowing to leave the city of his birth, and the verse informed him that he would return to it. The promise's structure is the sūrah's whole theology compressed into one clause: the same Hand that assigned the Book guarantees the homecoming — revelation and return issued by one Ordainer. The verse has been read on three levels: return to Makkah in victory, return to God at death, return at the Resurrection — and the layers do not compete; they nest, because every homecoming in a believer's biography is the small case of the last one.

**Say: My Lord Knows Best**

The command attached to the promise is Moses' answer from verse 37, assigned again: say — My Lord knows best who has come with guidance and who is in manifest error. The commentators noted the commission's role: the Prophet, consoled with return, is not handed a verdict against his persecutors — he is handed the referral. The reply's restraint is the mission's discipline to the end: the promise guarantees the road, not the sentencing; the case's judgment stays with the Lord who knows the breasts (28:69). The pairing of promise and referral teaches the shape of all waiting: the believer may hold certainty about God's outcome and silence about men's verdicts in the same breath, because the first is the Sustainer's field and the second is not ours to farm. The Prophet's enemies were not named, cursed, or priced in the verse — only the return was pledged, and the knowing was delegated.

## Verse 28:86

> You never expected this Book to be revealed to you, but ˹it came˺ only ˹as˺ a mercy from your Lord. So never side with the disbelievers ˹in their disbelief˺

**Mercy as the Book's Category**

The verse assigns the Book its classification: it was a mercy from your Lord — the same noun the mission wore at the mountain (28:46) and the epithet the Qur'an claims for itself across its pages. The commentators' note deserves its weight: the Book is not first a law code, a history, or an anthology — it is mercy, in the act of descending; its commands are mercy's grammar, its prohibitions mercy's fencing, its stories mercy's mirrors. The classification also answers the Book's prosecutors in every age: the document that ruins your schemes is not your enemy; it is the warning that arrived while the ruin was still repairable. The mercy's address — from your Lord — personalizes it: the same Lord who routed a basket, funded a hiding, and answered a shepherd's prayer sent the Book as His signature care, delivered to the man who needed it and through him to everyone who would ever need it.

## Verse 28:87

> Do not let them turn you away from the revelations of Allah after they have been sent down to you. Rather, invite ˹all˺ to ˹the Way of˺ your Lord, and never be one of the polytheists

**The Tradition's Two Audiences**

The commentators preserved a widening of the verse's address: some said it was revealed when the Quraysh tried to induce the Prophet to praise or magnify their gods — the compromise that would have dissolved the message into their shrine — while others held the real audience was the believers as a whole. The widening matters: whatever the first occasion, the command's grammar outgrew it, because the pressure it names is permanent. Every generation of believers inherits the same three-part charge — hold the delivered signs against the culture's pull, keep calling, keep the creed clean — because every generation hosts its own bargaining committee, its own soft demand that the recitation be slightly less recited. The verse's transferability is the Qur'an's design: instructions addressed to the Prophet at maximum pressure become the community's standing job description at every pressure after.

**The Verse Before the Closing Creed**

The verse's placement in the sūrah's last movement shows its function: the Prophet has been given the promise of return (85), the ownership check on the Book (86), and now the ministry's permanent orders (87) — before the chapter closes on the purest creed verse (88). The sequence is a commissioning in miniature: logistics, legitimacy, duty, and then the doctrine that fuels the whole. The commentators' summary carries the arrangement's logic: a messenger secured in his return, clear about his Book's authorship, and ordered into the call, requires one final sentence to hold it all — there is no god but Him — and the next verse supplies it. The reader standing here, at the chapter's edge, holds the same sequence as a personal commissioning: his own return is promised, his trust is assigned, his call is standing, and the creed is about to be recited one last time — in the sūrah's final verse, at full volume.

## Verse 28:88

> And do not invoke any other god with Allah. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. Everything is bound to perish except He Himself. All authority belongs to Him. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned

**Everything Perishes Except His Face**

The verse's center holds the chapter's heaviest clause: everything perishes except His Face — and the commentators' readings circled its exact sense with care. Some read the Face as God Himself, His Self, the Reality. Others as what is turned toward — every object of aspiration other than God coming to nothing; others as the qualifying exception — all things perish except what is done for His countenance, His religion, the deeds by which He is approached. The commentator tradition cited the Prophet's judgment on the poet Labīd — the truest word spoken by an Arab in poetry: Behold! Apart from Allah everything is vain (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2255a) — the pre-Islamic verse that arrived, unaided, at the Qur'an's doctrine. The commentators also carried al-Rāzī's garment image: the tattered robe is called perished though its fibers remain — creation's perishing is its removal from service, not its annihilation, as the Qur'an states elsewhere: the earth will be changed into a different earth and the heavens as well (14:48). Every reading converges on the clause's practical force: the perishable cannot be worshipped as if it held, because holding is precisely what it does not do.
