# Sūrah al-Jumuʿah (Chapter 62) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:1

> Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth ˹constantly˺ glorifies Allah—the King, the Most Holy, the Almighty, the All-Wise

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Opening Shared With Other Sūrahs**

This sentence, that whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth glorifies God, opens five Madinan sūrahs, and each of them continues into a different subject. Sūrat al-Ḥadīd states it briefly (57:1), Sūrat al-Ḥashr repeats it (59:1), Sūrat al-Ṣaff uses it before its rebuke of idle claims (61:1), and Sūrat al-Taghābun adds a word to the verb that marks the praise as unbroken (64:1). This sūrah keeps the sentence and then extends it with two names no other opening contains: the King, the Most Holy. The addition is the first clue about the sūrah's subject matter. The chapter is about the community that was given a messenger and a book, and about what happens to a people whose possession of revelation becomes a title rather than a trust. The names that open it establish who owns the authority that such a community is handling, and they establish it before the chapter says anything.

**The King, Whose Ownership Is Total**

*Al-Malik* is the sovereign, the one who holds the authority to command, and the name stands at the front of a sūrah that will describe a messenger raised up among people and a book entrusted to them. The Book states the same ownership in a verse of pure praise: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘O Allah, Master of all dominion! You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from whom You please.’” (3:26). That verse and this name together dismantle the assumption the sūrah's later passage will address, in which a people imagine that being chosen amounts to holding a permanent title. Authority in the Qur'anic scheme is delegated and revocable. It is given to a messenger for a mission, and to a community for a responsibility, and it belongs to no one by right. The name with which this sūrah opens is therefore more than a statement of God's greatness. It is the foundation of the whole chapter's argument about what it means for a people to be entrusted with anything at all.

**The Most Holy, Whose Purity Is Absolute**

*Al-Quddūs* belongs to the same root as *qudus*, holiness, and it describes a being entirely free of every defect, every limit, and every resemblance to the created thing. The Book uses the name to explain why the praise of creation is even possible: a creature that glorifies God is declaring Him beyond the shortcomings that creatures share, and the declaration would be meaningless if the One being addressed were merely the greatest among existing things. Holiness also explains why the Book calls the angel who brings revelation the Holy Spirit (2:87, in meaning): the message is fitted to the one who sends it. Every verse of this sūrah has to be read in that light. A messenger reciting God's revelation, a book entrusted to a community, a heart purified by teaching — all of these are transactions with a being whose purity cannot be matched by anything He has made, and the work of carrying His word is conducted in that difference.

**Praise That Never Pauses**

The verb form the translation renders as “constantly glorifies” describes an action in progress, a praise that is underway now and continuing, not a report of something done once in the past. The Book states the same continuity with unusual force: “There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification.” (17:44). Two claims stand together in that verse and in this opening. The praise of creation is universal, and it is not conducted in human language. A believer therefore reads the sentence as a statement of fact about the world he actually inhabits: the stones, the stars, the trees, and the animals are already engaged in the declaration that their Maker is free of every fault. What the sūrah will go on to describe is the entry of one community into the work of teaching and purification, which is a human form of the same glorification, offered by creatures whose praise can be heard and understood.

**Power and Wisdom as the Ground of the Opening Sentence**

The verse closes with the familiar pair, the Almighty and the All-Wise, and these are the two attributes that make the opening claim credible. Power is what allows every created thing to be engaged in praise without any of it being wasted or unheard; nothing in the universe is too small for the declaration to count, and nothing is too large to be exempt from it. Wisdom is what allows the praise of a stone and the praise of a prophet to belong to the same order, each in its own measure, without confusion between the creature and the Creator. The names are also placed here to bracket the whole chapter, so that the reader holds the two facts of power and wisdom while reading everything that follows. The sentence does not present the praise of creation as a poetic flourish on the way to the real subject; it presents it as the frame in which the chapter's rebukes and instructions make sense, since a command is only as weighty as the authority behind it. The sūrah will go on to describe authority delegated and revoked, teaching given and refused, and a community that carried a scripture it did not obey. Every one of those subjects rests on the two facts asserted in this line: the God who commands is able to see that His command is carried out, and He arranges the outcome of every refusal according to a purpose that does not fail.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:2

> He is the One Who raised for the illiterate ˹people˺ a messenger from among themselves—reciting to them His revelations, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom, for indeed they had previously been clearly astray—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Messenger Raised From Among the Illiterate**

*Al-ummiyyūn* is the word the Book uses for a people who possess no scripture and no tradition of reading one, and the term carries no contempt in this verse. It describes a condition, and the condition is the point. The Arabs of Mecca knew poetry with extraordinary precision and legal genealogy with obsessive care, but they had no book, no scribal class, and no inherited text to compare a new revelation against. Some exegetes read *al-ummiyyūn* as the peoples outside the People of the Book rather than as people who could not write, and in Mecca the two descriptions come to the same thing: no scripture, no scribes, and no scholarly class. The messenger who came to them was one of them, born and raised in their streets, and the Book makes a claim about the source of the mission by naming that fact first. Anyone who had watched him for forty years knew the limits of his training. The revelation he recited therefore had nowhere else to come from. In another sūrah the Book states the same arrangement with the same wording about being sent among you: “Since We have sent you a messenger from among yourselves—reciting to you Our revelations, purifying you, teaching you the Book and wisdom, and teaching you what you never knew” (2:151).

**Four Acts That Make One Mission**

The verse describes the messenger's work with four verbs, and their order repays study. He recites the revelations, which puts the spoken word first, since the message entered the world through a voice before it was ever a page. He purifies the hearers, which treats their moral condition as part of the work rather than as a side effect. He teaches them the Book, which names the text they did not previously have. And he teaches them wisdom, which is the capacity to apply what the Book says in situations the Book does not spell out. The four belong together, and the Book repeats the set whenever it describes the sending of a prophet — in Abraham's prayer for a messenger from among his descendants (2:129) and in the reminder to the believers of what God had done for them (3:164). The verses treat these four acts as a single favour, which means the community received its book and its moral formation from the same hand.

**Why Purification Comes Before Teaching**

The second verb sits between recitation and instruction, and it belongs there. *Tazkiya* means to make something grow and to cleanse it at the same time, and the Book uses the word for the growth of a person's moral self as well as the removal of its diseases. Teaching without it produces a person who can recite what he does not embody, and the Book has hard words for that condition, asking a group who read the Scripture why they preach righteousness and fail to practise it (2:44). The order given here explains why the Prophet's work with his Companions took years, and why his method was example before instruction and companionship before argument. Knowledge in the Qur'anic sense is not a load that a person carries; it is a state he lives in, and the one who is supposed to live in it needs to be prepared by purification before he can be trusted with the material. That is why the mission is described as a favour rather than a training programme.

**The State They Were In Before**

The verse closes its description with a diagnosis: they had previously been in clear error. *Ḍalāl mubīn* is the Book's term for being lost with the road nowhere in sight, and it does not soften the condition of Arabia before the mission. Idols stood in the House of Abraham, daughters were buried alive, blood feuds consumed generations, and the strong took whatever they wished from the weak. The Book states this without embarrassment because the contrast is the evidence. A community that produced, within a single generation, teachers of monotheism, law, and ethics from that starting point is a community whose transformation requires an explanation outside itself. Recitation, purification, and teaching are the explanation offered. The diagnosis is also a mercy in its plainness: it means that no one who hears the message needs to pretend he was already guided, and it means that the distance a person must travel is not the measure of whether he can arrive.

**The Prayer That Was Answered**

The Book records that this sending had been requested long before it happened. Abraham, standing with Ishmael by the foundations of the House, prayed: “Our Lord! Raise from among them a messenger who will recite to them Your revelations, teach them the Book and wisdom, and purify them. Indeed, You ˹alone˺ are the Almighty, AllWise.” (2:129). The four verbs of that prayer are the four verbs of this verse, which means the mission of the Prophet is presented in the Book as the answer to an earlier prophet's supplication. That connection matters for how the community reads its own history. What arrived in Mecca was not an accident of history or a response to favourable conditions. It was a request made at the founding of the sanctuary, carried through generations of a people who had mostly forgotten what their ancestor asked for, and granted at the time appointed. The names at the end of this verse, the Almighty and the All-Wise, appear at the end of Abraham's prayer as well. A promise made at the founding of the sanctuary and a mission granted centuries later belong, in the Book's telling, to a single plan.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:3

> along with others of them who have not yet joined them ˹in faith˺. For He is the Almighty, All-Wise

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Clause That Opens the Message Outward**

The verse attaches a second group to the unlettered people among whom the messenger was raised: others of them who have not yet joined them. The Arabic *lammā yalḥaqū bihim* describes people not yet arrived, not yet attached to the first company, and the exegetes read the clause as a widening of the mission beyond its immediate recipients. Grammatically the new group is joined to *al-ummiyyūn*, so the sending covers both the people present at the revelation and those who would come later. Several readings of the precise scope have been offered — a later generation of Arabs, non-Arabs entering the faith, or every community that receives the message until the end of time — and they are not mutually exclusive. What the clause guarantees is that no community can treat itself as the terminal owner of the message. A religion sent only to its first audience would be a tribal possession; a religion that includes in its founding document a clause about people who have not yet arrived is a trust for anyone who comes later. That is the force of the clause as the earliest readers understood it, and the tradition shows them pressing the Prophet for its meaning rather than leaving it to later scholars.

**The Question Asked Three Times**

The Companions themselves wanted to know who the clause referred to. The report in al-Bukhārī records Abū Hurayrah saying that when this sūrah was revealed and the verse was recited, he asked the Prophet who these people were, and received no answer until he had asked three times. Salman al-Fārisī was sitting among them, and the Prophet placed his hand on him and said, “If Faith were at ˹the place of˺ Ath-Thuraiya ˹the Pleiades, the highest star˺, even then ˹some men or a man from these people˺ would attain it.” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4897). The gesture and the sentence identify a Persian convert as a representative of the nations still to come, and the image of faith being snatched from the highest star conveys the effort such people would make to reach a truth that others had received without travelling for it. The report explains why the verse has always been read as a promise about the community's future reach. The Prophet's silence before he answered, and the three requests that preceded the answer, also teach something about how a community is allowed to press for knowledge of its own future: the answer came in time, and it came with a person named rather than a nation described.

**No Community Owns the Message**

The widening clause does more than predict numbers. It forecloses a claim the sūrah will shortly confront from another direction: the claim that a people's descent or prior possession of a scripture constitutes a permanent standing before God. By naming future arrivals in the founding verse of the community's identity, the Book sets the terms on which anyone may enter. The Book states the same universality in other places with equal clarity: the Prophet is described as a mercy to all worlds (21:107), and as sent to humanity at large rather than to a single nation (34:28). The history that followed the verse's revelation has a certain appropriateness to it. The community that received this Book carried it out of Arabia into Persia, Syria, Egypt, North Africa, and beyond, and the people named in the clause became the majority of the believers. Neither the clause nor the history leaves room for treating the message as the property of one lineage. The verse states in advance the shape of a religion that would not stay where it began.

**Why the Names at the End Belong Here**

The verse closes as the sūrah's opening closed, with the Almighty and the All-Wise, and the pairing does specific work at this point in the argument. Power answers a question the clause raises: how could a message pronounced in a small city reach people not yet born in lands not yet visited? The Book has already made the claim elsewhere that God's word is not bounded by the audience in front of the speaker. Wisdom answers the second question: why the delay, and why different peoples receive the message in different generations, some before they are born and some after centuries of waiting. The Book places the timing of guidance in God's knowledge rather than in human deserving, and it says so directly in the following verse. The names at the end of this verse are therefore not a formal close. They are the guarantee that the promise about those not yet joined will actually be fulfilled.

**A Promise That Became a Community**

The clause has a history that can be traced, and the tracing illuminates what the verse claims. Within the Prophet's lifetime, the community's composition changed: the majority of those who entered Islam in the years after the conquest of Mecca were not from Quraysh, and the delegations that came to Madinah came from across the peninsula. Within two generations the faith was established in Iraq, Syria, Egypt, and Iran, and the leading teachers of the Book in those lands were largely the descendants of the peoples the Book named as future arrivals. The Book elsewhere identifies this outcome as an act of divine choosing rather than of circumstance, and it sets the criterion plainly: God chooses whom He wills for His mercy, and the bounty is His to give. That is exactly the ground on which the next verse places the entire matter, and the clause about those not yet joined is its first instalment.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:4

> This is the favour of Allah. He grants it to whoever He wills. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Favour, Not a Wage**

The verse names the whole mission as a favour from God, and the word choice repays attention. A *faḍl* is an excess, something given beyond what is owed, a gift that the recipient had no claim upon and could not have demanded. The sending of a messenger from among an unlettered people, the recitation of revelations, the purification of hearts, and the teaching of a book and wisdom together are one favour, not several transactions. This is the Qur'an's consistent way of describing guidance, and its opposite is stated with equal clarity: the Book rejects the idea that God owes guidance to anyone, or that a people could earn a standing that obliges Him to keep them. The verse also answers a thought that any honest listener would have. A community that received such a messenger might be tempted to imagine that its reception proved something about itself. The sentence refuses that train of thought before it starts: what arrived was not paid for. The Book describes the sending of the Prophet in another place as a mercy to all worlds (21:107), and mercy is by definition something given rather than earned.

**Given Where the Giver Chooses**

The verse states that the favour is granted to whoever God wills, and the statement cuts in two directions. It blocks any claim of exclusive descent, which is precisely the claim the sūrah will confront a few verses later from a group who held that they were God's chosen people. And it explains the phenomenon the previous verse announced, that people who had not yet arrived in the world would one day be included in the same gift. The Book applies this principle to the whole economy of guidance: Abraham asks for a messenger for his descendants, and the answer comes with its own qualification, that God's covenant does not extend to the wrongdoers (2:124). The Book also describes the practice of the messenger himself in the same terms: he gives what he was given, and the bestowal belongs to God. A community that understands this cannot comfortably assume that its standing is an inheritance, and it cannot resent the arrival of others who receive what it was given. The same distribution is stated to the Prophet himself: “You are not responsible for people’s guidance ˹O Prophet˺—it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills.” (2:272).

**The Lord of Infinite Bounty**

The closing name, *dhū al-faḍl al-ʿaẓīm*, is possessive: a Lord who owns and dispenses great bounty, and whose giving does not diminish what He has. The construction guards against two errors. It excludes the idea that the favour given to the Prophet's community was taken from anyone else's share, since the giver's store is not a fixed quantity. And it excludes the idea that God's giving ends with the gifts already named. The Book repeats the phrase in contexts of generosity and increase: it promises those who believe and give that God will repay them in full and increase them from His grace (35:30), and it names forgiveness, mercy, and provision as the bounty of the One who gives it. The name at the end of this verse therefore answers a quiet fear that a thoughtful reader may have. The gift of a messenger was the largest thing their community ever received, and the verse says that its giver is not exhausted by it. The phrase returns in the Book's promises to those who give and strive, which means the same generosity that sent the messenger is at work in the reward that follows obedience.

**The Gifts Listed Just Before**

The verse's position in the sūrah matters. It follows a description of a messenger, a recitation, a purification, a teaching, and an extension to people not yet born, and it gathers all of them into a single phrase. The effect is to make the reader see the size of what has just been described. The Book frequently sums up its own lists this way, and the pattern teaches the community how to read its own blessings. A messenger arrived from nowhere in a city that had no book; a community was turned from idolatry to the worship of one God; a text appeared whose words were memorised by thousands of people who owned no written copies; and the same message travelled to nations that had not yet existed. Read as a sentence, the list is astonishing. Read as a wage, none of it makes sense, and the verse insists on the correct accounting: this is a favour, granted to whom it is granted, from a giver whose generosity has no ceiling.

**Favour as a Recurring Answer to Envy**

The Book has already answered those who resented the gift in terms that match this verse exactly. One passage asks: “Do they envy those people for what Allah has given them out of His bounty?” (4:54), and continues by describing the favour given to the family of Abraham. Another reports that many of the People of the Book wished they could turn the believers back to disbelief out of envy after the truth had become clear to them (2:109). The Book's response in all these passages is the same one this verse gives: the bounty is God's to place where He wills, and envy of it is an argument with the Giver rather than with the recipient. The sūrah is about to describe a community that had been entrusted with a scripture and failed to carry it, and it closes the present verse with the name of a Lord whose bounty is infinite. Both halves of the chapter belong together.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:5

> The example of those who were entrusted with ˹observing˺ the Torah but failed to do so, is that of a donkey carrying books. How evil is the example of those who reject Allah’s signs! For Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Animal That Carries What It Cannot Read**

The comparison is deliberately coarse, and its force comes from a physical image everyone knows. A donkey loaded with books bears the full weight of learning; the load is real, the pages are real, and the animal arrives carrying more of them than any student in the city. What it does not have is the slightest access to what it is carrying, and no amount of weight changes that. The Book uses the picture for a community entrusted with the Torah, and the sting lies in the word *entrusted*. These were not people who had lost the scripture or never found it; they had been made responsible for it and had let the responsibility sit on them without reaching them. The verse does not describe a failure of possession. It describes a failure of the traffic between what a person holds and what a person is, and it chooses the one image in which the difference is impossible to mistake. The Book uses animals for this kind of rebuke elsewhere, comparing those who turn away from the reminder to “spooked zebras fleeing from a lion” (74:50–51), and the effect is the same: a reader is shown himself from outside.

**What “They Did Not Carry It Out” Means**

The Arabic *lam yaḥmilūhā* means they did not take it up, did not shoulder it, did not carry out the obligations the text imposed. The Book accuses no one here of losing the scrolls; the scrolls were being studied and copied. The accusation concerns performance. The Book states the substance of the charge elsewhere when it describes a covenant taken from those given the Scripture that they would make it known to humanity rather than conceal it, and then says of them that they cast it behind their backs (3:187). It also records the command that leaves no room for private possession: “You have no ground to stand on unless you uphold the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to you from your Lord.” (5:68). The legal requirement of a revealed text, in this framework, is not that it be preserved and revered. It is that it be obeyed, and the failure named in the verse is the gap between a community's reverence and its conduct.

**Understanding Without Obedience**

The donkey is not indicted for reading badly. The image works because the animal cannot read at all and is not blamed for it; the burden simply passes through its body unchanged. What the verse attacks is the human version of that condition, in which knowledge passes through a person without transforming anything. The Book treats this as a distinct danger for religious communities, because knowledge of the text can become a possession that confers rank, an identity that produces pride, or a professional asset that is defended rather than practised. The Qur'an asks a hard question of a group in exactly this position: “Do you preach righteousness and fail to practise it yourselves, although you read the Scripture? Do you not understand?” (2:44). That question and this image belong to the same diagnosis. What the Book demands of a person who knows is that the knowledge move him, and where it does not, the load he carries is a measure of how far he has failed to travel.

**From One Community to the General Rule**

The verse moves from the particular group to a general verdict at the end: how evil is the example of those who reject the signs of God, and God does not guide the wrongdoing people. The shift is meaningful. The Torah-carriers are given as a case, and the rule that follows applies to anyone who treats revelation as cargo rather than as command. The Arabic word for the crime is *kufr*, which carries the sense of covering, and the Book's use of it here draws attention to a fact about religious language: a person who knows the truth and refuses to act on it is covering it, whether or not he denies it with his tongue. That is why the verse's closing sentence is not a change of subject but a restatement. Refusing to carry a message one has accepted is a way of rejecting the message, and the wrongdoers who are not guided in this verse are people whose refusal has taken a quieter form than outright denial.

**The Warning That Travels With the Verse**

The Book's habit of applying earlier failures to later communities is visible in the way it hands this image on. The same Prophet's community is warned in its own scripture against the condition described here: those who recite the Book are rebuked by a verse that asks them whether they preach what they do not practise (2:44), and the sūrah of the ranks, immediately before this one, rebukes a community that said what it did not do (61:2). The Book never mentions the failure of a previous nation as a piece of history alone. It is always a photograph of a possible future, and the community that receives revelation after them is placed in the same position the donkey-verses describe. What differs between communities is not the size of the load. It is whether the ones carrying it read, understand, and act on what they have been given, which is why the verse ends by naming the wrongdoers rather than the ignorant as the people who are not guided.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:6

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O Jews! If you claim to be Allah’s chosen ˹people˺ out of all humanity, then wish for death, if what you say is true.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim Tested by Its Own Logic**

The verse addresses a group who claimed a status and then asks them to submit that claim to a test they cannot rig. The claim is exclusivity before God; the test is a wish. If the Hereafter belongs to you alone, and if you are as beloved to God as you say, then the death that stands between you and your exclusive reward is not an enemy but a doorway. A person who was certain of that would want it opened. The Book does not ask the claimants to renounce anything or to fight anyone. It asks them to want what their own doctrine makes desirable, and it treats their reaction as evidence. That is a powerful form of argument because it moves the case out of the realm of inherited claims and into the inner life of the person making them. No document, genealogy, or tradition can hide a man from his own reluctance to stand in front of his Lord. The challenge is a mercy in disguise, since it makes a public claim answerable to a private test that no one can perform on another's behalf.

**What the Qur’an Grants and Denies About Chosenness**

The Book does not deny that Israel was favoured. It states the favours in the plainest words: remember all the favours I granted you and how I honoured you above the others (2:47). It also names the families that God chose: Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ʿImrān, chosen above all people of their time (3:33). What the Book denies is the assumption that election is unconditional and permanent. Abraham, the father of the line, asked that his offspring be included in the covenant of leadership, and the answer came with a limitation built into it: “My covenant is not extended to the wrongdoers.” (2:124). The same qualification is being tested here, and the test has force precisely because the claimants knew their own record. A chosen people, in the Qur'anic sense, is a people chosen for a task and answerable for it, and the promise is never detached from the conduct that the promise requires. The Book tells the descendants of Israel to remember the favours and warns them in the same breath, which shows that the memory of election was meant to produce gratitude rather than exemption (2:47).

**Why a Wish Would Settle the Matter**

The logic of the challenge is sound and does not depend on the eloquence of the challenger. If a man is convinced that a meeting with his Lord awaits him, with a reward that no one else can share, the desire for that meeting is a natural consequence of his belief, and reluctance at the door is a natural symptom of doubt. The Book's argument is therefore psychological before it is theological. It does not say the claimants are dishonest in their claim; it says that their own inner life will disclose the truth of it, because a person's wants follow his convictions and cannot be ordered about. The test also has the virtue of being available to everyone who reads the verse. Any community that claims a special standing, and any individual who imagines that his group's status secures his own, is asked by these words whether he would welcome the appointed meeting or would prefer it delayed. That is why the Book treats the test as dispositive rather than merely polemical: it turns the argument inward, where a person cannot be deceived by his own slogans.

**Directed at a Claim, Not at a People**

The address is specific, and the specificity should not be read as a general statement about descent. The verse confronts a group who had turned a real favour into a private entitlement; it does not pronounce on every individual of any ancestry, since the Book states its own universal rule elsewhere: whoever believes in God and the Last Day and does good has his reward with his Lord, with no fear and no grief (2:62). The Book's confrontation with the claim is about the claim. The Qur'an reserves some of its hardest language for people who use their religion as a status rather than a discipline, and this is one of those places. It is no accident that the verse comes in a sūrah that has just described a people who carried a scripture and never fulfilled it, and is about to describe a community that preferred a caravan to a sermon. The defect being addressed is not the lineage but the confidence.

**The Same Test in Sūrat al-Baqarah**

The challenge is stated earlier in almost the same words, and the comparison shows how precisely the Book repeats its diagnostics: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘If the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter with Allah is exclusively for you ˹Israelites˺ out of all humanity, then wish for death if what you say is true!’” (2:94). That verse is followed immediately by the prediction of this one, that they will never wish for it (2:95), and by a further description of their clinging to life (2:96). The Qur’an thus states the argument, the outcome, and the psychology in sequence, and the sūrah of Friday states the same argument and the same outcome in the same order. Two audiences, two eras, one instrument of testing: the Book measures claims of privilege by the desires of the people who make them, and it lets the measurement stand as the verdict. The repetition is characteristic of the Book, which states its central arguments in more than one sūrah so that no community can claim it was never told.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:7

> But they will never wish for that because of what their hands have done. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Admission Contained in the Refusal**

The verse states the outcome before any of the claimants has spoken: they will never wish for it. The prediction is not a prophecy about a particular group's stubbornness but a conclusion drawn from their own condition, and the reason is given in the same sentence: because of what their hands have done. The Book draws the connection between the two halves with complete precision. The claim to exclusive standing requires a certain kind of inner life; the record requires a different one; and the two cannot be held together. What the claimants fear is not death as such, and it is not the loss of the world — the next verse will describe a flight from death that the Book treats as futile. What they fear is the meeting, and their fear is a testimony about their record delivered by the only witness whose honesty is beyond dispute. The Book draws conclusions from inner states in this way throughout, and it does so without claiming to read minds, because the states it names have already become visible in conduct.

**“What Their Hands Have Done”**

The phrase places responsibility on the deeds themselves, since the hands are the Book's standard figure for what a person has earned. That idiom runs through the whole scripture and is the basis of its account of justice: the reward or the punishment of the Hereafter is described in the same words in more than one place, and always with the same qualification that God is never unjust to His creation (3:182; 8:51). The Book does not treat the fear under discussion as an irrational dread of a fact of nature. It treats it as the accurate self-assessment of a person who knows what he has done. That is why the verse can join the conscience of the individual to the knowledge of God in a single line, since the two are describing the same record from two sides. The Book also adds the reason for its confidence about the future of the claimants: nothing about a person's inner condition is hidden from the One who keeps it. The phrase also rules out any suggestion that the punishment is a matter of caprice: what is feared is a record, and the record was written by the one being judged.

**The Fear That Contradicts a Claim**

The psychology is worth isolating, because the Book treats it as diagnostic. A person who believes that a great reward awaits him at the end of a road does not usually postpone the journey for its own sake. The claimants had inherited a story about their standing with God, and the story had become the basis of a public position; the fear at the bottom of it was never allowed to surface, because surfacing it would have required a different public position. The Book's rebuke in the nearby sūrah is more direct: “You will surely find them clinging to life more eagerly than any other people, even more than polytheists. Each one of them wishes to live a thousand years. But even if they were to live that long, it would not save them from the punishment.” (2:96). The wish for a thousand years and the refusal to wish for death are two descriptions of the same attachment, and both of them stand in open contradiction to a claim that the next life is the better portion. A person's wants are the most honest part of him, which is why the Book so often lets them testify.

**The Knowledge That Closes the Verse**

The verse ends by naming God as having perfect knowledge of the wrongdoers, and the words chosen are not a threat attached at the end for effect. The claim under examination had been presented as knowledge about God's intentions: we are the beloved, the reward is ours, the others have no share. The final clause answers such claims on their own ground. Knowledge of who stands where does not belong to the claimants, and the One who possesses it does not judge by a community's account of itself. The same closing formula appears immediately after the same challenge in Sūrat al-Baqarah (2:95), which means the two passages end with the same correction: what people assert about their own standing is not the record, and the record rests with the One whose knowledge of the wrongdoers is complete. What is being corrected is a claim to know God's decision in advance, and the correction returns the matter to the only party who actually holds it.

**The Warning That Remains Available**

A reader should notice what this verse does not say. It does not say that the people addressed are beyond correction, and it does not close the door on them. The Book states plainly that whoever believes in God and the Last Day and does good will have his reward with his Lord (2:62), and it accepts into the community anyone who comes with faith, whatever his ancestry and whatever his previous position. That means the verdict in this verse is not a verdict about descent but about a position that has hardened into a refusal; and a position can change. The Book's argument with these claimants is built on their own fear of the meeting, and fear of the meeting is a form of knowledge about the meeting. The verse leaves that knowledge in their hands, along with the choice of what to do with a truth that has been announced rather than merely argued. Anyone who reads the verse and finds the fear of the meeting in himself has received the diagnosis before the record is presented to him.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:8

> Say, “The death you are running away from will inevitably come to you. Then you will be returned to the Knower of the seen and unseen, and He will inform you of what you used to do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Door That Opens Wherever You Are**

The verse names the flight directly: the death you are running away from will inevitably come to you. The Book states this fact in several ways, and each of them closes a different escape route. It says that every soul will taste death (3:185), which settles the question of whether anyone is exempt. It says that wherever you may be, death will overcome you, even if you were in fortified towers (4:78), which settles the question of whether a safe place exists. And it says here that the very thing being fled is the thing that will arrive, which settles the question of whether running is a strategy at all. The Book also refuses the idea of a postponement that can be bought, whether with strength, wealth, or lineage, since the appointment is fixed by the One who set every term in the first place. The three statements are not repetitions of an obvious point. They are a systematic dismantling of the assumption on which a person builds a life spent postponing the reckoning, and the sūrah places the dismantling directly after the claim of privilege that preceded it.

**Death Described as an Appointment**

The Arabic describes death as something that will meet the fugitive, an encounter rather than an event that happens to an organism. The language is consistent throughout the Book, which treats death as a rendezvous that cannot be missed and cannot be rescheduled, and treats the timing of it as fixed in a register no creature can inspect. Certainty about the meeting and ignorance of its hour belong together, and that combination is the structure of human responsibility. That is why the Book never presents the fear of death as irrational. What it presents as irrational is the belief that the appointment can be avoided by arrangement. Whoever has looked into the inescapable end of every human life and accepted it has removed one enormous source of false security, and the sūrah's context shows why the removal matters. The people who refused to wish for the meeting had built a position on the assumption that the meeting was far off, and the verse undercuts the assumption before it ever addresses the position.

**Returned to the Knower of the Seen and Unseen**

The end of the journey is stated with a name that carries the whole argument: you will be returned to the One who knows the seen and the unseen. The Book uses this pairing of the hidden and the visible when it wants to make plain that nothing is outside the scope of the reckoning. “He is the One Who created the heavens and the earth in truth… He is the Knower of all—seen or unseen.” (6:73). “˹He is the˺ Knower of the seen and the unseen—the All-Great, Most Exalted.” (13:9). The point is not merely that God is well informed; it is that the boundary humans rely on most — the line between what other people can see and what they cannot — does not exist for the One to whom they return. Every concealed motive, every private calculation, every act performed in the dark is inside the zone of the seen for the One before whom the journey ends. The Book names the same pair of attributes when it describes the God who gathers humanity, which is why the verse can move from flight to return without changing its subject (59:22).

**Informed of What You Used to Do**

The verse's final clause describes the reckoning in the language of information: He will inform you of what you used to do. The Book consistently presents the judgement as disclosure rather than invention, and its account of the final day includes the production of a record that even the person himself cannot dispute. Sūrat al-Zalzalah states the same principle in the smallest possible measure: people will proceed in separate groups to be shown the consequences of their deeds, and whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it (99:6–8). What awaits the person who fled is not an arbitrary sentence imposed from outside. It is the complete and accurate account of his own life, delivered by the One who recorded it, and the reason the Book describes this as a meeting to be feared by the guilty is that the account is the truth.

**Why This Verse Follows the Claim of Privilege**

The placement of the verse is part of its argument. A community that had built its identity on a claim of exclusive standing had a stake in the postponement of the reckoning, because the reckoning is where claims are examined and standings are decided by the record rather than by the register. The verse answers the claim by removing the possibility of deferral. Every soul arrives at the appointment with its own file and no representative, and the informer at the end of the road is the One who knows what was seen and what was hidden. Read in sequence, the three verses of this passage state the challenge, the refusal, and the appointment; they are the complete account of a claim examined and settled, and the Book provides the same account in Sūrat al-Baqarah, which shows that the examination is a standing rule rather than a single episode. What the passage offers is not comfort but clarity, and the Book prefers clarity: the reader is told the truth about the appointment long before the appointment arrives.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:9

> O believers! When the call to prayer is made on Friday, then proceed ˹diligently˺ to the remembrance of Allah and leave off ˹your˺ business. That is best for you, if only you knew

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Summons and the Movement It Commands**

The verse addresses the believers as the people who have accepted the call, and it lays on them a duty that begins the moment the Friday announcement is made. The Arabic phrase rendered as “proceed ˹diligently˺” comes from a root that means to move with purpose, to exert oneself toward a goal, and exegetes discuss whether the command requires hurrying or simply going with intent; the latter reading has the stronger support in the community's practice, since the Book elsewhere rejects agitation in the approach to worship. What is beyond dispute is the direction of the movement. The time of the summons is not a period for finishing a deal; it is appointed for a specific assembly, and the believer is asked to set out for it rather than to drift into it. The obligation is communal as well as personal, since a summons presupposes a gatherer and an assembly, and the address to all the believers shows that the duty belongs to the community rather than to its leading men alone. The verse names the destination as the remembrance of God, which covers the sermon and the prayer together, and it fixes the whole act in a single phrase that keeps the purpose visible even after the mechanics have become familiar.

**Leaving the Market Behind**

The second command is explicit: leave off your business. The word *bayʿ* is commerce in its ordinary sense, buying and selling, the activity on which the merchants of Madinah depended for income. The command stops it for the duration of the assembly, and the Book does not apologise for the interruption. This is not a rejection of trade, and the sūrah itself will send the believers back out among their affairs as soon as the prayer is finished. What the verse does is establish a hierarchy: the market can wait an hour, and the One who sustains the market is entitled to a portion of the day. Sūrat al-Nūr draws the portrait of the men who live by this hierarchy, describing those whom neither buying nor selling distracts from God's remembrance, prayer, and charity, and who fear a day on which hearts and eyes will tremble (24:37).

**Those Who Stay Away Three Times**

The seriousness of the obligation is visible in the warning the Prophet attached to it, and the report is sound enough to be stated with its citation: the Prophet said that whoever leaves the Friday prayer continuously for three Fridays out of slackness, Allah will print a stamp on his heart (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 1052, graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī). The language of stamping is the language of the previous verses: the heart that keeps refusing the summons is marked by its refusal, and the mark becomes a settled character. The warning is aimed with care: it threatens neither wealth nor body but the organ that receives guidance. The Friday assembly was not only an obligation to be discharged but a weekly arrangement through which the community was taught, corrected, and held together, and the one who removes himself from it removes himself from the channel. The week had a fixed appointment for instruction and correction; a person who stays away is choosing to be corrected by other means, and the hadith states where that choice ends.

**“That Is Best for You”**

The verse closes with the same comparative statement the sūrah of the ranks used a few verses earlier in the Book's order (61:11), and the repetition of the formula is instructive. The Book does not leave the obligation as a bare command with a sanction attached. It makes an assertion about the outcome: obedience in this matter is better for the person who obeys, and the gain is measured against what he gives up by closing his stall. The veil drawn over the calculation is acknowledged in the same breath: if only you knew. The knowledge of what the assembly actually produces — the teaching, the unity, the correction, the remembrance — is hidden from the man who only sees the hours of trade going by. The verse thus treats the Friday obligation as a matter of clear interest, and it leaves the reader to supply the confidence that God's arithmetic is better than his own. The confidence is not a demand for blind obedience; it is a claim that the command serves the interest of the person who obeys, and the claim invites examination.

**Why the Remembrance Is Named Rather Than the Prayer**

The verse calls the destination “the remembrance of Allah” rather than naming the sermon, the prayer, or the mosque, and the choice is deliberate. What is being protected is not an event on a calendar but the continuity of a community's consciousness of God. The next verse will instruct the believers to remember God often after they disperse, which shows that the remembrance is not confined to the assembly but is expected to continue in the market, the field, and the home. The Friday gathering exists to renew what the rest of the week consumes. That is why the Book attaches the strongest warnings to neglecting it and the most generous promises to observing it, and why the sūrah places the whole instruction immediately after its rebukes of communities that carried revelation without living it. The remembrance of God is the one burden that a person must not merely carry, and the verse commands the community to put everything down in order to pick it up. The day is arranged around the remembrance, and the rest of it flows from what the remembrance produces.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:10

> Once the prayer is over, disperse throughout the land and seek the bounty of Allah. And remember Allah often so you may be successful

**Expanded Commentary**

**Worship Ends and Work Begins Again**

The instruction opens with a temporal clause: once the prayer is over. The Book does not ask the community to remain in the mosque after the assembly, and it does not treat the merchant's return to his trade as a fall from a higher state. It tells the believers to disperse through the land, and the verb is the same one used for the scattering of seed and of people across a region. The community of Madinah was not a monastic settlement; it was a working town of farmers, traders, and craftsmen whose economic life had to continue. What the verse establishes is a rhythm rather than a retreat. One hour belongs to the assembly, and the rest of the day belongs to the pursuit of provision undertaken by people who have just been reminded who provides it. The Book states the same pattern in Sūrat al-Muzzammil, where it acknowledges that some believers travel the land seeking God's bounty while others fight in His cause (73:20), and it treats both activities as part of the life of the same community.

**Ordinary Work Described as Seeking God's Bounty**

The phrase the verse uses for trade is *faḍl Allāh*, the bounty of God, and the choice of words dignifies what might otherwise be considered a merely worldly pursuit. A merchant who sets out after the prayer is not described as serving his own appetite; he is described as seeking the provision that God has placed in the world for him to find. The Book applies the same language elsewhere to travel and migration undertaken for legitimate need: whoever emigrates in the cause of Allah will find safe havens and bountiful resources throughout the earth (4:100). This way of naming work does not turn commerce into worship by itself, and the verse does not pretend that every transaction is a religious act. It places commerce inside the frame of God's provision, so that the trader's daily activity is conducted in the presence of the One whose bounty he is asking for whether he says so or not. A believer who understands this carries his prayer into the market rather than leaving it at the door. The same frame covers the merchant's risk: the loss he fears and the profit he hopes for are both inside the arrangement the verse has just named as God's bounty.

**Remembering God After the Assembly**

The instruction that follows the dispersal is the one that keeps the two halves of the day connected: remember Allah often, so that you may be successful. The Book repeats this command in many forms, and in Sūrat al-Aḥzāb it stands as an independent obligation addressed to the believers: always remember Allah often (33:41). What the sūrah adds here is the timing. Remembrance is most under pressure in the hours of transaction, when the mind is occupied with prices and competition, and that is precisely why the command is given for the period after the assembly. The Friday gathering trains a person for the market, and the market tests whether the training took. Its placement is a safeguard against a religion of weekly performances: the assembly is the beginning of the week's working life, not a substitute for it. The closing clause supplies the motive: success. The word in Arabic carries the sense of reaching what one aimed for after difficulty, and the Book consistently uses it for the outcome of the whole life rather than for a good afternoon of sales.

**The Success the Verse Has in View**

The final word of the verse redefines the goal of the activity it has just commanded. The believers are told to seek bounty in the land and to remember God so that they may succeed, and the Book's use of the term throughout establishes what success means in its vocabulary. It is the state of the person whose account closes well, which is why the terms of the trade in the sūrah of the ranks ended with the same phrase: the ultimate triumph. A community that measured success by the ledger alone would find the Friday obligation an intrusion and the prayer an interruption of business. A community that measures it by the account that opens at death finds that the hour spent in the assembly and the remembrance that survives the afternoon are the most profitable part of the week. The verse does not disparage the work that follows the prayer. It orders the whole day around the achievement that no bankruptcy can touch.

**A Rhythm the Book States Elsewhere**

The same rhythm is stated in other places with the same emphasis on balance. “And seek the ˹reward of the˺ Hereafter through what Allah has granted you, but do not forget your share of this world.” (28:77) That command, addressed to a people who had been given wealth, keeps both hands on the table at once. And when the Book praises the community in Sūrat al-Nūr, it praises men engaged in buying and selling who are not distracted from God's remembrance by what they do (24:37). The two passages and the instruction here describe a single arrangement: the world is to be worked in, and the Hereafter is to be sought in it, and neither is served by pretending the other does not exist. Friday is the weekly proof of the arrangement. The community gathers, is reminded, is released, and is told to go and seek what God has put in the earth while keeping the One who put it there in mind.

## Sūrah al-Jumuʿah 62:11

> When they saw the fanfare along with the caravan, they ˹almost all˺ flocked to it, leaving you ˹O Prophet˺ standing ˹on the pulpit˺. Say, “What is with Allah is far better than amusement and merchandise. And Allah is the Best Provider.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Caravan That Emptied the Assembly**

The verse brings the sūrah to a close with a scene that the tradition preserves in detail. A caravan arrived in Madinah carrying provisions from Syria while the Prophet was delivering the Friday sermon, and the people rose and went out to it, leaving only twelve of them with him. The reports in al-Bukhārī record the incident from Jābir ibn ʿAbdillāh with the number of those who remained (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 936; see also 2064 and 4899), and the occasion reports describe drums announcing the arrival, which is what the verse means by fanfare. The Qur’anic text is unsparing about the scale: they almost all flocked to it. The revelation was not describing a hypothetical temptation to be weighed by later readers. It was recording a fact about the community that had received the finest teacher in human history, and it recorded the fact in the sūrah that carries that community's name for the day of gathering. The reports show that even the strongest of the community were not exempt from the temptation: the number who remained is told precisely because it was small, and those twelve were the remnant who made the rebuke intelligible to everyone else.

**“Leaving You Standing”**

The image at the centre of the verse is a man left alone in front of a pulpit. The Prophet was standing while he addressed the community, and the community walked out, and the Book states the detail without commentary because the detail is the commentary. The scene connects to an earlier sūrah in the Book's order, where the Prophet is told to remember Moses saying to his people, why do you hurt me when you already know I am Allah's messenger to you (61:5). The pattern of a messenger abandoned by his own audience is not new, and the sūrah of the ranks has already shown that such treatment is one of the standing trials of prophethood. What makes this instance sharper is the triviality of the cause. The people who left were not driven out by persecution or argument; they were drawn out by merchandise, and they left the word of God in mid-sentence.

**The Comparison the Verse Rejects**

The words placed on the Prophet's lips after the scene supply the correction: what is with Allah is far better than amusement and merchandise. The Book has spent the whole sūrah establishing the terms of that comparison. It has described a messenger reciting, purifying, and teaching; it has described the trading community whose hope is in an exchange that will never fail; it has described the Friday assembly as the point at which the week is reordered toward God. Against all of that, the arrival of a caravan is a small event whose benefit lasts as long as the goods, and the crowd's choice measured the two as equal. The correction is not that commerce is evil. It is that the people had mispriced a good, and the error was measured in a pulpit left standing. That is the same mispricing the sūrah's earlier passage exposed in the people who preferred a claim of privilege to the meeting they claimed to desire. The chapter has supplied the correct valuation throughout, from the trade that saves to the hour set apart, and this closing scene shows a community that had heard the whole argument and still ran.

**The Name at the End**

The verse closes with the name of God as the best of providers, *khayr al-rāziqīn*, and the choice of this name completes the argument that has run through the sūrah. The people ran after food because they feared scarcity and trusted what they could see, and the answer names the One who supplies all of it. Every provision in the caravan from Syria had been produced by His arrangement: the rain, the soil, the merchants who financed the journey, the roads, the security. The verse therefore does not simply tell the community that the afterlife is more valuable than a shipment of goods. It tells them that the shipment itself came from the One they walked away from, which means their rush was a movement away from the source of the thing they were rushing toward. The sūrah's last word leaves a community holding its ledgers and its prayers with a single criterion attached to both of them.

**A Warning Repeated in the Next Sūrah**

The Book repeats the correction in the sūrah that follows, and the repetition shows that the fault is not an incident but a tendency with which every trading community lives. “O believers! Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah. For whoever does so, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.” (63:9). The two passages identify the same mechanism — the goods and the household are real and legitimate, and they are capable of being used as an excuse for a smaller vision of life. The Book does not resolve the tension by forbidding the trade or the family. It resolves it by naming the outcome, and by placing the reminder where the community gathers to hear it every week. The caravan will come again; the assembly will come again; and the last verse of this sūrah leaves the choice where the first verse of the next sūrah assumes it will be found. The Book's final word on the day of gathering is therefore a comparison and a name, and it leaves a community the means to make the comparison for itself every time a caravan of its own appears.
