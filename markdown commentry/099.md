# Sūrah al-Zalzalah (Chapter 99) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Zalzalah 99:1

> When the earth is shaken ˹in˺ its ultimate quaking

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence That Does Not Finish Where It Starts**

The verse opens with *idhā*, when, and the sentence it begins is not completed until verse four. Three verses of conditions — when the earth is shaken, when it throws out its contents, when humanity cries out — accumulate before the main clause arrives. Arabic permits this suspension, and the Qur'an uses it to build pressure. The listener is held inside a series of events with no grammatical resolution, which mirrors the experience the verses describe: things happening, one after another, before anyone understands what they add up to. The construction is not a stylistic flourish. It is the shape of the event, and the reader encounters it before being told what it means.

**Zilzālahā: The Shaking That Belongs to the Earth**

The noun *zilzālah* is followed by the possessive suffix — her shaking — and the construction is a cognate accusative, a standard Arabic device for intensification. The earth is shaken with the shaking that is hers, the shaking proper to it, the maximum of which it is capable. The effect is to exclude comparison with any earthquake a person has witnessed. Ordinary seismic events are partial; this one is the earth's own. The Qur'an uses the same device elsewhere for emphasis, and its use here tells the reader that the verse is not describing a large earthquake but a different kind of event. The earth is not being struck from outside; it is doing what it was made capable of doing at the end.

**The Quaking of the Hour in the Qur'an's Other Accounts**

The event has parallels, and the parallels establish its place in the Qur'an's eschatology. O humanity, fear your Lord, for the violent quaking at the Hour is surely a dreadful thing (54:1); and the sūrah of al-Ḥāqqah describes the earth and mountains being lifted and crushed with a single blow (69:14). Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah describes the earth shaking violently and the mountains crumbling into dust (56:4–6). Al-Zalzalah's account is the most concentrated of these: one verb, one noun, and a suffix. The sūrah does not describe the destruction of the mountains or the splitting of the sky, which other passages do. It keeps to the ground, and the concentration gives the verse its force.

**The Ground Rather Than the Sky**

Most eschatological descriptions in the Qur'an begin with the heavens — the sky splitting, the stars scattering, the sun folding up. This sūrah begins beneath the listener's feet. The choice is deliberate and it changes the effect. A person can imagine the sky changing while remaining, in imagination, in the same place. He cannot imagine the ground under him doing this and remain anywhere. The sūrah is addressed to embodied people standing on soil, and it starts with the one thing they cannot step away from. The verse that follows continues in the same register: the earth does not merely shake, it expels what it has been holding.

**Past Tense for a Future Event**

The verbs in the sūrah's opening are in the perfect tense — the earth *was* shaken, *threw out* — although the event is future. Arabic uses the perfect for future events to indicate certainty, and the Qur'an uses the device repeatedly in its descriptions of the Day of Judgment. The effect is that the events are not predictions but reports of something already settled. A reader encountering the past tense is being told, in the grammar itself, that the outcome is not contingent. The device does the work that an assertion of certainty would otherwise have to do explicitly, and it does it in the first word of the sūrah.

**Why the Earth Is the Sūrah's Subject**

The sūrah takes its name from this verse, and the naming reflects its content. Of its eight verses, five concern the ground: the shaking, the expelling, the recounting, the inspiration to speak, the showing of deeds. The human being appears in two verses and the reckoning in two. This is an unusual distribution for a sūrah about the Day of Judgment, and it produces a specific emphasis. The stage of the final events is not a heavenly court but the ground people walked on, and the ground has been keeping a record. The sūrah's structure makes that claim before any verse states it.

**A Sūrah That Begins in the Middle of an Event**

There is no introduction, no oath, no address to the Prophet, and no statement of theme. The sūrah opens with a clause inside an unfolding sequence, as though the reader has arrived at a moment already in progress. Many short Makkan sūrahs begin with an oath or a command; this one begins with a subordinate clause. The effect is immediacy. The reader is not being prepared for a description of the end; he is being placed inside it, and the grammatical resolution he is waiting for arrives only after three events have been listed. By then the question in verse three has already been asked, and the reader is in the same position as the people described in it.

**The Verse and the Experience of an Earthquake**

The sūrah's first listeners lived in a region that knows seismic events, and the verse uses the one natural phenomenon nobody in Arabia could dismiss as legend. An earthquake is the specific event in which the most stable thing in human experience — the ground — becomes unstable, and it lasts long enough to be remembered. The verse takes that memory and extends it to its maximum: the earth's own shaking, the one it was made capable of. The rhetorical move is from the familiar to the absolute, and it requires no explanation to a person who has felt the floor move. This is why the sūrah opens here rather than with the sky or the trumpet. Every listener has a private reference for the event being described, and the verse supplies the scale by adding the suffix that makes the shaking the earth's own.

## Sūrah al-Zalzalah 99:2

> and when the earth throws out ˹all˺ its contents

**Expanded Commentary**

**Athqālahā: The Earth's Burdens**

*Athqāl* is the plural of *thiql*, a burden or heavy load, and the possessive suffix attaches the burdens to the earth: its burdens, the heavy things it has been carrying. The word choice implies that the earth has been holding something against its nature, or at least under weight, and that the shaking releases it. Commentators identify the burdens in two ways, and both are plausible on the wording: the dead buried in the ground, and the treasures hidden in it. The two are not rivals. The verse describes an expulsion of everything the earth contains, and the Qur'an's own account of the resurrection requires the first while its descriptions of the end require the second. The plural form allows both without deciding.

**The Dead and the Treasure**

The reading that identifies the burdens with the dead connects the verse to the general resurrection, which the Qur'an describes as the graves spilling out what is in them (82:4; 100:9). The reading that identifies them with treasure connects it to the collapse of worldly value at the end. Both readings are supported by the authenticated report about the event, which mentions treasures explicitly. The two together produce a picture: the ground gives back both the people and the wealth, and the wealth is then seen for what it was. The verse does not comment on the juxtaposition, but it arranges it.

**The Verified Report About the Earth's Contents**

The Prophet ﷺ described this event in detail. Abū Hurayrah reported that he said: the earth will vomit out long pieces of its liver like columns of gold and silver; the murderer will come and say, for this I committed murder; the breaker of family ties will come and say, for this I severed ties; and the thief will come and say, for this my hands were cut off. Then they will leave it and take nothing from it. The report is recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1013. Its detail is what the verse leaves out, and the detail is the point: the gold appears at the moment it is worthless, and the three men named are those who destroyed their lives for it. The report supplies the moral reading of the verse without the verse needing to state one.

**The Parallel in Sūrat al-Inshiqāq**

The same event is described in another short sūrah: and when the earth is stretched out, and ejects all its contents and becomes empty (84:3–4). The parallel adds one word that al-Zalzalah leaves implicit — *takhallat*, it becomes empty. The earth is not partially emptied; it is cleared. The two descriptions agree on the expulsion and differ only in emphasis, al-Zalzalah stressing the shaking that causes it and al-Inshiqāq stressing the emptiness that results. Read together they describe a complete reversal: the ground that received bodies and hid wealth gives up both and is left with nothing. The emptiness is the verse's unstated conclusion.

**An Earth That Empties Itself**

The verb *akhrajat* — it brought out, it threw out — is active, and the earth is its subject. The ground is not being emptied by an external agent in this verse; it expels. The construction matches the previous verse, where the earth is shaken with its own shaking. Al-Zalzalah consistently gives the earth an active role, and the next two verses continue the pattern: it recounts, and it does so because it was inspired. The sūrah's picture is of a creation that participates in the final events rather than merely undergoing them. The earth is not a stage; it is an agent, and its agency is described in verbs usually reserved for persons.

**What the Emptying Means for the Living**

The verse is addressed to people who will be standing on this ground when it happens, and the address is implicit in the detail. A person who has buried relatives, hidden savings, or built a house has a relationship with the earth's contents, and the verse announces the end of that relationship. Everything placed in the ground comes out. The Qur'an's descriptions of the end consistently reverse the arrangements people made in life, and this is one of the clearest: the storage places are opened. The verse does not moralise about it, but the report from Muslim supplies the moral without commentary — the murderer, the kin-breaker and the thief standing in front of what they ruined themselves for, and then walking away from it.

**What Was Buried, and What the Verse Does With It**

The verse touches something every human society does. Bodies are placed in the ground, wealth is hidden in it, waste is deposited in it, and structures are built on top of it. The earth is the storage place of a civilisation, and this verse announces that the storage has an end. The Qur'an's descriptions of the last day consistently reverse the arrangements people made, and the reversal here is total: nothing stays buried. The verse states this without emotion, which is what makes it effective. There is no rebuke of those who buried treasure or grief for those who buried relatives; there is only the fact of the expulsion. A reader who takes the verse literally will understand that the ground beneath him is temporary in both directions — it holds what is put in it, and it will return it.

**The Expulsion as the First Act of the Reckoning**

The order of the sūrah's first four verses is itself an account of procedure. The ground shakes, the ground gives up what it holds, the people react, the ground testifies. The expulsion comes before the testimony, which means the reckoning begins with the physical evidence being produced. Nothing is argued before the contents are out. The arrangement mirrors a legal process in which the exhibits are laid on the table before anyone speaks, and it explains why the human reaction is placed between the two acts of the earth. People are shown the evidence and only then does the ground begin to narrate. The verse is therefore not an isolated catastrophe; it is the second step in a sequence that ends in judgement.

## Sūrah al-Zalzalah 99:3

> and humanity cries, “What is wrong with it?”—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Only Human Speech in the Sūrah**

Eight verses, and this is the only one in which a person speaks. The earth shakes, the earth expels, the earth recounts, the earth is inspired — and then, in the third verse, humanity says four words. The placement is significant. Human speech arrives after two events it did not cause and cannot affect, and what it says is a question about what is happening rather than a statement about what to do. The sūrah gives human beings one line, and the line is bewilderment. Everything else in the chapter is action — divine, terrestrial, judicial — and the human contribution is a question that contains no information.

**Mā Lahā: A Question of Astonishment**

The Arabic is three words: *mā lahā*, what is the matter with it, what has happened to it. The construction expresses astonishment rather than inquiry. The speakers are not asking for an explanation they intend to act on; they are reacting to something beyond their categories. The same formula appears elsewhere in the Qur'an in moments of shock, and its force is that the speaker has run out of frameworks. A person who asks "what is the matter with it" has already established that nothing in his experience covers the case. The verse is therefore not reporting curiosity but collapse — the collapse of the assumption that the ground is stable, which is the assumption every human life is built on.

**Who Is Speaking**

Commentators differ on whether the question comes from all humanity or only from those who denied the resurrection. Both readings are textually available, since *al-insān* can be generic or specific. The argument for the restricted reading is that a believer knows the world ends; the astonishment would be out of place in someone who has been told repeatedly and has accepted it. The argument for the general reading is that knowing an event is coming and witnessing it are different experiences, and that the shaking described in verse one exceeds anything the believer was told about in sensory terms. The general reading is the stronger, because the verse says humanity without qualification, and the Qur'an does not usually restrict a term it leaves open.

**The Question as the Fulfilment of Denial**

There is an irony in the verse that the sūrah does not point out. The people asking what is wrong with the earth are, on the most common account of the sūrah's audience, the same people who denied that the earth would ever give back its dead. Their question is not "what have we done" but "what is wrong with it" — the event is still being attributed to the earth rather than to themselves. The verse records the last moment in which the denial can survive contact with the fact, and it records it without comment. The speakers are surprised by something they were told about repeatedly, and their surprise is the measure of how little they had listened.

**Why the Qur'an Records the Question Instead of Answering It**

The verse could have supplied the answer. It does not. The next verse explains what happens instead — the earth recounts — but it does not address the questioners or correct them. The omission is characteristic of the Qur'an's eschatological passages, which frequently record the speech of the damned without responding to it: the cry of woe in front of the record (18:49), the request to be sent back, the argument between the followers and the followed. In each case the speech is recorded and left unanswered, because the time for answers has passed. Al-Zalzalah's three words belong to that category. They are the last thing humanity says before the reckoning begins.

**The Contrast With the Believer's Response**

The Qur'an supplies the alternative response elsewhere, and the contrast is instructive. The believers are described as those whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of God (13:28), and the sūrah of al-Fajr ends with the addressed soul returning to its Lord, well pleased and well pleasing (89:28). A person who has lived in expectation of this day meets it differently from one who has not, and the difference is visible in what each says. Al-Zalzalah records only the cry of astonishment, but the sūrah's remaining verses describe what follows for both groups: the deeds shown, the atom's weight of good, the atom's weight of evil. The question in verse three is what the whole chapter is an answer to, and the answer is not addressed to the questioner.

**The Question and the Silence After It**

What follows the cry is not an answer. The next verse describes the earth recounting its news, which is information but not a response to the question asked. Nobody tells the speakers what is wrong with the earth. The silence is the sūrah's most economical device, and it does more than an explanation would. A person who asked "what is the matter with it" and received an explanation would still be in a conversation; a person who asks and is answered by the ground beginning to testify has moved out of conversation and into judgement. The verse marks that transition without narrating it. The last human words in the sūrah are a question addressed to nobody, and the reply comes from the earth rather than from God.

**Astonishment as the Last Refuge of Denial**

Astonishment is not knowledge, and the verse treats it as the final position available to someone who refused to know. A person who was told that the ground would shake and give back its dead, and who did not accept it, has no framework left when the event occurs. All he can say is that something is wrong with the earth. The framing is significant: even in the moment of fulfilment, the fault is placed outside himself. The Qur'an records this displacement consistently in its accounts of the last day, where people argue with their own limbs, blame their leaders, and ask to be returned. The three words here are the shortest form of the same move, and they are the last thing the sūrah lets humanity say.

## Sūrah al-Zalzalah 99:4

> on that Day the earth will recount everything

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tuḥaddithu Akhbārahā: Speech Attributed to the Ground**

The verb *tuḥaddith* is from *ḥaddatha*, to tell or narrate — the same verb used of human speech and of the telling of news. Its subject is the earth, and its object is *akhbārahā*, her news, her accounts, her chronicles. The construction is not metaphorical in the loose sense; the Qur'an attributes real speech to created things elsewhere and treats it as a fact of the last day. The verse says the earth will narrate, and the following verse supplies the mechanism: it will do so because it was inspired to. A reader who wants to reduce the statement to a figure of speech has to explain verse five as well, and verse five describes a specific divine act directed at the earth.

**The Verified Explanation of the Verse**

The Prophet ﷺ explained this verse directly. Abū Hurayrah reported that he recited *yawmaʾidhin tuḥaddithu akhbārahā* and said: do you know what its news is? They said, God and His Messenger know best. He said: its news is that it will bear witness against every male and female servant about what they did upon its surface, saying, he did such and such on such a day. These are its news. The report is in Sunan al-Tirmidhī 3353, and al-Tirmidhī graded it ṣaḥīḥ. The report settles the meaning of the verse in the clearest possible way: the news is testimony, the testimony concerns deeds, and it is given by the ground on which the deeds were done. The phrase *upon its surface* is the detail that makes the testimony specific to place.

**The Qur'an's Wider Account of Witnessing**

The testimony of the earth belongs to a larger arrangement. On the last day the Qur'an describes ears, eyes and skin testifying against what people used to do, and when they ask their skin why it testified, the answer is: We have been made to speak by Allah, Who causes all things to speak (41:20–21). The structure is identical to al-Zalzalah's: created things speak, and they speak because they were made to. The verse in Sūrat Fuṣṣilat supplies the general principle that al-Zalzalah applies to the ground — God causes all things to speak, and on that day the capacity is extended to what has no tongue. The earth's testimony is therefore not an isolated marvel but one instance of a comprehensive disclosure.

**Akhbār: News, Reports, Chronicles**

The plural *akhbār* carries a range of senses — news, accounts, reports, chronicles — and the choice of a plural is meaningful. The earth does not give one statement but many, because what it holds is many events committed by many people at many times. The word is used in ordinary Arabic for the reports a traveller brings back and for the historical accounts of a people. Applied to the ground, it produces the idea of a place that has been keeping a record. The Qur'an uses the same vocabulary for the written record of deeds elsewhere, and the parallel suggests that the earth's news and the book of deeds are two forms of the same disclosure — one in a document, one in a voice.

**Why the Earth and Not Only the Book**

The Qur'an already provides for a written account of every deed: the book will be laid open and the wicked will find everything they did present (18:49). The addition of the earth's testimony is not redundant. A document can be disputed; a place cannot. The earth's testimony has a specificity no record has — it was there, it felt the weight, it received what was done on it. The Qur'an's inclusion of a spatial witness alongside the documentary one closes a gap that a sceptic might exploit. The deeds were not merely recorded; they occurred somewhere, and the somewhere will speak. The verse is therefore about the completeness of the disclosure rather than about its novelty.

**The Testimony of Place**

The idea that a place bears witness has an application beyond the last day. A mosque, a road walked to prayer, a house in which the Qur'an was read — the Qur'an and the prophetic reports treat places as retaining what was done in them, and the concept underlies the Islamic practice of valuing locations associated with worship. Al-Zalzalah states the principle in its most literal form: the ground will narrate. A reader who takes the verse seriously will understand that no deed is location-less, and that the location is not a neutral container. What was done on the surface of the earth is held by it, and on the day described in this sūrah, it is released as speech.

**Ordinary Ground, and What It Has Been Holding**

The verse assigns a function to the most ordinary surface there is. A person's daily life is conducted on soil, asphalt, floorboards and stone, and none of it appears to retain anything. The verse says otherwise, and it says so in the vocabulary of narration — the earth will tell, will recount, will give its news. The claim is not that the ground keeps a physical trace, which would be a statement about matter, but that it will be given speech about what occurred on it. The distinction matters for how the verse is read. It is not a naturalistic claim about memory in matter; it is a statement about a disclosure that God will arrange. The ordinary character of the surface is what gives the verse its force, since nothing a person does happens anywhere else.

**Testimony From Below Rather Than From Above**

Witnesses in the Qur'an's eschatology are usually described as descending or standing above — angels recording, prophets testifying against their communities, a witness raised from every faith-community (16:84). Al-Zalzalah adds a witness from underneath, and the addition is what makes the testimony inescapable. A person can turn away from a prophet, dispute a record, or forget an angel. He cannot leave the ground he stood on. The verse closes the last direction in which a deed could be said to have gone unwitnessed. The earth is the one witness that was present at every act performed on it, and the sūrah is the only place in the Qur'an where that presence is given a voice.

## Sūrah al-Zalzalah 99:5

> having been inspired by your Lord ˹to do so˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Awḥā Lahā: Inspiration Addressed to a Non-Human**

The verse explains the previous one in three words. The earth recounts *because* God inspired it — *bi-anna rabbaka awḥā lahā*. The verb *awḥā* is the verb of revelation, the same one used of God's communication to prophets, and here its object is the ground. The preposition *lahā*, to it or for it, is the construction used when revelation is directed at someone. The verse is therefore making a precise claim: the earth's speech is not a natural consequence of the shaking but a commissioned act. Nothing in the earth's constitution produces testimony; the testimony is assigned to it. The verse closes the question of mechanism by naming the source.

**The Range of Waḥy in the Qur'an**

The Qur'an uses *waḥy* for communication well beyond prophecy, and the range establishes this verse's place in a pattern. Your Lord inspired the bees: make your homes in the mountains, the trees, and in what people construct (16:68). We inspired the mother of Moses: nurse him, and when you fear for him, put him into the river (28:7). God inspired the disciples to believe in Him and His messenger (5:111). The same verb is used of the heavens in the account of creation, where God assigned to each heaven its mandate (41:12). Al-Zalzalah's use of the verb for the earth belongs to this established range and extends it to testimony.

**Why the Verse Is Needed**

Without verse five, verse four could be read as poetry. The earth speaks; a reader might take that as an image for the disclosure of hidden deeds, without any claim that the ground literally narrates. The fifth verse removes that option by specifying a divine act directed at the earth, using the technical vocabulary of revelation. The two verses together form a complete statement: the earth will testify, and it will testify because it was instructed to. The structure is characteristic of the Qur'an's method in describing the unseen — a striking claim followed immediately by its ground. The sūrah does not leave the striking claim standing on its own rhetorical force.

**The Shift to Your Lord**

The sūrah has been impersonal until this point. The earth, humanity, the deeds — all described in the third person. Here the phrase is *rabbuka*, your Lord, in the second person singular, and the addressee is the Prophet ﷺ and, through him, whoever hears the verse. The shift is small and its effect is large. The event described is not a general catastrophe happening to a general humanity; it is an event arranged by the Lord of the person being addressed. The possessive attaches the reader to the one who will inspire the earth to speak. The sūrah's two remaining verses then address humanity in general again, but the connection has been made.

**Inspiration as Command Rather Than Consciousness**

Commentators discuss whether the earth's inspiration implies that it becomes a conscious, knowing being, and the discussion is worth stating carefully. The Qur'an does not say the earth acquires understanding. It says the earth was inspired to speak, and the parallel cases — the bee, the heavens — involve direction without any claim of reasoning. The most economical reading is that the earth is given the capacity to discharge a specific function at a specific time, in the way the bee is given the capacity to build. The testimony is real and it is specific, and it requires no theory about the interior life of soil. The verse describes a commissioned act, not an awakening.

**The Earth as an Obedient Servant**

The verse places the earth in a category the Qur'an applies widely: everything created responds to God's direction, and only human beings and jinn have the option not to. The heavens and the earth were told to submit, willingly or unwillingly, and both responded, we submit willingly (41:11). The sun and the moon, the stars and the mountains, the trees and all that prostrates — the Qur'an describes a creation in continuous compliance (55:6). The earth's testimony in al-Zalzalah is an instance of that compliance at the moment of maximum consequence. Human beings are the only creatures in the sūrah who will be surprised by what happens, and the surprise recorded in verse three is the mark of the difference.

**Why the Mechanism Is Given in the Vocabulary of Revelation**

The verse uses the verb of revelation to describe what happens to the earth, and the choice is not incidental. *Waḥy* in the Qur'an is how God communicates intention to a creature — to a prophet, to a mother, to a bee, to the heavens. Applying it to the ground places the earth's testimony inside the same category as prophetic speech: something said because God intended it to be said. The verse therefore raises the earth's testimony above the level of a natural phenomenon. The shaking in verse one could be understood as an event; the speech in verse four cannot, and verse five is what makes that clear. A creation that can be addressed is a creation with a role, and the role assigned here is that of a witness.

**Inspiration, Command, and the Absence of Choice**

The earth was inspired, and it testifies. Nothing in the verse allows for the possibility of refusal, and the absence is the point. The Qur'an distinguishes creatures who receive direction and can decline it from creatures who receive direction and cannot, and the earth belongs to the second category along with the bee and the heavens. Human beings belong to the first, and the sūrah's two final verses describe what happens to them as a result. The contrast is quietly devastating: the ground discharges its assigned function perfectly, while the people who walked on it will be shown what they did with theirs. The verse is a statement about obedience before it is a statement about eschatology.

## Sūrah al-Zalzalah 99:6

> On that Day people will proceed in separate groups to be shown ˹the consequences of˺ their deeds

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yuṣdarūna: Issuing Forth**

The verb is from the root *ṣ-d-r*, which denotes issuing or coming forth from a place, and it is the same root used for the return of a traveller or of a flock from water. The Qur'an uses it here in the passive: people will be issued forth, brought out. The passive matters, because it removes the initiative. The people described in verse three were standing bewildered on shaking ground; here they are being moved. The verse is the transition in the sūrah from catastrophe to judgement. The earth has shaken, emptied and spoken; now the people are brought forward. Nothing in the verse suggests that anyone walks to this proceeding voluntarily.

**Ashtātan: Separate, Scattered, Divergent**

The word *ashtātā* is a plural of *shatt*, which carries separation and dispersion. Commentators read it in two related ways: people emerging in scattered groups, and people emerging with divergent destinations. The two readings describe the same event from different angles. A crowd that is heading to two different places is, in the act of moving, scattered; and a crowd that is scattered by its destinations is separated by something more fundamental than arrangement. The word is the sūrah's compressed statement of the division that the whole Qur'an's eschatology is built on. No one in this procession is going where the person beside him is going, and the divergence is determined by what the next two verses describe.

**Groups and Yet Alone**

The Qur'an describes people rising in groups elsewhere — every community will be seen on its knees, and every community will be summoned to its record of deeds (45:28), with a witness raised from every faith-community (16:84). Against that, it also states the individual character of the day: now you have come to Us alone, just as We created you the first time, and you have left behind what We gave you (6:94). The two descriptions are not in conflict. People are gathered in groups because they shared a life and a community, and each stands alone because the account is his own. Al-Zalzalah's verse names the groups and the two verses after it name the individual reckoning, in that order.

**To Be Shown Their Deeds**

The purpose clause at the end of the verse is *li-yuraw aʿmālahum* — that they may be shown their deeds. The showing is the reason for the procession. People are not brought out to be told something new; they are brought out to be shown what they already did. The Qur'an uses the same idea in its description of the record: they will find whatever they did present before them (18:49). The verb is one of seeing rather than hearing, and the shift is significant. A report can be argued with; a thing shown cannot. The sūrah's next two verses then describe what is shown, down to the smallest unit of measurement available to its first listeners.

**The Parallel of the Scattered Moths**

Sūrat al-Qāriʿah describes the same day with a different image: on that Day people will be like scattered moths (101:4). The comparison emphasises helplessness and confusion — moths have no direction and no organisation, and they move toward a light that destroys them. Al-Zalzalah's *ashtātā* is cooler and more judicial: the scattering is not chaos but separation by destination. The two images complement each other. The moths describe how the day feels to the people in it; the separate groups describe what it actually is. A reader who holds both has the Qur'an's full picture — overwhelming in experience, precise in structure.

**Why This Verse Comes Before the Two Measures**

The verse is placed between the earth's testimony and the statement about the atom's weight, and the placement is the argument. First the disclosure: the ground speaks. Then the procession: people are brought forward to be shown. Then the measure: an atom's weight of good, an atom's weight of evil. The order reproduces the procedure of a trial — evidence, appearance of the parties, presentation of the record. The sūrah does not describe a judge, a court or a sentencing. It describes a sequence, and the sequence is intelligible to anyone who has ever waited to be called. The final two verses are the reading of the record, and they occupy the last two lines of the sūrah because they are what everything before them was for.

**The Procession and the End of Collective Excuse**

The verse describes people moving in groups, and the grouping is the last form of protection available to a person. In life, belonging to a community, a family or a party provides cover: responsibility is shared, blame is distributed, and an individual is rarely assessed alone. The procession described here retains the groups while removing the cover. People are issued forth together and shown their deeds individually, and the two verses that follow use the singular throughout — whoever does, he will see. The Qur'an states the same principle explicitly: you have come to Us alone, as We created you the first time (6:94). The verse is therefore the point at which the collective ends and the account begins, and it is placed immediately before the two verses that measure the account.

**Being Shown Rather Than Being Judged**

The purpose clause says people will be shown their deeds, not that they will be judged, and the difference is worth marking. Judgement implies a decision that might have gone another way; showing implies a presentation of what already happened. The Qur'an's descriptions of the last day repeatedly use the vocabulary of sight for this moment, and the sūrah's final two verses continue it — he will see it, he will see it. The verdict is not produced on that day so much as disclosed. This is why the sūrah can move from the procession to the atom's weight without a courtroom scene. The trial, in the Qur'an's telling, consisted of the life itself, and the day described here is when the evidence becomes visible to everyone at once.

## Sūrah al-Zalzalah 99:7

> So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mithqāl Dharrah: The Smallest Weight There Was**

*Dharrah* denotes the smallest thing its audience could identify — an ant, in some readings, or the mote of dust visible in a shaft of light — and *mithqāl* is the weight of a thing. The phrase therefore means the weight of the smallest measurable unit. The Qur'an uses it as its standard for the minimum, and it uses it consistently: God does not wrong anyone even by an atom's weight (4:40); not an atom's weight escapes Him in the heavens or on earth (10:61). The choice of unit is deliberate. A larger minimum would leave a range of small deeds unaccounted for, and the sūrah's purpose is to close that range. The verse is saying that nothing is too small to be recorded, and it says so in the units its first listeners used to weigh gold.

**The Atom in the Qur'an's Own Usage**

The same measure appears in a verse that pairs it with multiplication. God never wrongs anyone, even by an atom's weight; and if it is a good deed, He multiplies it many times over and gives a great reward out of His grace (4:40). The pairing is important for reading al-Zalzalah. The minimum is stated in one place and the multiplication in another, and together they describe an accounting in which the smallest good deed is both recorded and increased. Sūrat Yūnus makes the same point about the comprehensiveness of the record: not the weight of an atom in the heavens or the earth escapes your Lord, nor is there anything smaller or larger, except that it is in a clear record (10:61). Al-Zalzalah's verse is the application of that principle to the individual's own deeds.

**Seeing Rather Than Being Told**

The verse says he will see it, not that he will be informed of it. The verb of sight is the Qur'an's preferred verb for the disclosure of the record: they will find whatever they did present (18:49); and each soul will know what it has sent forth or left behind (82:5). Seeing is unarguable in a way that hearing is not. A person told of a deed can dispute the report, the context, the intention; a person shown the deed has nothing to dispute. The sūrah's use of the verb in both verse six and verse seven establishes the character of the disclosure: it will be visual, immediate, and impossible to reinterpret.

**The Small Deed, and the Prophetic Encouragement**

The verse has a practical application that the Prophet ﷺ drew out. He urged his Companions to protect themselves from the Fire even by giving half a date-fruit in charity. The report is from ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim and stands in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1417, where al-Bukhārī made the phrase a chapter heading, and again in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1016, whose wording adds the alternative: and if you cannot find that, then with a good word. The report is the practical counterpart to this verse. A half date is a trivial amount, and the verse explains why it is not trivial: it will be seen. The two together form the basis of the Islamic teaching that small consistent acts carry real weight, which is the opposite of the assumption most people operate under.

**The Multiplication of Good**

One further feature of the Qur'an's accounting should be noted, since it modifies how this verse is read in isolation. Good deeds are multiplied; the Qur'an gives the parable of a grain that produces seven ears, each with a hundred grains (2:261), and states elsewhere that God multiplies for whom He wills. Evil deeds are not multiplied in the same way; they are recorded as they are. Al-Zalzalah's two final verses state the recording of both in symmetrical terms, which is a statement about the minimum rather than about the total. The person who does an atom's weight of good will see it, and what he sees will not be reduced by anything — the accounting in the Qur'an runs in one direction only.

**Why the Verse Uses the Language of Weight**

The imagery of weighing belongs to the Qur'an's description of the last day: We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least, and even if a deed is the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it forth (21:47). Al-Zalzalah uses a smaller unit than the mustard seed and omits the scales entirely, which makes its statement more severe in one respect and simpler in another. Nothing is described except the deed and its visibility. The verse is not about the mechanics of judgement; it is about the impossibility of a deed being lost. A person who has done a small good and forgotten it will not have lost it, and the verse exists to tell him so.

**Encouragement Addressed to People With Little to Give**

The verse has a social application that the Prophet ﷺ drew out in practice. Most people cannot perform large acts of charity; most days contain no dramatic virtue. A teaching that recognised only large deeds would address a minority and leave everyone else outside it. The atom's weight does the opposite: it makes the smallest possible good deed count, and it does so at the level of the record rather than as a consolation. The effect on the earliest community was practical. Charity in Islam was never restricted to the wealthy, and the half date in the prophetic report is the measure of that inclusiveness. The verse is the theological ground for a practice in which a poor person's small gift carries the same standing in the record as a rich person's large one.

## Sūrah al-Zalzalah 99:8

> And whoever does an atom’s weight of evil will see it

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Symmetry of the Two Verses**

The final verse repeats the previous one exactly, changing three elements: good becomes evil, and the conjunction shifts from *fa* to *wa*. Nothing else differs. The repetition is the sūrah's conclusion and its method at once — the same measure applied in both directions, with no adjustment for the difference between the two categories. A reader who expects the accounting to be asymmetric, with good deeds counted finely and evil deeds generously, is told otherwise in the starkest available form. The symmetry is not a claim that good and evil are equivalent in weight; the Qur'an states the opposite about multiplication. It is a claim that both are recorded at the same resolution. Nothing falls below the threshold of the record on either side.

**The Report About the Two Men**

Early exegetes connected these two verses to a specific report from Muqātil ibn Sulaymān about the occasion of revelation. Two men were described: one thought it beneath him to give a beggar a date, a crust or a walnut, reasoning that God only rewards what a person actually values; the other belittled small sins — a lie, backbiting, a forbidden glance — reasoning that only major sins carry a threat. The verses were revealed to correct both. [UNVERIFIED: this report is transmitted in the tafsīr literature and was not independently verified in this session.] Whether or not the report is exact, the correction it describes is the plain sense of the text.

The verses also carry a verified prophetic statement of their own. Asked about donkeys, the Prophet ﷺ replied that nothing had been revealed to him concerning them except this comprehensive verse which covers everything — whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it (99:7–8). The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4962, in the Book of Tafsīr under the chapter heading for 99:7. He called the two verses *jāmiʿah*, comprehensive, and the description is exact: they cover every act a person performs, down to the treatment of an animal, by naming the smallest possible unit in both directions. The two errors are common, they are opposite, and the verses answer both with the same unit of measure.

**Scales, Books, and the Absence of Both**

The Qur'an describes the disclosure of evil deeds in two complementary ways. There is the book: the record will be laid open and the wicked will cry that it does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted (18:49). There are the scales: We will set up the scales of justice, so no soul will be wronged in the least, and even if a deed is the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it forth (21:47). Al-Zalzalah uses neither image. It says only that the deed will be seen. The omission is significant, because it removes any intermediary between the person and the act. There is no book to interpret, no scale to dispute, no procedure to navigate. The verse describes a direct encounter with what a person did.

**What Seeing the Evil Will Mean**

The verse does not describe the reaction, and the Qur'an describes it elsewhere. The wicked will cry woe before the record (18:49); and every soul will wish that its misdeeds were far off (3:30). The seeing is therefore not neutral observation. It is the confrontation of a person with acts he has already lived through, presented without the context that made them seem reasonable at the time. This is the severity of the verse, and it is not located in the punishment but in the sight. A person can endure a penalty more easily than he can endure an accurate view of himself.

**Every Excuse Closed in Eight Verses**

Both final verses remove a category of argument. No one will be able to say that a deed was too small to matter, since the measure is the smallest weight there is. No one will be able to say that a deed was forgotten, since the earth has been keeping its own account and the record leaves nothing out. No one will be able to say that a deed was private, since the testimony comes from the ground it was done on. The sūrah closes every avenue of defence in eight verses, and it closes them without argument — by describing a procedure in which the defences are simply unavailable. The finality is what gives the chapter its weight, and it is achieved by description rather than by warning.

**The Sūrah Ends Where It Began: On the Ground**

Al-Zalzalah opens with the earth shaking and closes with a person seeing an atom's weight of his own conduct. The distance between those two statements is the whole of a human life, and the sūrah's structure insists that they are connected. The ground a person walks on is the same ground that will shake, empty itself, and testify. The deeds he does are done on its surface, and it is holding them. Nothing in the chapter is abstract; everything is located in a place a person can point to. The final verse leaves the reader standing exactly where he was when he began reading it, with one addition to his knowledge: that this ground will speak, and that what it will say includes the smallest thing he ever did.

**What the Final Verse Leaves the Reader Doing**

The sūrah ends on evil rather than on good, and the order is deliberate. A chapter that closed with the promise of the atom's weight of good would leave the reader comforted; closing with the atom's weight of evil leaves him examining. There is no summary, no exhortation and no closing formula. The last thing the sūrah says is that the smallest wrong will be seen, and the last thing it implies is that the reader is holding such deeds at this moment. The effect is not despair, since the same verse applied to good is a promise, but attention. A person who finishes this sūrah has been given a unit of measurement and told that the record is kept at that resolution. What he does with the information is left, as it is throughout the Qur'an, to him. The measurement is exact, the record is complete, and the ground beneath the reader is the witness that will deliver it. No part of that description is softened in the final verse, and none of it needs to be: the smallest deed will simply be seen.
