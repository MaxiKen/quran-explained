# Sūrah Ghāfir (Chapter 40) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Forgiver of Sins and the Believer in the House of Pharaoh**

Ghāfir, "The Forgiver"—equally renowned across classical interpretive and hadith literature as *Al-Muʾmin* (The Believer)—is the fortieth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 85 verses of late Makkan revelation. It holds a sacred, historic station as the majestic portal to the celebrated *Ḥā-Mīm* Septet: a contiguous sequence of seven sublime chapters (Sūrahs 40 through 46) that open with the mystical disconnected letters *Ḥā-Mīm*. In prophetic tradition, these seven chapters are revered as the *Ḥawāmīm*—designated by ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd as *“the crowns of the Qur'an”* and by Ibn ʿAbbās as *“the aromatic silk gardens of scripture.”* It takes its primary name, *Ghāfir*, from verse 3, which presents a breathtaking cascade of divine attributes: *“The Forgiver of sin, the Accepter of repentance, the Severe in punishment, the Owner of abundance; there is no deity except Him; to Him is the final destination.”* Its alternative title, *Al-Muʾmin*, honors the heroic, anonymous Egyptian prince in verses 28–45: a secret believer from the royal household of Pharaoh who stepped forward during a royal council plotting the assassination of Moses, risking his life to deliver an impassioned, brilliant defense of faith and conscience.

**The Structural Architecture and the Secret Prince**

The architecture of Sūrah Ghāfir is organized around four magnificent movements. The opening movement (1–22) establishes the Book revealed from the Almighty, the All-Knowing, warns against the intellectual vanity of those who dispute divine signs without authority. Unveils the awe-inspiring cosmic scene of the Throne: the supreme archangels carrying the Throne of God and those encircling it, glorifying their Lord with praise and continually weeping in intercessory supplication for the fallible believers on earth: *“Our Lord, You have encompassed all things in mercy and knowledge, so forgive those who have repented and followed Your way and protect them from the punishment of Hellfire”* (40:7). The second and dramatic movement (23–46) stages the confrontation between Moses, Pharaoh, Hāmān, and the *Believer of Pharaoh's House* (*Mu'min Āli Firʿawn*). The secret believer intervenes with masterly rhetoric: *“Would you kill a man merely because he says, 'My Lord is Allah,' while he has brought you clear proofs from your Lord?”* (40:28). He warns his court of the *Day of Mutual Calling* (*Yawm al-Tanād*), recalls how Joseph brought clear proofs to Egypt generations before, and exposes the absurdity of Pharaoh ordering Hāmān to build a lofty brick tower to reach the clouds and spy on the God of Moses. As the court turns against him, the noble believer declares his immortal reliance: *“And I entrust my affair to Allah. Indeed, Allah is Seeing of the servants”* (40:44), whereupon God shields him from their evil plots and drowns Pharaoh's regime, exposing them to the Fire morning and evening in their graves. The third movement (47–68) portrays the pathetic mutual recriminations of the damned in Hell, begging the guards of the Fire to ask their Lord for a single day's reduction of torment, only to be answered: *“Did there not come to you your messengers with clear proofs?”* It affirms the eternal divine decree of verse 51: *“Indeed, We will support Our messengers and those who believe during the life of this world and on the Day when the witnesses will stand.”* The closing movement (69–85) contrasts the immense creation of the heavens and earth against the fragile conceit of man, commands the Prophet to be patient for the promise of God is true. Delivers the famous invitation of verse 60: *“Call upon Me; I will answer you!”*, concluding with the catastrophic realization of ancient deniers whose deathbed faith availed them nothing when the overwhelming might of God descended.

## Verse 40:1

> Ḥâ-Mĩm

**The Threshold of the Seven Sūrahs**

Ghāfir stands at the threshold of a distinct and unified sequence within the Qur'anic corpus: the seven consecutive chapters, spanning sūrahs 40 through 46, that each begin with the paired letters *ḥāʾ* and *mīm*. Classical scholarship refers to this magnificent septet as the *Ḥawāmīm*. Transmitted reports from the Companions highlight the deep reverence accorded to this sequence by the early community. ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd remarked that the *Ḥawāmīm* are the fine silk and ornament of the Qur'an (*dībājat al-Qurʾān*), a notable assessment preserved by al-Bayhaqī in *Shuʿab al-Īmān*. Revealing themselves during the middle Makkan period when the confrontation between the fledgling Muslim community and the pagan aristocracy of Quraysh was reaching intense hostility, these chapters share a striking harmony of tone, cadence, and thematic architecture. Every single sūrah in this sequence opens with these two mysterious phonemes and immediately directs the listener to the majesty, descent, and divine authority of sacred scripture. They were sent down as an intellectual and spiritual fortress for the believers when societal pressure threatened to overwhelm their resolve.

**Keys to the Divine Treasuries and the Unity of Names**

Beyond the linguistic challenge, classical commentaries preserve significant traditions about the symbolic and religious depth of *Ḥā-Mīm*. Several early scholars linked the letters to the divine declaration: *“To Him belong the keys of the heavens and the earth”* (39:63). Under this interpretation, the formula represents the supreme keys that unlock the boundless treasuries of divine power, mercy, and knowledge. Another tradition, transmitted on the authority of ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās, posits an underlying structural connection across various sūrahs: the sequences *Alif-Lām-Rā*, *Ḥā-Mīm*, and *Nūn* can be understood as distributed components that together form the Divine Name *al-Raḥmān* ("the Most Compassionate"). This insight invites the contemplative reader to perceive the entire Qur'an as an unified organic unity where individual chapters speak to one another across textual boundaries.

## Verse 40:2

> The revelation of this Book is from Allah—the Almighty, All-Knowing

**The Pedigree and Descent of the Text**

The opening declaration immediately identifies the origin, pedigree, and transcendent source of the scripture: *tanzīl al-Kitāb min Allāh*—the revelation of this Book is from Allah. Classical Arabic grammarians, including al-Zajjāj and al-Farrāʾ, analyzed the syntactic possibilities of this phrase. One parsing treats *tanzīl* as the predicate (*khabar*) to an implicit demonstrative pronoun: "This is the revelation of the Book." An equally authoritative parsing links this verse directly to the preceding letters, reading: "*Ḥā-Mīm* is the revelation of the Book." In either construction, the real verbal noun *tanzīl* carries profound religious weight. In Qur'anic usage, *tanzīl* means a deliberate, progressive sending down over time, contrasting with *inzāl*, which often refers to sending down all at once. For twenty-three years, the text was imparted in measured stages, directly addressing human emergencies, moral dilemmas, and historical conflicts. By identifying God as the sole originator of this descent, the verse immediately dismisses pagan allegations that the discourse was an ecstatic poetic utterance or borrowed human folklore. The scripture is firmly situated as an uncreated message sent into time to reshape the destinies of men and nations.

**The Shift from Wisdom to Omniscience**

The closing clause anchors the revelation in two complementary divine attributes: *al-ʿAzīz* (the Almighty) and *al-ʿAlīm* (the All-Knowing). A comparative study of the Qur'anic openings illuminates the precision of this phrasing. In Sūrah al-Zumar (39:1), which directly precedes this chapter in the muṣḥaf, the Book is described as descending from *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm*—the Almighty, the All-Wise. In Ghāfir, however, the second name shifts deliberately to *al-ʿAlīm*. Exegetes such as Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī pointed out that this variation aligns perfectly with the sūrah's dominant themes. Ghāfir addresses the psychological subterfuge of those who dispute divine signs, the covert plots of ancient tyrants, and the secret faith maintained by believers under persecution.

## Verse 40:3

> the Forgiver of sin and Accepter of repentance, the Severe in punishment, and Infinite in bounty. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. To Him ˹alone˺ is the final return

**Four Names in One Verse**
The verse lines up four of God's attributes in a row: *ghāfir al-dhanb, qābil al-tawb, shadīd al-ʿiqāb, dhī al-ṭawl* — the Forgiver of sin, the Accepter of repentance, the Severe in punishment, the Lord of abundance. Al-Rāzī noticed the arrangement and thought it worth a comment of its own. The severity sits in the middle, and it is covered on both sides: mercy stands before it in two forms — the forgiving of what has passed and the accepting of those who turn — and generosity stands after it in the abundance that never runs dry. The order is not decoration. A reader meets the sternness of God inside a frame of mercy, which is where the Book always places it.
**What the Arrangement Is Saying**
The Prophet gave the same picture in an image: God wrote above His throne, *My mercy prevails over My wrath*, in the report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7453. Severity in this verse is not the description of God's mood; it is the boundary that preserves the moral order, the thing that stops the tyrant, protects the oppressed, and gives the warnings of the sūrah their force. The verse then closes the way the sūrah's opening verses do: *there is no god but He; to Him is the final return*. Every name listed belongs to the One to whom the whole account will be handed back, and the reader who has understood the mercy around the severity knows what he is turning toward when he repents.

## Verse 40:4

> None disputes the signs of Allah except the disbelievers, so do not be deceived by their prosperity throughout the land

**What the Argueing Is**
*Mā yujādilu fī āyātillāhi illā alladhīna kafarū* — none disputes the signs of God except those who disbelieve. The verb *yujādilu* is built from a root that describes twisting strands together, and the picture is argument used as a tool for tangling rather than for finding anything out. The sūrah keeps its eye on this habit and names it four times — here, and at verses 35, 56 and 69 — which is a sign of how much of the Makkan opposition consisted of debate rather than refusal to hear: the same charge returns at 40:35, 40:56 and 40:69.
**What the Verse Is Not Saying**
The scholars drew the line carefully. The verse does not censure a man who asks an honest question, or a man who cannot understand and says so, or a Muslim who wants a matter explained. Al-Zamakhsharī and al-Rāzī both note the difference between asking in order to learn and arguing in order to escape: the first is the road into the Book, the second is a way of standing outside it while pretending to be at the door. What marks the second is that the man is not looking for an answer; he is looking for a way to keep his position, and the argument is the instrument. The verse names the practice and leaves the reader to recognize which of the two he is doing; the Book separates them explicitly elsewhere, where the Prophet is told to argue with the People of the Book only in the best manner (16:125).
**What Comes After the Sentence**
The verse does not stop at the diagnosis. It tells the Prophet what to do with it: *let it not grieve you that they turn about in the lands* — the merchants of Quraysh with their caravans, the men who mocked him, travelling and prospering and sure of themselves. The instruction is not to ignore them but to stop weighing the outcome by their ease, and the next verse gives the reason: the people of Noah and the nations after them were also secure once, and the account closed on them. The Book says the same to the Prophet in a pair of consoling lines - do not let their words grieve you (36:76), do not let their going about in the land deceive you (3:196) - and it gives the same right of argument to the nations that disputed before (22:68). The verse is a repositioning of the eye rather than a promise of comfort.

## Verse 40:5

> Before them, the people of Noah denied ˹the truth˺, as did ˹other˺ enemy forces afterwards. Every community plotted against its prophet to seize him, and argued in falsehood, ˹hoping˺ to discredit the truth with it. So I seized them. And how ˹horrible˺ was My punishment

**The People of Noah Came First**
*Kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥ* — before them, the people of Noah denied. The sūrah begins its history with the oldest story in the record. Noah called his people for nine hundred and fifty years, as the sūrah of the Spider reports (29:14), and what he received was mockery, contempt, and a boycott led by the men of standing. The verse is placed here to steady the Prophet in Makkah. The resistance he was meeting was not a sign that he had failed, nor was it new; it was the same reception the first great caller had received, and the outcome of Noah's story is known.
**Every Community Plotted Against Its Messenger**
The verse names what the opposition actually wanted: *wa-hammat kullu ummatin bi-rasūlihim li-yaʾkhudhūh* — every community schemed against its messenger to seize him. *Hamma* is more than an impulse: it is a decision carried with intent. And *li-yaʾkhudhūh* covers the whole range that the word can cover — to take hold of him, to jail him, to silence him, to kill him. Words failed them, so they went for the speaker, which is what the powerful always do when an argument they cannot answer starts to spread. In Makkah the same council met in the same way to decide between imprisonment, exile and murder (8:30). The sūrah tells the Prophet that the room he was standing in had been used before, by older and greater nations, and that the men in it had also failed.

**The Falsehood They Argued With**
The verse describes the method of the opposition as well as its aim: they argued with falsehood in order to cancel the truth. The Book reports the same technique across the sūrahs — the demand for a different Qur'an or a change to this one (10:15), the instruction of the leaders to make noise while the Qur'an was recited so that no one would hear it (41:26), the argument of those who dispute about God without knowledge or guidance or an enlightening book (22:8). The answer the Book gives to each of them is the same: falsehood has no standing of its own, and the truth is what breaks it (21:18; 34:49). The verse's place in the sūrah is a comfort to the man facing the argument: the dispute is not a sign that the message has failed, and the arguing is the work of men who have already decided.

**"So I Seized Them"**
The verse finishes with the shortest possible account of the outcome: so I seized them, and how terrible was My punishment. The same sentence closes the stories of the nations elsewhere, and the sūrah repeats it in the passage that follows on the people of Pharaoh, whose end is presented as the standing example for everyone who prefers power to proof (40:45-46; and see 3:11, 29:14). The pattern is the Book's answer to the long argument the Makkans were running: no community has ever outlasted the message it refused. The sūrah ends the thought where it began, by showing the Prophet that the men in front of him are the latest in a line and not the first.

## Verse 40:6

> And so your Lord’s decree has been proven true against the disbelievers—that they will be the inmates of the Fire

**The Word That Stood**
The historical review closes with a sentence that reads like a court's final entry: *wa-kadhālika ḥaqqat kalimatu Rabbika ʿalā alladhīna kafarū* — and so the word of your Lord came due against those who disbelieved. *Ḥaqqat* comes from the root of truth and of a thing becoming binding, and that is the sense: a debt that has fallen due. Nobody pronounced the verdict in this verse; it had been standing since before these nations were born. The verse states the content of the word at the end of the same sentence: *that they are the companions of the Fire*. The sentence is not a threat about the future but a statement of where the road goes.
**The Same Sentence Elsewhere**
The Book states the decree in the same terms in more than one place, so that the reader hears it as a settled principle and not a passing remark. In the sūrah of Jonah: so the word of your Lord came due against those who rebelled — that they do not believe (10:33). In the sūrah of the Crowds, the people of the Fire arrive and hear it announced at the gate: the word of punishment has fallen on the disbelievers (39:71). The three verses together tell the reader what the "word" is doing: it is not a mood in God that might change, and it is not a sentence handed down after a long negotiation. It is the outcome that follows from a settled choice, and the verse is written as a report of something already entered.
**Why It Is Said Here**
The sūrah has just walked through the fates of the peoples who denied their messengers, and the sentence at the end is the verdict on the whole sequence. The reader is meant to notice that the same word is available to every generation that makes the same decision — which is why the sūrah does not end the discussion with this verse but goes on to argue, in the verses that follow, about the signs and the call of the believers. A verdict is announced so that people will change their course before the entry is read out over them, and the sūrah continues to argue for eleven more verses after this one.

## Verse 40:7

> Those ˹angels˺ who carry the Throne and those around it glorify the praises of their Lord, have faith in Him, and seek forgiveness for the believers, ˹praying:˺ “Our Lord! You encompass everything in ˹Your˺ mercy and knowledge. So forgive those who repent and follow Your Way, and protect them from the torment of the Hellfire

**The Celestial Assembly at the Cosmic Apex**

The seventh verse dramatically elevates the discourse from the sordid historical squabbles of earthly disputants to the sublime assembly of the highest celestial beings: *alladhīna yaḥmilūna al-ʿArsha wa-man ḥawlahu*—those angels who carry the Throne and those around it. Classical Islamic thought regards the Throne (*al-ʿArsh*) as the supreme created ceiling of the universe, the celestial locus from which sovereign divine decrees emanate to govern the cosmos. The bearers of the Throne (*ḥamalat al-ʿArsh*) and the angels who encircle its radiant perimeter constitute the most exalted tier of the angelic hierarchy. In Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah, the scripture specifies their Last-Day majesty: *“and eight will bear the Throne of your Lord above them on that Day”* (69:17). The structural contrast within the sūrah is breathtaking: while arrogant tribal chieftains plot conspiracies in the dusty council chambers of Makkah, the most magnificent beings in existence are gathered at the apex of creation, completely absorbed in worship and divine service.

**Cosmic Solidarity with Earthly Believers**

The third action of the Throne-bearers unveils an astonishing reality of spiritual solidarity: *wa-yastaghfirūna li-lladhīna āmanū*—and seek forgiveness for the believers. In Sūrah al-Shūrā, angels are described as seeking forgiveness broadly for those on earth: *“and ask forgiveness for those on earth”* (42:5). Here in Ghāfir, however, the angelic supplication narrows with intense, protective intimacy toward the believers. For the early Muslims in Makkah, who were despised, tortured, and cast out as social refuse by their own clansmen, this revelation had immense psychological power. A destitute slave bleeding under the desert sun was informed that the highest entities in the cosmos were actively interceding on his behalf before the Lord of the worlds.

**Mercy and Knowledge as the Pillars of Intercession**

The recorded supplication opens by anchoring itself in two all-encompassing divine attributes: *Rabbanā wasiʿta kulla shayʾin raḥmatan wa-ʿilmā*—Our Lord! You encompass everything in Your mercy and knowledge. Exegetes noted the profound rhetorical and religious wisdom of coupling these two qualities. Knowledge without mercy would be terrifying for a fallible creature, as omniscience observes every hidden fault, shameful deed, and secret weakness. Conversely, mercy without knowledge might appear arbitrary or uninformed. By uniting both attributes, the angels acknowledge that God’s exhaustive knowledge of human frailty does not diminish His boundless compassion. This mirrors the universal principle revealed elsewhere in scripture: *“My mercy encompasses all things”* (7:156). Classical commentators such as al-Ṭabarī observed that mentioning mercy before knowledge in this supplication means that divine benevolence precedes and frames the execution of divine judgment, providing the ultimate legal and moral foundation for human forgiveness.

## Verse 40:8

> Our Lord! Admit them into the Gardens of Eternity which You have promised them, along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants. You ˹alone˺ are truly the Almighty, All-Wise

**The Gardens They Are Told to Enter**

The angelic supplication continues its ascending trajectory from deliverance from punishment to the highest realization of divine reward: *Rabbanā wa-adkhilhum jannāti ʿadnin allatī waʿadtahum*—Our Lord! Admit them into the Gardens of Eternity which You have promised them. In the old Arabic dictionaries, the root *ʿ-d-n* means permanent residence, unshakeable rootedness, and stability from which there is neither departure nor decay. Classical commentators such as al-Qurṭubī noted that the Gardens of Eden (*Jannāt ʿAdn*) designate the most sublime inner sanctuary of Paradise, an exalted domain reserved for prophets, truth-tellers, martyrs, and leaders of exceptional piety.

**The Transgenerational Reunion of Faith**

The petition immediately incorporates a magnificent familial dimension: *wa-man ṣalaḥa min ābāʾihim wa-azwājihim wa-dhurriyyātihim*—along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants. The Qur'anic vision of salvation does not dissolve the authentic bonds of human love into cold, detached individualism; rather, it purifies, perfects, and immortalizes them in the celestial realm. This theme is confirmed in Sūrah al-Ṭūr: *“And those who believed and whose descendants followed them in faith—We will join with them their descendants, and We will not deprive them of anything of their deeds”* (52:21). The angels echo this divine decree, asking that the joy of Paradise be magnified through the shared companionship of loved ones. A parallel passage in Sūrah al-Raʿd depicts the angels greeting the righteous in Eden alongside their pious forebears, partners, and offspring (13:23), illustrating that ultimate human happiness includes the mutual delight of righteous family fellowship.

## Verse 40:9

> And protect them from ˹the consequences of their˺ evil deeds. For whoever You protect from the evil of their deeds on that Day will have been shown Your mercy. That is ˹truly˺ the ultimate triumph.”

**The Petition and Its Word**
*Wa-qihim al-sayyiʾāt* — and protect them from the evil deeds. The angels have been asking forgiveness for the believers, and their prayer now reaches for the last thing they need. Al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr read the phrase in two ways, and the two readings belong together. One asks God to keep the believers from committing evil in the first place. The other asks Him to shield them from the punishment their evil deeds would earn, so that the record does not reach them on the day it is opened. Both readings take the same view of the human being: he needs to be guarded before he acts and forgiven after he acts, and the prayer covers both.
**The Sentence That Defines the Day**
The verse then states the principle in general terms: whoever is protected from evil deeds on that day, God has shown him mercy. The angels are speaking, but the rule they state applies to everybody in the gathering. Forgiveness without protection would leave the person exposed again; protection without forgiveness would leave the earlier record standing. The prayer asks for both, which is why the Book places it here as the perfect summary of what a believer wants for himself and for his family.
**Why the Confidence Is Reasonable**

The verse's confidence in mercy rests on something stated at the beginning of the creation. The well-known report in Bukhārī has God writing above His Throne, when He created the world, that His mercy overcomes His anger. A prayer that asks to be spared on the strength of mercy is not asking for a favour outside the order of things; it is asking for the thing the whole arrangement was built to deliver.

**The Name for the Outcome**
The verse closes with the words the Sūrah of the Table uses for the reward of the truthful: that is the great victory (5:119). The same name is given in the Sūrah of Repentance to the gardens, the rivers and God's good pleasure (9:72). The sūrah of the Forgiver uses the phrase for a person who comes through the Day without a punishment landing on him, and the choice says what the Book thinks safety is. A victory in the ordinary sense is something a man wins; this one is something a man is given, and the ones asking for it in these verses are the angels who carry the Throne and who believe in God and ask forgiveness for the believers (40:7).

## Verse 40:10

> Indeed, it will be announced to the disbelievers, “Allah’s contempt for you—as you disbelieved when invited to belief—was far worse than your contempt for one another ˹Today˺.”

**A Voice Across the Gathering**
*Inna alladhīna kafarū yunādawna* — the disbelievers will be called out. The verb is passive and impersonal: the call comes from a distance, over a vast crowd, and everyone present hears it. The scene has just shown the believers safe and the angels asking forgiveness for them (40:9), and the contrast is drawn by nothing more than this announcement, spoken aloud in front of creation.
**The Word That Is Used**
What is announced is the measure of God's *maqt* — His loathing, His contempt, His holding of a person in abhorrence. Ibn Manẓūr, in the *Lisān al-ʿArab*, ranks *maqt* as the heaviest word in the language for hatred, the anger of one who has been insulted and will not overlook it. The Qur'an uses the same root for the worst acts, among them marrying a stepmother, which it calls an indecency and a cause of hatred (4:22). The verse says this is what a life of denial earned, and it says it is greater than anything the condemned feel about themselves.
**Greater Than Their Own Hatred**
*Allah's loathing of you was greater than your loathing of yourselves.* The scene is the moment when each person sees the record and turns against himself, and the Sūrah of the Cow describes the same self-hatred among the followers of the powerful, who disown one another the instant the punishment is in front of them (2:167). The verse's argument is about timing. The call went out in the world: believe, and the offer stood open for years. The hatred of God was there then, and no one paid it any attention; the hatred people feel for themselves on that day comes far too late.
**What the Verse Leaves Out**
It gives no detail of the punishment. It gives only the announcement and the comparison, and the comparison is the sentence. God's dislike of the disbelief was greater than the sinner's dislike of his own end — and the One whose dislike is being spoken of is the One who gave the warnings, sent the messengers and kept the door open. The next verses in the sūrah move on from the call itself to the reason the people give, and then to the answer they are handed.

## Verse 40:11

> They will plead, “Our Lord! You made us lifeless twice, and gave us life twice. Now we confess our sins. So is there any way out?”

The eleventh verse captures the desperate plea of the condemned as they experience the crushing reality of divine judgment: *qālū Rabbanā*—they will plead, “Our Lord!” The sudden transformation in their vocabulary is profound. Throughout their earthly existence, the leaders of disbelief treated divine Lordship (*al-Rubūbiyyah*) with cynical derision, mocking the Prophet’s warnings and rejecting the notion of resurrection as an ancient fairy tale.

**The Twofold Cycle of Death and Life**

The condemned try to construct an argument based upon their retrospective comprehension of cosmic history: *amattanā ithnatayni wa-aḥyaytanā ithnatayn*—You made us lifeless twice, and gave us life twice. The commentators extensively debated the precise identification of these two deaths and two lives. The dominant interpretation, transmitted on the authority of ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd and ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās, links this verse directly to the sequence described in Sūrah al-Baqarah: *“How can you disbelieve in Allah when you were lifeless and He brought you to life. Then He will cause you to die, then He will bring you [back] to life, and then to Him you will be returned?”* (2:28). In this understanding, the first lifeless state was the condition of nonexistence before biological conception in the loins of their ancestors; the first life was their mortal earthly existence; the second death was their physical demise in the world; and the second life is their bodily resurrection on the Day of Judgment. An alternative interpretive perspective, recorded by al-Qurṭubī, interprets the intermediate life and death as the temporary awakening and subsequent slumber during the questioning of the grave (*ʿadhāb al-qabr*). Under either reading, the disbelievers finally acknowledge that the power that brought them forth from nothingness and revived them from dust has absolute mastery over life and death.

**The Confession of Guilt Without Defense**

Having recognized the unbroken continuity of divine power across their existence, the condemned offer an absolute admission of guilt: *fa-ʿtarafnā bi-dhunūbinā*—now we confess our sins. The Arabic verb *iʿtarafa* means unambiguous acknowledgment without prevarication, legal deflection, or excuse. In the courtroom of the Hereafter, the elaborate argument, philosophical sophistries, and social justifications with which they once defended their disbelief dissolve into ash. This mirrors the universal posture of the damned described in Sūrah al-Mulk: *“And they will confess their sins, but far off are the companions of the Blaze”* (67:11). The confession is exhaustive: they do not accuse God of injustice, nor do they claim that the revelation was unclear. They admit that their catastrophic end was the direct consequence of their own deliberate rebellion (*dhunūb*), acknowledging that they freely chose darkness when light was offered to them.

**The Futility of Conditional Remorse**

The tragedy of their plea lies in its metaphysical impossibility and psychological insincerity. The Qur'an unmasks the internal reality of this conditional remorse in Sūrah al-Anʿām: *“Even if they were sent back, they would certainly return to what they were forbidden, and indeed they are liars”* (6:28).

## Verse 40:12

> ˹They will be told,˺ “˹No!˺ This is because when Allah alone was invoked, you ˹staunchly˺ disbelieved. But when others were associated with Him ˹in worship˺, you ˹readily˺ believed. So ˹Today˺ judgment belongs to Allah ˹alone˺—the Most High, All-Great.”

**The Sentence and Its Reason**
*Dhālikum bi-annahu* — this is because. The condemned have just asked for a way out, and the answer does not merely refuse them. It states the case: when God was called on alone, you disbelieved; and when others were joined to Him in worship, you believed. Then it closes with the two names — the Most High, the Most Great — and the names explain why the exclusive claim was never going to be relaxed.
**Two Reactions to One Name**
The Sūrah of the Troops paints the same picture from the inside. When God alone is mentioned, the hearts of those who do not believe in the Hereafter shrink with disgust; when others are mentioned alongside Him, they are glad (39:45). The verse here is the courtroom version of that scene. Nobody in it is ignorant that God exists. The Quraysh acknowledged the Creator and called Him the greatest of the gods; what they could not stomach was a claim that made every other name in their temple meaningless.
**Why the Door Stays Shut**
The Book never presents the denial as a shortage of evidence, and this verse is one of its plainest statements of the reason. The hearing was offered again and again, and what was refused was the second half of the sentence — His being one. The answer adds that the One making the claim is *al-ʿAlī al-Kabīr*, high above every partner anyone could assign to Him and too great to be joined with a creature. It is the same name the sūrah uses elsewhere when it tells the believers how to call on God (40:14).
**What the Reader Is Meant to Do with It**
The verse is a diagnosis, and the sūrah is full of them. Moses' people argued, the court of Pharaoh argued, the man in the family of Pharaoh argued back on the believers' behalf. The one thing no argument managed was to make the objection reasonable. So the sūrah keeps returning the reader to the same plain point: the creed was not hard to understand. It was hard to accept, and the verse shows where that ends.

## Verse 40:13

> He is the One Who shows you His signs and sends down ˹rain as˺ a provision for you from the sky. ˹But˺ none will be mindful except those who turn ˹to Him˺

**The Signs in the Sky and the Provision Below**

The thirteenth verse shifts the religious gaze from the Last-Day reckoning of the Hereafter to the living, magnificent canvas of the present world: *Huwa alladhī yurīkum āyātihi*—He is the One Who shows you His signs. Creation is not portrayed as a blind, mechanistic automaton operating in cold metaphysical isolation; rather, it is an open, luminous theater continuously displaying the active artistry, wisdom, and omnipotence of its Creator. Classical commentators emphasized that these signs (*āyāt*) encompass both the macrocosmic architecture of the celestial horizons (*al-āfāq*) and the astonishing microcosmic subtleties of the human self (*al-anfus*). This mirrors the foundational principle explained in Sūrah Fuṣṣilat: *“We will show them Our signs in the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth”* (41:53). Every sunrise, every celestial orbit, and every biological miracle stands as a clear divine signature, calling humanity to recognize the solitary Sovereign who governs existence.

**The Descent of Provision from the Heavens**

The verse immediately couples cosmic majesty with direct, nurturing benevolence: *wa-yunazzilu lakum min al-samāʾi rizqā*—and sends down rain as a provision for you from the sky. In the rhetorical brilliance of Arabic expression, the scripture designates rain directly as "provision" (*rizq*), identifying the physical cause by the name of its ultimate harvest. Water cascading from the clouds penetrates the parched earth, awakening dormant seeds, replenishing subterranean springs, and generating an abundance of grain, dates, and life-giving vegetation. This theme is confirmed in Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt: *“And in the heaven is your provision and whatever you are promised”* (51:22). This tangible provision forcefully exposes the absolute, inescapable vulnerability of mortal human beings.

**The Prerequisite of Inābah: Turning Back to God**

The text expresses the non-negotiable spiritual condition required for authentic intellectual illumination: *wa-mā yatadhakkaru illā man yunīb*—˹But˺ none will be mindful except those who turn to Him. In the old Arabic dictionaries, *al-inābah* means returning repeatedly, pivoting back with contrition, and orienting the heart toward God in humble surrender. Exegetes such as Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr observed that the moment a person abandons moral arrogance and turns to God in sincere penitence, the dark clouds obscuring the intellect dissolve. The soul is endowed with clear spiritual discernment (*baṣīrah*), enabling it to perceive the divine light permeating creation. The Prophet ﷺ expressed this illuminated state of consciousness when he counseled: "Be mindful of the insight of the believer, for indeed he sees by the light of Allah." Repentance purifies the cognitive mirror of the soul, allowing it to reflect divine realities with crystal clarity.

---

## Verse 40:14

> So call upon Allah with sincere devotion, even to the dismay of the disbelievers

**The Injunction of Pure Supplication**

The fourteenth verse establishes an uncompromising imperative that flows directly from the cosmic realities explained in the preceding verse: *fa-udʿū Allāh*—so call upon Allah. The causative conjunction *fa-* ("so" or "therefore") binds this command inextricably to the recognition that God alone reveals cosmic signs and sends down heavenly provision. Because the universe is sustained by a solitary Provider, all devotional invocation must be addressed exclusively to Him. Classical commentators, including al-Zamakhsharī and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, explained that the verb *udʿū* encompasses two interrelated levels of Qur'anic prayer. It includes the prayer of worship (*duʿāʾ al-ʿibādah*), which embraces canonical prayer, fasting, and ritual devotion, as well as the prayer of petition (*duʿāʾ al-masʾalah*), in which the needy servant implores the Lord for spiritual guidance, physical sustenance, and protection from harm. Supplication is the classic marrow of worship, serving as the direct lifeline between creature and Creator.

**The Crucible of Ikhlāṣ: Purifying Religion**

The imperative specifies the essential inward quality required for acceptable worship: *mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīn*—with sincere devotion, or dedicating religion completely to Him. In the old Arabic dictionaries, the root *kh-l-ṣ* means the rigorous process of purifying a substance until every trace of foreign dross, alloy, or contamination is completely eliminated, just as pure milk is refined from between blood and bodily waste. Applied to the spiritual realm, *ikhlāṣ* demands cleansing one's faith, rituals, and interior motivations from all forms of polytheistic contamination (*shirk*), ostentatious display (*riyāʾ*), and the subtle desire for social admiration. This mandate forms an unyielding refrain across the Qur'an: *“And they were not commanded except to worship Allah, [being] sincere to Him in religion”* (98:5), and again in Sūrah al-Zumar: *“So worship Allah, [being] sincere to Him in religion”* (39:2). Devotion cannot be partitioned between God and worldly idols; it must be offered as a pure, undivided offering to the Solitary King. True sincerity demands that one's private reality matches one's public profession, establishing a seamless moral integrity that seeks only the pleasure of the Creator.

**The Liturgical Seal of Prophetic Prayer**

The enduring spiritual authority of this verse is vividly illustrated by its central role in the daily devotional practice of the Prophet ﷺ. As recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 594* on the authority of ʿAbdullāh ibn al-Zubayr, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ would proclaim aloud at the conclusion of every obligatory prayer immediately after completing the *taslīm*: “There is no god but Allah alone, having no partner. To Him belongs the dominion, and to Him belongs the praise, and He has power over all things. There is no strength and no power except with Allah. There is no god but Allah, and we worship none but Him. To Him belongs all favor, all grace, and all beautiful praise. There is no god but Allah, devoting religion completely to Him, even to the dismay of the disbelievers.” By incorporating the very phrasing of 40:14 into the regular post-prayer liturgy, the Prophet ﷺ trained his community to renew their covenant of uncompromised monotheism five times each day, embedding holy defiance deep into their daily consciousness.

## Verse 40:15

> ˹He is˺ Highly Exalted in rank, Lord of the Throne. He sends down the revelation by His command to whoever He wills of His servants to warn ˹all˺ of the Day of Meeting—

**The Lord of the Sovereign Throne**

The second title anchors this transcendent majesty in cosmic governance: *Dhū al-ʿArsh*—Lord of the Throne. The sūrah earlier depicted the magnificent angels who bear the Throne and encircle its luminous boundaries (40:7); here, the revelation directs the heart past the celestial servants to the sovereign Master of the Throne Himself. In the Qur'anic conceptual framework, the Throne (*al-ʿArsh*) represents the apex of creation, the celestial center of universal command from which divine decrees issue to govern galaxies, life, and destiny (17:42; 85:15). Describing God as *Dhū al-ʿArsh* reminds humanity that the ruler cosmic is not an indifferent, distant abstraction. He is the active Sovereign whose authority permeates every corner of existence, ensuring that earthly tyrants who imagine themselves unassailable masters of men are merely dust before the true Emperor of reality. Cosmic dominion resides exclusively in His grasp.

**The Transmission of the Spirit: The Revelation**

From this cosmic throne descends the divine communication that alters human history: *yulqī al-rūḥa min amrihi*—He sends down the revelation by His command. The Qur'an frequently designates prophetic revelation as *al-rūḥ* ("the Spirit"), employing a magnificent religious metaphor that illuminates the relationship between scripture and human life. Just as a physical body deprived of its biological spirit (*rūḥ*) becomes an inert, decaying corpse, so too does human society without divine revelation degenerate into moral paralysis, spiritual death, and predatory chaos. This concept is confirmed in Sūrah al-Shūrā: *“And so We have revealed to you an inspiration [a Spirit] of Our command”* (42:52). Revelation breathes transcendent life, moral vitality, and intellectual lucidity into the human spirit. The casting (*ilqāʾ*) of this divine spirit by His command occurs through the agency of the Archangel Gabriel, infusing human history with celestial light. The text stresses the sovereign, uncoerced nature of divine election: *ʿalā man yashāʾu min ʿibādihi*—to whoever He wills of His servants. This declaration directly dismantled the arrogant objections of the pagan Quraysh, who petulantly demanded why the Qur'an was not sent down to a wealthy aristocrat of Makkah or a tribal lord of Ṭāʾif (43:31).

## Verse 40:16

> the Day all will appear ˹before Allah˺. Nothing about them will be hidden from Him. ˹He will ask,˺ “Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme

**The Emergence onto the Level Plain**

The sixteenth verse continues the apocalyptic exposition of the Day of Meeting, detailing the absolute exposure of all creation before the divine presence: *yawma hum bārizūn*—the Day all will appear before Allah. The active participle *bārizūn*, rooted in the classical Arabic term for an open, unobstructed desert plain (*al-barāz*), means emerging completely from hiding into unshielded visibility. All human beings across every epoch will be raised simultaneously from their subterranean graves, stepping forth onto an earth stripped bare of its familiar geography. Sūrah Ṭā Hā describes this cosmic transformation as leaving the landscape as a level, desolate plain in which no crookedness or elevation can be seen (20:106–107). Mountain fortresses, impenetrable bunkers, deep shadows, and royal palaces are obliterated. Creation stands in naked vulnerability upon a single vast horizon, unable to retreat into physical shelter or construct artificial barriers against the divine gaze.

**The Eradication of All Concealment**

The second clause proclaims the terrifying epistemic transparency of that fateful gathering: *lā yakhfā ʿalā Allāhi minhum shayʾ*—nothing about them will be hidden from Him. In earthly life, human wrongdoers routinely exploit the limitations of mortal perception. Tyrants bury the bodies of their victims in unmarked mass graves, corrupt oligarchs conceal illicit wealth in offshore accounts, and hypocrites mask their betrayal behind smiles and religious platitudes. In the Last-Day court of God, the very concept of concealment is annihilated. Not a single physical atom of the body, not a single whispered conspiracy, and not a single fleeting thought of the heart remains veiled from divine omniscience. This total exposure mirrors the universal summons in Sūrah Ibrāhīm: *“The Day when the earth will be replaced by another earth, and the heavens as well, and all will appear before Allah, the One, the Prevailing”* (14:48). Every human soul is exposed in its absolute moral reality. The artificial masks constructed by wealth, tribal prestige, and sophisticated public relations crumble into dust, leaving each individual to be judged strictly by the truth of their character and the reality of their deeds before the All-Seeing Creator.

**The Sovereign Interrogative Across the Silence**

Across the hushed expanse of the resurrected cosmos rings a thunderous divine question: *li-man al-mulku al-yawm*—Who does all authority belong to this Day? Classical commentaries, including the tafsīr traditions recorded by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, depict the awe-inspiring cosmic context of this moment. After the final blast of the Trumpet, all mortal creatures, earthly kings, military conquerors, and celestial angels have perished, and an overwhelming, breathless silence envelopes the universe. Into this primordial stillness, the Lord of Majesty proclaims His sovereign interrogative.

## Verse 40:17

> Today every soul will be rewarded for what it has done. No injustice Today! Surely Allah is swift in reckoning.”

**The Individualization of Universal Reckoning**

The seventeenth verse inaugurates the active execution of the final judgment with dramatic temporal immediacy: *al-yawma tujzā kullu nafsin bimā kasabat*—Today every soul will be rewarded for what it has done. The opening temporal marker *al-yawm* ("Today") carries immense judicial weight. The long epoch of earthly trial, moral hesitation, and deferred consequence has definitively closed; the era of pure recompense (*al-jazāʾ*) has began. The universal scope *kullu nafsin* ("every soul") dismantles the collective illusions of tribal society. In pagan Arabia, an individual's moral guilt or virtue was routinely subsumed into the lineage of the clan, allowing powerful elites to shield their members from legal responsibility. The Qur'an completely individualizes the moral equation: every single human soul steps forward as an autonomous moral agent, evaluated strictly according to the fruits of its own conscious earning (*kasb*), as confirmed in Sūrah al-Jāthiyah (45:22). No soul can hide behind the prestige of its ancestors, nor can anyone be forced to carry the spiritual burden of another.

**The Absolute Negation of Injustice**

Across the courtroom of eternity resounds a majestic guarantee: *lā ẓulma al-yawm*—no injustice Today! The commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī, unpacked the concrete legal meaning of this declaration. Retribution is not a malicious injury inflicted by a vengeful ruler; it is the exact, uncorrupted mirror of the soul's own earthly actions (3:117).

**The Divine Prohibition of Oppression in Ḥadīth Qudsī**

The absolute justice of the final reckoning reflects the sublime religious reality that the Almighty has voluntarily bound His sovereign power to the standard of flawless equity. This eternal principle is expressed in the renowned *ḥadīth qudsī* preserved in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2577* on the authority of Abū Dharr al-Ghifārī, in which the Prophet ﷺ reported that God declared: “O My servants! I have forbidden injustice for Myself and have made it unlawful among you, so do not wrong one another. O My servants! Verily, it is only your deeds that I record for you and then recompense you for in full. So whoever finds good, let him praise Allah; and whoever finds other than that, let him blame none but himself.” Divine justice is not a standard imposed upon God from an external authority, for there is no authority above Him; rather, it is an immutable expression of His own perfection.

**The Precision and Speed of Divine Accounting**

The verse seals its judicial decree with an affirmation of divine capacity: *inna Allāha sarīʿu al-ḥisāb*—surely Allah is swift in reckoning. In mortal human legal systems, adjudicating complex disputes involving millions of individuals requires exhausting years of investigation, testimony, and bureaucratic deliberation, often resulting in tragic delays, lost evidence, and clerical errors. In the divine court, God’s reckoning is instantaneous (*sarīʿ*). Classical scholars such as Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī pointed out that just as God simultaneously sustains, hears. Provides for billions of distinct organisms across the globe in a single instant without being distracted by one from another, He calculates the exhaustive moral accounts of all resurrected beings simultaneously in the blinking of an eye: *“And Our command is but one, like the blinking of an eye”* (54:50). Divine omniscience experiences no administrative fatigue, allowing the accounting of billions of lifetimes to unfold with effortless speed and microscopic accuracy.

## Verse 40:18

> Warn them ˹O Prophet˺ of the approaching Day when the hearts will jump into the throats, suppressing distress. The wrongdoers will have neither a close friend nor intercessor to be heard

**The Mandate of Last-Day Warning**

The eighteenth verse issues an urgent pastoral command to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ: *wa-andhirhum yawma al-āzifah*—warn them ˹O Prophet˺ of the approaching Day. The Last-Day title *al-Āzifah* is derived from the classical Arabic verb *azifa*, which means an event that is imminent, swiftly advancing, and lurking immediately on the horizon. Although mortal human beings trapped within physical chronology routinely perceive the Day of Judgment as a remote, abstract possibility delayed by millennia, in the cosmic perspective of the divine reality it is racing forward with unceasing speed: *“The approaching Day has drawn near”* (53:57). The imperative *andhirhum* ("warn them") charges the Prophet with the solemn duty of piercing through human complacency. He is commanded to strip away the worldly distractions and commercial busyness that dull the spiritual senses of his audience, confronting them with the terrifying certainty of an event that will overturn all created stability.

**The Severing of Intimate Friendship**

The verse dismantles the fundamental social security systems of tribal society: *mā li-al-ẓālimīna min ḥamīm*—the wrongdoers will have neither a close friend. In Arabic, the noun *ḥamīm* designates a fiercely loyal, intensely loving friend or close kinsperson who passionately defends their companion against all adversaries. In the clannish culture of pre-Islamic Arabia, an individual's security depended completely upon having powerful *ḥamīm* protectors who would take up arms to defend them regardless of guilt. The Qur'an announces the total extinction of this tribal solidarity on the Day of Resurrection: *“And no loyal friend will ask about a friend”* (70:10), because *“For each person that Day is an affair to make him heedless of all else”* (80:37). Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah seals this tragic abandonment: *“So today he has no loyal friend here”* (69:35). In the courtroom of God, every corrupt alliance dissolves, leaving the unrepentant oppressor in absolute, terrifying isolation. Earthly patrons who promised protection are revealed to be utterly helpless, unable to offer even a single word of comfort to those they led astray.

**The Collapse of Intercessory Delusions**

The text shatters the superstitious religious illusions that sustained pagan idolatry: *wa-lā shafīʿin yuṭāʿ*—nor intercessor to be heard, or obeyed. In the pagan worldview, idols and demigods were conceptualized as supernatural patrons capable of forcing divine leniency. The polytheists openly declared: *“We only worship them so that they may bring us nearer to Allah”* (39:3). They falsely believed that their favored deities had sovereign leverage and could demand the acquittal of their devotees. The phrase *yuṭāʿ* ("to be obeyed" or "heeded") definitively destroys this idolatrous fantasy. No created entity has autonomous authority before God; no intermediary can compel the Almighty or force His judicial decree. True intercession (*shafāʿah*) belongs exclusively to God, granted only by His sovereign leave to those with whom He is well-pleased, leaving idolaters and corrupt tyrants with zero legal advocacy on the Day of Judgment.

---

## Verse 40:19

> Allah ˹even˺ knows the sly glances of the eyes and whatever the hearts conceal

**The Microscopic Omniscience of God**

The nineteenth verse unveils the astonishing, microscopic depth of divine omniscience: *yaʿlamu khāʾinata al-aʿyun*—Allah ˹even˺ knows the sly glances of the eyes. Classical Arabic lexicographers, including Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab* and al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī in *Mufradāt Alfāẓ al-Qurʾān*, observed that *khāʾinah* is an intensive verbal noun meaning subtle betrayal, stealthy theft, or covert dissimulation. Applied to the human eye, *khāʾinat al-aʿyun* means the swift, furtive glance that a person steals toward a forbidden sight when they believe no human onlooker is watching, or an eyelid that flutters with simulated innocence while concealing malicious calculation.

**The Architecture of Inner Concealment**

The second clause expands the scope of divine vigilance from external biological organs to the deepest interior sanctuary of human consciousness: *wa-mā tukhfī al-ṣudūr*—and whatever the hearts conceal. In Qur'anic physiology, the "chests" (*al-ṣudūr*) represent the protective container housing the spiritual heart (*al-qalb*), the central command station of human volition, emotion, and moral discernment. The human breast is capable of concealing vast architectures of secret motives: suppressed resentments, covert hypocrisies, unexpressed malice, private lusts, and unspoken doubts. This profound Last-Day transparency is confirmed in Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt: *“And that which is in the breasts is made evident”* (100:10). In the presence of God, the thick biological walls of the human ribcage become completely transparent, leaving every subtle intention, secret motive, and silent prayer exposed to the light of divine truth. Nothing within human interiority can remain hidden from the penetrating gaze of the Creator.

**The Prophetic Standard of Transparency**

The rigorous moral standard established by the phrase *khāʾinat al-aʿyun* is vividly illustrated by a celebrated historical report from the life of the Prophet ﷺ. During the conquest of Makkah, when the fugitive ʿAbdullāh ibn Saʿd ibn Abī Sarḥ was brought before the Messenger of Allah ﷺ to offer his oath of allegiance after a period of apostasy, the Prophet hesitated three times, remaining deliberately silent in the hope that one of his Companions would recognize the traitor's guilt and execute him before the pledge was finalized. When the oath was eventually accepted and a Companion later asked, "Why did you not signal to us with a gesture of your eye, O Messenger of Allah?" the Prophet ﷺ replied with profound moral majesty: “It is not fitting for a prophet to possess treacherous eyes (*khāʾinat al-aʿyun*).” This tradition, recorded in *Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4359* and *Sunan al-Nasāʾī 4067*, shows that prophetic leadership demands absolute moral transparency, completely purged of stealthy signals, hidden malice, or double-dealing.

**The Salvation of the Sound Heart**

The revelation of divine omniscience over the secrets of the breast provides the essential criterion by which human lives are evaluated in the Hereafter. The verse follows the description of the approaching Day because Last-Day salvation is determined not by external rituals, public prestige, or formal social compliance, but by the spiritual purity of the inward heart. As Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ proclaims: *“The Day when neither wealth nor children will benefit, but only one who comes to Allah with a sound heart”* (26:88–89). A human being might successfully deceive an entire society through theatrical displays of piety and charity, yet if the heart harbors arrogance, malice, and disbelief, those external deeds become scattered dust.

## Verse 40:20

> And Allah judges with the truth, while those ˹idols˺ they invoke besides Him cannot judge at all. Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing

**The Judge Who Judges Justly**

The twentieth verse establishes the supreme judicial contrast that resolves the religious and moral conflicts of existence: *wa-Allāhu yaqḍī bi-al-ḥaqq*—and Allah judges with the truth. In Arabic jurisprudence, the verb *qaḍā* means far more than delivering a speculative academic opinion; it means issuing an authoritative, unalterable legal verdict accompanied by the irresistible power of immediate execution. To judge *bi-al-ḥaqq* ("with truth" and "in accordance with reality") means that divine verdicts are anchored in absolute, incorruptible equity. God differentiates between human souls not according to superficial social reputation, inherited aristocratic status, or theatrical displays of righteousness, but in precise alignment with the interior realities exposed in the preceding verse—the secrets of the breast and the intentions of the heart. Every created being receives its uncompromised due (*ḥaqq*), ensuring that cosmic justice operates with absolute moral perfection. This unwavering standard of truth ensures that no earthly bias, prejudice, or political pressure can ever warp the scales of divine judgment.

**The Absolute Impotence of False Deities**

The narrative immediately turns to expose the utter vacuity of idolatrous religion: *wa-alladhīna yadʿūna min dūnihi lā yaqḍūna bi-shayʾ*—while those idols they invoke besides Him cannot judge at all. The phrase *lā yaqḍūna bi-shayʾ* ("they do not decree anything whatsoever") delivers a crushing of being verdict against all fabricated objects of worship. The deities revered by the pagan Quraysh—such as al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Hubal—were merely inanimate physical artifacts constructed from carved stone, wood, and precious metals. They had neither biological life, nor conscious intellect, nor moral discernment, nor executive agency. As the Qur'an challenges elsewhere: *“Indeed, those you call upon besides Allah cannot create even a fly, even if they all met together for it”* (22:73).

## Verse 40:21

> Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them? They were far superior in might and ˹richer in˺ monuments throughout the land. But Allah seized them for their sins, and they had no protector from Allah

The twenty-first verse pivots from the religious and Last-Day declarations of the opening section to a direct empirical summons: *a-wa-lam yasīrū fī al-arḍi fa-yanẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu alladhīna kānū min qablihim*—have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those destroyed before them? The merchant caravans of Quraysh traveled twice a year along ancient trading routes leading northward to the Levant and southward to Yemen, passing directly through the pulverized remnants of Thamūd at al-Ḥijr, the wind-sculpted wastes of ʿĀd, and the mineral ruins of the cities of Lot.

**Pastoral Rebuke to Contemporary Oligarchs**

For the chieftains of Makkah who fiercely opposed the Prophet ﷺ, this verse functioned as a devastating pastoral challenge. The leaders of Quraysh strutted through the sacred precincts of the Kaʿbah, boasting of their wealth, their diplomatic alliances, and their ancestral prestige. The revelation holds up the pulverized remains of Thamūd and ʿĀd as a mirror, asking them with cutting simplicity: If civilizations of vastly superior strength and architectural glory were annihilated because they defied God, what possible guarantee of survival can your fragile merchant city claim? For the persecuted Muslim minority, conversely, the verse provided an anchor of serene fortitude. It reassured the vulnerable believers that the arrogance of their oppressors was an ephemeral bubble doomed to burst, anchoring their patience in the eternal justice of the Almighty.

**The Command to Look at the Road**
The verse asks whether the men have not travelled in the earth and seen what the end of those before them was, and it answers with a comparison: those people were greater than these in strength, and they left traces in the land, and God overtook them for their sins, and they had no protector against Him. The Sūrah of the Romans gives the same command in its own words (30:9), and the Sūrah of the Cattle repeats it (6:11), and every place the Book gives the command it points at ruins rather than at scenery.
**What the Verse Establishes About Power**
The verse says plainly that the earlier peoples were stronger than the men who were hearing the recitation, and it then says that their strength availed them nothing. The Sūrah of the Romans makes the same point in the same chapter of its own argument (30:9), and the Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān states the rule about the alternation of days (3:140). The men of Makkah in front of the Prophet were being told that their own city was not a permanent arrangement.
**Where the Passage Turns**
The verse sits in the sūrah's account of Moses and Pharaoh, and it serves the argument of the whole passage: the court of Egypt was stronger than the men in front of the Prophet, and it ended in the sea. The verse after it asks the same question about the men who cried out and found no help (40:21 continues into the passage on the calling of the drowned), and the sūrah keeps the reader moving between the ruins of the road and the record of the palace.

## Verse 40:22

> That was because their messengers used to come to them with clear proofs, but they persisted in disbelief. So Allah seized them. Surely He is All-Powerful, severe in punishment

**Why They Were Destroyed**
The verse gives the reason in three words: *dhālika bi-annahum* — that was because they disbelieved the signs of God and disobeyed His messengers. The demonstrative *that* points back at the destruction described in the verse before, and the sentence rules out the two explanations people reach for when a nation falls — bad luck, or a God who acts on a whim. The Qur'an states the principle in plain terms in the sūrah of the Night Journey: God does not punish until He has sent a messenger (17:15). The record of a destroyed people is never the record of a people who were never told.
**What the Messengers Had Already Done**
By the time the destruction arrives, the case has been made in public: the messenger has spoken, the signs have been shown, and the community's leaders have had their chance to answer the evidence and chosen to answer the man instead. The Book is careful about this. It does not describe nations swept away for the ignorance they could not help; it describes nations that had the truth standing in front of them and put it off, season after season, until their habits had hardened into a position. The verse's word for that is *kafarū* — they covered it over, kept the light out of the house while it was shining on the doorstep.
**Why the Sūrah Keeps Telling the Story to the Quraysh**
The men hearing this in Makkah were not being asked to admire ancient history. The verse is one of a long run in the sūrah that names the peoples who came before, and the reason is in the audience: they had a messenger in their streets, the same kind of case to answer, and the same range of choices their predecessors had. The sūrah tells them what the choice cost elsewhere so that no one can say afterwards that the news came too late.

## Verse 40:23

> Indeed, We sent Moses with Our signs and compelling proof

**Moses Enters the Sūrah**
*Wa-laqad arsalnā Mūsā bi-āyātinā wa-sulṭānin mubīn* — We sent Moses with Our signs and a clear authority. This verse opens the longest historical passage in the chapter, and the story it begins has been told in the Book many times. Sūrah Hūd opens Moses' account with the same two things, signs and a clear authority (11:96), and the Sūrah of the Story has God promise Moses both the signs and the authority (28:35). The Sūrah of the Night Journey states the number of the signs and names them: nine clear signs shown to Pharaoh (17:101). The Muslim reader therefore meets a story he has heard before, and the sūrah tells it again for its own argument, because the audience in Makkah is doing what the court of Egypt did.
**The Two Things He Was Given**
The signs are the wonders shown in Egypt, and the authority is the case he was given to make in front of the king. The word *sulṭān* here means a proof that carries its own weight, which is why al-Qurṭubī and al-Zamakhsharī note that the two go together: the signs were evidence for the eye, and the argument was evidence for the mind. The verse's wording also states who sent him and what he was sent with, and it does not mention a book or an army, because the man was a fugitive who had left Egypt under a warrant and was returning on foot with a staff.
**Where the Account Goes**
The next verses name the court he faced: Pharaoh, Hāmān and Qārūn, and they call him a sorcerer and a liar (40:24), which is the same pair of words the Book records Makkah using against the Prophet. Then the sūrah moves into the arguments, the threats, and the believer from the family of Pharaoh who speaks up for him (40:28). The whole passage is an argument about what happens when a message reaches a place that holds power and does not want it, and the sūrah tells the story of one such place from beginning to end before it turns to the day the same argument will be settled.

## Verse 40:24

> to Pharaoh, Hamân, and Korah. But they responded: “Magician! Total liar!”

**Three Names, One System**
The verse sends Moses and Aaron *to Pharaoh, Hāmān and Qārūn* — and the three names are the point. Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr read the trio as the three legs on which a tyrannical state stands. Pharaoh is the ruler: the army, the prisons, the claim to be obeyed as a god. Hāmān is the administration: the deputy who carries the ruler's orders out, runs the projects, builds the tower of clay bricks to reach the sky, and manufactures the propaganda that goes with it (28:38). Qārūn is the money: the man of great wealth who says his fortune came from his own cleverness and owes nobody anything (28:76). Violence, bureaucracy and capital standing together, each protecting the other — the pattern is not ancient history, it is a description that still fits.
**The Betrayal From Inside**
Qārūn was not a foreign enemy. He was an Israelite, of the same tribe as Moses, one of the wealthiest men in the camp (28:76). That is why his name hurts. Pharaoh was the persecutor from outside and Hāmān was the servant of that persecution, but Qārūn belonged to the oppressed people himself and crossed over to the court, using his access to build a fortune while his own kinsmen laboured. Every oppressed community knows this wound, and scripture records it plainly, so that no one should be surprised when it recurs.
**The Verdict They Gave**
The verse ends with the answer all three produced: *they said, a magician, a liar* (40:24). Notice how short it is. Three men with the apparatus of a state behind them, confronted by two brothers with a staff, and what they had to say was a label. The word for magician was meant to turn the signs into stage tricks; the word for liar was meant to turn the claim into ambition. The Qur'an notes that this is not a Makkan invention — no messenger came to the peoples before them without being called a sorcerer or a madman (51:52). When the case cannot be answered, the messenger is slandered, and the slander is always the same one. Quraysh said it of the Prophet too, calling him a sorcerer and a liar (38:4); they had borrowed their lines from a regime already under water.
**The Mirror in the Sūrah**
The sūrah means the parallel for its first hearers to see. In Makkah the parts were all filled: a despot who made life unbearable, men of prestige who wrote the case against the Prophet, and merchants whose caravans made them sure that God had no claim on their wealth. The verse told them that the play had been performed before and that the ending of it was known.

## Verse 40:25

> Then, when he came to them with the truth from Us, they said, “Kill the sons of those who believe with him and keep their women.” But the plotting of the disbelievers was only in vain

**The Arrival of Transcendent Truth**

The twenty-fifth verse records the definitive confrontation between divine revelation and state terror: *fa-lammā jāʾahum bi-al-ḥaqqi min ʿindinā*—then, when he came to them with the truth from Us. The emphasis upon *al-ḥaqq* ("the truth") coupled with the prepositional marker *min ʿindinā* ("from Our presence") establishes the objective of being reality of the Mosaic message. Moses did not arrive in Egypt carrying human political ideologies, revolutionary slogans, or private tribal grievances; he delivered absolute, uncreated truth descending directly from the Lord of the Throne. This divine truth pierced the elaborate cultural illusions of the pharaonic court, exposing the absurdity of state-sanctioned polytheism and the grotesque injustice of imperial slavery. The arrival of unvarnished truth confronted the ruling establishment with an inescapable existential ultimatum: either humble themselves before the Creator of the cosmos, or reveal the naked, savage brutality of their illegitimate rule. Truth strips tyranny of its philosophical pretensions, forcing it to display its violent core.

**The Renewal of State-Sanctioned Infanticide**

Stripped of moral arguments, the imperial regime resorted to absolute violence, commanding: “Kill the sons of those who believe with him and keep their women” (40:25). Classical commentators, including Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, clarified that this verse documents a second, distinct wave of state-sponsored infanticide decreed by Pharaoh. The first slaughter of male infants had occurred decades earlier, before the birth of Moses, motivated by superstitious astrological fears that a male child born among the Hebrews would overthrow the throne (28:4). This second slaughter, narrated here, was a retaliatory, targeted policy of collective punishment enacted in response to the public miracles performed by Moses. As recorded in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, the court officials panicked at the growing influence of the prophetic message, prompting Pharaoh to declare: *“We shall slay their sons and spare their women, and indeed we are dominant over them”* (7:127). The regime believed that terrorizing children would halt the spiritual revolution.

## Verse 40:26

> And Pharaoh said, “Let me kill Moses, and let him call upon his Lord! I truly fear that he may change your traditions or cause mischief in the land.”

**The Hesitation Behind Despotic Bravado**

The twenty-sixth verse captures the psychological complexity and political tension within the imperial council: *wa-qāla Firʿawnu dharūnī aqtul Mūsā*—and Pharaoh said, “Let me kill Moses...” The opening imperative *dharūnī* ("leave me" or "let me") reveals an astonishing dynamic within the autocracy. Pharaoh was an absolute monarch who routinely executed thousands of enslaved Hebrews without seeking legal permission from any council or court. Why then does he command his ministers to "let him" execute Moses? Classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, observed that this phrasing exposes deep hesitation within the royal retinue. Some of Pharaoh's closest advisors, having witnessed the devastating miracles of the serpent and the radiant hand, were gripped by supernatural dread. They cautioned the king against executing the prophet, warning that shedding Mosaic blood might unleash immediate cosmic devastation upon Egypt. Pharaoh’s loud command was theatrical bravado designed to mask his own profound hesitation and reassert control over an anxious council that was beginning to fracture under spiritual terror.

**The Rhetoric of Cultural Preservation**

Pharaoh immediately justifies his violent intentions by appealing to the collective anxieties of his people: *innī akhāfu a yubaddila dīnakum*—I truly fear that he may change your traditions. In ancient Egypt, *al-dīn* signified far more than private devotional rituals; it encompassed the entire sociopolitical, economic, and legal system centered upon the divine status of Pharaoh himself (28:38).

**The Inversion of Moral Reality: Fasad in the Land**

The tyrant adds a second existential justification: *aw a yuẓhira fī al-arḍi al-fasād*—or cause mischief in the land. This statement represents the ultimate Orwellian inversion of ethical reality. Pharaoh—who slaughtered innocent nursing infants, enslaved entire populations, and declared himself the supreme god—brazenly casts himself as the protector of public order, while accusing the noble prophet of God of sowing societal corruption (*al-fasād*). This moral inversion is the universal hallmark of corrupt ruling classes, as expressed in Sūrah al-Baqarah: *“And when it is said to them, ‘Do not cause corruption on the earth,’ they say, ‘We are only reformers!’”* (2:11).

**Pastoral Mirror for the Makkan Sanctuary**

Within the historical context of early Islam, this verse provided the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ and his Companions with profound diagnostic insight into their own oppressors. The chieftains of Quraysh in Makkah employed precisely the same rhetorical playbook against the early Muslim community. Abū Jahl and his confederates publicly accused the Prophet ﷺ of fracturing clan unity, insulting ancestral traditions, and bringing ruin to the sacred valley of Makkah. They too presented their violent persecution, torture, and boycotts as a noble defense of Arabian cultural heritage. The revelation exposed this propaganda as an ancient, unoriginal falsehood.

## Verse 40:27

> Moses replied, “I seek refuge in my Lord and your Lord from every arrogant person who does not believe in the Day of Reckoning.”

**The Answer to a Death Threat**
The verse before this one has Pharaoh saying that he will kill Moses and let him call on his Lord, with a complaint that the man wants to change their religion or fill the land with corruption (40:26). The threat is made in public, at court, by a king whose word was the law of the land. Moses' answer is in this verse: *I take refuge in my Lord and your Lord from every arrogant one who does not believe in the Day of Account*. He does not argue, does not bargain, and does not look for protection among the courtiers; he steps under the protection of the One who made them.
**The Words He Chose**
The phrase *bi-Rabbī wa-Rabbikum*, my Lord and your Lord, is doing deliberate work in that hall. Moses was a fugitive, a former shepherd, standing among men who held the levers of the most powerful state of the age, and the sentence he says out loud puts their Lord above their king. He does not say my God against yours; he says that the Lord he is seeking refuge in is the Lord of the men who are threatening him, which turns the threat into a matter for its owner. And the refuge he seeks is named against a specific type: the arrogant man who does not believe in the Day of Account. The Book gives that arrogance its own words elsewhere — Pharaoh telling his people, I am your most exalted lord (79:24) — and it is that claim that makes the threat in the hall serious only from the One standing above him.
**What Seeking Refuge Meant in Practice**
The verse records the act of turning to God when the physical options have run out, and the Book treats it as an action rather than a mood. Moses did not leave the hall, hire guards, or organize an escape. He stated, in front of everyone, whose protection he was under, and the following verse records what happened next: a man from Pharaoh's own household spoke up on his behalf (40:28). The Book records the same posture in other mouths: Abraham asking protection from the king's aggression (44:20), and the end reserved for the arrogant man, who enters the Fire and is told, this is what you used to deny (16:29). The answer to the threat came from inside the court, which is where the reader of the sūrah is meant to see the hand that had been asked for.

## Verse 40:28

> A believing man from Pharaoh’s people, who was hiding his faith, argued, “Will you kill a man ˹only˺ for saying: ‘My Lord is Allah,’ while he has in fact come to you with clear proofs from your Lord? If he is a liar, it will be to his own loss. But if he is truthful, then you will be afflicted with some of what he is threatening you with. Surely Allah does not guide whoever is a transgressor, a total liar

**The Emergence of the Secret Believer**

The twenty-eighth verse introduces the central human protagonist who gives Sūrah Ghāfir its alternative title, *al-Muʾmin* (The Believer): *wa-qāla rajulun muʾminun min āli Firʿawna yaktumu īmānahu*—a believing man from Pharaoh’s people, who was hiding his faith, argued. The commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī, identified this remarkable figure as an elite Egyptian aristocrat, likely a cousin or close kinsperson of Pharaoh who held an influential advisory post within the imperial administration. His practice of concealing his faith (*kitmān al-īmān*) establishes a vital religious and legal precedent within Islamic jurisprudence. When a believer is trapped within a totalitarian society where open profession of faith invites summary execution, concealing one's internal convictions while waiting for an opportune moment to perform strategic intervention is fully permissible. This courtier used his insider position not for self-enrichment, but to preserve his life until the decisive moment arrived when his voice could stay the executioner's hand and protect the prophet of God.

**The Moral Outcry: Killing a Man for Faith Alone**

The believer opens his intervention by exposing the grotesque moral bankruptcy of state execution: *a-taqtulūna rajulan a yaqūla Rabbī Allāh*—will you kill a man only for saying, “My Lord is Allah”? The believer highlights the irrational cruelty of persecuting someone purely for professing monotheism, echoing Sūrah al-Burūj: *“And they resented them not except because they believed in Allah, the Exalted in Might, the Praiseworthy”* (85:8). With exquisite diplomatic subtlety, the speaker introduces the phrase "your Lord" (*Rabbikum*), gently prompting the courtiers to consider that the God Moses proclaims is their own Creator.

**The Prophetic Echo in the Courtyard of the Kaʿbah**

The dramatic moral challenge expressed by the Believer of Pharaoh’s house was directly reenacted during the darkest hours of early Islam in Makkah. In a celebrated tradition recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3856*, ʿUrwah ibn al-Zubayr asked ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr to describe the most violent physical assault perpetrated by the pagan Quraysh against the Prophet ﷺ. He recounted that while the Messenger of Allah ﷺ was prostrating in the courtyard of the Kaʿbah, the pagan aristocrat ʿUqbah ibn Abī Muʿayṭ approached, wrapped his thick cloak violently around the Prophet’s neck, and began choking him with lethal force. Suddenly, Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq rushed forward, seized ʿUqbah by the shoulder, hurled him away from the Prophet. Cried out with tears streaming down his face, quoting this exact verse: “Will you kill a man only for saying, ‘My Lord is Allah,’ while he has come to you with clear proofs from your Lord?” Abū Bakr stepped into the heroic shoes of the Egyptian believer, risking his life to defend prophetic truth against mob violence.

**What the Verse Introduces**
The verse brings in the man from the family of Pharaoh who believed, and it gives his first speech: he asks the men whether they will kill a man for saying that his Lord is God when he has brought clear proofs from his Lord, and he warns them that if he is lying the lie is on him, and if he is telling the truth then some of what he promised will reach them. The Book then names the consequence for the men who go too far: God does not guide the man who is extravagant and a liar. The verse is the beginning of the sūrah's central passage, and it is the longest speech by any figure in the chapter.
**What the Believer Argues**
The man's argument is not about miracles but about risk, and the Book gives it in the plainest form: either the man is lying and the harm falls on him, or he is telling the truth and the punishment for opposing him will arrive. The Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān records the same logic in the words of the men of the Book who weighed their options (3:199), and the Sūrah of the Cave gives the same reasoning in the parable of the two men. The passage in front of us takes the argument to the powerful men who were deciding a man's life, which is where the Book usually puts it hardest.
**Where the Account Goes**
The verses that follow record the believer's warnings about the fates of the nations, his own placing of his affair with God, and the protection given to him while the family of Pharaoh was surrounded by the punishment (40:44-46). The sūrah then turns to Moses and the signs. The man is not named in the Book and the reports give him no certain name, which the commentators took as the point of the passage: the one person in the palace who spoke up for the truth is described by his faith rather than by his house.

## Verse 40:29

> O my people! Authority belongs to you today, reigning supreme in the land. But who would help us against the torment of Allah, if it were to befall us?” Pharaoh assured ˹his people˺, “I am telling you only what I believe, and I am leading you only to the way of guidance.”

**The Diplomatic Address: Solidarity and Vulnerability**

The twenty-ninth verse continues the remarkable speech of the Believer of Pharaoh’s household, illustrating his profound rhetorical diplomacy and pastoral concern: *yā qawmi lakumu al-mulku al-yawma ẓāhirīna fī al-arḍ*—O my people! Authority belongs to you today, reigning supreme in the land. The speaker addresses the assembled courtiers with intimate communal affection: *yā qawm* ("O my people!"). He does not speak as an alienated dissident or a foreign critic hurling insults from outside; he speaks as a devoted insider, an aristocratic kinsman whose own destiny is tied to the survival of Egypt. He begins by graciously validating their political achievements: they are *ẓāhirīn*, reigning supreme over the fertile Nile valley and commanding vast military and administrative supremacy. Yet his inclusion of the temporal qualification *al-yawm* ("today") carries a subtle, brilliant warning. Worldly dominance is inherently ephemeral; the empire that reigns supreme today may easily find itself pulverized tomorrow if it oversteps the moral boundaries established by the Sovereign of existence. Feeling the logical and moral force of the believer's argument swaying the council, Pharaoh abruptly interrupts to reassert control: *qāla Firʿawnu mā urīkum illā mā arā*—Pharaoh assured his people, “I am telling you only what I believe, and I am leading you only to the way of guidance” (40:29). Pharaoh declares that his personal perception (*mā arā*) is the sole authorized window through which the nation is permitted to view reality.

**The Cynical Fabrication: The Way of Guidance**

The monarch seals his authoritarian pronouncement with a staggering claim to moral leadership: *wa-mā ahdīkum illā sabīla al-rashād*—and I am leading you only to the way of guidance. This assertion represents the absolute apex of political hubris. In Pharaoh's imperial lexicon, *sabīl al-rashād* ("the path of rectitude and flourishing") meant executing prophetic reformers, slaughtering nursing infants, and forcing human beings to prostrate before the golden statues of the royal dynasty. The Qur'an systematically dismantles this despotic boast, recording the definitive divine verdict in Sūrah Ṭā Hā: *“And Pharaoh led his people astray and did not guide them”* (20:79), and confirming in Sūrah Hūd: *“and the command of Pharaoh was not right”* (11:97). The tyrant poses as a wise shepherd leading his flock to green pastures, while in reality he is driving them directly toward the precipice of drowning and eternal ruin, masking destruction behind the vocabulary of enlightenment.

**Internal Denial and Willful Blindness**

The commentators, notably Ibn Kathīr, investigated the psychological state behind Pharaoh's confident rhetoric, examining whether the king genuinely believed his own words or was engaged in conscious political theater. The Qur'an provides the definitive answer in Sūrah al-Naml: *“And they rejected them, while their inner selves were convinced of them, in injustice and haughtiness”* (27:14). Pharaoh's confident dismissal of Moses was not born of innocent philosophical disagreement; his own soul was secretly terrified and convinced by the undeniable miracles he had witnessed.

## Verse 40:30

> And the man who believed cautioned, “O my people! I truly fear for you the doom of ˹earlier˺ enemy forces—

**The Man Stands Up a Second Time**
*Wa-qāla alladhī āmana* — and the man who believed spoke again. The verse before it ends with Pharaoh claiming that he only guides his people to the right path (40:29). A ruler has just said the last word in his own court. What happens next is that a member of his household speaks over him, and speaks gently: *yā qawm* — my people. The sūrah gives him no name, and the tradition reports only that he was a believer from the family of Pharaoh who kept his faith hidden.
**The Warning**
He does not argue about Pharaoh's claim. He tells them what has happened to other peoples who took the same road: the like of the case of the people of Noah, and ʿĀd, and Thamūd, and those who came after them (7:59; 38:12). Then he names the day they will meet it, the Day of the Summoning, when they will turn and run with no one to shield them (40:32-33). The argument is historical rather than abstract. Every nation he names was stronger than the people listening to him, and every one of them was gone.
**Why He Keeps Speaking**
The courtier has nothing to gain. He is inside the system he is warning, and what he expects for himself is plainly stated a few verses later: he places his affairs with God (40:44). The sūrah places him in the middle of the account of Moses for this reason, and it lets the one man with the least protection give the longest speech in the chapter — the family of Pharaoh contains both the tyrant and his conscience, and the Book takes care to show who was really alone.

## Verse 40:31

> like the fate of the people of Noah, ’Ȃd, Thamûd, and those after them. For Allah would never will to wrong ˹His˺ servants

**The Fate of the Nations Before**

The thirty-first verse provides the concrete historical specification to the general warning of the "day of the confederacies" expressed in the previous verse: *mithla daʾbi qawmi Nūḥin wa-ʿĀdin wa-Thamūda wa-alladhīna min baʿdihim*—like the fate of the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and those after them. In the old Arabic dictionaries, the noun *daʾb* means habitual conduct, persistent custom, entrenched lifestyle, and the resultant fate or outcome that naturally flows from that behavior. The courtier enumerates the classic triad of ancient destroyed nations whose memories were deeply etched into Near Eastern historical consciousness. He begins with the people of Noah, who were submerged by the universal deluge after a millennium of mocking their prophet. Proceeds to ʿĀd, the towering builders of Iram who were crushed by the screaming arctic tempest; and culminates in Thamūd, the rock-carvers of al-Ḥijr whose proud civilization was shattered by a single seismic blast. By adding *wa-alladhīna min baʿdihim* ("and those after them"), he encompasses the overturned cities of Lot and the vanished merchants of Madyan, confronting the Egyptian council with an exhaustive historical catalogue of divine justice.

**The Absolute Negation of Divine Injustice**

The courtier immediately follows this terrifying historical review with a fundamental religious axiom: *wa-mā Allāhu yurīdu ẓulman li-l-ʿibād*—for Allah would never will to wrong His servants. In Arabic syntax, the negative particle *mā* combined with the present-tense verb *yurīdu* ("He wills" or "He desires") delivers a sweeping, absolute negation. God does not merely refrain from executing injustice; He does not even have the desire or inclination to wrong His creatures. In Qur'anic theology, *al-ẓulm* means placing something outside its proper place, violating rights, withholding earned rewards, or punishing the innocent. Because God is the Self-Sufficient Lord of absolute perfection, possessing infinite majesty and needing nothing from creation, injustice is fundamentally impossible in His divine nature (3:108). The destruction of ancient civilizations was never an act of malicious cruelty; it was the rigorous, equitable vindication of moral order, punishing unrepentant oppressors and rescuing the righteous.

**The Theological Implication of Divine Will (Irādah)**

Classical theologians extensively examined this verse as a primary foundation for understanding divine justice (*al-ʿAdl*). Sūrah Hūd confirms this principle about the ancient confederacies: *“And We did not wrong them, but they wronged themselves”* (11:101).

## Verse 40:32

> O my people! I truly fear for you the Day all will be crying out ˹to each other˺—

**The Linguistic Anatomy of Yawm al-Tanād**

In the old Arabic dictionaries, the Last-Day title *Yawm al-Tanād* is derived from the verbal roots *munādāh* and *tanādī*, meaning the act of calling out loudly, shouting across distances, and engaging in desperate, reciprocal cries. Classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, noted that the Day of Resurrection is designated by this title because it is a day saturated with auditory chaos, desperate appeals, reciprocal shouting, and judicial summonses.

**The Reciprocal Calling Across the Cosmic Partition**

The primary interpretive dimension of *al-tanād* explored in classical tafsīr focuses upon the dramatic, reciprocal cries exchanged between the inhabitants of Paradise and the inmates of Hell across the cosmic barrier (*al-Aʿrāf*). In Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, the scripture unveils this dialogue with terrifying clarity: the blessed souls in the Garden call out across the partition to the condemned, asking: *“We have found what our Lord promised us to be true. Have you found what your Lord promised to be true?”* to which the damned miserably respond, *“Yes”* (7:44). Later, as the flames consume them and excruciating thirst sets in, the inhabitants of the Fire scream out across the chasm to the righteous, pleading: *“Pour down upon us some water or some of what Allah has provided you!”* only to receive the crushing, unalterable reply: *“Indeed, Allah has forbidden them both to the disbelievers”* (7:50). The title captures the agony of these futile shouts across eternity. The physical distance between the two realms reflects an unbridgeable metaphysical divide created by choices made during earthly life, leaving the damned to cry in hopeless desolation.

**The Desperate Cries of Hypocrites and Deniers**

A second poignant manifestation of *al-tanād* occurs during the terrifying passage across the darkness of the resurrection plain. In Sūrah al-Ḥadīd, the Qur'an portrays the desperate cries of those who compromised their faith for worldly prestige: *“The hypocrite men and hypocrite women will call out to those who believed, ‘Wait for us that we may acquire some of your light!’”* (57:13). A towering wall with an impenetrable gate will be slammed shut between them, isolating the hypocrites in darkness while the believers proceed in radiant light.

**The Angelic Summons and the Call of the Ledger**

Classical scholars observed that the Day of Mutual Calling also refers to the formal judicial heralds who summon individual souls before the Supreme Judge. Traditional transmissions depict celestial angels calling out every mortal human being by name, lineage, and deeds: "Where is so-and-so, the child of so-and-so? Step forth to present your account before your Lord!" In that overwhelming assembly, every individual will hear their personal summons echoed across the cosmos, leaving no room for concealment or escape. For the righteous, the calling is an occasion of sublime joy, as the angels herald their triumph: *“This is the Paradise you have inherited for what you used to do”* (7:43). For the unrepentant oppressor, however, the angelic herald is the terrifying beginning of eternal exposure, summoning the soul to confront the ledger of its crimes.

## Verse 40:33

> the Day you will ˹try in vain to˺ turn your backs and run away, with no one to protect you from Allah. And whoever Allah leaves to stray will be left with no guide

**The Futile Impulse of Flight**

The thirty-third verse continues the harrowing description of the Day of Mutual Calling, depicting the instinctive, panicked reaction of the resurrected: *yawma tuwallūna mudbirīn*—the Day you will try in vain to turn your backs and run away. The classical Arabic active participle *mudbirīn*, derived from *idbār* (turning one's back and fleeing in headlong retreat), captures the ultimate physical somatic expression of apocalyptic terror. In Sūrah al-Qiyāmah, the scripture expresses this universal panic: *“Man will say on that Day, ‘Where is the escape?’ No! There is no refuge! Unto your Lord that Day is the place of settlement”* (75:10–12).

**The Social Dissolution: Fleeing from One's Own Kin**

Classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, observed that this desperate flight is not only a physical retreat from the visual horror of the Fire, but an agonizing psychological flight from one's own kinsmen. In earthly crises—whether earthquakes, floods, or warfare—human families instinctively draw together for mutual comfort and physical solidarity. In the Last-Day reckoning, however, the terror is so immense that all natural human affection dissolves into sheer self-preservation. Sūrah ʿAbasa depicts this absolute social collapse: *“On the Day a man will flee from his brother, and his mother and his father, and his wife and his children”* (80:34–36).

**The Total Absence of Divine Shielding (ʿĀṣim)**

The verse proclaims the absolute impossibility of external defense: *mā lakum mina Allāhi min ʿāṣim*—with no one to protect you from Allah. In the old Arabic dictionaries, an *ʿāṣim* is an infallible protector, a fortified citadel, a high mountain sanctuary, or an unyielding patron capable of sheltering a fugitive from impending destruction. The Believer of Pharaoh’s household reminds the imperial council of the primordial lesson taught during the great deluge of antiquity. When Noah called his rebellious son to board the ark of salvation, the youth proudly declared: *“I will seek refuge on a mountain to protect me from the water,”* to which Noah issued the timeless reply: *“There is no protector (lā ʿāṣim) today from the decree of Allah except for whom He has had mercy”* (11:43). Pharaoh’s courtiers were reminded that before the sovereign decree of the Creator, all Egyptian stone fortresses, military legions, and pagan deities offered zero protective shielding.

**The Judicial Sealing of Misguidance (Iḍlāl)**

The narrative concludes with an essential religious principle of divine governance: *wa-man yuḍlili Allāhu fa-mā lahu min hād*—and whoever Allah leaves to stray will be left with no guide. In Qur'anic theology, divine misguidance (*al-iḍlāl*) is never an arbitrary or malicious act imposed upon an innocent, truth-seeking heart. Rather, it represents the just, retributive consequence of persistent moral rebellion. When an individual repeatedly witnesses manifest divine signs, understands rational proofs, and yet stubbornly chooses to silence their conscience, suppress the truth, and persecute prophetic reformers, God withdraws His protective grace (*tawfīq*). The rebellious soul is judicially abandoned to the delusions of its own ego. As Sūrah al-Aʿrāf confirms: *“Whoever Allah sends astray—there is no guide for him”* (7:186). Once divine guidance is forfeited through persistent arrogance, no created entity can illuminate the darkened heart.

Sūrah al-Kahf diagnoses this tragic condition: *“And whom He leaves to stray—you will never find for him a protecting guide”* (18:17).

## Verse 40:34

> Joseph already came to you earlier with clear proofs, yet you never ceased to doubt what he came to you with. When he died you said, ‘Allah will never send a messenger after him.’ This is how Allah leaves every transgressor and doubter to stray—

**Joseph Had Come Before**
The believer speaking to Pharaoh's court reaches for the one thing the court cannot dismiss: their own history. *Wa-laqad jāʾakum Yūsufu min qablu bi-al-bayyināt* — Joseph came to you before this with clear proofs. The reference is not vague. Joseph had lived in Egypt, had come out of a prison to take charge of the land's stores, and had carried the country through seven barren years (12:55). And what did he bring? He called his own people to the one God with exactly the question the believer has been putting to the court: are separate lords better, or God, the One, the Prevailing? (12:39).
**What the Court Did With Him**
The verse does not leave the comparison at Joseph's success; it gives the sequel, which is the part the men in the hall would rather not hear. *And you did not cease to be in doubt about what he brought, until he died; then you said, God will not send a messenger after him* — that is how God leaves the extravagant and the doubtful to stray. A whole generation heard the message, kept arguing about it, and then settled the matter at Joseph's death by deciding that no messenger would come again. Then came Moses, and the town did to him what it had done with the first messenger: the same arguments, the same labels, the same confidence that the story was finished. The believer's argument is that the present court is not being asked to do anything new. It is being asked to stop repeating what its own archives record Egyptian rulers doing twice.

## Verse 40:35

> those who dispute Allah’s signs with no proof given to them. How despicable is that for Allah and the believers! This is how Allah seals the heart of every arrogant tyrant.”

**The Argument With No Authority**
*Alladhīna yujādilūna fī āyāti Allāhi bi-ghayri sulṭānin atāhum* — those who argue about the signs of God with no authority that has come to them. The verse names the crime in the sūrah's own vocabulary. The word *sulṭān* is the proof a person can produce, and the verse says plainly that these people are arguing without one. The sūrah strikes the same note three more times: the argument against God's signs appears at 40:4, at 40:56, and again at 40:69, and each time the lack of evidence is part of the charge. Al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī and al-Zamakhsharī all treat the phrase as the definition of the offence, and it is the same offence the Book records in the men of Makkah, who asked for proof and then argued when it came.
**What God Thinks of It**
The sentence then states the judgement: it is greatly hated by God and by those who believe. The word for hatred is *maqt*, the same word the sūrah used for the hatred the disbelievers would discover on the Day (40:10), and the verse turns it around to name what God detests. The second half of the clause is worth noticing: the believers also hate it. A community that has been given evidence is meant to find the manufacture of arguments distasteful, and the Book puts the two hatreds in one line rather than treating the verdict as a private matter between God and the arguer.
**The Sealing of the Heart**
The verse closes with the consequence: thus God seals the heart of every arrogant tyrant. The Sūrah of the Cow describes the same sealing, on hearts and hearing and eyes (2:7), and the commentators explain the order: the person chooses the argument, persists in it, and the seal is the result rather than the beginning. The Book's name for that state elsewhere is that the heart is covered over, and the case in this sūrah is the man who had everything explained and preferred his own account. Bukhārī and Muslim report the Prophet's warning that God hates the stubborn disputer, which is the same picture in a sentence.

## Verse 40:36

> Pharaoh ordered, “O Hamân! Build me a high tower so I may reach the pathways

**The Tyrant Changes the Subject**
For seven verses the believer of Pharaoh's household had been speaking (40:28–35): he answered the charge of lying, rehearsed how earlier nations were destroyed, warned of the Day of Mutual Calling, and named the disease of the court. Pharaoh was left with nothing to say. So he turns to his minister and orders a tower. *Yā Hāmān* — kindle me a fire on the clay and build me a high structure, so that I may reach the pathways (28:38). This is the move of a man who has lost the argument: when you cannot answer the case, produce a spectacle. The building project was ordered to make the council forget what had just been said, and to remind everyone who held the treasury.
**Hāmān and the Technical Servant**
The order is given to the administrator, not to the army, and the sūrah does this deliberately. Hāmān stands for the whole class of skilled men who serve a tyrant — the engineer, the accountant, the official — and ask no question about what the work is for. A dictator cannot build anything alone; he needs experts who will place their craft at his disposal. In verse 38 of al-Qaṣaṣ the order is for bricks and a tower, and thousands of labourers pay for the vanity of one man. Craft without conscience is what makes that possible.
**The Pathways He Meant to Reach**
*Laʿallī ablughu al-asbāb* — so that I may reach the *asbāb*. The word means ropes, ways, connections, the means by which one thing reaches another (22:15). Pharaoh pictures the sky as something with doors and ladders, a place a builder might climb to if the ramp were long enough. The man who had said in front of his court that he knew of no god for them but himself (40:29) is now shown scrabbling at the roof of the world with bricks. A strange end for a man who insisted he owned everything.
**What the Verse Is Showing**
The verse was placed here to make verse 35 visible: this is how God seals the heart of every arrogant tyrant (40:35). The sealing is not a mystery. It is what the scene shows — a man confronted with truth, who cannot take it in, and who answers with building works. The best description of a closed mind is not a lecture on the subject but a demonstration like this one: a king with an empire, swinging a hammer at the sky.

## Verse 40:37

> leading up to the heavens and look for the God of Moses, although I am sure he is a liar.” And so Pharaoh’s evil deeds were made so appealing to him that he was hindered from the ˹Right˺ Way. But the plotting of Pharaoh was only in vain

**Reach Up Through the Sky**

The thirty-seventh verse completes Pharaoh's megalomaniacal architectural decree begun in the previous verse: *asbāba al-samāwāti fa-aṭṭaliʿa ilā ilāhi Mūsā*—leading up to the heavens and look for the God of Moses... The monarch reveals the grotesque, literalist materialism that defined Egyptian pagan theology. Pharaoh imagines that by ascending a monumental staircase of baked mud-brick, he will be able to lean over a parapet and physically look upon the God of Moses as if peering at an elevated celestial neighbor. This pagan anthropomorphism stands in staggering contrast to the spiritual encounter granted to Moses on Mount Sinai. When Moses longed for a vision of divine glory, he pleaded with profound humility: *“My Lord, show me [Yourself] that I may look at You”* (7:143), only to fall unconscious when divine light shattered the mountain. Moses recognized that God is the infinite, uncontained Creator before whom creation dissolves; Pharaoh, blinded by pagan idolatry, imagined God to be a finite physical object residing on an upper shelf of the sky. The monarch immediately confesses the bad-faith cynicism underlying his architectural project: *wa-innī la-aẓunnuhu kādhibā*—although I am sure he is a liar, or “truly I deem him a liar.” This brief clause completely exposes Pharaoh's scientific posturing as a fraudulent political charade. He knew that when he climbed to the top of his tower and saw only empty clouds, he would descend and theatrically proclaim to the Egyptian masses: "Behold, I searched the heavens and found no God of Moses, proving that he is a liar!" His investigation was an engineered stunt designed to ratify a pre-ordained political conclusion.

**The Seductive Delusion: Evil Made Appealing**

The narrative unveils the profound psychological and spiritual pathology that ensnared the monarch: *wa-kadhālika zuyyina li-Firʿawna sūʾu ʿamalihi*—and so Pharaoh’s evil deeds were made so appealing to him. In Qur'anic moral psychology, *tazyīn* (the beautification and adornment of evil) represents the most advanced stage of moral degeneration. Through a combination of unchecked narcissism, the constant flattery of sycophantic courtiers who praised every royal whim as divine genius, and Satanic deception (16:63), Pharaoh lost the capacity for objective self-evaluation. His grotesque crimes—the drowning of newborn boys, the enslavement of populations, and his absurd architectural farce—appeared to his distorted mind as noble acts of statesmanship and civilizational glory. Sūrah Fāṭir expresses this fatal human delusion: *“Is he whose evil deed is made appealing to him so that he considers it good [like one who is guided]?”* (35:8).

**The Inevitable Ruin: Kayd in Tabāb**

The verse delivers an unsparing, definitive verdict upon the ultimate fate of all pharaonic conspiracies: *wa-mā kaydu Firʿawna illā fī tabāb*—but the plotting of Pharaoh was only in vain, leading to naught but ruin. In the old Arabic dictionaries, the noun *tabāb* means catastrophic loss, total disintegration, bankruptcy, and annihilation, carrying the same devastating resonance found in the opening of Sūrah al-Masad: *“May the hands of Abū Lahab be ruined, and ruined is he!”* (111:1).

## Verse 40:38

> And the man who believed urged, “O my people! Follow me, ˹and˺ I will lead you to the Way of Guidance

**The Direct Rebuttal to Pharaoh's Claim**

The courtier delivers a direct, electrifying rhetorical counter-strike against the monarch: *ittabiʿūnī ahdikum sabīla al-rashād*—follow me, and I will lead you to the Way of Guidance. The commentators, including al-Zamakhsharī and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, highlighted the astonishing political audacity of this statement. In verse 29, Pharaoh had arrogantly boasted to the identical council: *“I am leading you only to the way of guidance”* (40:29). The believer now takes that exact imperial phrase—*sabīl al-rashād*—and forcefully strips it from the hands of the monarch, claiming it exclusively for the monotheistic summons of Moses. In the context of an absolute autocracy where the king claimed divinity and absolute legislative infallibility, publicly declaring that the king was leading the nation toward ruin while offering oneself as an alternative moral guide was an act of breathtaking bravery that bordered on capital treason, shattering the court's sycophantic consensus and demonstrating unyielding fidelity to truth.

**The Semantic Depth of Sabīl al-Rashād**

In the old Arabic dictionaries, *al-rashād* is derived from the root *r-sh-d*, which means mature intellectual discernment, spiritual integrity, moral rectitude, and the practical attainment of ultimate goodness. It stands in diametrical opposition to *al-ghayy* (deliberate transgression, blindness, and ruin). Sūrah al-Aʿrāf describes the tragic psychological calcification of the arrogant about this path: *“If they see the way of righteousness (sabīl al-rushd), they will not adopt it as a way; but if they see the way of error, they will adopt it as a way”* (7:146). The believer clarifies that *sabīl al-rashād* is not a physical highway across the desert, nor is it an engineering ramp constructed of baked clay to reach atmospheric clouds. It is the inward spiritual highway of pure monotheism, social justice, and humble obedience to the Lord of the worlds, leading the soul into eternal security and shielding the community from divine wrath.

**Exposing the True Misleader of the Nation**

The believer's proclamation forced the imperial council to confront an inescapable binary choice between two opposing leaders. On one side stood Pharaoh, the despotic monarch who claimed to guide them to rectitude while actively leading them into moral madness, child slaughter, and divine wrath, as the Qur'an confirms in Sūrah Ṭā Hā: *“And Pharaoh led his people astray and did not guide them”* (20:79). On the other side stood the Believer, the enlightened statesman who pointed them toward the eternal sanctuary of divine forgiveness.

## Verse 40:39

> O my people! This worldly life is only ˹a fleeting˺ enjoyment, whereas the Hereafter is truly the home of settlement

**The Ephemeral Mirage: Matāʿ**

The thirty-ninth verse expresses a profound metaphysical diagnosis of the human condition, as the Believer of Pharaoh’s household exposes the fatal delusion that blinds imperial dynasties: *yā qawmi innamā hādhihi al-ḥayātu al-dunyā matāʿ*—O my people! This worldly life is only a fleeting enjoyment... In the old Arabic dictionaries, the noun *matāʿ* means temporary provisions gathered by a traveler for a journey—a flask of water, a travel pouch, or an utensil that is used briefly and inevitably wears out. The restrictive particle *innamā* ("only" or "merely") reduces all the glittering monuments, vast agricultural riches, and imperial prestige of Egypt to this single descriptive category: transient utility. The Qur'an regularly unmasks the seductive illusions of mortality, designating earthly existence as *matāʿ al-ghurūr*—the enjoyment of delusion (3:185; 57:20). Worldly treasures, military ranks, and royal titles are not permanent possessions; they are temporary tools provided for an existential test, destined to dissolve like morning mist before the dawn of eternity, leaving the soul with nothing but the moral harvest of its deeds and the quality of its faith.

**The Hadith of the Traveler in the Shade**

The courtier's evaluation of earthly transience reflects the precise spiritual orientation showed by the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ. In an authentic tradition recorded in *Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2377* on the authority of ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, the Companion recounted that the Prophet ﷺ awoke from sleep with the rough patterns of a straw reed mat deeply etched into his skin. Moved by compassion, Ibn Masʿūd said: "O Messenger of Allah, will you allow us to prepare a soft mattress for you to sleep upon?" The Prophet ﷺ replied with profound metaphysical detachment: “What have I and this world to do with one another? The likeness of me and this world is nothing but that of a traveling rider who seeks shade beneath a tree on a scorching day, rests for a brief hour. Then departs, leaving the tree behind.” This prophetic image captures the exact truth the Believer sought to impress upon his Egyptian peers: their opulent palaces along the Nile were merely temporary shade trees under which mortal riders rested for a brief hour before departing.

**The Abode of Eternal Settlement (Dār al-Qarār)**

The courtier contrasts the fleeting mirage of mortality with ultimate Last-Day reality: *wa-inna al-ākhirata hiya dāru al-qarār*—whereas the Hereafter is truly the home of settlement. In Arabic, the noun *qarār* means unshakeable permanence, rooted tranquility, and an enduring resting place that experiences neither decay, nor eviction, nor anxiety. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt affirms this reality: *“And indeed, the home of the Hereafter—that is the true life, if only they knew”* (29:64). In earthly existence, every human joy is perpetually shadowed by the dread of loss, illness, aging, and death; the wealthiest monarch lives in constant anxiety of palace coups, foreign invasions, and biological demise. In the Hereafter alone does the human soul enter *Dār al-Qarār*—the permanent home where joy is unmarred by fear, life is untouched by death, and the righteous dwell in unceasing security in the presence of their Lord, liberated from the fleeting disappointments of mortality.

## Verse 40:40

> Whoever does an evil deed will only be paid back with its equivalent. And whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, they will enter Paradise, where they will be provided for without limit

**Nothing Beyond the Deed**
*Man ʿamila sayyiʾatan fa-lā yujzā illā mithlahā* — whoever does an evil deed is repaid with nothing but its like. The sentence is a rule of proportion, and it is stated twice in the Book with the same care: whoever brings an evil deed is recompensed only with its like, and they are not wronged (6:160). No penalty in that court is inflated, no bad deed becomes two, and no sentence is handed down for something other than what the hand did. The verse puts the rule first because everything else in it depends on the fairness of the ledger.
**The Other Half of the Rule**
The same sentence then gives the reward: *and whoever does a righteous deed, male or female, and is a believer, those will enter the Garden, provided without measure*. The symmetry breaks exactly here, and deliberately. Punishment is measured; reward is not. The Book says the same in the sūrah of the Cattle, where the good deed comes back tenfold while the evil deed comes back once (6:160). A man who reads only the first clause would think of God as an accountant; the verse gives him the second so that he does not. The Book states the measure of the increase elsewhere in round numbers - two hundredfold for a grain of charity sown in good soil (2:261), ten times for a good deed (6:160), and the promise that God will double and multiply for whoever comes with belief and good work (4:40). Justice is the floor of the arrangement, generosity is the ceiling, and the human being has to hold both when he thinks about what he has done.
**Why the Verse Names Men and Women**
The clause *male or female* is the sūrah's way of settling a question the reader might carry in without noticing it. The promise of the Garden rests on the deed and the faith, and the verse states the two sexes explicitly so that nobody carries away the impression that the account is kept differently in two columns. The verse before this one had named the groups the sūrah has been arguing against — the man who brings the truth and the one who affirms it — and this one widens to everyone who acts. The same clause recurs across the Book as the sūrahs settle the question directly: male and female believers whose deeds are righteous are promised a good life (16:97), they are among the people of the Garden (4:124), they are written into the answer God gives the supplicants (3:195), and they are named in the long list of the faithful (33:35).

## Verse 40:41

> O my people! How is it that I invite you to salvation, while you invite me to the Fire

**The Agonized Cry of Astonishment**

The forty-first verse records an intensely personal, sorrowful exclamation from the Believer of Pharaoh’s household: *wa-yā qawmi mā lī adʿūkum ilā al-najāti wa-tadʿūnanī ilā al-nār*—O my people! How is it that I invite you to salvation, while you invite me to the Fire! The interrogative particle *mā lī* ("how is it with me," or "what is the matter with this situation") expresses profound bewilderment, grief, and intellectual protest. The courtier steps out of formal religious lecturing to express the tragic, irrational absurdity confronting him. He had showered his people with compassionate counsel, showed diplomatic goodwill, and appealed to their reason and historical memory. Yet instead of reciprocal goodwill, he finds himself surrounded by colleagues who actively try to drag him into spiritual destruction. His cry reflects the profound emotional pain of a sincere reformer whose overflowing benevolence is met with lethal hostility, exposing an existential breach between divine truth and political obstinacy. He watches with sorrow as his beloved people blindly rush toward their own demise, ignoring the luminous signs of divine truth.

**The Loneliness of the Prophetic Caller**

The dynamic captured in this verse forms a recurring archetype throughout prophetic history. Every messenger of God and righteous reformer experiences the bitter paradox of offering selfless mercy only to be met with aggressive resistance. The Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ encountered the identical tragic blindness among the Quraysh of Mecca, who repaid his compassionate concern with economic boycotts, physical violence, and assassination plots. In Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, the Prophet Ṣāliḥ lamented to the ruined remnants of Thamūd: *“O my people, I had certainly conveyed to you the message of my Lord and advised you, but you do not like advisers”* (7:79). Truth-telling in an unjust society inevitably generates profound existential loneliness, as the solitary caller stands between a merciful God and a self-destructive society determined to extinguish the light that seeks to save it, enduring alienation for the sake of divine principle.

## Verse 40:42

> You invite me to disbelieve in Allah and associate with Him what I have no knowledge of, while I invite you to the Almighty, Most Forgiving

**The Epistemology of Shirk: Ignorance and Delusion**

The forty-second verse exposes the profound intellectual and epistemological bankruptcy of polytheism through the incisive words of the Egyptian courtier: *tadʿūnanī li-akfura bi-llāhi wa-ushrika bihi mā laysa lī bihi ʿilm*—You invite me to disbelieve in Allah and associate with Him what I have no knowledge of... Classical commentators emphasized that the believer does not merely critique polytheism as a moral transgression; he unmasks it as an intellectual absurdity. In Islamic account of knowledge, legitimate religious belief must be grounded in either conclusive rational demonstration (*burhān*) or authentic divine revelation (*waḥy*). Associating partners with God has neither. The phrase *mā laysa lī bihi ʿilm* ("that of which I have no knowledge") indicates that false deities have no of being reality, no verifiable proof, and no demonstrable efficacy. Polytheism is born of baseless speculation, ancestral imitation (*taqlīd*), and psychological projection. This principle mirrors the sacred counsel given across generations of prophetic wisdom, such as Luqmān’s admonition to his son: *“And if they strive to make you associate with Me that of which you have no knowledge, do not obey them”* (31:15; cf. 22:71). Blindly accepting inherited religious customs without demanding evidentiary proof leads directly to intellectual slavery and spiritual ruin.

**The Divine Name Al-Ghaffār: Inexhaustible Pardon**

Alongside supreme might, the believer reveals the breathtaking tenderness of divine mercy: *Al-Ghaffār*—the Most Forgiving. In Arabic grammar, *Al-Ghaffār* is an intensive form (*sighat mubālaghah*) meaning continuous, repetitive, and all-encompassing pardon. It means a Lord who forgives the most catastrophic transgressions time and time again when the sinner turns in sincere repentance. This inclusion illustrates the astonishing magnanimity of the prophetic summons. Despite centuries of pharaonic oppression, the systematic murder of newborn Hebrew children, and the blasphemous arrogance of the Egyptian court, the gate of divine mercy remained wide open. Moses and the Believer did not enter the palace seeking vengeance; they came as ambassadors of reconciliation, offering the ruling class an opportunity to step away from the brink of destruction and immerse themselves in the boundless ocean of divine grace (40:3).

## Verse 40:43

> There is no doubt that whatever ˹idols˺ you invite me to ˹worship˺ are not worthy to be invoked either in this world or the Hereafter. ˹Undoubtedly,˺ our return is to Allah, and the transgressors will be the inmates of the Fire

**The Sentence the Believer Delivers**
*Lā jarama annamā tadʿūnanī ilayhi laysa lahu daʿwatun fī al-dunyā wa-lā fī al-ākhirah* — no doubt that what you call me to has no claim at all, in this world or the next. The man from the family of Pharaoh has listened to the offer and given his verdict in one sentence. The phrase *lā jarama* is the strongest form of certainty in the language, and he uses it to state that the things they worship have no case to make anywhere. Then he states the two facts the whole argument turns on: the return is to God, and those who go beyond the limits are the companions of the Fire.
**What the Idols Can and Cannot Do**
The Book presses the same point in the Sūrah of the Creator, where the idols are told that those who call on them cannot hear the call, and even if they heard they could not answer (35:14). The Sūrah of the Sand Dunes makes the comparison with a man calling on something that will not answer until the Day of Judgment (46:5). The verse in front of us states the same thing from the legal side: a god who has no claim in this world and no claim in the next is not a god, and no plea offered to him can be heard. The believer is not arguing about the size of the idols. He is pointing out that the court is not in session for them.
**The Word for the People Who Go Too Far**
He calls them *musrifūn* — those who go beyond the limit — which is the sūrah's own word for Pharaoh, used at the beginning when Moses is told that his king has transgressed (40:24; 40:43). The verse before this one ends by saying the same thing about the Fire, and the Sūrah of Jonah states the return to God in the same phrasing (10:4). The believer has been speaking to his own people since verse 28 and has been warned that they plan to kill him, and his last argument is the simplest one available: what you are trusting has no answer to give you, and the One you have forgotten does.

## Verse 40:44

> You will remember what I say to you, and I entrust my affairs to Allah. Surely Allah is All-Seeing of all ˹His˺ servants.”

**The Prophetic Premonition of Regret: Fa-Sa-Tadhkurūn**

The forty-fourth verse records the dramatic conclusion of the Believer’s monumental address to Pharaoh's imperial council: *fa-sa-tadhkurūna mā aqūlu lakum*—You will remember what I say to you... In Arabic grammar, the future particle *sa-* attached to the verb *tadhkurūn* means absolute certainty of future realization. The courtier observed the hardened faces, sullen silence, and hostile glances of his royal colleagues. Recognizing that their hearts had calcified against rational proof and divine counsel, he delivers a chilling, prophetic premonition. He tells them that a day of agonizing remembrance is inevitable.

**Tafwīḍ as the Sanctuary Against Fear**

The believer’s posture provides an enduring spiritual blueprint for believers confronting oppressive systems and existential peril across all eras. When an individual stands up for truth against powerful institutions, fear and anxiety naturally threaten to paralyze the human soul. The antidote revealed in this verse is authentic *tafwīḍ*. Spiritual surrender does not denote fatalistic passivity; the courtier spoke with exhaustive diplomatic skill, rigorous logic, and immense courage. However, once a servant has exhausted their righteous efforts, they must surrender the ultimate outcome to God, realizing that no tyrant can inflict harm except what the Almighty has already decreed: *“Sufficient for us is Allah. He is the best Disposer of affairs”* (3:173); *“And whoever relies upon Allah—then He is sufficient for him”* (65:3). This surrender breaks the psychological power of tyranny, replacing dread with divine tranquility and infusing the believer with invincible spiritual power that worldly weapons can never crush or intimidate. When God is one's guardian, all the hosts of earth are rendered utterly powerless.

## Verse 40:45

> So Allah protected him from the evil of their schemes. And Pharaoh’s people were overwhelmed by an evil punishment

**God Shielded Him**
*Fa-waqāhu allāhu sayyiʾāti mā makarū* — so God protected him from the evil of what they plotted. The verse is the answer to the sentence just before it, where the believer says aloud that he places his affair with God (40:44). The verb *waqā* means to shield, and here it states the outcome: the plot was real, and it did not reach him. The Book says the same thing in other words in several places, where the schemers plan and God plans, and where the plotting is described as the kind that would move mountains (8:30; 3:54; 14:46).
**What Happened to the Others**
The verse puts the two groups side by side in one sentence. The man is saved, and the family of Pharaoh is surrounded by punishment, exposed to the Fire morning and evening (40:45-46). The Book does not record a trial, an escape or a hiding place. It records the verdict, and it leaves the reader with the picture the sūrah has been building: the two men in the same palace, one safe because he lost everything, the other lost with everything still in his hand. Ibn Kathīr and al-Baghawī relate the reports that Pharaoh's people moved against the believer and that God took him anyway; the verse itself goes no further than to say who did the shielding.
**The Comfort in the Verse**
The sūrah is full of courts and arguments, and this is the one place where the argument ends in deliverance. It is placed before the account of Moses and the sea, so that the reader has already been told how the story of the palace ends before the water closes over it.

## Verse 40:46

> they are exposed to the Fire ˹in their graves˺ morning and evening. And on the Day the Hour will be established ˹it will be said˺, “Admit Pharaoh’s people into the harshest punishment ˹of Hell˺.”

**Morning and Evening in the Fire**
The verse says that the people of Pharaoh are brought in front of the fire morning and evening, and that on the day the Hour comes it will be said to the angels to admit the people of Pharaoh into the harshest punishment. The verse describes the state of these men between death and the resurrection, and then the day itself.

**What the Wording Means**
The mention of morning and evening tells the reader that this is taking place within the ordinary time of the world as it continues, because the next life has no night or day. The teachers of the community read the verse as a picture of the punishment that begins in the grave and continues until the final account. The action described is passive: the men are brought and shown the destination that waits for them, which is a torment of its own.

**The Report of the Prophet**
The Prophet described the same thing for every person in a report the two main collections carry: when one of you dies, his seat is shown to him morning and evening; if he is among the people of the garden he is shown the seat that belongs to the people of the garden, and if he is among the people of the fire he is shown the seat that belongs to them, and it is said to him that this is his place until God raises him on the day of resurrection (al-Bukhārī 1379 and Muslim 2866 preserve the report on the authority of Ibn ʿUmar). So the verse in front of the reader is describing one case of a rule that applies to every person, and the case is the family of the most famous tyrant in history.

**Why the Family of Pharaoh**
The sūrah is about men who were given power and used it to lead people away from God, and Pharaoh is the chief example in the Book. His punishment is named in the harshest terms because he had the clearest proof in front of him and refused it while his own magicians submitted. The Book says in this sūrah that he was shown the signs and denied them, and that God's punishment is severe.

**What the Verse Leaves With the Reader**
The verse leaves the reader with the fact that death does not end the account. The men who lived as their king told them to live are given a view of where that road ends before the final day arrives, and the final day adds the rest. The sūrah goes on to say what the men who were led astray will say on that day, and what the men who led them will not be able to do for them. The verse in front of the reader is the first instalment, and the sūrah names the rest.

## Verse 40:47

> ˹Consider the Day˺ when they will dispute in the Fire, and the lowly ˹followers˺ will appeal to the arrogant ˹leaders˺, “We were your ˹dedicated˺ followers, will you then shield us from a portion of the Fire?”

**The Two Groups in the Fire**
The scene opens with the two classes of a corrupted society facing each other at the end of the road: *the weak will say to those who were arrogant*. The Book has described both often. The arrogant — *alladhīna istakbarū* — are the men who ran the affairs: the court, the treasury, the religious establishment that rubber-stamped the arrangement. The weak are the people who followed: not merely the poor, but everyone who handed over his judgement to a man with a title and then stopped asking questions.
**What the Weak Say**
Their words are in the sūrah of Sheba, where the scene is drawn in full: if it were not for you, we would have been believers (34:31). The same plea is on the lips of the followers in the sūrah of the Confederates, where they say to God about their leaders, our lords and our chiefs led us astray from the path (33:67) - it is the sentence every generation of followers says when the account arrives, and the Book records it in the same form in three places — here, in Ibrāhīm (14:21), and in the sūrah of the Heights, where the followers tell their leaders that they were the ones who brought the night, and the leaders answer that they were the ones who chose it (7:38-39). Nobody in the exchange is innocent. The leader who commanded is not excused by the follower's obedience, and the follower is not excused by the leader's authority, because each of them had the same messenger and the same choice.
**The Question the Scene Puts to the Reader**
The scene is placed in the sūrah to be read in the present tense. Every society distributes the blame differently: the man in the crowd says the fault was the platform's, and the man on the platform says he only said what the crowd wanted. The verses refuse the exchange. What the reader is left with is not a description of somebody else's last day but a warning about the arithmetic of following, which is the one transaction in this world that cannot be done in someone else's name.

## Verse 40:48

> The arrogant will say, “We are all in it! ˹For˺ Allah has already passed judgment over ˹His˺ servants.”

**Parallels Across the Qur'anic Last-Day Vision**

The tragic dialogue captured in this verse forms a consistent, repeating theme across the Qur'anic depiction of the Day of Judgment. In Sūrah Ibrāhīm, the damned leaders offer the identical pathetic admission of total impotence to their followers: *“The weak will say to those who were arrogant, ‘Indeed, we were your followers. Can you avail us at all against the punishment of Allah?’ They will say, ‘If Allah had guided us, we would have guided you. It is all the same for us whether we show intolerance or are patient; there is for us no place of escape’”* (14:21). Similarly, Sūrah al-Aʿrāf records the bitter mutual curses exchanged between successive generations of corrupt rulers and their enablers as they tumble into the abyss (7:38). Scripture consistently reiterates this grim reality to break the hypnotic trance of political subservience, warning humanity that complicity with tyrants leads inevitably to a shared grave of eternal sorrow, where former allegiances transform into everlasting hatred and mutual despair.

**The Stark Reality of Shared Damnation: Innā Kullun Fīhā**

The leaders deliver an unvarnished declaration of shared catastrophe: *innā kullun fīhā*—"We are all in it!" The inclusive noun *kull* ("all" or "every single one of us") delivers a crushing psychological blow to the desperate underlings who had pleaded for protection (v. 47). In the earthly hierarchy, the elite enjoyed luxurious villas, cool gardens, and privileged status while the masses labored under the scorching sun. But within the egalitarian retribution of Hellfire, all social stratification dissolves completely. The master and the slave, the prime minister and the commoner, the dictator and the sycophant burn within the identical flames, bound in identical chains, and subjected to the identical divine wrath. The former leaders cannot lift an ounce of punishment from their followers because they are themselves completely engulfed and paralyzed by the agonizing reality of their own damnation, discovering that earthly power was an empty phantom that vanished the moment death arrived.

**The Ultimate Failure of Human Intermediaries**

This infernal exchange systematically dismantles the universal fallacy of worldly clientelism and human mediation. Throughout human history, subordinate classes have tolerated moral corruption and enabled oppressive regimes under the seductive assumption that their powerful patrons would shield them from catastrophic consequences. The Qur'an shows that in the Last-Day reality, every created being is utterly bankrupt. In the presence of the Almighty, worldly status carries zero currency: *“Every soul will come accompanied by a driver and a witness”* (50:21), and every individual is held strictly hostage by their own moral choices (74:38). The political bosses, corporate oligarchs, and ideological influencers who demanded moral compromises from their followers during life are completely powerless to deflect even an atom of retribution from themselves, let alone from those they exploited, revealing the absolute vanity of all mortal patronage.

## Verse 40:49

> And those in the Fire will cry out to the keepers of Hell, “Pray to your Lord to lighten the torment for us ˹even˺ for one day!”

**The Collapse of Horizontal Hope and the Shift to Angelic Wardens**

The forty-ninth verse depicts a new, agonizing chapter in the psychological collapse of the damned: *wa-qāla alladhīna fī al-nāri li-khazanati jahannam*—And those in the Fire will cry out to the keepers of Hell... Having exhausted all horizontal appeals to their former earthly masters and received only the hollow, helpless admission of shared damnation (v. 48), the inmates of Jahannam realize that human solidarity is completely bankrupt. Sūrah al-Taḥrīm describes them as *“angels, stern and severe; they do not disobey Allah in what He commands them but do what they are commanded”* (66:6).

**The Inability to Pray Directly: Udʿū Rabbakum**

The exact wording of the inmates’ appeal reveals the profound spiritual alienation that characterizes hell: *udʿū rabbakum*—"Pray to your Lord..." the commentators, including Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī and Burhān al-Dīn al-Biqāʿī, reflected deeply upon why the damned do not raise their voices directly to Allah in supplication. First, their moral crimes, unrepentant arrogance, and decades of defiance have filled their souls with paralyzing shame, rendering them terrified to address the Holy Sovereign whom they so casually dismissed during earthly life. Second, they dread receiving the final, devastating divine rebuke recorded in Sūrah al-Muʾminūn: *“Remain despised in it and do not speak to Me”* (23:108). Third, their phrasing—"your Lord" rather than "our Lord"—exposes the agonizing breach in their relationship with the divine. Having lived as if God did not exist, they acknowledge that the angels have a sanctified standing and intimacy with the Creator that they themselves irrevocably squandered through earthly heedlessness, leaving them eternally severed from divine intimacy.

**The Diminishing Horizons of Despair: Yukhaffif ʿAnnā**

The psychological regression of the damned is captured with acute precision in their specific petition: *yukhaffif ʿannā*—"to lighten the torment for us..." During their earthly tenure, these individuals had voracious, insatiable ambitions: demanding absolute political dominion, vast architectural legacies, endless sensual pleasures, and perpetual youth. Within the furnace of divine justice, however, their existential horizon shrinks to absolute zero. They do not have the audacity to ask for release from Hell; they do not even ask for their sentence to be ended or commuted. Their shattered spirits plead merely for *takhfīf*—a minor mitigation, a modest reduction in temperature, or a momentary dilution of agony. The Qur'an reveals that even this pathetic hope is an illusion: *“Death will not be decreed for them so they might die, nor will its torment be lightened for them”* (35:36; cf. 43:75).

The arrogant dismissed the celestial messages with cynicism, demanded to see physical angels as a spectacle of entertainment (25:21), or mocked the prophetic warnings as ancient fables.

## Verse 40:50

> The keepers will reply, “Did your messengers not ˹constantly˺ come to you with clear proofs?” They will say, “Yes ˹they did˺.” The keepers will say, “Then pray! Though the prayer of the disbelievers is only in vain.”

**The Angelic Cross-Examination: Did Messengers Not Come?**

The fiftieth verse records the devastating judicial response delivered by the angelic wardens to the pleading inmates of Hell: *qālū awalam taku taʾtīkum rusulukum bi-l-bayyināt*—The keepers will reply, “Did your messengers not constantly come to you with clear proofs?” (40:50)... The angels do not deliver an immediate, curt rejection; instead, they conduct a sharp, devastating cross-examination designed to extract an absolute confession of guilt from the damned. The phrasing *bi-l-bayyināt* means radiant, undeniable evidentiary proofs—rational arguments, cosmic signs, moral legislation, and verifiable miracles that left no reasonable room for doubt. By questioning them about the messengers, the angelic wardens establish the foundational premise of divine justice: God never punishes any human collective until the prophetic warning has been thoroughly established upon them: *“And never would We punish until We sent a messenger”* (17:15). The angels compel the damned to confront the truth that their current damnation is not an arbitrary misfortune, but the direct result of their deliberate rejection of clear guidance. Faced with the piercing interrogation of the angels, all evasive rhetoric, philosophical sophistry. Political deflection completely dissolve: *qālū balā*—They will say, “Yes they did.” in Arabic grammar, the affirmative particle *balā* is used exclusively to overturn a negative question, meaning an unqualified, absolute affirmation: "Yes indeed, without any question, the messengers arrived with undeniable proofs!" This single word represents the total collapse of all human excuses.

**The Closing of Dār al-Taklīf (The Abode of Testing)**

The angelic response illuminates a fundamental religious doctrine about the structure of human existence: the absolute distinction between the abode of moral obligation (*dār al-taklīf*) and the abode of recompense (*dār al-jazāʾ*). Earthly life is the exclusive arena in which human beings have moral agency, where deeds can be performed, sins can be pardoned, and sincere supplication transforms destiny. The moment death occurs and the Hereafter begins, the ledger of moral action is sealed forever. Scriptural parallels throughout the Qur'an reiterate this irrevocable closure. In Sūrah al-Mulk, the keepers of Hell pose the identical question to new arrivals, who lament: *“If only we had been listening or reasoning, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze”* (67:8–10; cf. 39:71). Regret in the Hereafter has zero legal or spiritual efficacy; tears shed in Hell cannot wash away a single sin, for the time of repentance has expired.

## Verse 40:51

> We certainly help Our messengers and the believers, ˹both˺ in this worldly life and on the Day the witnesses will stand forth ˹for testimony˺—

**The Promise in Two Halves**
*Innā la-nanṣuru rusulanā wa-alladhīna āmanū* — indeed We help Our messengers and those who believe. The sentence is emphatic, and it is placed in a sūrah where the believer from Pharaoh's household has just been saved while his own people were surrounded (40:45). It names two times for that help: in the life of this world, and on the day the witnesses stand. The commentators read the two times together, because the Book's pattern is that some messengers are vindicated in their own lifetimes and others are not. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr all note that the promise of help is not a promise of comfort, and that the help shown to a messenger may be the survival of what he brought rather than the safety of his person.
**What the Help Looked Like Here**
The sūrah has just shown the shape of it in the most difficult form. The man who spoke up in the court of Pharaoh was protected while the conspiracy continued, and the community he was speaking for was saved out of the same country years later (40:45-46). The Book gives the same instruction to those who ask for help, telling the story of the young men of the cave and their prayer for mercy and right guidance (18:10). The verse is therefore a promise with a direction in it: help is given to the messenger and the believer in the form each of them needs, and the day the witnesses stand is when the full account is settled.
**Why the Verse Is Placed Here**
The passage runs from the protection of the believer to the help of the messengers, and it names the day on which the help is completed. The men in Makkah who were listening to this sūrah had watched the Prophet for years without seeing the cities fall, and the verse tells them where the deadline is. The Book never promises a messenger that he will see the outcome. It promises that the outcome exists, and it names the day the court sits to prove it.

## Verse 40:52

> the Day the wrongdoers’ excuses will be of no benefit to them. They will be condemned, and will have the worst outcome

**The Day Excuses Run Out**
The verse follows the promise of the verse before, where God undertakes to help His messengers and the believers on the Day the witnesses stand (40:51). Then comes this scene: *yawma lā yanfaʿu al-ẓālimīna maʿdhiratuhum* — the Day the wrongdoers' excuses will not help them. The negation *lā yanfaʿu* leaves nothing standing: no partial hearing for excuses, no lighter sentence for a good argument, nothing at all.
**The Arguments That Will Not Work**
*Maʿdhirah* is the whole family of defences — the apology, the alibi, the explanation that moves the blame onto someone else. The commentators describe what the condemned will try: that they were ignorant, that they were only following what their forefathers did, that the state forced them, that their leaders misled them. The last of these is in the Qur'an itself, where the followers say to their leaders, you came to us from the right side and led us away (33:67). The verse's answer is not that the argument is rejected; it is that the argument will not be reached. They were given a conscience that told them, a mind that could weigh the matter, and messengers whose truth was clear (40:50), and they still chose. A man who chose with his eyes open cannot later be excused for having them shut.
**What Remains**
What is left after the excuse is gone is what the verse names: condemnation, and the worst home — *sūʾ al-dār*, the evil dwelling. The Qur'an sets that phrase against the two names of the good end, the house of settlement (40:39) and the house of peace (10:25). This is not a failure of the court but the truth of the case: the home a person reaches is the one his own life built. The Day does not create the verdict, it only removes everything that was covering it.

## Verse 40:53

> And indeed, We gave Moses ˹true˺ guidance, and made the Children of Israel inherit the Scripture—

**Guidance Given to Moses**
*Wa-la-qad ātaynā Mūsā al-hudā* — and We certainly gave Moses the guidance. The construction is emphatic, and the thing given is *al-hudā* with the definite article: not an opinion or a philosophy, but the way itself. The verse then adds a second gift: *and We made the Children of Israel inherit the Book*. Two things are being said at once. Prophethood is not something a man arrives at by thinking well; it comes as a bestowal, which is why the sūrah keeps using the verb *give*. And the gift outlived the man: the Book Moses brought was inherited by the generations after him and carried the guidance forward.
**Why the Verse Stands Here**
It is placed immediately after the scene of Pharaoh's people being shown the Fire morning and evening, and the two belong together as a verdict on two paths. One dynasty had power, wealth, an army, and a claim to divinity; the other had a leader who stuttered and a people who had been slaves, and that is the side that received the guidance and the Book. The Book states the same reversal elsewhere in plain words: God willed to favour those who were oppressed and make them leaders and heirs (28:5). The sūrah is not consoling the Muslims with a pretty story. It is telling a community under pressure which line in history actually holds.
**What Was Inherited**
The inheritance is worth noticing, because the verse gives it to the descendants as a whole rather than to a line of priests or a single house. The guidance was meant to travel, and the sūrah of Ghāfir will go on to make the same claim about the Book given to Muḥammad. The pattern is one the sūrah uses repeatedly: the message comes to a man, and then it is handed to a people to carry, and the story of every generation is what they did with what they were handed.

## Verse 40:54

> a guide and a reminder to people of reason

**Guidance and Reminder**
The verse describes the Book named in the verse before it, and the two words it chooses cover the whole of a person's needs: *hudan wa-dhikrā* — a guidance and a reminder. *Hudā* looks forward. It shows the road, marks the turns, and tells a man what to do with the day in front of him. *Dhikrā* looks back. It wakes up what a person already knows in the depth of himself — that he did not make himself, that he owes thanks to someone, that he was told this before. One word keeps him from straying; the other keeps him from forgetting.
**Why the Two Belong Together**
Revelation is not a heap of information, and it does not work by winning an argument and stopping there. It leads and it reminds, in that order and together. A person who is only reminded becomes sentimental about what he once knew. A person who is only directed becomes a man following instructions he no longer understands. The Book does both: it tells the traveller where to go and why the road is there.
**For Whom It Works**
The verse adds who receives this: *li-ulī al-albāb*, for people of understanding — literally the people of the inner kernels, those who think with what they have been given. The Book speaks to everyone, but it does not open the same way for everyone. A man who has decided in advance that his ancestors were right, or that his position depends on not looking, can read the clearest verse and find only an old story. The Qur'an says elsewhere: in that there is a reminder for whoever has a heart, or listens while his mind is present (50:37). The condition is not cleverness. It is attention.

## Verse 40:55

> So be patient ˹O Prophet˺, ˹for˺ Allah’s promise is certainly true. Seek forgiveness for your shortcomings. And glorify the praises of your Lord morning and evening

**Three Commands in One Sentence**
The verse gives the Prophet a regimen in three parts: be patient, ask forgiveness for your sin, and glorify your Lord with praise at the two ends of the day. Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī note the order. Patience comes first, because the promise he is being asked to trust is stated immediately after it: the promise of God is true. Then comes the seeking of forgiveness, which is remarkable in a verse addressed to a man whose sins the Book says were already forgiven. The command is a matter of standing rather than of account: the highest of God's servants is taught never to stop asking, so that no one after him can think he has no need of it.
**The Two Ends of the Day**
*Al-ʿashiyy* is the late afternoon and evening, and *al-ibkār* is the early morning, and the phrase covers the day at both ends. The Book gives this instruction in the same language to the whole community of believers, telling them to glorify God in the evening and the morning and at other times as well (30:17-18), and the command to the Prophet in this verse is the pattern the sūrahs return to when a messenger is under pressure. Muslim reports that these commands are the discipline of a heart that will not let the pressure collect, and the placement of the command in the middle of a sūrah of arguments is deliberate: the remedy for the argument is not more argument.
**The Promise Hidden in the Middle**
The reason the Prophet is told to be patient is given in the words that follow: God will help His messengers and those who believe, in this world and on the day the witnesses stand (40:51). That sentence is the sūrah's own answer to the court of Pharaoh and the arguing crowds of Makkah. The command to endure and the sentence about help belong to each other, and the verse puts them within a few lines. The sūrah has shown the believer in Pharaoh's household doing exactly this — placing his affair with God and going on speaking — and now it turns and gives the same instruction to the Prophet himself.

## Verse 40:56

> Surely those who dispute Allah’s signs—with no proof given to them—have nothing in their hearts but greed for dominance, which they will never attain. So seek refuge in Allah. Indeed, He alone is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing

**Why They Dispute Without Proof**

The fifty-sixth verse penetrates beneath the surface rhetoric of anti-religious skepticism to expose the hidden psychological pathology driving opposition to divine revelation: *inna alladhīna yujādilūna fī āyāti al-llāhi*—Surely those who dispute Allah’s signs... In the old Arabic dictionaries, the verbal form *yujādilūn* means contentious disputation, aggressive debate, and rhetorical maneuvering aimed not at discovering objective truth, but at defeating an ideological opponent. Throughout Sūrah Ghāfir, this term recurs as a primary diagnostic category for the deniers of prophetic truth (vv. 4, 35, 56), exposing the underlying spiritual pathology that corrupts human discourse. The verse makes clear that these polemicists do not engage the prophetic message in good faith. Their intellectual objections are not genuine philosophical inquiries born of sincere perplexity, but aggressive tries to obscure and extinguish the signs of divine truth that threaten their entrenched worldly power, seeking to maintain an unjust social hierarchy at all costs.

**The Concealed Pathology: Greed for Dominance (Kibr)**

The revelation rips away the intellectual facade to expose the true driving force hidden within the disputers' souls: *in fī ṣudūrihim illā kibrun*—have nothing in their hearts but greed for dominance, or pride. The restrictive construction *in... Illā* ("nothing... Except") delivers a devastating psychological diagnosis. The root cause of their obstinacy is not intellectual confusion, but *kibr*—arrogance, grandiosity, and an insatiable desire for supreme dominance, honor, and authority. In classical moral psychology, *kibr* is defined as the inflated ego's refusal to submit to truth and its contempt for other human beings. The Meccan aristocrats, like Pharaoh before them, could not endure the thought of bowing to a Creator who demanded moral accountability, nor could they tolerate following a Prophet from humble social origins. They craved absolute supremacy (*riʾāsah*) and resented any divine message that dismantled their artificial hierarchy, choosing spiritual blindness rather than humbling their pride before the Lord of the worlds. In an authentic tradition recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 91*, the Prophet ﷺ defined this exact spiritual malady: “Arrogance (*al-kibr*) is rejecting the truth and looking down upon people.” The verse pronounces the ultimate cosmic frustration of their hubristic ambitions: *mā hum bi-bālighīhi*—which they will never attain. In Arabic syntax, the negative particle *mā* combined with the prepositional prefix *bi-* on the active participle means categorical impossibility.

## Verse 40:57

> The creation of the heavens and the earth is certainly greater than the re-creation of humankind, but most people do not know

**The All the more Cosmic Argument: Khalqu al-Samāwāti wa-l-Arḍ**

The fifty-seventh verse deploys a majestic rational argument *all the more* (*al-qiyās al-awlawī*) to shatter human skepticism about bodily resurrection: *la-khalqu al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi akbaru min khalqi al-nās*—The creation of the heavens and the earth is certainly greater than the recreation of humankind... In Arabic rhetoric and Islamic dialectics (*ʿilm al-kalām*), this verse represents the pinnacle of rational cosmic demonstration. The argument operates with self-evident mathematical and physical logic: if an artisan has the power, knowledge, and sovereign capacity to engineer an immense, intricate palace spanning vast continents, it is intuitively obvious that the same artisan can easily reconstruct a tiny cup within that palace.

**The Logical Absurdity of Denying Resurrection (Baʿth)**

The Qur'an frequently deploys cosmic creation as an irrefutable proof text for the reality of the Resurrection (*al-baʿth*). Sūrah Yā-Sīn poses the identical rhetorical challenge: *“Is not He who created the heavens and the earth able to create the likes of them? Yes, [it is so]; and He is the Knowing Creator”* (36:81; cf. 46:33; 79:27–33). From an of being perspective, initiating a colossal creation *ex nihilo* (from absolute nonexistence) is infinitely more wondrous than simply repeating a creation that already existed in prototype. The pagan Meccans who held up decayed bones, asking mockingly who could revive them, were suffering from a profound failure of religious imagination. They measured the infinite, untamable power of the Creator against the puny limitations of mortal creatures, failing to recognize that resurrecting all of humanity is as effortless for the Almighty as creating a single soul: *“Your creation and your resurrection will not be but as that of a single soul”* (31:28). For the Sovereign Lord, recreation requires merely the divine command: Be, and it is.

## Verse 40:58

> Those blind ˹to the truth˺ and those who can see are not equal, nor are those who believe and do good ˹equal˺ to those who do evil. Yet you are hardly mindful

**The Blind and the Seeing Are Not Equal**
*Wa-mā yastawī al-aʿmā wa-al-baṣīr* — the blind man and the seeing man are not the same. The sentence is put as a denial of equality, and the two adjectives are plain: one man cannot see where he is walking, the other can. The sūrah then adds the second pair, those who believe and work righteousness beside those who do evil, and closes with the line that gives the whole comparison its edge: *little do you reflect*.
**What the Two Comparisons Are Doing**
The verse is a summary of the argument the sūrah has been running since its fourth verse. Two men can be standing on the same street with the same facts in front of them — the sky, the signs, the messenger speaking — and one of them receives them while the other walks past. The Book calls the difference sight, and calls the man who lacks it blind, without meaning anything about his eyes - it says elsewhere that it is not the eyes that go blind but the hearts inside the chests (22:46). That is why the comparison does not end with the blind man's helplessness: the blindness described here is chosen, and the next verse makes it worse — the deaf and the blind will not be guided, though the man who does not hear and does not see can still turn and be shown (40:58, 40:13). The verse is an invitation rather than a verdict, which is what the last two words say so quietly: reflect.
**Where the Comparison Comes From**
The image is one the Book uses for this subject whenever it wants to state the difference in the plainest terms, and the sūrah of the Cattle puts it with the same starkness: the blind and the seeing are not equal, nor are darkness and light (6:50 is the standing; 35:19-22 sets it out in a run of five pairs). The reader who meets the pair here has met it before, and the sūrah uses it as a conclusion to a long argument: after the proofs have been laid out, the only question left is what a man does with them.

## Verse 40:59

> The Hour is certainly coming, there is no doubt about it. But most people do not believe

The fifty-ninth verse delivers an absolute, unshakeable proclamation about the final destination of human history: *inna al-sāʿata la-ātiyatun*—The Hour is certainly coming... In Arabic grammar and rhetoric, this statement is constructed with the highest degree of grammatical corroboration (*taʾkīd*): the opening particle of absolute certitude *inna*, followed by the corroborative prefix *lām* (*la-*), attached to the active participle *ātiyah* ("arriving" or "actively approaching"). In Arabic semantics, the use of the active participle (*ism al-fāʿil*) rather than a simple future verb indicates not merely that the Hour will occur at some distant date, but that its arrival is an active, ongoing reality already set in motion. Every passing second, every heartbeat, and every planetary rotation brings the cosmos inexorably closer to this catastrophic terminus, unveiling the fragility of all mortal existence. The Hour is marching forward with relentless mathematical inevitability, drawing all created beings toward an appointment from which there is no escape or postponement.

**The Of being Transformation of the Cosmos**

In Qur'anic the teaching about the Last Day, *al-Sāʿah* (the Hour) means the universal climactic rupture that ends the current physical order. It is the moment when the cosmic scaffolding is dismantled: the sky is peeled away, the stars are scattered, the oceans are set ablaze, and the earth expels its buried contents (81:1–14; 82:1–5). The Hour is not only an ending, but a glorious new beginning: the resurrection of all human bodies, the absolute unveiling of every secret deed, and the establishment of divine justice across all creation, crowning history with ultimate moral vindication. Classical theologians, including Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, noted that without the Hour, the entire universe would be an irrational, tragic farce—a realm where oppressors torture the innocent without consequence and saints suffer without vindication. The Hour is the cosmic necessity that restores ultimate harmony, meaning, and justice to the universe, fulfilling the primordial purpose for which the heavens and the earth were created.

**The Tragedy of Human Skepticism: Akthara al-Nāsi Lā Yuʾminūn**

The second half of the verse introduces a tragic psychological paradox: *wa-lākinna akthara al-nāsi lā yuʾminūn*—But most people do not believe. Despite the self-evident rationality of the moral argument (v. 58), the overwhelming majesty of cosmic creation (v. 57), and the continuous warnings of prophetic messengers across centuries, the majority of humanity remains entrenched in skepticism and disbelief. The Qur'an frequently highlights this tragic demographic reality: *“And most of the people, although you strive [for it], are not believers”* (12:103).

## Verse 40:60

> Your Lord has proclaimed, “Call upon Me, I will respond to you. Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled.”

**Call on Me and I Will Answer**
*Wa-qāla rabbukumu udʿūnī astajib lakum* — your Lord says: call upon Me and I will answer you. The command to call comes first and the answer follows it in the very next word, the two joined as tightly as cause and effect. No gatekeeper stands between the servant and his Lord: no priestly order, no offering that must be bought, no chain of intercessors. The invitation is open to everyone, and it stays open — the verse sets no condition of rank or of past record before it.
**Supplication Is Worship**
The verse then reaches its other half: *those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, brought low*. Pride is the one thing that closes this door, and the Prophet made plain how high the door's worth is. Al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr reported that he said, "Supplication is worship itself," and then recited this same verse (al-Tirmidhī 2969, Abū Dāwūd 1479). Scholars such as al-Ghazālī and al-Rāzī explained what that identity rests on. A person who raises empty hands admits two things at once: that he has nothing and needs everything, and that God has everything and needs nothing. That admission is worship in its purest form, which is why a Muslim may ask for a loaf of bread and be performing worship while he asks.
**How an Answer Comes**
The promise *astajib lakum*, I will answer you, led the commentators to ask what an answer looks like when the thing asked for does not arrive. In a report from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī, the Prophet said that no believer asks God for anything — so long as the request is not for sin or for cutting off family ties — without God giving one of three things: the request itself in this life, or a reward stored for him in the next, or an equal harm turned away from him (al-Tirmidhī 3573, Aḥmad 11133). The answer is certain then; what varies is the form, because the One answering knows more about what serves the servant than the servant knows himself. A wise doctor refuses what the patient begs for when it would hurt him, and gives something better instead. What nobody in this verse loses is the hearing, and what nobody should risk losing is the asking.

## Verse 40:61

> It is Allah Who has made the night for you to rest in and the day bright. Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most people are ungrateful

**The Two Halves of the Day**
*Allāhu alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-layla li-taskunū fīhi wa-al-nahāra mubṣiran* — God is the One who made the night for you to rest in, and the day to give light. The verse takes the two halves of a life and states the purpose of each in one word: rest, and sight. The Sūrah of the Story asks the same question in the same form — if God had made the night permanent until the Day of Judgment, who would bring you light? (28:71). Al-Zamakhsharī notes the order, with the night named first, and the pattern is the same in the creation passages of the Book, where sleep and work are paired as gifts rather than as a schedule.
**The Word for the Day**
The day is described as *mubṣir*, making things visible, and the word carries the meaning of a light in which a person can see his way. The Book calls the night a covering and the day a livelihood in the Sūrah of the Tidings, and it says in the Sūrah of the Bee that the day is made bright so that a person can seek God's bounty in it (16:14). The verse is therefore not describing a clock. It is describing the arrangement that makes work, travel, trade and study possible, and it asks the reader to see the arrangement as something provided on purpose.
**What the Verse Asks For**
The sentence ends by saying that God is full of bounty towards people, but most of them do not give thanks. The sūrah is running its argument about ingratitude, and the verses around this one list the earth, the sky, the forms of the human being, food and drink, and the road (40:64-67). The Book's word for thanks in these passages is not a feeling. It is what a person does with the gift, and the verse places the reader in front of the simplest gift he has and asks whether he has used it that way. The next verse names the call that belongs to the same God alone (40:60).

## Verse 40:62

> That is Allah, your Lord, the Creator of all things. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. How can you then be deluded ˹from the truth˺

**That Is God, Your Lord**

The sixty-second verse delivers a sweeping religious synthesis, gathering all the cosmic and earthly signs named earlier into a single, definitive identification: *dhālikumu al-llāhu rabbukum*—That is Allah, your Lord... In Arabic rhetoric, the distant demonstrative pronoun *dhālikum* ("that is") functions as an index of supreme majesty, transcendence, and unmistakable clarity. It indicates: That sovereign Being who fashioned the heavens and the earth (v. 57), who created night for your peaceful rest and day for your productive labor (v. 61), and who sustains your biological existence—that is Allah, your true Lord (*Rabb*), the supreme Ruler of eternity. In classical usage, *al-Rabb* means the Master who owns, cherishes, educates, and guides His creation through successive stages of perfection. By directly appending *rabbukum* ("your Lord, Sustainer, and Cherisher"), the revelation personalizes the divine reality, reminding human beings that the cosmic Architect is not an aloof, disinterested metaphysical force, but the intimate Nurturer who brought each individual into existence and continuously attends to their flourishing, weaving mercy into the fabric of everyday experience.

**The Rational Imperative: Creator Implies Sole Deity**

The internal logic of the verse presents an inescapable intellectual challenge to pagan polytheism and modern secular idolatries. The pagan Arabs of Mecca conceded that God was the supreme Creator of the heavens and the earth (as acknowledged in 31:25 and 43:87), yet they persistently bowed before minor deities and tribal idols, claiming they were intercessors. The verse exposes the grotesque intellectual incoherence of this theology: How can you acknowledge God as the sole Creator of all things (*khāliqu kulli shayʾ*), yet direct your supplications, fears, and devotion to created beings who have no power to create a single fly? By demonstrating that supreme creative power belongs exclusively to God, the Qur'an leaves polytheism without a shred of rational justification, proving that authentic monotheism is the only worldview consistent with cosmic reality, and liberating the human spirit from the degrading shackles of false devotion.

## Verse 40:63

> This is how those who used to reject Allah’s signs were ˹also˺ deluded

**The Law of Being Turned Around**
*Kadhālika yuʾfaku alladhīna kānū bi-āyāti al-llāhi yajḥadūn* — in this same way were turned away those who rejected God's signs. The demonstrative *kadhālika* — this is how it always goes — ties the mockery of Quraysh in Makkah to the ruin of older nations. Their case is not new; delusion is not an accident that befalls an unlucky generation. It runs like a law.
**The Verb That Means Inversion**
*Yuʾfaku* is passive, from the root *a-f-k*, which means to turn a thing over — to overturn, to invert. The picture is a person who has been flipped upside down inside: the false is loved as true, and truth pains him. Note how quietly the verse places the blame. God is not pictured as tricking anyone. Their own stubborn rejection is the hand on the scale, and being turned around is what follows it, as naturally as the echo follows the shout.
**Denial Is Not Ignorance**
The clause describes them as those *who used to reject* — *kānū ... yajḥadūn* — and *jaḥada* is a precise word. It is not *jahl*, innocent not-knowing. It means to deny what one knows to be true, to beat back the light with the hand while the eye still sees it. The Qur'an gives this exact portrait of Pharaoh's court: those who rejected the signs while their own hearts were convinced of them, out of injustice and pride (27:14). That is the anatomy of *jaḥd* — knowledge held down by choice, and a conscience that has learned to argue. Every generation that repeats the mockery repeats the mechanism, which is why the verse says *kadhālika*: this is how it goes, and it has gone this way before.

## Verse 40:64

> It is Allah Who made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy. He shaped you ˹in the womb˺, perfecting your form. And He has provided you with what is good and lawful. That is Allah—your Lord. So Blessed is Allah, Lord of all worlds

**A Settled Place and a Roof**
*Allāhu alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-arḍa qarāran wa-l-samāʾa bināʾan* — God is the One who made the earth a place of rest for you, and the sky a building. *Qarār* is a floor that does not move under the foot, the word used of water that has stopped running; *bināʾ* is a structure, a roof raised over a space. The verse takes the two facts no one on earth can arrange for himself — that the ground holds and that something is over it — and puts them where they belong, with *lakum*, for you. The Book says the same about the canopy elsewhere: a protected ceiling, though people turn away from its signs (21:32).
**Your Forms Made Good**
The verse then turns from the world outside to the person: *wa-ṣawwarakum fa-aḥsana ṣuwarakum* — He shaped you and made your forms good. The claim is not that the human frame is useful for a purpose, but that it is beautiful as a thing made, which is why the Book says elsewhere that God formed the human being in the best of moulds (95:4). The eye that reads the verse is one of the things being described; the hand holding the page is another.
**The Provision and the Conclusion**
The fourth gift is *razaqakum min al-ṭayyibāt* — provision from the good things, not merely what keeps a person alive but what is pleasant to eat and to use. The verse then closes on the sentence the four gifts were leading to: that is God, your Lord; blessed be God, Lord of all the worlds. The argument of the verse is not a lecture in cosmology but a list a person can verify by standing on the ground, looking up, looking at himself, and eating a meal. Gratitude is what that list asks for, and the praise the verse ends with is the form it takes.

## Verse 40:65

> He is the Ever-Living. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. So call upon Him with sincere devotion, ˹saying,˺ “All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds.”

**He Is the Ever-Living**

The sixty-fifth verse opens with an awe-inspiring revelation of divine self-subsistence: *huwa al-ḥayy*—He is the Ever-Living... In Islamic belief (*uṣūl al-dīn*), the divine name *Al-Ḥayy* designates the primordial attribute of absolute, uncaused, eternal life. All biological and cosmic life is contingent, derived, and fragile—originating from nonexistence, sustained by external nutrients and divine providence, and inevitably culminating in death and decay. In contrast, the life of God is uncreated, possessing neither beginning (*azal*) nor end (*abad*), untouched by slumber, fatigue, aging, or demise (2:255). It is absolute self-subsistence (*al-qayyūmiyyah*), requiring no external cause, no nourishment, and no physical support to endure throughout eternity. The commentators noted that *Al-Ḥayy* serves as the anchor for all other active divine attributes: because He is Ever-Living, He eternally knows (*Al-ʿAlīm*), wills (*Al-Murīd*), hears (*Al-Samīʿ*), sees (*Al-Baṣīr*), and creates (*Al-Khāliq*). The living Lord is the eternal fountainhead from which all existence continuously derives its being, upholding galaxies and subatomic particles alike with uninterrupted divine presence.

**The Universal Liturgy of Doxology: Al-Ḥamdu li-Llāhi Rabbi al-ʿĀlamīn**

The verse provides the precise verbal formulation through which humanity participates in the cosmic symphony of adoration: *al-ḥamdu li-llāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīn*—“All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds” (40:65). This sacred phrase is the eternal crown of monotheistic liturgy, echoing the foundational opening verse of Sūrah al-Fātiḥah (1:2). In Arabic semantics, *al-ḥamd* means comprehensive, loving praise and boundless gratitude rendered to God on account of His intrinsic perfections and His continuous bestowal of unearned favors. By instructing the faithful to conclude their supplication with *al-ḥamdu li-llāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīn*, the revelation aligns human worship with the perpetual praise of creation. The angels encircling the Divine Throne, the celestial bodies traversing their orbits, and the ecosystems of the earth continuously celebrate the praise of the Lord of all realms, weaving a cosmic chorus of thanksgiving that echoes through all dimensions of reality.

**The Theological Contrast with Mortal Deities**

The internal architecture of the verse delivers a devastating, silent argument against ancient and modern forms of idolatry. Throughout history, human cultures have deified mortal beings—worshiping kings as incarnate gods, elevating ancestors to divine guardians, or treating ideological leaders as infallible saviors. The Qur'an systematically demolishes these illusions by reminding humanity of mortality. A deity that bleeds, hungers, grows weary, and dies is an of being contradiction, incapable of offering ultimate salvation or cosmic guidance to human souls. In an authentic tradition recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7383*, the Prophet ﷺ supplicated with words that echo this exact verse: “I seek refuge in Your might, none has the right to be worshiped but You, the One who never dies. The jinn and humans die.” The verse liberates the human heart from the pathetic tragedy of placing ultimate faith in mortal creatures who are destined for the grave, elevating human consciousness to eternal reality.

## Verse 40:66

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I have been forbidden to worship those ˹idols˺ you worship besides Allah, since clear proofs have come to me from my Lord. And I have been commanded to ˹fully˺ submit to the Lord of all worlds.”

**A Command to Declare**
*Qul innī nuhītu an aʿbuda alladhīna tadʿūna min dūni Allāh* — say: I have been forbidden to worship those you call upon besides God. The imperative *qul* marks this as a proclamation, not a personal preference: what the Prophet is told to say is a decree delivered, not a view offered. The passive form *nuhītu*, I have been forbidden, puts him on the same footing as everyone else who hears the command. He is a servant under the law he brings, with no licence to trade away any part of it for a quieter life in Makkah.
**The Two Halves of the Declaration**
The verse does not stop at the prohibition. It gives the reason and it gives the alternative: clear proofs have come to me from my Lord, and I have been commanded to submit to the Lord of all worlds. So the refusal of idols is not a bare denial of something, but the other side of turning to God, and the proofs are named as the ground of it. The Meccans had offered compromise in various forms — honour, wealth, a share in the leadership if the message softened. This verse closes that door by making the answer a matter of command rather than negotiation. The line drawn here is not a political position that can be adjusted. It is the same line the whole sūrah has been drawing, and the last part of the verse states what stands on the other side of it: submission to the Lord of all the worlds.

## Verse 40:67

> He is the One Who created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then He brings you forth as infants, so that you may reach your prime, and become old—though some of you ˹may˺ die sooner—reaching an appointed time, so perhaps you may understand ˹Allah’s power˺

**Until You Reach Full Strength**

Following the extreme vulnerability of infancy and childhood, the divine design guides the human organism toward physical and cognitive strength: *thumma li-tablughū ashuddakum*—so that you may reach your prime. In the old Arabic dictionaries, *ashudd* means the zenith of physical vigor, muscular endurance, intellectual clarity, and emotional maturity, traditionally associated with the fourth decade of life (typically around age forty, as expressed in 46:15). During this golden phase, the human being has maximum physical power, creative productivity, and social influence. However, the Qur'an contextualizes this strength not as an autonomous achievement to induce pride, but as a temporary gift and a serious spiritual responsibility. The strength of youth is a transient loan granted by God to be invested in righteous labor, social justice, and divine worship before the inevitable decline of physical vitality begins.

**The Vulnerability of Senescence and Mortality: Shuyūkhan**

The biological trajectory inevitably bends downward toward physical frailty: *thumma li-takūnū shuyūkhan*—and become old... In Arabic, *shuyūkh* (plural of *shaykh*) designates the elderly who experience graying hair, weakening eyesight, stooped posture, diminished mobility, and the fading of youthful strength. Sūrah al-Rūm captures this symmetric lifecycle: *“Allah is the one who created you from weakness, then made after weakness strength, then made after strength weakness and white hair”* (30:54). And, the verse notes that this biological progression is not guaranteed to every individual: *wa-minkum man yutawaffā min qabl*—"though some of you may die sooner." Some perish in the womb, some die in infancy or youth, and some are cut down in their prime. Humanity controls neither the beginning of their life, nor its duration, nor the timing of its end.

**The Sovereign Inviolability of the Appointed Term: Ajal Musammā**

The diversity of lifespans is governed by immutable celestial decree: *wa-li-tablughū ajalan musamman*—reaching an appointed time. In Islamic belief, every individual human being is assigned a precise lifespan (*ajal*) recorded in the Preserved Tablet (*al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ*). When that appointed moment arrives, it can neither be delayed by modern medical technology nor accelerated by imperial assassins: *“When their term has come, they will not remain behind an hour, nor will they precede it”* (7:34). Recognizing that life is governed by *ajal musammā* liberates the believer from debilitating fear of mortal threats: a tyrant can only end physical life if the divine decree has already arrived. It infuses the human heart with urgency, reminding mortals that every heartbeat is a step toward an unalterable appointment with the Sovereign Judge of all things, encouraging the faithful to invest each passing day in righteous deeds.

## Verse 40:68

> He is the One Who gives life and causes death. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, “Be!” And it is

**The Divine Monopoly Over Life and Death: Yuḥyī wa-Yumīt**

The sixty-eighth verse concentrates the boundless sovereign agency of God into two foundational cosmic prerogatives: *huwa alladhī yuḥyī wa-yumīt*—He is the One Who gives life and causes death... In Arabic syntax, the isolated personal pronoun *huwa* ("He alone") serves as a pronoun of restriction (*damīr al-faṣl*), meaning absolute exclusivity. The bestowal of biological animation and the imposition of mortality are exclusive divine monopolies that belong to no other entity in existence. No monarch, no council of physicians, no naturalistic process, and no mythological deity has the power to breathe life into inanimate matter or decree the final departure of a soul from the body. Ancient tyrants like Nimrod claimed the power of life and death by executing one prisoner and pardoning another, but Abraham exposed the childish vanity of this claim by pointing to the cosmic scale of divine governance (2:258). The verse establishes that the threshold of life and death is divinely guarded, remaining an inviolable frontier of celestial sovereignty that no mortal can breach, proving that human life is held perpetually in the hands of the Creator.

**The Of being Command of Instantaneous Creation: Kun Fa-Yakūn**

The verse reveals the highest statement of creative power: *fa-innamā yaqūlu lahu kun fa-yakūn*—He simply tells it, “Be!” And it is! (40:68). In classical Islamic belief (*uṣūl al-dīn*), the command *kun* ("Be!") is not a physical sound traveling through atmospheric air, but a majestic picture, drawn in human terms, of the instant obedience of all creation to the divine will. The moment God wills the existence of something, that thing immediately crosses the threshold from nonexistence into being without hesitation or friction. This formulation recurs across foundational Qur'anic passages addressing cosmic origination and bodily resurrection, as in Sūrah al-Baqarah: *“Originator of the heavens and the earth. When He decrees a matter, He only says to it, ‘Be,’ and it is”* (2:117; cf. 16:40; 36:82). To the Creator, resurrecting the entire human race from decomposed dust requires no more effort than bringing a microscopic cell into existence, demonstrating that the infinite majesty of the Almighty knows no limits, bounds, or exhaustion.

**Dismantling the Illusion of Materialist Causality**

The declaration *kun fa-yakūn* delivers a profound philosophical critique of mechanical, materialist determinism. While God established predictable secondary causes (*al-asbāb*) within the natural order—gravity, thermodynamics, biological processes—to regulate terrestrial life, these secondary causes have no autonomous creative agency. Fire does not burn by its own intrinsic sovereign power; medicine does not heal through autonomous divinity; and natural laws do not create reality. The Almighty is the continuous, unmediated Sustainer (*Al-Qayyūm*) who recreates the cosmos at every single instant. Whenever divine wisdom decrees, the natural order bends instantly to the celestial command, as when the blazing furnace of Nimrod became cool and peaceful for Abraham (21:69). Divine power reigns supreme above all natural laws, establishing an unshakeable order where miracles manifest divine benevolence.

## Verse 40:69

> Have you not seen how those who dispute Allah’s signs are turned away

**The Rhetorical Interrogative of Wonder: A-Lam Tara**

The sixty-ninth verse opens with a piercing rhetorical question addressed directly to the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ and, by extension, to every perceptive observer of human history: *alam tara ilā alladhīna yujādilūna fī āyāti al-llāh*—Have you not seen how those who dispute Allah’s signs are turned away?... In Arabic rhetoric, the formula *a-lam tara* ("have you not seen" or "have you not considered") is deployed not merely to inquire about physical vision, but to evoke intense intellectual astonishment, moral contemplation, and spiritual perplexity. It commands the observer to examine a bizarre, tragic phenomenon unfolding directly before their eyes. In the preceding verses (vv. 57–68), the Qur'an laid out a staggering sequence of cosmic and biological proofs: the vast architecture of the heavens and the earth, the soothing sanctuary of the night, the bright arena of the day, the miracle of embryology from dust to maturity, and the sovereign reality of life and death commanded by *kun fa-yakūn*. In light of this radiant display of divine majesty, how can any rational creature engage in contentious dispute against the signs of the Almighty, turning away from clear proofs into fabricated darkness?

## Verse 40:70

> ˹They are˺ the ones who reject this Book and all ˹scriptures˺ We sent Our messengers with. So they will know ˹the consequences˺

**One Revelation, One Line**
The verse names the offence precisely: those who reject *this Book and all that We sent Our messengers with*. The two halves belong together. Revelation is not a set of unrelated deliveries; it is one message carried by many hands. From Adam and Noah to Abraham, Moses, Jesus and Muḥammad, every true messenger brought the same core: that God is One, that people owe Him worship, that conduct is judged, and that there is another life. To accept one messenger and throw out the rest is not selective loyalty. Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī make this point from the verse itself — rejecting one link tears the chain, and the one who rejects the last of the messengers has rejected every messenger whose books he claims to respect.
**The Habit of Choosing From the Menu**
Groups that divide the prophets are not new, and the Qur'an names them plainly: those who believe in some messengers and disbelieve in others and mean to draw a line between God and His messengers — those are the disbelievers, truly (4:150–151). The Meccans are the standing example. They honoured Abraham and Ishmael as their forefathers and named them in their rites, while rejecting the messenger who came with the same message they said they followed. That is the old habit: keep the ancestor as a flag, and refuse the living claim he made. The Book is not a table from which a man takes what flatters him and leaves what costs him something.
**So They Will Know**
The verse closes with three words: *so they will know*. Nothing is explained, no punishment is named, no date is given. The statement is left as it stands, because the day the knowledge arrives is the whole answer. Every argument about the Book, every clever dismissal, every label stuck on a messenger, ends at that sentence.

## Verse 40:71

> when shackles will be around their necks and chains ˹on their legs˺. They will be dragged

**When the Chains Go On**
The verse opens a window on the moment the previous verse promised (40:70): *when the shackles are around their necks, and the chains* — *idh al-aghlālu fī aʿnāqihim wa-al-salāsil*. The word *idh*, "when", pulls the reader out of the future tense and sets him at the scene, so that the coming of that day stops being a fact to be discussed and becomes a thing to be watched. The men whose voices had filled the council chambers and who had dismissed the messenger as a storyteller are shown with their hands bound, and the verse gives them no words.
**What the Image Says About the Talk**
The verse's work is the contrast, not the hardware. Earlier in the sūrah these same men sat at ease and disputed the signs, and they asked the Prophet to hasten what he warned of; here their hands are held at their necks, which is the part of a man that argues. Nothing remains of the sharp tongues that ran the argument. The verse says the case has ended, not that the discussion will continue somewhere else under worse conditions — and it leaves the reader to think about which of the two positions he is standing in while there is still time to say something.

## Verse 40:72

> through boiling water, then burned in the Fire ˹as fuel˺

**The Agonizing Circuit: Fī al-Ḥamīm**

The seventy-second verse continues the uninterrupted, horrifying sequence began in the preceding verse: being dragged in iron shackles and chains *fī al-ḥamīm*—through boiling water... In Arabic grammar, this clause functions as the prepositional complement to the verb *yusḥabūn* ("they will be dragged") in verse 71. The condemned are not dragged across ordinary earthly terrain; they are dragged directly into *al-ḥamīm*—an abyssal reservoir of scalding, superheated liquid. In the old Arabic dictionaries, *ḥamīm* means boiling fluid that has reached the absolute apex of intensity. The commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, noted the terrifying physical reality: *al-ḥamīm* is water heated to absolute maximum thermal boiling capacity since the creation of the cosmos. When the shackled bodies of the damned are dragged through this churning cauldron, flesh dissolves from bone, nerve endings are overwhelmed with searing agony, and the skin melts in a terrifying display of infernal retribution, leaving the soul in absolute devastation.

**The Scalding Dynamics of Al-Ḥamīm**

In Qur'anic the teaching about the Last Day, *al-ḥamīm* represents a recurrent, terrifying instrument of divine justice, recurring across many descriptions of Jahannam (e.g., 22:19–20; 55:44; 56:42). Sūrah al-Ḥajj depicts its devastating physiological impact: *“Scalding water will be poured upon their heads, with which what is in their bellies and their skins will be melted”* (22:19–20). Immersion in boiling liquid is particularly horrifying to human psychology because liquid envelops the entire surface area of the body simultaneously. There is no surface left untouched, no posture that affords relief, and no cool air to breathe, generating an overwhelming sense of physical and psychological claustrophobia. The damned are completely submerged within boiling fluid, experiencing total sensory engulfment by heat that penetrates into the marrow of their bones, stripping away all illusions of physical strength, and leaving no corner of their being untouched by pain.

**The Horrific Fate of Becoming Fuel: Yusjarūn**

The final verb of the verse unveils a terrifying of being degradation: *yusjarūn*—they are burned in the Fire as fuel. In the old Arabic dictionaries, the verb *sajara* means to kindle, stoke, and fuel a massive furnace, as when dry logs are thrown into an oven to intensify its heat: *al-tannūru al-masjūr*. The Qur'an regularly reveals this grim reality: *“Fear the Fire, whose fuel is men and stones”* (2:24; cf. 21:98; 66:6). The damned are not only passive victims who burn within an external fire; they themselves become the active fuel (*waqūd*) that sustains and inflames the inferno! Their own bodies, dense with the spiritual impurities of polytheism, arrogance, and cruelty, ignite like petroleum, generating the very flames that consume them. This represents the ultimate humiliation: human beings, created to be the noble crowns of creation, are degraded to the level of combustible kindling in the cellar of the cosmos, providing perpetual fuel for the fire that torments them.

In an authentic tradition recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1417* and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1016*, the Prophet ﷺ pleaded: “Guard yourselves against the Fire, even with half a date given in charity!” The verse urges the reader to take refuge in divine mercy today through sincere repentance, humble prayer. Compassionate charity, securing eternal protection from the scalding torment of Jahannam, and inheriting the tranquil gardens of everlasting peace.

## Verse 40:73

> Then they will be asked, “Where are those ˹idols˺ you used to associate

**The Last-Day Inquest: Thumma Qīla Lahum**

The seventy-third verse introduces a devastating verbal inquest amidst the physical torments of Hell: *thumma qīla lahum ayna mā kuntum tushrikūn*—Then they will be asked, “Where are those idols you used to associate... In Arabic syntax, the conjunction *thumma* marks a significant pivot from pure bodily punishment (being dragged in chains through boiling water and burned as fuel, vv. 71–72) to excruciating psychological and moral interrogation. The passive verbal construction *qīla lahum* ("it will be said to them" or "they will be asked") carries formidable judicial majesty: the question is delivered either directly by the Almighty or through the stern angelic wardens of Jahannam. The interrogation is not posed to extract new information, for God has perfect, eternal knowledge of all things. Rather, it functions as a formal Last-Day indictment (*al-tabkīt wa-l-taqrīʿ*) designed to compel the condemned to confront the absolute bankruptcy of their lifelong religious delusions, stripping away every residual pretense of innocence or excuse.

**The Total Impotence of False Protectors**

Classical commentators, including Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr, highlighted the profound religious lesson embedded in the disappearance of false deities. In the hour of ultimate catastrophe, authentic power is revealed by its ability to rescue. If an entity is truly divine, it should be capable of descending into the abyss, shattering the iron shackles, extinguishing the boiling water, and delivering its devotees from harm. Yet the idols, saints, kings, and worldly forces associated with God have zero agency, zero presence, and zero power in the Hereafter. Scripture portrays this identical interrogation across multiple Last-Day passages, as in Sūrah al-Anʿām: *“Where are your partners whom you used to claim?”* (6:22; cf. 10:28; 28:62). The complete disappearance of these false deities shows that idolatry was a grotesque cosmic fraud, leaving those who staked their eternal salvation on human inventions completely bankrupt and without hope.

## Verse 40:74

> with Allah?” They will cry, “They have ˹all˺ failed us. In fact, we did not invoke anything ˹real˺ before.” This is how Allah leaves the disbelievers to stray

**The Question and the Empty Answer**
The verse completes the interrogation that began one verse earlier: where are the partners you used to call on besides God? (40:73). The sentence continues into the answer, and the answer is said by the condemned themselves. They look around and say that the gods have left them, and then the sentence corrects them mid-speech: no, they never called on anything at all. The Book gives the same confession in the Sūrah of the Cattle, where the only words left to the idolaters on that day are a flat denial of what they used to do (6:23). What is left after a lifetime of devotion is the discovery that nothing had been there to answer.
**The Phrase That Does the Work**
*Min dūni Allāh* is the phrase the verse turns on: besides God, short of God, in a position lower than His. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī point out how much the words carry. The charge is not that these people denied God. It is that they set other things in a rank He does not share, and the verse shows the rank collapsing when it is tested. The gods, the ancestors, the stone, the market of opinion — none of it is there, and none of it was ever doing anything.
**The Sentence at the End**
The verse closes by stating what follows a life spent this way: God sends the disbelievers astray. That is not caprice and it is not added punishment. It is the naming of what already happened in the world, expressed once more, and the sūrah returns to it at 40:12, where the same people are told why their petition is refused. Between the two verses the reader is shown the same man three times: refusing the offer in this world, arguing about it on the way, and standing in front of a question he cannot answer.

## Verse 40:75

> ˹They will be told,˺ “This ˹punishment˺ is for being prideful on earth unjustly and for acting arrogantly

**The Reason Given After the Verdict**
*Dhālikum bimā kuntum tafraḥūna fī al-arḍi bi-ghayri al-ḥaqqi wa-bimā kuntum tamraḥūn* — that is because you used to exult in the earth without right, and because you behaved insolently. The verse follows a set of questions put to the people of the Fire, and it gives the cause of the sentence in the same sentence as the sentence itself. The Book usually states the punishment and then names the behaviour behind it, and the habit matters here because the punishment is the kind that looks disproportionate until the reason is read.
**The Two Words in the Verse**
The first is a rejoicing that has no right attached to it — *bi-ghayri al-ḥaqq* — and the second is arrogance in movement, the swagger of a person who walks as though he owns the road. The Sūrah of the Night Journey forbids the same thing: do not walk about the earth exultantly (17:37), and the advice of Luqmān to his son includes turning the face away from people and walking in a measured way, because God does not love any arrogant boaster (31:18). The verse therefore describes the two symptoms rather than the crime: the pleasure the powerful take in themselves, and the way they carry it when they walk into a room.
**Joy That Is Allowed**
The Book does not forbid gladness. In the Sūrah of Jonah it commands it: in the bounty of God and His mercy, let them rejoice (10:58). What the verse condemns is joy in a thing a person has no right to be joyful about — the joy of a man who has built a life on taking what is not his, or on being served by people he has reduced, and who finds the whole arrangement pleasant. The sūrah's point is that the pleasure is part of the offence. The man is not a reluctant wrongdoer; he is enjoying it, and the verse names the enjoyment as part of what the recompense answers. The reader is left with a small check that is harder than it looks: what does he take pleasure in?

## Verse 40:76

> Enter the gates of Hell, to stay there forever. What an evil home for the arrogant!”

**The Irrevocable Command of Eviction: Udkhulū Abwāba Jahannam**

The seventy-sixth verse delivers the final, crushing judicial decree that concludes the Last-Day trial of the rejecters: *udkhulū abwāba jahannama*—Enter the gates of Hell... In Arabic syntax, the imperative *udkhulū* ("enter") carries absolute judicial finality. Following the physical torments of chains, boiling water, and blazing fire (vv. 71–72), and the humiliating public inquest about their worthless idols (vv. 73–75), the condemned are formally evicted from the theater of cosmic probation into irreversible perdition.

**The Architecture of Stratified Retribution: Abwāb Jahannam**

The mention of the plural *abwāb* ("gates") reveals the complex, stratified architecture of infernal punishment: *abwāba jahannam*. In Islamic Last-Day theology, Hell is not a monolithic cavern, but a multi-tiered abyss consisting of distinct descending levels (*darakāt*), each entered through a specific gate corresponding to the gravity and nature of human transgression. Sūrah al-Ḥijr explicitly affirms this structure: *“It has seven gates; for each gate is of them a portion divided”* (15:44; cf. 39:71–72), establishing a meticulous organization of retribution across the infernal abyss. Exegetes observed that the gates of Hell correspond to the avenues through which human beings sin: the eye, the ear, the tongue, the stomach, the private parts, the hands, and the feet; or they represent distinct categories of moral corruption, such as tyranny, hypocrisy, polytheism, and sadistic cruelty. The arrogant are ushered through the specific gate that matches the precise configuration of their earthly sins, experiencing a closely tailored punishment that reflects every dimension of their rebellion against God.

**The Comprehensive Diagnosis: Mathwā al-Mutakabbirīn**

The verse culminates with an unshakeable moral verdict that summarizes the entire religious argument of Sūrah Ghāfir: *fa-biʾsa mathwā al-mutakabbirīn*—What an evil home for the arrogant! The exclamatory particle of dispraise *biʾsa* ("how wretched," "how evil") combines with the noun *mathwā* (an enduring abode, dwelling place, or final resting ground) to deliver an absolute condemnation. Crucially, the condemned are designated not merely as disbelievers, but specifically as *al-mutakabbirīn*—the arrogant, the proud, and the haughty. Throughout this chapter, arrogance (*kibr*) has been diagnosed as the primordial spiritual poison that blinds human intellects (v. 56), corrupts states (v. 35), and prevents souls from praying to their Lord (v. 60). Hell is revealed not as an arbitrary torture chamber, but as the exact of being home (*mathwā*) tailored for the arrogant ego: a place of absolute degradation, chains, and humiliation that permanently shatters all human conceit, demonstrating the total triumph of divine majesty over creaturely pride. The seventy-sixth verse concludes the grand narrative and Last-Day sequence of the Believer of Pharaoh's household, serving as a solemn, urgent crossroads for every reader. Scripture presents humanity with two mutually exclusive sets of gates: the seven gates of Hell (*abwāb Jahannam*), entered in chains of humiliation by the arrogant; or the eight gates of Paradise (*abwāb al-Jannah*), entered in radiant honor by the humble and righteous, where angels greet them with peace (39:73).

The damned are locked within an eternal present of retribution where death itself has been slaughtered, as reported in authentic prophetic traditions (*Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4730*; *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2849*).

## Verse 40:77

> So be patient ˹O Prophet˺. Surely Allah’s promise is true. Whether We show you some of what We threaten them with, or cause you to die ˹before that˺, to Us they will ˹all˺ be returned

**Be Patient — the Promise Is True**
*Fa-ṣbir inna waʿda Allāhi ḥaqq* — so be patient; the promise of God is true. The command comes twice in this sūrah, here and at verse 55, and the second time the situation has grown heavier: the verses just before this one have described the fires and the chains in detail, and the verse before that has told the Prophet that the men who argue about the signs are only deluding themselves. The order to wait is given to a man who has no leverage, no army, and no prospect of one in the years ahead.
**What the Patience Rests On**
The verse grounds the patience in a fact rather than a feeling: the promise is *ḥaqq*, true and binding. The Book uses that word for the promise of help, of the Hour, of the Garden, and it uses it when men are asked to hold a position that the visible facts contradict. A man can only be patient like that if he has something firmer than his own optimism to stand on, and the verse supplies it in four words before it gives any instruction about the outcome.
**The Two Things the Verse Offers as Relief**
Then the verse turns to what the reader's eye can actually watch. Either God shows the Prophet some part of what He has promised them, or He takes him before it comes — and in either case the duty is the same, because the Messenger is only a warner. The two outcomes are named side by side so that the man who is waiting does not make the timetable the point - and the Book states the same arrangement to the Prophet in another sūrah, where the duty named is only to deliver the message and the reckoning is left to God (13:40). Whether he lives to see the fulfilment or not, the message has done its work when it has been delivered, and the account is not his to settle.
**What the Sūrah Shows Him Looking At**
The verse hands the reader a task as well: if there are people in the world who have been given a taste of the outcome already, the road is open to them, and looking at what happened to earlier nations is the consolation the Book offers a messenger who sees no change in his own. The instruction is not to stop caring; it is to stop standing at the window watching for the day, and get on with the work.

## Verse 40:78

> We already sent messengers before you. We have told you the stories of some of them, while others We have not. It was not for any messenger to bring a sign without Allah’s permission. But when Allah’s decree comes, judgment will be passed with fairness, and the people of falsehood will then be in ˹total˺ loss

**The Sacred Canon vs. Unrecorded Messengers**

The verse expresses an essential religious principle about the scope of revealed scriptural history: *minhum man qaṣaṣnā ʿalayka wa-minhum man lam naqṣuṣ ʿalayk*—"We have told you the stories of some of them. Others We have not." In Islamic belief, the canonical Qur'an names twenty-five specific prophets (such as Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Saleh), providing detailed parables of their missions. However, the revelation explicitly clarifies that these named figures represent merely a modest sample of a vastly larger prophetic reality. In authentic traditions, the Prophet ﷺ indicated that God dispatched tens of thousands of prophets across human history to every distinct nation, tribe, and linguistic group on earth: *“And for every nation is a messenger”* (10:47; cf. 16:36; 35:24). This expansive theology shatters ethnocentrism, establishing that divine light and moral guidance were universally distributed across global civilizations, even if historical records of those ancient envoys were lost or corrupted over time. The verse directly addresses the obstinate demands of the pagan Quraysh, who constantly demanded that the Prophet perform customized, on-demand cosmic miracles: *wa-mā kāna li-rasūlin a yaʾtiya bi-āyatin illā bi-idhni al-llāh*—It was not for any messenger to bring a sign without Allah’s permission. In Islamic belief (*uṣūl al-dīn*), this clause defines the strict boundary between divine sovereignty and prophetic agency.

**The Uncompromising Fairness of Divine Judgment: Quḍiya bi-l-Ḥaqq**

The execution of the divine decree is characterized by flawless equity: *quḍiya bi-l-ḥaqq*—judgment will be passed with fairness, or "decided in absolute truth." In classical theology, *al-ḥaqq* means that divine judgment is completely free from bias, favoritism, cruelty, or error. God does not punish an individual based on hearsay, nor does He overlook a single atom of good or evil. Every intention is weighed, every action is examined, and every mitigating circumstance is considered by the All-Knowing Judge. The righteous are rewarded with boundless grace, and the transgressors are requited with exact retributive justice (40:40). No soul is wronged by the slightest fraction. The verdict of God commands universal assent from the consciences of all created beings, compelling even the damned to acknowledge the absolute justice of their condemnation, leaving creation in awe of divine righteousness.

## Verse 40:79

> It is Allah Who made cattle for you so that you may ride some and eat others

**Cattle Made for You**
*Allāhu alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-anʿām* — God is the One who made the livestock for you. *Al-anʿām* is the grazing animals: camels, cattle, sheep and goats, the four kinds the sūrah named at its opening as food and as the basis of the sacrifice (40:79 is followed by a full list). The verse does not simply say the animals exist; it says they were made *for you*, and the *for you* is the sentence's point. The Book keeps coming back to this: the animals are not part of a blind natural order that a person happens to use, but a provision arranged with the human being in mind.
**What the Verse Actually Lists**
The next verse gives the inventory, and the inventory is worth reading closely. *So that you may ride some of them, and eat some of them* (40:79 continues), and then more: you have benefits from them, and you may reach needs in your hearts through them, and on them and on ships you are carried. Riding and eating; milk, wool and hides; the journey that gets a man his living; and the verse adds the ships in the same breath, because a person could object that camels only help on land. The list covers the transport of a whole civilisation and the dinner on its tables, and it is laid out as an argument about who gave it. The sūrah of the Cattle puts the same animals to the same witness stand, naming them as livestock and grazing flocks (6:142), and the sūrah of Yā Sīn takes the reader to the same place: do they not see that We created for them, out of what Our hands have made, the livestock they own? (36:71).
**Where the Sign Is Being Looked For**
The sūrah has just shown the reader the universe (40:57, and the running argument about the signs from verse 3 onward), and here it brings the eye down to the level of the animals the listener sees every morning. The instruction that follows the list is the one the whole passage is leading to: *so which of the favours of your Lord will you deny?* (that is the refrain of the sūrah of the All-Merciful, 55:13, and the same logic closes this passage, and the sūrah of the Bee uses the same list at 16:5-8). The verse is a lesson in where arguments are lost: not at the level of the cosmos, which nobody can deny, but at the level of the animal a man rode that morning and gave no thought to.

## Verse 40:80

> Also, you find in them ˹other˺ benefits. And by means of them you may reach destinations you desire. And you are carried upon ˹some of˺ them and upon ships

**What the Cattle Were For**
*Wa-lakum fīhā manāfiʿu* — and you have benefits in them. The verse continues the passage on the animals, and it names what they give beyond food: warmth, hides, labour, milk, and the wealth that comes from selling them. The Sūrah of the Bee lists them in the same order, naming the warmth and the other benefits and the food, and adding the beauty they lend at the two ends of the day when they are brought in and taken out (16:5-6). The Book attaches the praise to the arrangement rather than to the animals, and it lists the uses one by one because a person who has grown up with them stops seeing them.
**The Needs in the Heart**
The next clause is more interesting: *wa-li-tablughū ʿalayhā ḥājatan fī ṣudūrikum* — so that you may reach, on them, a need in your hearts. That is the language of a long journey — a merchant carrying goods to a distant market, a family moving to another town — and the need in the heart is the whole inward world of plans and hopes that moves a person to travel. The Sūrah of the Romans states the same dependence in its creation passage, where the ships and the animals are given to carry people and their trade (30:46), and the Sūrah of Luqmān says that the ships run on the sea by God's favour so that people may seek His bounty (31:31).
**The Ships and the Whole Argument**
The verse ends by pairing the animals with the ships: on them and on the ships you are carried. The Sūrah of Abraham names the same pair among the mercies, with the ships placed under human control by God's command (14:32), and the Sūrah of the Bee joins the sea, the ships, and the fish that provides food (16:14). The passage in this sūrah is building one question, and the verse closes the ground for it: God shows you His signs; so which of the signs of God will you deny? (40:81). The list is not about livestock. It is about the fact that a person travels to another country and arrives alive.

## Verse 40:81

> And He shows you His signs. Now which of Allah’s signs will you deny

**The Incessant Revelation of Cosmic Tokens: Wa-Yurīkum Āyātih**

The eighty-first verse concentrates the entire cosmic, historical, biological, and technological discourse of Sūrah Ghāfir into a luminous religious climax: *wa-yurīkum āyātihi*—And He shows you His signs... In Arabic grammar, the imperfect verbal form *yurīkum* means continuous, repetitive, and unceasing divine display across all realms of being. God does not merely reveal His signs once in ancient history and then withdraw into celestial silence; He continuously, dynamically manifests His tokens to every generation of humanity. These signs (*āyāt*) are inscribed across three distinct domains: first, in the vast macrocosm of the heavens and the earth, the alternation of night and day, and the protective canopy of the atmosphere (vv. 57, 61, 64); second, in the intimate microcosm of the human anatomy, embryology, and the mysterious arc of life and death (vv. 67–68); and third, in the environmental provisions of livestock and maritime transport that sustain human society (vv. 79–80).

**The Intertextual Echo of Sūrah al-Raḥmān**

The wording of this challenge establishes a profound intertextual resonance with the famous refrain of Sūrah al-Raḥmān: *“So which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?”* (55:13, repeated 31 times). Classical commentators, including al-Zamakhsharī and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, noted the subtle, significant lexical distinction between the two chapters. While Sūrah al-Raḥmān challenges humanity about divine favors (*ālāʾ*), emphasizing gratitude for physical and spiritual blessings, Sūrah Ghāfir challenges humanity about divine signs (*āyāt*), emphasizing intellectual conviction and rational recognition of God’s sovereignty. In Sūrah Ghāfir, every favor is simultaneously a sign: the domestic camel is a physical blessing, but it is also an intellectual sign proving divine design.

## Verse 40:82

> Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those who were ˹destroyed˺ before them? They were far superior in might and ˹richer in˺ monuments throughout the land, but their ˹worldly˺ gains were of no benefit to them

**Travelling Through the Land**

The eighty-second verse commands a transformative empirical journey across terrestrial geography and historical time: *a-wa-lam yasīrū fī al-arḍi fa-yanẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu alladhīna min qablihim*—Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those who were destroyed before them?... In Arabic rhetoric, the interrogative *a-wa-lam* ("have they not then") functions as a severe reprimand against intellectual provincialism and historical amnesia across all epochs. The Qur'an commands humanity to engage in active physical and intellectual travel (*al-sayr fī al-arḍ*). Travel in the Qur'anic pattern is not an exercise in idle recreation, hedonistic tourism, or commercial exploitation; it is a sacred, empirical epistemological method designed to awaken the human heart. By walking among the ruins of past civilizations, observing silent tombs, shattered colossi, and abandoned fortress walls, the human mind is forced to confront the concrete, tangible reality of moral cause and historical effect, dispelling the seductive arrogance of the present moment.

---

The command to travel is the Book's standing invitation to read history on the ground, and it appears with the same words in several sūrahs: in the sūrah of the Romans (30:9), the sūrah of the Bee (16:36) and the sūrah of the Pilgrimage (22:46), where the verse adds that it is not the eyes that are blind but the hearts in the chests. The result the verse reports — a people greater in strength and in traces left on the land — is the argument from contrast: what they built did not save them, and what the hearers are building will not save them either. The next verse completes it by naming the messengers and the ridicule they met, so the travel is offered as a way to see the end of that pattern before it is repeated.

## Verse 40:83

> When their messengers came to them with clear proofs, they were prideful in whatever ˹worldly˺ knowledge they had, and were ˹ultimately˺ overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule

**When the Proofs Arrived, They Mocked**
*Fa-lammā jāʾathum rusuluhum bi-l-bayyināt* — when their messengers came to them with clear proofs. The proofs are named *bayyināt*: signs plain enough to be weighed, arguments that speak to a mind and a conscience rather than to fear. The messengers did not come to shut reason down; they came to free it from what had been holding it — the ancestors, the market, the throne. The verse says the nations were not short of evidence. What they did with it is the story.
**The Laughter of the Comfortable**
The reaction is named in one word: *yastahziʾūn*, they mocked. It was not calm disagreement but ridicule, and ridicule comes easily to people who are sure of their own position — the wealthy, the well-armed, the ones whose technology and walls made them feel safe from any threat, including a threat from God. The Qur'an records the standing line of that mockery: they ask when the promise will come, if the messengers are telling the truth (10:48; and see 21:38, 36:48). It is a question that sounds like a challenge to prove something, and is really a way of not thinking about the answer.
**What Happened to the Laughter**
The verse does not leave them with the last word. It ends by saying that what they used to mock closed in around them. The very thing they laughed at — the warning, the Day, the account — was the thing that came. That is the pattern the sūrah keeps returning to: the mockery is not harmless, and it does not stay a joke. It becomes the ground they are standing on when the promised thing arrives.

## Verse 40:84

> When they saw Our punishment, they cried, “˹Now˺ we believe in Allah alone and reject what we had been associating with Him!”

**When They Saw the Punishment**

The eighty-fourth verse captures the instantaneous, psychological collapse of human arrogance at the physical onset of divine retribution: *fa-lammā raʾaw baʾsanā*—When they saw Our punishment... In the old Arabic dictionaries, *al-baʾs* means overwhelming might, violent distress, crushing military force, and catastrophic divine torment that shatters all physical resistance across human history. The verb *raʾaw* ("they saw") marks a profound epistemological transition: the shift from the realm of the unseen (*al-ghayb*), where faith requires moral discernment and intellectual reflection, to the realm of direct visual witnessing (*al-shahādah*). Up to this moment, the proud elites had disputed divine signs, mocked prophetic warnings, and rested securely within their stone fortresses and technological expertise (vv. 82–83). But the instant the sky darkened with celestial fury, the earth convulsed beneath their feet, or the waters of the Red Sea rose above their heads, all philosophical skepticism dissolved in an instant, replaced by raw, unadulterated terror.

**The Pharaonic Archetype of Belated Submission**

The commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, highlighted the profound thematic resonance between this verse and the historical climax of Pharaoh’s life recorded in Sūrah Yūnus. When the split waters of the Red Sea collapsed upon Pharaoh and his elite cavalry, drowning the imperial superpower in roaring waves, Pharaoh cried out with identical desperate words: *“I believe that there is no deity except that in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am of the Muslims”* (10:90). Pharaoh’s deathbed conversion represents the universal archetype of authoritarian collapse: the tyrant who claimed absolute divinity and murdered innocents is reduced in his final drowning gasps to groveling for the mercy of the very Lord he mocked, providing an eternal lesson for all tyrants. The verse shows that every oppressor throughout history will ultimately prostrate before God; the only question is whether they do so voluntarily in life or involuntarily in death.

## Verse 40:85

> But their faith was of no benefit to them when they saw Our torment. This has ˹always˺ been Allah’s way ˹of dealing˺ with His ˹wicked˺ servants. Then and there the disbelievers were in ˹total˺ loss

The eighty-fifth verse delivers the definitive, solemn conclusion to Sūrah Ghāfir, establishing an unalterable cosmic and judicial law governing human repentance: *fa-lam yaku yanfaʿuhum īmānuhum lammā raʾaw baʾsanā*—But their faith was of no benefit to them when they saw Our torment... In Arabic syntax, the negative formulation *fa-lam yaku yanfaʿuhum* ("it did not avail them in the least") means the absolute, categorical nullification of legal and spiritual efficacy. When the deniers witnessed the physical arrival of divine destruction (v. 84), their frantic confessions of monotheism—*“we believe in Allah alone”*—were rejected by the celestial court.

**The Hadith of the Throat Rattle and the Closing of Repentance**

This foundational Qur'anic principle is mirrored across scripture and explicitly codified in prophetic tradition. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ affirms this exact threshold of repentance: *“And repentance is not of any use for those who do evil deeds till, when death confronts one of them, he says, ‘Truly now I repent,’ nor for those who die while they are disbelievers”* (4:18; cf. 6:158; 10:90–91). In an authentic tradition recorded in *Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3537* on the authority of ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ expressed the precise biological boundary of divine pardon: “Indeed, Allah accepts the repentance of a servant as long as the death rattle has not reached his throat (*mā lam yugharghir*).” Once the physical symptoms of death manifest and the angels of the next world become visible, the ledger of earthly deeds is sealed forever. The gate of repentance remains wide open throughout life, but it slams shut irrevocably the moment the veil of the material world is torn away.

**The Unalterable Cosmic Pattern: Sunnat Allāh**

The verse identifies this judicial principle not as an ad-hoc emergency measure, but as an eternal, structural law of divine governance: *sunnata al-llāhi allatī qad khalat fī ʿibādih*—This has always been Allah’s way of dealing with His servants. In Arabic theology, *Sunnat Allāh* designates the established, immutable pattern, norm, and protocol through which the Almighty governs human history and moral reality. The Qur'an stresses the unshakeable permanence of this divine pattern: *“And you will never find in the way of Allah any change, and you will never find in the way of Allah any alteration”* (35:43; cf. 33:62; 48:23). The phrase *qad khalat* ("which has passed" or "been established across previous eras") reminds humanity that this rule applied to the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, Sodom, and Pharaoh. God never accepts coerced faith at the point of destruction; to alter this rule would compromise divine justice and render the moral struggles of the righteous meaningless.

**The Theological Balance of Mercy and Justice in Sūrah Ghāfir**

The concluding verse provides an exquisite religious bookend that harmonizes the entire thematic architecture of Sūrah Ghāfir. The chapter opened in its earliest verses by revealing the majestic, dual attributes of the Almighty: He is *Ghāfir al-dhanb* (the Forgiver of sin) and *Qābil al-tawb* (the Accepter of repentance), but He is simultaneously *Shadīd al-ʿiqāb* (Severe in punishment) and *Dhū al-ṭawl* (Possessor of boundless bounty) (40:1–3). The eighty-fifth verse shows how these attributes operate in absolute harmony across human history. Divine mercy is vast, patient, and welcoming to every repentant sinner during their earthly probation, as showed by the Believer's summons in the royal court (vv. 28–44). However, when human beings deliberately abuse divine forbearance, persisting in arrogance, oppression, and skepticism until the final hour strikes, divine justice activates in its full, unyielding severity (*shadīd al-ʿiqāb*), leaving the unrepentant without defense.
