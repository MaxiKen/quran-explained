# Sūrah Hūd (Chapter 11) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Sūrah of Prophetic Burden and Moral Uprightness**

Hūd is the eleventh chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 123 verses of late Makkan revelation. It takes its name from the Arabian prophet Hūd, sent to the towering, arrogant civilization of ʿĀd, whose dramatic confrontation occupies verses 50–60. In prophetic tradition, Sūrah Hūd carries an extraordinary emotional and spiritual gravity: when Abū Bakr remarked on the premature whitening of the Prophet's hair, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ replied: *“Sūrah Hūd and its sisters have aged me”* (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3297). Exegetes note that this holy trembling was specifically induced by verse 112: *“So stand firm as you have been commanded, along with those who have turned to Allah with you, and do not transgress.”* This command demanded unswerving, lifelong uprightness (*istiqāmah*) without bending toward compromise, tyranny, or fatigue. Preceded by Yūnus and succeeded by Yūsuf, Hūd stands as a terrifyingly vivid courtroom of prophetic sacred history, where the covenantal relationship between prophets and their recalcitrant communities is laid bare with uncompromising moral clarity.

**The Architecture and Movement of the Discourse**

The structure of Sūrah Hūd is built around a majestic doctrinal frame enclosing six epic narrative suites. The prologue (1–24) opens with the *Alif-Lām-Rā* of the Decisive Book, presenting the universal call to monotheism, exposing the absurdity of those who wrap their chests to hide from God, celebrating the divine guarantee of sustenance for every crawling creature on earth (11:6), and challenging the skeptics to produce ten fabricated sūrahs like it (11:13). The second movement (25–49) presents the longest and most emotionally devastating account of Noah in the Qur'an: the building of the Ark under the mockery of his chieftains, the bursting forth of the subterranean ovens and torrential skies. The heartbreaking dialogue between Noah and his disbelieving son, who seeks refuge on a mountain only to be swept away by the surging wave, prompting the divine rebuke that an unrighteous offspring is no true part of a prophet's spiritual household. The third movement (50–95) rolls out the successive destructions of ancient civilizations: Hūd and the catastrophic wind that shattered the giants of ʿĀd; Ṣāliḥ and the sacred she-camel hamstrung by the insolent elders of Thamūd; Abraham receiving the angelic messengers bound for Sodom; the terrifying twilight confrontation of Lot with the depraved mob surrounding his guests; Shuʿayb warning the merchants of Midian against fraud and economic injustice; and Moses confronting Pharaoh's tyranny. The fourth movement (96–123) extracts the universal moral laws of history: that God never destroys a town unjustly while its people are actively reforming (*muṣliḥūn*, 11:117), culminating in the thunderous injunction to stand firm (*fa-staqim*, 11:112), to establish the prayer at both ends of the day and in the watches of the night to erase evil deeds (11:114). Sealing the chapter with the serene assurance: *“And to Allah belongs the unseen of the heavens and the earth, and to Him the whole matter will be returned; so worship Him and rely upon Him.”*

## Verse 11:1

> Alif-Lãm-Ra. ˹This is˺ a Book whose verses are well perfected and then fully explained. ˹It is˺ from the One ˹Who is˺ All-Wise, All-Aware

**Three Letters That Refuse to Explain Themselves**

Three consonants stand alone before any sentence begins: alif, lām, rāʾ. They form no word, carry no grammatical case, and attach to nothing that follows them. Twenty-nine of the Qurʾān's 114 sūrahs open with letters of this kind, and those twenty-nine draw on only fourteen of the twenty-eight letters of the Arabic alphabet. No explanation of them comes from the Prophet himself, and the absence of one is doing work. An audience that measured a man's worth by his command of speech met, in the first breath of this recitation, the bare material of its own language laid out like unworked stone, and then met a book assembled from that material which it could not match. The same triad heads Yūnus (10:1), Yūsuf (12:1), Ibrāhīm (14:1) and al-Ḥijr (15:1); in al-Raʿd it carries an added mīm (13:1). Repeated across a family of sūrahs, the letters function as a signature rather than a stray ornament, marking a set of chapters that all begin by naming the Book before naming anything else.

**The Chapter That Turned the Prophet's Hair Grey**

A report connects this sūrah to a physical change in the Prophet. Abū Bakr remarked on the whiteness of his hair. The answer named this chapter among its causes: Hūd, al-Wāqiʿah, al-Mursalāt, ʿAmma yatasāʾalūn and Idhā al-shamsu kuwwirat had turned it grey (Sunan al-Tirmidhī 3297, from Ibn ʿAbbās, called ḥasan gharīb by al-Tirmidhī; a companion form in al-Shamāʾil al-Muḥammadiyyah 42 records Abū Juḥayfa asking the same question and receiving the shorter answer, Hūd and her sisters). Qurtubī preserves Ibn ʿAbbās's judgement that no verse came down harder on the Prophet than the command to hold firm, *"So be steadfast as you are commanded"* (11:112), and the hair report is normally tied to that verse. What greys a man here is not fear of the next world by itself. Hūd stands out for the density with which it narrates destruction inside history: the flood, the blast that left Thamūd lifeless in their own houses, the stones marked and rained on Lūṭ's towns, the cry that flattened Midian. A messenger told to expect that treatment from his own townsmen could not read these pages as literature.

**What Firmness Demands of the Reader**

A text that has been set firm does not bend to suit whoever opens it, and that is where the opening line stops being a boast and becomes a demand. If the verses are placed and free of contradiction, then an apparent difficulty marks the limit of a reader's understanding rather than a flaw in the text, and the honest response is to keep reading instead of amending. The Qurʾān puts the same point in a harder form elsewhere: *"The Word of your Lord has been perfected in truth and justice. None can change His Words"* (6:115), and it presents itself as *"a confirmation of what came before, and an explanation of the Scripture"* (10:37). Firmness also fixes the terms on which the sūrah's argument will run. Its accounts of Nūḥ, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Ibrāhīm, Lūṭ, Shuʿayb and Mūsā are not offered as edifying legend that can be trimmed to taste; they are set down as a closed record with one moral running through all of it. That is why the sūrah can move from three isolated letters straight to the demand for exclusive worship without any bridge. A Book whose joints are true needs none.

The same double claim runs through the Qurʾān's account of itself, which calls it *"a Book whose verses are perfectly explained—a Quran in Arabic for people who know"* (41:3) and stakes its divine origin on the absence of internal conflict: *"Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies"* (4:82).

## Verse 11:2

> ˹Tell them, O Prophet,˺ “Worship none but Allah. Surely I am a warner and deliverer of good news to you from Him

**Warning First, Good News Second**

The verse names two offices and their order here is unusual. Across the Qurʾān the pairing normally runs the other way: God sends His messenger *"as a deliverer of good news and a warner to all of humanity"* (34:28), and again *"as a witness, a deliverer of good news, and a warner"* (48:8). In those statements the good news leads. Here, and in the one other place where the Prophet states the office in his own voice — *"I am only a warner and deliverer of good news for those who believe"* (7:188) — the warning comes first. The difference tracks the audience. When God describes the mission in general terms, mercy is foregrounded. When a messenger stands in front of a specific town that has just been told to abandon its gods, the danger is the nearer fact. Mekka in this period was not asking for reassurance. It was being asked to surrender the religious economy of its own sanctuary, and a warning placed ahead of the good news is honest about the price of the question being put.

**Neither Office Can Be Dropped**

Joining the two functions protects the message from the two ways it is ordinarily ruined. A messenger who only warns becomes a figure of doom, and the hearer learns to treat him as an enemy rather than an adviser. A messenger who only reassures becomes a source of comfort without consequence, and the hearer concludes that nothing is required of him. The Qurʾān closes both escapes by binding the roles into one person, and it does this not only for this Prophet but for the office itself: *"We do not send the messengers except as deliverers of good news and warners"* (18:56). The reason for the pairing is given in another verse: they were sent *"so humanity should have no excuse before Allah after ˹the coming of˺ the messengers"* (4:165). An excuse is removed only when both halves arrive. A warning unheard leaves the hearer unaccountable; a promise unheard leaves him without hope. Together the two offices produce the single condition under which a person can be judged fairly — fully informed and not coerced.

## Verse 11:3

> And seek your Lord’s forgiveness and turn to Him in repentance. He will grant you a good provision for an appointed term and graciously reward the doers of good. But if you turn away, then I truly fear for you the torment of a formidable Day

**Two Movements, Not a Single One**

The verse gives two instructions in sequence: seek forgiveness, then turn. The order is not decorative. *Istighfār* is a request addressed outward — a person asks that a debt be cancelled. *Tawbah* is a movement of the body and the will — the same person turns around and walks the other way. A request without the turn is a plea that costs nothing; a turn without the request is self-improvement that never settles what is owed. Classical exegesis reads the pair against the specific situation of an idolatrous town: abandoning the idols is itself the seeking of forgiveness for the sin of setting partners beside God, while devoting oneself wholly to Him is the turning. That reading makes the two verbs concrete rather than merely devotional. It also explains why the demand follows the command to worship none but Allah in the previous verse. Worship is the destination; forgiveness and turning are the two steps by which a person actually leaves where he is standing. The Qurʾān joins them again at the very end of revelation: *"then glorify the praises of your Lord and seek His forgiveness, for certainly He is ever Accepting of Repentance"* (110:3).

**What "Good Provision" Really Promises**

The reward offered for repentance is not mainly otherworldly. *Mataʿan ḥasanan* — a good provision, a good enjoyment — belongs to this life, and the commentators take it as sustenance and blessing while a person is still alive. One reading pushes it further and treats the good provision as the quality of the life itself: the believer enjoys what he has because he hopes in God and is content with His decree, while the denier can enjoy only the objects themselves. But you can hope to receive from Allah what they can never hope for"* (4:104).

**An Appointed Term Is Still a Term**

The provision is granted *for an appointed term*, and the term is death. The Qurʾān states the same limit in a verse about dying: *"No soul can ever die without Allah's Will at the destined time"* (3:145).

**Grace Added on Top of the Wage**

After the provision comes a second, different reward: God will graciously give to the doers of good. The shift of register matters. The first gift was described as provision, something that meets a need; the second is described as grace, something given beyond what the act deserves. Exegetes take this as the reward of the Hereafter, apportioned to the quality of a person's obedience, and they read it as exceeding the deed rather than matching it. The Qurʾān's ordinary language of recompense makes the same point: *"For them are ranks in the sight of their Lord, and forgiveness and a generous provision"* (8:4). A strict wage would be intolerable, since no act of worship equals the blessings already received; grace is what makes the accounting survivable.

## Verse 11:4

> To Allah is your return. And He is Most Capable of everything.”

**Four Words That Carry the Whole Case**

This is the shortest verse in the opening of the sūrah and it does the heaviest work. *To Allah is your return* — three words in Arabic, and the entire moral structure of the chapter rests on them. Nothing before this point has established a court, a judge, or an accounting. The command to worship, the call to repentance, the promise of provision, the fear of a formidable Day: each of these assumes that the story does not end inside a lifetime. This verse supplies the assumition. Without a return, the promise of reward is sentiment and the threat of punishment is rhetoric, since the wicked who die prosperous would have won. With a return, both become statements about a settled fact that has not yet arrived. The formulation is not peculiar to this sūrah; the Qurʾān repeats it as a refrain, from *"To Him is your return all together"* (10:4) to *"To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. And to Allah is the final return"* (24:42). Its repetition is itself evidence of its function: it is the axiom on which every other claim in the Book depends.

**A Reversion to Ownership, Not a Journey**

The word translated as return does not describe travel. It describes something going back to where it already belongs. The same root appears in the words a believer says at a death: *"Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return"* (2:156), where belonging comes first and returning second. That ordering is the meaning. A person is not an independent agent who travels to God at the end of his life; he is held property throughout it, and death is the moment the holding becomes visible. The commentators draw the consequence for the Day: what happens there is not the arrival of a stranger before an unfamiliar judge but the accounting of a tenant whose lease has run out. This reading also explains why the verse is placed at the end of a call to worship rather than at the end of a threat.

**Power Is Named Exactly Where Escape Would Be Imagined**

The second clause is a statement of capability, and its placement is strategic. *He is Most Capable of everything* follows immediately on the return, and it closes the two doors through which a hearer might try to leave the argument. The first door is avoidance: if the return can be dodged, hidden, delayed or bargained out of, the whole demand collapses. The second door is incompetence: perhaps the One who commands is willing but unable, in which case the threats are aspirations. Naming absolute capability after naming the return shuts both. The Qurʾān pairs the two ideas repeatedly and in the same order, as in *"To Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. He gives life and causes death. And He is Most Capable of everything"* (57:2), and in the verse that ties capability to interior knowledge: *"Whether you reveal what is in your hearts or conceal it, Allah will call you to account for it... And Allah is Most Capable of everything"* (2:284). That last pairing matters here, because the very next verse of this sūrah is about people who fold their chests to hide what is inside them.

## Verse 11:5

> Indeed, they enfold ˹what is in˺ their hearts, ˹trying˺ to hide it from Him! But even when they cover themselves with their clothes, He knows what they conceal and what they reveal. Surely He knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart

**Folding the Chest Over Its Own Contents**

The central image is physical. *Yathnūna ṣudūrahum* — they fold, bend or double their chests. The commentators give the gesture two readings and both are worth keeping. The first is that a person curls inward, hunching the chest over the heart so that what is inside will not leak out through the face or the voice; the posture of a man sitting in a gathering, hearing something he hates, and trying not to show it. The second reading is transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, who is said to have recited the word in a form meaning *their chests were folded over them*. Who connected the verse to people who disliked exposing themselves to the sky; the sense is a person covering and curling at the same time.

**Concealment Aimed at the Only One It Cannot Work Against**

The object of the hiding is given as *minhu* — from Him. Not from the neighbours, not from the gathering, not from the Prophet: from God. That single preposition contains the whole indictment. Concealment from other people is ordinary social behaviour and often legitimate; a person is entitled to keep his thoughts from a hostile room. Concealment from God is a category error, because it treats the divine knowledge as located somewhere it can be blocked — behind a wall, outside a tent, on the other side of a curtain. The verse answers the error by escalating: they fold their chests, and He knows; they pull their garments over their heads, and He knows; the knowledge is stated twice, the second time in a form that reaches the interior of the heart itself. The Qurʾān makes the same point about its own knowledge of the unseen, extending it to objects far smaller than a thought: *"Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth or anything—green or dry—but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record"* (6:59).

**The Garment Used as a Door**

The second gesture is *yastaghshūna thiyābahum* — they cover themselves with their clothes. The same combination of gestures appears in Nūḥ's complaint about his own people, and there the sequence is explicit: *"And whenever I invite them to be forgiven by You, they press their fingers into their ears, cover themselves with their clothes, persist ˹in denial˺, and act very arrogantly"* (71:7).

**What the Heart Carries Is the Thing That Is Known**

The verse closes by naming the object of divine knowledge precisely: He knows best *what is in the heart* — literally *dhāt al-ṣudūr*, the owner-possessor of the chests, the thing that the chest contains. The idiom is worth holding, because it does not say that God knows the chest. He knows its contents, and He knows them better than anyone, including the person carrying them. The phrase is a standing description across the Book, attached wherever divine knowledge is set against human pretence: *"Whether you speak secretly or openly—He surely knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart"* (13:13), and *"Indeed, Allah is the Knower of the unseen of the heavens and the earth. He surely knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart"* (35:38). Two consequences follow. The first is that no performance is safe. A well-delivered speech with a bad intention behind it is not evaluated on its delivery. The second is subtler and more uncomfortable: a person may not fully know his own motives, and the knowledge that judges him is more accurate than his self-assessment. The verse that follows turns from the heart to the creature that crawls, and the movement is not random. The same knowledge that tracks the fold of a chest tracks the provision of every living thing.

## Verse 11:6

> There is no moving creature on earth whose provision is not guaranteed by Allah. And He knows where it lives and where it is laid to rest. All is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record

**Every Moving Thing Has an Address With God**

The verse opens with the widest possible scope: not one creature on earth moves whose provision is not with God. The Arabic *dābba* covers everything that crawls, walks or creeps, and the Qurʾān uses the word elsewhere to include birds and to make a larger claim about animal life: *"All living beings roaming the earth and winged birds soaring in the sky are communities like yourselves. We have left nothing out of the Record"* (6:38). The scope here is deliberately exhaustive, and the exhaustiveness is the argument. If provision is guaranteed for a creature no one has named, in a place no one has visited, then the question of a believer's sustenance is settled before it is asked. The verse is often read devotionally, as reassurance, and that reading is not wrong, but it misses the sharper edge. Provision guaranteed is also provision allotted: the amount, the timing and the route are fixed, and effort changes the route without changing the amount. The Qurʾān states the same fact about creatures that cannot plan at all: *"How many are the creatures that cannot secure their provisions! ˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ provides for them and you ˹as well˺"* (29:60).

**"Upon Allah" Without Making Allah a Debtor**

The phrase translated as guaranteed is *ʿalā Allāh*, literally upon God, and it raised a problem that the commentators address directly. Read literally, an obligation resting upon God would seem to contradict the supremacy of His will: a being who owes something is constrained by the debt. Their resolution is that the obligation is one God has taken on Himself by promise, not one imposed on Him from outside. The distinction is precise and it matters. A self-assumed commitment binds because the promiser is truthful, not because the promiser is compelled. The Qurʾān elsewhere names God as the source of provision without qualification: *"Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ is the Supreme Provider—Lord of all Power, Ever Mighty"* (51:58), where the title is joined to power precisely to keep the giving free. So the verse asserts a certainty on the creature's side and no compulsion on God's. A believer can rely on the provision absolutely; nobody can demand it as a debt. Both halves are needed, because a promise that could be broken is no comfort and a God who could be forced is not God.

**Nothing Falls Outside the Record**

The verse ends on a Book. *All is in a clear Record* — a kitāb mubīn, plain, manifest, leaving nothing ambiguous. Commentators identify this with the Preserved Tablet, the same reality described elsewhere: *"˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet"* (85:22), while others take it simply as God's knowledge, which amounts to the same claim from a different angle. The same formula appears twice more in almost identical words, and both times it closes a statement about the reach of divine knowledge. In al-Anʿām it follows the falling leaf and the grain in the darkness of the earth (6:59). In Yūnus it follows an assertion that no deed goes unobserved: *"Not ˹even˺ an atom's weight is hidden from your Lord on earth or in heaven; nor anything smaller or larger than that, but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record"* (10:61). The three occurrences together define what the Record is: not a diary of great events but an inventory of small ones. That is the note on which the sūrah's opening turns from doctrine to history. The verses that follow will describe creation, the delay of punishment and the demand for a miracle, and every one of those subjects is governed by the same fact — nothing is unrecorded, so nothing is unaccounted for.

The Qurʾān makes the same promise to those who leave something for His sake: *"and provide for them from sources they could never imagine"* (65:3).

## Verse 11:7

> He is the One Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days—and His Throne was upon the waters—in order to test which of you is best in deeds. And if you ˹O Prophet˺ say, “Surely you will ˹all˺ be raised up after death,” the disbelievers will certainly say, “That is nothing but pure magic!”

**A Throne Over Water Before There Was Any Sky**

The clause *and His Throne was upon the waters* creates an immediate difficulty, and the commentators face it squarely. If the heavens and the earth were still being made, what was the water sitting in, and what was the throne resting on? Rāzī's resolution is that *upon* here does not describe a location in space, since there was no space yet to be located in. The phrase indicates something else: the manner in which the heavens were raised and held without any support beneath them. That reading has a parallel in the Qurʾān's own description: *"It is Allah Who has raised the heavens without pillars—as you can see"* (13:2). The image of water is also the oldest available image of unformed matter, and it appears in the first chapter of Genesis in the same position. A report in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim supplies the traditional frame for the phrase: ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ heard the Prophet say that Allah ordained the measures of creation fifty thousand years before He created the heavens and the earth, and His Throne was upon water (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2653b). The decree precedes the making.

**Six Days and No Seventh of Rest**

The six days recur across the Qurʾān — in al-Aʿrāf, Yūnus, al-Furqān, al-Sajdah, Qāf and al-Ḥadīd — and in most of those places the account continues with God establishing Himself on the Throne (7:54; 10:3; 25:59). Two features of the number matter. The first is that it is a number at all: creation is described as ordered, staged and completed, not as a single undifferentiated act. The second is what does not follow it. The biblical account ends with a day of rest; the Qurʾānic account explicitly refuses fatigue: *"Indeed, We created the heavens and the earth and everything in between in six Days, and We were not ˹even˺ touched with fatigue"* (50:38). The stages are for the creature's comprehension, not the Creator's convenience. That refusal of exhaustion carries directly into the argument about resurrection, since a being who tires might reasonably decline to begin the work a second time. The denial of fatigue removes that excuse before it can be made.

**The Purpose Clause Reorders the Whole Verse**

Everything before the phrase *to test* is preparation for it. God created the heavens and the earth — and the stated end is a trial. The clause is precise about what is being measured: *which of you is best in deeds*, not which of you is most many, most eloquent, or most fortunate. The formulation is repeated with a variation that sharpens it further, where death and life themselves are described as instruments of the same measurement: *"˹He is the One˺ Who created death and life to test which of you is best in deeds"* (67:2). Another verse puts the adornment of the earth in the same frame: *"We have indeed made whatever is on earth as an adornment for it, to test which of them is best in deeds"* (18:7). Three passages, one formula. The world is fitted out as an examination room, and the examination grades quality of action rather than volume of outcome. That is why a life that produces little but does it well outranks one that produces much carelessly.

**"Pure Magic" Is the Last Word of an Exhausted Opponent**

The verse ends by rehearsing the response: announce the resurrection and the answer is that this is nothing but pure magic. *Siḥr mubīn* — evident, manifest sorcery — is the standard dismissal across the Qurʾān, applied to Mūsā's signs, to ʿĪsā's, and to this recitation: *"And the disbelievers say of the truth when it has come to them, 'This is nothing but pure magic!'"* (34:43). The word choice deserves attention. Sorcery is what a person calls a sign he cannot explain and does not intend to examine.

The trial is real, and its fairness is guaranteed by a limit stated as a rule: *"Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford"* (2:286).

## Verse 11:8

> And if We delay their punishment until an appointed time, they will definitely say, “What is holding it back?” Indeed, on the Day it overtakes them, it will not be averted from them, and they will be overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule

**A Delay Read as a Withdrawal**

The verse describes a specific failure of reasoning, and it names the moment at which it happens. A punishment is promised, time passes, nothing occurs, and the hearer concludes that the promise was empty. The inference feels natural and is completely wrong, because it treats the passage of time as information about the promise rather than as part of its terms. The verse is explicit that the delay is *until an appointed time* — the delay has a boundary set before it begins.

**An Appointed Time Is Set Before the Delay Begins**

The phrase *until an appointed time* does the quiet work of the verse. *Ajal musammā* — a term named, specified, marked down — is the same expression used for the fixed span of a human life and for the fixed term of a community. Its presence here means the delay is not open-ended and therefore not evidence of anything. It also means the delay is itself part of the decree rather than a failure of it. The Qurʾān states the same principle in general terms: *"If Allah were to hasten evil for people as they wish to hasten good, they would have certainly been doomed"* (10:11). The delay is mercy operating on people who are reading it as indifference. That is the uncomfortable core of the verse. Nothing is being withheld out of weakness or indecision; something is being withheld out of forbearance, and the recipient is citing the forbearance as proof that there is nothing to forbear about. The appointed time guarantees that the interval closes.

**Mockery Comes Back Around on the Mocker**

The verse closes with a reversal stated in one clause: they will be overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule. The thing they laughed at becomes the thing that envelops them, and the formula is used repeatedly in the Qurʾān as a standing law rather than a local description. *"˹Other˺ messengers had already been ridiculed before you ˹O Prophet˺, but those who mocked them were overtaken by what they used to ridicule"* (6:10) — the same sentence appears again at 21:41. Three other verses repeat the final clause with only the tense changed: *"and they were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule"* (16:34; 39:48; 45:33). The repetition is doing argumentative work. Mockery is not treated as a free act with no consequence; it is treated as an investment in a particular outcome, and the outcome arrives on the mocker's side of the ledger. That is why this sūrah, which narrates town after town destroyed after warning, keeps returning to ridicule as the common note. Each of those peoples had a moment in which the warning was funny. This verse identifies the joke as the turning point.

## Verse 11:9

> If We give people a taste of Our mercy then take it away from them, they become utterly desperate, ungrateful

**Despair as the First Response**

The first of the two descriptions is *yaʾūs* — despondent, cut off from hope. The word denotes not ordinary sadness but the collapse of expectation: a person who has stopped believing that anything better is coming. The commentators locate the cause precisely. The despair arises from a deficit of hope in God, a lack of reliance on Him, and an inability to wait. None of those is a statement about the size of the loss. They are statements about the person's orientation while the loss occurs. A person anchored in God treats a withdrawal as a change in circumstance; a person anchored in the thing itself treats it as a change in his worth. The Qurʾān names the same collapse in a shorter verse: *"When We grant people Our favours, they turn away, acting arrogantly. But when touched with evil, they lose all hope"* (17:83), and again: *"One never tires of praying for good. And if touched with evil, they become desperate and hopeless"* (41:49). The pairing is exact and it exposes the asymmetry. The same person is insatiable in asking and bottomless in despairing.

**Ungrateful Because There Was Nothing to Be Grateful To**

The second description, *kafūr*, is stronger than the English suggests. It is the intensive form of the root behind unbelief: not merely unthankful but actively covering over. The word describes a person who receives a benefit and then behaves as though no benefit had been given. That is the logical shape of the verse's complaint. When the mercy is withdrawn, the person's account of the past is rewritten: the years in which he was given become, in his memory, years of nothing, and the giver disappears from the record. Ingratitude here is not a lapse in manners but a revision of history. The Qurʾān treats it as the ordinary failure of unanchored people and describes its extreme form in a man whose posture is given an image: *"And there are some who worship Allah on the verge ˹of faith˺: if they are blessed with something good, they are content with it. But if they are afflicted with a trial, they relapse ˹into disbelief˺, losing this world and the Hereafter"* (22:11). Standing on a verge means that either wind can push you off.

**A Pattern, Not an Incident**

The verse is written in the conditional, and that generality is what makes it a diagnosis rather than a story. *If We give... Then We take away... They become.* No town is named, no individual is singled out, and no period is specified. The Qurʾān repeats the same conditional structure often enough that it functions as a law of the soul: *"If We give people a taste of mercy, they become prideful ˹because˺ of it. But if they are afflicted with an evil for what their hands have done, they instantly fall into despair"* (30:36). Note what that parallel adds. The pride on receiving and the despair on losing are two faces of one error — the person has attached his estimate of himself to his circumstances, so both directions of change move him. The sūrah's own sequence makes the same point by putting the two conditions in adjacent verses, and the reader is meant to see a single character in both. Generality also makes the verse apply forward. Every reader is inside the conditional; nobody is exempt from having tasted something and lost it.

The commentators attach to this passage a saying the Prophet reported from his Lord: *I am as My slave thinks I am* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7405).

## Verse 11:10

> But if We give them a taste of prosperity after being touched with adversity, they say, “My ills are gone,” and become totally prideful and boastful

**The Sentence They Say Out Loud**

The verse reports speech, and the speech is short: *My ills are gone.* Three words carry the entire diagnosis. Not *the hardship has ended*, which would be a statement about circumstances, but *my ills*, which is a statement about a personal condition that has now been removed. The commentators take *al-sayyiʾāt* here as the afflictions themselves — the difficulties that had pressed on the person — and the significance lies in what the sentence leaves out. Nothing in it acknowledges a giver. The relief has arrived and the person describes it as a change in his own state, the way one might describe a fever breaking. This is the precise moment the verse is isolating: not the joy of recovery, which is unremarkable, but the shape of the sentence in which the joy is expressed. A person's grammar under relief reveals his theology more reliably than his grammar under prayer.

**A Claim About Cause, Made Without Evidence**

*My ills are gone* is not only a description; it is an account of causation. The person has concluded that the ills are gone, and the natural completion of that thought is *because of something I did*. The Qurʾān states the completion explicitly in a parallel verse: *"Then when We shower Our blessings upon them, they say, 'I have been granted all this only because of ˹my˺ knowledge.'"* — and then refuses it outright: *"Not at all! It is ˹no more than˺ a test. But most of them do not know"* (39:49).

**Prideful, Then Boastful**

Two adjectives follow the speech, and they mark two directions of the same movement. *Fariḥ* describes an inward swelling — delight that has become self-satisfaction. *Fakhūr* describes its outward expression: the person who announces his excellence to others. The order is significant. The inward state comes first and produces the public behaviour, so the boasting is not a separate fault but the visible half of one fault. The Qurʾān treats this pair as the predictable consequence of mercy received without recognition: *"If We give people a taste of mercy, they become prideful ˹because˺ of it"* (30:36), and again *"when We let someone taste a mercy from Us, they become prideful ˹because˺ of it"* (42:48). The preposition in those verses — prideful *because of it* — identifies the mercy itself as the occasion of the pride. The gift becomes the evidence of superiority. That is the mechanism by which a blessing turns into a spiritual liability, and it explains why the Qurʾān so often pairs the description of divine generosity with a warning rather than a celebration.

## Verse 11:11

> except those who patiently endure and do good. It is they who will have forgiveness and a mighty reward

**Patience as One Half of Faith**

The weight given to patience here is not local to this sūrah. A saying of ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, preserved in the works of the early scholars and quoted by Ibn al-Qayyim, divides faith into two halves: one half patience, the other gratitude. The same combination appears in the Qurʾān itself, where the people capable of reading the signs are described in one breath: *"Surely in this are signs for whoever is steadfast, grateful"* (14:5). The pairing is structural rather than ornamental. Faith requires a person to do what has been commanded and to leave what has been forbidden, and both halves require endurance: doing takes it, and abstaining takes it more. The Qurʾān commands patience as a standing obligation and attaches a promise to it that no other virtue carries in the same form: *"O believers! Seek comfort in patience and prayer. Allah is truly with those who are patient"* (2:153). Company is the reward here, not a prize. That is what makes patience a condition of the exception in this verse rather than an admirable extra.

**Forgiveness Placed Before Reward**

Two things are promised, and their order matters. Forgiveness comes first, and the mighty reward comes second. A person carrying an unpaid debt cannot enjoy an incoming payment; the payment is consumed by the debt before it reaches him. Placing forgiveness first acknowledges that every human being arrives at the accounting owing something, and it removes the objection that would otherwise meet any promise of reward — namely, that the deeds offered are not clean enough to be paid for. The Qurʾān makes the same arrangement in its covenants: *"I will certainly forgive your sins and admit you into Gardens under which rivers flow"* (5:12), where the cancellation precedes the gift.

**A Mighty Reward, Not a Proportionate One**

The reward is described as *great* — ʿaẓīm — and the adjective is doing work against any try to calculate it. Patience is not compensated in proportion to its cost; if it were, the person who suffered most would receive most, and suffering would become a currency. The Qurʾān elsewhere removes the arithmetic altogether: *"Only those who endure patiently will be given their reward without limit"* (39:10). The phrase *without limit* — bi-ghayri ḥisāb — describes a payment that is not counted, and it is applied to patience alone among the virtues. That is a striking allocation. Prayer, charity and pilgrimage all have specified rewards; patience is the one whose reward the Book refuses to quantify. Read against this verse, the *mighty reward* is not a large number but the same uncounted thing described in a shorter phrase. It follows that the exception here is not a trade. Nobody endures hardship to purchase an outcome; the reward is attached to a disposition, and the disposition is what is being formed by the enduring.

## Verse 11:12

> Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ may wish to give up some of what is revealed to you and may be distressed by it because they say, “If only a treasure had been sent down to him, or an angel had come with him!” You are only a warner, and Allah is the Trustee of All Affairs

**A Verse Addressed to the Messenger's Chest**

Most of the sūrah's early verses speak to the disbelievers or about them. This one turns inward and addresses the Prophet's own interior: a wish he might entertain, and a tightness in his chest. That is rare in the Qurʾān and it changes the register of the passage. We are not being shown an argument here but a strain. The strain has a concrete cause, and the sūrah reports it in the disbelievers' own words. They had asked him to bring a different recitation or to alter the one he brought, as recorded in the preceding sūrah: *"Bring us a different Quran or make some changes in it"* (10:15). They had also demanded that a mountain be turned to gold as proof of his mission, or that angels accompany him to testify.

**"You Are Only a Warner" Read as a Relief**

Four words close the first half of the verse, and they are usually heard as a rebuke. They are also, and more immediately, a release. *You are only a warner* — the office has a boundary, and the boundary is not failure. The same sentence appears as a consolation elsewhere: *"You ˹O Prophet˺ are only a warner. And every people had a guide"* (13:7). A person who understands his role as producing belief in others will measure himself by their response, and every refusal will register as his own deficiency. A person who understands his role as delivering the warning measures himself by the delivery. The Prophet's distress in this verse came partly from the first understanding; the sentence corrects it. This is the same clarification he states about himself: *"I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God"* (18:110). The office is a channel with a defined content, and its success is not identical with its effect.

**The Trustee of All Affairs**

The verse ends where the pressure actually sits. *Allah is the Trustee of All Affairs* — *wakīl*, the one entrusted with the management of a matter, the guarantor who holds it on behalf of another. The commentators draw the implication directly: guidance and misguidance are not in the messenger's hands but in God's alone. That single clause dissolves the entire problem the verse began with. The Prophet's distress arose from carrying a responsibility that was never assigned to him — the responsibility for whether his townsmen believed. Once the affair is in the hands of a Trustee, the messenger's duty reduces to accuracy and endurance, both of which are within his control. The Qurʾān makes the same assignment explicit: *"But if they turn away, We have not sent you ˹O Prophet˺ as a keeper over them. Your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺"* (42:48). The verse therefore does not merely rebuke a wish. It removes the ground the wish was standing on.

## Verse 11:13

> Or do they say, “He has fabricated this ˹Quran˺!”? Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Produce ten fabricated sûrahs like it and seek help from whoever you can—other than Allah—if what you say is true!”

**The Accusation Turned Into a Test**

The verse opens by reporting an accusation and immediately converts it into an experiment. The charge is fabrication: the Qurʾān was made up, and therefore its author is a liar. The response does not deny the charge in words. It accepts the charge's own logic and pushes it to its conclusion. If this is a human composition, then human composition can produce another one, and the test of the claim is to produce it. That move is characteristic of the Qurʾān's method throughout: rather than assert its origin, it invites a disproof and defines the conditions under which the disproof would count. The accusation of fabrication appears at several points in the Makkan period — *"Or do they claim, 'He made it up!'?"* (10:38), and *"This ˹Quran˺ is no more than a fabricated lie"* (34:43) — and each time it is met with the same challenge rather than with a defence. A defence would concede that the burden lies on the messenger. A challenge places it where the accusation put it.

**Three Challenges and One Escalation**

The Qurʾān issues this challenge in three forms, and comparing them shows a deliberate gradation. The broadest states that no collaboration could succeed: *"If ˹all˺ humans and jinn were to come together to produce the equivalent of this Quran, they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported each other"* (17:88). The middle form asks for ten sūrahs, which is this verse and its parallel at 10:38. The narrowest asks for a single sūrah: *"then produce a sûrah like it"* (2:23). The movement runs downward in difficulty while the demand for assistance stays constant. A person who declines the first challenge cannot accept the second on the grounds that it was too hard, since the third is smaller still. The escalation also has a diagnostic function. It shows what the objectors were never asking for. Nobody negotiating in good faith about a text's origin refuses progressively easier versions of the same test; the refusal of all three indicates that the charge of fabrication was not an actual position but a way of not engaging.

## Verse 11:14

> But if your helpers fail you, then know that it has been revealed with the knowledge of Allah, and that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him! Will you ˹not˺ then submit ˹to Allah˺

**Silence Treated as an Answer**

The verse begins by anticipating the outcome of the challenge issued before it. *If they do not respond to you* — no argument, no counter-text, no try. The remarkable thing is that the verse treats the silence as a result rather than as a non-event. In an ordinary dispute, a failure to answer leaves the question open. Here it closes it, because the challenge was framed as a test whose failure condition was specified in advance. The opponents had been told exactly what would count, and they did not produce it. The commentators draw the inference the verse makes explicit: if the accusation of fabrication were true, the text would be reproducible; the fact that it has not been reproduced by the people with the greatest motive to reproduce it establishes that it is not a human composition. The instruction is *know* — a command to move from observation to certainty.

**"With the Knowledge of Allah" Is Not a Weak Formula**

The phrase translated as *revealed with the knowledge of Allah* is precise, and commentators note that it carries more than permission. A revelation sent *by* God's knowledge is one whose content is known to Him, whose timing is set by Him, and whose text He has determined. The formulation closes a loophole that a lesser phrasing would leave open. If the verse said only that the Qurʾān came from God, an objector could argue that it came through an intermediary who composed it. Saying it was sent down *with His knowledge* attaches the composition itself to the divine knowledge, and the Qurʾān elsewhere insists on the same point in stronger terms: *"It is not ˹possible˺ for this Quran to have been produced by anyone other than Allah. In fact, it is a confirmation of what came before, and an explanation of the Scripture"* (10:37). The two statements together answer the whole charge.

## Verse 11:15

> Whoever desires ˹only˺ this worldly life and its luxuries, We will pay them in full for their deeds in this life—nothing will be left out

**The Condition Is a Desire, Not an Enjoyment**

The verse begins with wanting, and that is where the diagnosis starts. It does not say *whoever has this world* or *whoever enjoys it*. It says *whoever desires* it — and the qualifier *only*, supplied by the translators and understood by the exegetes, is what makes the statement precise. Enjoying the lawful goods of this life is not condemned anywhere in the Qurʾān; the sūrah itself has promised the repentant a good provision. What the verse isolates is a desire that has no second object, a person for whom this life and its adornment are the whole of what is wanted. The commentators put the limit exactly there: the description can in one sense touch believers and disbelievers alike, since both take pleasure in lawful things, but here it names those who want this world and deny that there is anything after it. The parallel verse states the same division in agricultural terms: *"Whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter, We will increase their harvest. And whoever desires ˹only˺ the harvest of this world, We will give them some of it, but they will have no share in the Hereafter"* (42:20).

**A Warning That Reaches Inside the Community**

Some exegetes read this verse as addressed partly to believers, and specifically to the problem of ostentation — deeds performed to be seen. On that reading, the person who acts for public recognition receives his recognition and no more. The Qurʾān names this type elsewhere in two words: *"those who ˹only˺ show off"* (107:6). The connection is drawn to the foundational report of the tradition, the first ḥadīth recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, in which ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb reports the Prophet saying that actions are judged by intentions and that every person will have only what he intended (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1). Read with that report, this verse becomes an internal audit rather than an external threat. The question it puts to a believer is not whether he enjoys the world but where his deeds are addressed. A prayer performed for an audience has been paid for by the audience. The verse therefore does two jobs at once: it explains the prosperity of the denier, and it warns the worshipper that the same mechanism applies inside the community.

## Verse 11:16

> It is they who will have nothing in the Hereafter except the Fire. Their efforts in this life will be fruitless and their deeds will be useless

**Nothing Left Except the Fire**

The weight of the verse is carried by one word: *except*. Those who work for this world alone will have nothing in the Hereafter except the Fire — not a lesser reward, not a partial standing, not a small credit carried over from this life. The exclusiveness answers an assumption people naturally make, that a person who did many good things must have something left over. The verse denies the leftover.

The commentators read the passage as one account that has already been settled: such people took their payment in this life, and what follows is not a second bill for the same deeds but the state of someone who arrives with an empty account. The verse then uses two different words for wasted effort. *Ḥabiṭa*, the root behind *their efforts will be fruitless*, is used elsewhere of crops destroyed and of animals grazing a field down to nothing, and *bāṭil*, rendered *their deeds will be useless*, is the word for what is null and void in itself. Deeds that were done for show rather than for God are described as both: burned down and worthless. The verse is the closing line of a passage about those whose worship in this life was aimed entirely at people.

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the same outcome in its own words: their works have failed in this world and in the Hereafter, "and they will have no helpers" (3:22).

## Verse 11:17

> ˹Can these people be compared to˺ those ˹believers˺ who stand on clear proof from their Lord, backed by ˹the Quran as˺ a witness from Him, and preceded by the Book of Moses ˹which was revealed˺ as a guide and mercy? It is those ˹believers˺ who have faith in it. But whoever from the ˹disbelieving˺ groups rejects it, the Fire will be their destiny. So do not be in doubt of it. It is certainly the truth from your Lord, but most people do not believe

**A Comparison Posed as a Question**

The verse opens with a particle that expects an answer, and the answer it expects is *no*. The implied comparison runs back to the people described in the verses before — those who wanted only this world, whose deeds came to nothing. Against them the verse sets a figure described in four phrases, and the contrast is not between a bad person and a good person but between someone standing on nothing and someone standing on evidence. The rhetorical question is the Qurʾān's habitual way of forcing a judgement rather than announcing one. It appears again in a related form: *"Are those who know equal to those who do not know?"* (39:9). The device works because the reader supplies the answer himself, and an answer supplied is harder to abandon than one received. The verse then does not stop at the comparison. It moves through a description of the evidence, a statement about who accepts it, a warning to whoever rejects it, and a direct instruction not to doubt. Five movements in one verse, all governed by the initial question.

**The Book of Moses Arriving First**

The third phrase places the witness in a sequence: *and before it, the Book of Moses as a guide and a mercy*. This is the verse's most consequential claim. The Qurʾān does not present itself as a new beginning but as the latest item in an order that already includes the Torah, described here in the two terms the Qurʾān most often applies to revelation — guidance and mercy. The sūrah returns to the same subject near its end, noting that Moses was given the Scripture and that differences arose over it (11:110). The continuity matters for the argument the sūrah is running with Mekka.

**"Most People Do Not Believe"**

The verse ends on a statistic, and the statistic is not offered as a comfort. *Most people do not believe* is repeated in the Qurʾān as a standing observation: *"What has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ from your Lord is the truth, but most people do not believe"* (13:1), and *"The Hour is certainly coming, there is no doubt about it. But most people do not believe"* (40:59). Some commentators narrow the reference here to the majority of Makkah at the time, and that reading has contextual support, but the generality of the phrasing is what gives it force. Truth is not established by counting. The verse has just spent its length describing evidence — a proof, a witness, a prior Scripture — and then records that the evidence does not persuade most people. The contrast is the point. A reader who expects conviction to follow automatically from argument has misunderstood what the sūrah has been saying about concealment, ridicule and the desire for this world.

## Verse 11:18

> Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah? They will be brought before their Lord, and the witnesses will say, “These are the ones who lied against their Lord.” Surely Allah’s condemnation is upon the wrongdoers

**A Question That Has Already Been Answered**

*Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah?* The form is interrogative and the expectation is negative: nobody. The Qurʾān uses this construction repeatedly, and it uses it as a superlative rather than as a genuine enquiry. The same sentence appears in other sūrahs with the same object: *"And who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or reject the truth after it has reached them?"* (29:68), and *"Who then does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah?"* (18:15). The repetition establishes a ranking of wrongs, and the top of the ranking is not murder, not oppression, not theft. It is inventing a falsehood about God. That placement is the sūrah's moral premise stated in one line, and it explains why the whole of the Qurʾān's argument with Mekka runs on the question of who composed this Book. The charge the disbelievers made against the Prophet was, in the sūrah's own terms, the gravest wrong there is — turned back on them.

**Brought Before, Not Brought Down**

The verse shifts tense and scene. *They will be brought before their Lord.* The passive verb is important: they do not come, they are brought. Throughout their lives these people acted as though the accounting were optional, and the verse removes the option with a single grammatical choice. The commentators read the phrase as the summons to account on the Day of Judgment, and the wording matches the courtroom imagery the sūrah develops. Notice what the scene does not contain. There is no negotiation, no representative, and no procedural objection. The same clause appears in a verse about escape: *"Do not think ˹O Prophet˺ that the disbelievers can escape in the land"* (24:57). Being brought is the negation of escaping. The verse places the scene immediately after the rhetorical question, so the reader moves from the abstract ranking of wrongs to a specific moment in which the ranking is applied. The wrong was committed in private, in a town, in a lifetime; it is adjudicated in public, before the one it was committed against.

**The Witnesses Who Speak Before the Accused**

The most striking feature of the scene is who speaks first. *The witnesses will say: "These are the ones who lied against their Lord"* (11:18). The accused are not asked for an account; they are identified by others. The commentators give the witnesses two identities, and both are supported elsewhere in the Qurʾān. The first is the angels appointed to record deeds: *"not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺"* (50:18), and *"There is no soul without a vigilant angel ˹recording everything˺"* (86:4). The second is the prophets, who testify about the peoples they were sent to. The two together close the case completely. The recorders establish what was done; the messengers establish what was said to them and what they were told to do. No part of the account depends on the accused's own memory or honesty, which is the practical answer to the concealment described in the sūrah's fifth verse. A person who folded his chest to hide what was inside it will be described aloud by someone else.

## Verse 11:19

> who hinder ˹others˺ from Allah’s Path, striving to make it ˹appear˺ crooked, and disbelieve in the Hereafter

**Three Features of One Wrong**

The verse continues the sentence begun before it and specifies the wrongdoers by three characteristics. They obstruct the path, they work to make it look crooked, and they deny the Hereafter. The three are not a list of separate offences. They form a sequence, and the sequence explains how a person arrives at the position the previous verse ranked as the worst there is. Denial of the Hereafter removes the future in which actions are settled. Obstruction removes the road for other people. Distortion removes the road's appearance, so that even a person who looks cannot find it. The last is the most damaging and it comes second in the verse because it is the method by which the first is sustained. The commentators read all three as descriptions of the same people — the disbelievers in God and His message — and their reading is supported by an almost identical verse elsewhere: *"those who hindered ˹others˺ from Allah's Way, strived to make it ˹appear˺ crooked, and disbelieved in the Hereafter"* (7:45).

**Making the Straight Road Look Bent**

The second characteristic is the most precise and the least often noticed. *Striving to make it appear crooked* — the verb carries effort and intent. The path itself is not being altered; its appearance is. The reference point is the straight path that the Qurʾān's opening sūrah asks for by name: *"Guide us along the Straight Path"* (1:6). What these people do is present that path as devious, so that a seeker who looks at it concludes it will not lead where it claims. The technique is familiar. A teaching is summarised in its least defensible form; a commandment is quoted without its context; a messenger's life is described in the terms his opponents would choose. The verse names this as a *striving*, which means it is deliberate and sustained, not a misunderstanding. That is what places it at the top of the sūrah's ranking of wrongs. An honest error can be corrected by better information. A deliberate distortion is designed to survive better information, and it must be identified as a distortion before it can be answered.

## Verse 11:20

> They will never frustrate Allah on earth, and they will have no protector besides Allah. Their punishment will be multiplied, for they failed to hear or see ˹the truth˺

**No Escape Inside the Territory**

The verse opens by denying a possibility that the previous verses might seem to leave open. These people obstruct, distort and deny — and none of it has worked against them yet. The natural inference is that they have succeeded, or at least that they are beyond reach. The verse closes that inference in four words: *they will never frustrate Allah on earth.* The phrase *on earth* is doing work. It names the location of the try, and it implies that there is no other location available. A person can flee a town, a region or a country; he cannot flee the earth, and the earth is where the frustration was tried. The Qurʾān makes the same statement repeatedly and in almost the same words: *"You can never escape ˹Him˺ on earth, nor do you have any protector or helper besides Allah"* (42:31), and *"Do not let those disbelievers think they are not within reach. They will have no escape"* (8:59). The point is spatial and therefore absolute. There is no outside from which the decree can be avoided.

**A Punishment That Is Multiplied**

The third clause is a statement about quantity, and the commentators give the reason. The punishment is doubled because these people are punished twice over: once for their own misguidance and once for the misguidance of others. That follows directly from the previous verse. A person who only disbelieves carries his own case. A person who obstructs the path and makes it look crooked carries his own case plus the cases of everyone he turned away. The Qurʾān states the principle in a scene from the Hereafter, where followers ask that the punishment of their leaders be increased: *"Our Lord! They have misled us, so multiply their torment in the Fire"* (7:38). The multiplication is not arbitrary and it is not vindictive. It is the arithmetic of influence. A wrong that stays with one person has one weight; a wrong that is transmitted has as many weights as it has recipients. This is the same reasoning that placed fabrication against God at the top of the ranking two verses earlier.

**"They Could Not Hear, Nor Did They See"**

The final clause explains the multiplication, and it does so by describing a defect rather than a decision. Rāzī reads the phrase as deafness of the heart and blindness of the soul, and the reading is consistent with the Qurʾān's habitual use of the two senses for spiritual perception. Those who cannot receive the message are called blind and deaf throughout the Book: *"˹They are wilfully˺ deaf, dumb and blind so they have no understanding"* (2:171). The grammar of the clause is worth attention. It does not say they did not hear. It says they were not able to hear, and then adds that they did not see.

## Verse 11:21

> It is they who have ruined themselves, and whatever ˹gods˺ they fabricated will fail them

**The Loss Is Self-Inflicted**

The verse states the loss and names its author in the same clause: they have lost themselves. The verb is *khasirū anfusahum* — they made a loss of their own souls. The reflexive construction is the whole point. Nobody took anything from these people. No external agent seized their souls, no enemy captured them, no accident befell them. The loss is recorded as an achievement of their own, in the active voice, with the subject and the object being the same person. This is the Qurʾān's standard account of damnation, and it appears at the moment of judgment as a confession rather than an accusation: the wrongdoer is the one who declares the loss. The verse is therefore not a sentence passed from outside. It is a description of a transaction the person completed himself, over a lifetime, in small increments that never looked like losses at the time. That is why the surrounding verses describe a process rather than an event.

**The Fabricated Gods Fail at the Moment They Are Needed**

The second clause turns from the loss to its cause. *Whatever they fabricated will fail them.* The verb *ḍalla* carries the sense of going astray, disappearing, being lost — the fabricated gods do not merely refuse to help; they are simply not there. The Qurʾān describes this repeatedly and in almost identical terms: *"See how they will lie about themselves and how those ˹gods˺ they fabricated will fail them!"* (6:24), and *"They will offer ˹full˺ submission to Allah on that Day, and whatever ˹gods˺ they fabricated will fail them"* (16:87). The repetition is not decorative; it fixes a fact about the objects of worship. They were fabricated, which means they were made by the people who then relied on them, and a thing made by a person cannot protect its maker. The verse uses the same root as the accusation of fabrication discussed earlier in the sūrah, which links the two passages tightly: the lie invented about God produces objects that cannot act, and the reliance on those objects produces the loss.

## Verse 11:22

> Without a doubt, they will be the worst losers in the Hereafter

The brevity is part of the argument. Five verses of dense specification precede this one, and what follows it is a description of the opposite party. Between them stands a single line with no elaboration, no explanation and no qualification. The effect is that of a verdict read aloud after the evidence has been presented. Everything that needed saying has been said: the ranking of wrongs, the courtroom scene, the witnesses, the condemnation, the obstruction, the incapacity, the multiplied punishment, the loss of the soul, the failure of the fabricated gods. The verse adds nothing and needs to add nothing. A reader who finds the sentence abrupt has not followed the case. This is a method the sūrah uses again in its narratives, where a long account of a prophet and his people closes in a clause of similar brevity — *so away with Thamūd*, *so away with Midian* — and the shortness is the point rather than a defect.

---

The phrase "the worst losers" is used by this sūrah a few verses earlier for the same group, and its shape there is instructive: those who deny the meeting with their Lord and the measuring of their deeds are the ones who lose most, since they lose what they could have had without gaining what they thought they were buying (11:18–22). The Book uses the same description for people whose work perishes in this world and the next (9:69), and the sūrah of the Troops states the loss in the plainest terms: what they spent and what they trusted comes to nothing on the Day (39:15). The verse is placed after a list of the attributes of the prosperous (11:23) so that the two ends of the same passage can be read together.

## Verse 11:23

> Surely those who believe, do good, and humble themselves before their Lord will be the residents of Paradise. They will be there forever

**Humility as Settled Ground**

The two glosses look opposed and are in fact two stages of one condition. A person who has just turned is penitent and afraid; a person who has been turning for years is quiet. The Qurʾān describes the second stage directly: *"those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort"* (13:28). The connection to this verse is exact. *Akhbatū* describes the heart that has found its level, and a heart at that level is neither swollen by prosperity nor collapsed by loss — which is precisely the failure the sūrah diagnosed in verses 9 and 10. The person who despairs when mercy is withdrawn and boasts when it returns has a heart that moves with his circumstances. The person described here has a heart that has settled on something that does not move. That is why humility is placed after good action rather than before it: it is what remains when the doing has become a habit and the fear has become a settled relation.

**Why Humility Is Placed With the Lord**

The third condition carries a preposition: humble *before their Lord*. The humility is not general modesty before people, and it is not low self-regard. It is a specific orientation toward a specific being, and that is what makes it the condition that holds the other two in place. Good action performed before an audience inflates; good action performed before God does not, because the audience is already aware of everything and there is nothing to gain by display. The Qurʾān describes the same orientation in the words of those who feed the poor: *"We feed you only for the sake of Allah, seeking neither reward nor thanks from you"* (76:9). The refusal of thanks is the mark of the humility this verse names. It is also the precise opposite of the boastfulness diagnosed in verse 10, where the person given relief announces that his ills have gone. One man reports his goodness to people; the other reports it to no one, because the one who matters already knows.

---

## Verse 11:24

> The example of these two parties is that of the blind and the deaf, compared to the seeing and the hearing. Can the two be equal? Will you not then be mindful

**A Question That Expects One Answer**

*Can the two be equal?* The interrogative form is the Qurʾān's standard way of compelling a judgement, and the expected answer is a flat no. The device appears in a parallel comparison about knowledge rather than perception: *"Are those who know equal to those who do not know?"* (39:9). Both questions share a structure: two categories are placed side by side, a difference between them has already been showed, and the reader is asked to confirm the obvious. The force of the device lies in who answers. A statement can be resisted; a question the reader answers himself cannot easily be unsaid. The verse has spent six verses establishing the difference between the two parties and then asks the reader to state the conclusion aloud. That is why the parable is placed at the end of the section rather than at its beginning. It is not an introduction to the argument; it is the argument handed to the reader to complete.

---

The image is the Qur'an's standard for the difference the message makes, and the sūrah of the Originator gives it in its own words: the blind and the seeing are not alike, nor the darknesses and the light, nor the shade and the heat, nor the living and the dead (35:19–22). The sūrah of the Cattle uses the same pair for the answer to the idolaters' claim of inherited religion (6:50), and the sūrah of the Ranged records Abraham's appeal to his father with the same contrast of sight and blindness (37:84–87). The purpose of the comparison in this sūrah is to close the argument by the audience's own judgement: the inequality is granted before the conclusion is drawn.

## Verse 11:25

> Surely We sent Noah to his people. ˹He said,˺ “Indeed, I am sent to you with a clear warning

**Why Nūḥ Comes First**

The order of the sūrah's narratives is chronological, and Nūḥ stands at the head of the sequence for reasons the sūrah itself makes clear. His mission was the longest recorded in the Qurʾān, his people's refusal was the most complete, and their destruction was the most total. He is also the first messenger sent to a people who had begun to worship other beings, which makes his case the origin of the whole dispute the sūrah is running. The Qurʾān tells his story in at least seven places — in al-Aʿrāf, Yūnus, al-Muʾminūn, al-Shuʿarāʾ, al-Ṣāffāt, al-Qamar, and in a sūrah named after him — and each telling emphasises a different part. This one is the fullest in a single respect: it is the only account that includes the dialogue with his son and the exchange about his family. The sūrah chooses the version that gives the most detail about the cost of the mission to the messenger himself, which is what a messenger under pressure in Mekka would need.

**"Sent to You" Fixes Who Is Speaking to Whom**

The messenger's opening words are carefully constructed. *I am sent to you.* Three features matter. The passive voice places the sending with someone else; he does not appoint himself. The preposition *to you* identifies a specific addressee — not humanity in general but this town, these people, the ones standing in front of him. And the present tense describes a standing condition rather than a past event: he is currently, continuously, in the position of one sent. The Qurʾān's own account of the sending uses the same construction: *"Indeed, We sent Noah to his people ˹saying to him˺, 'Warn your people before a painful punishment comes to them'"* (71:1). The command to warn is given before the warning is delivered, and the sūrah shows the delivery. That sequence matters for the argument. A messenger who warns has discharged a duty assigned to him by another, and the responsibility for what happens next belongs to the people warned.

**A Warning Described as Clear**

The word for clear is *mubīn*, the same adjective the Qurʾān applies to its own signs, to the Book, and to the miracle given to Mūsā. Applied to a warning, it means two things at once. Nūḥ's self-description here matches the one he gives in the sūrah named after him, where he opens with the same formula: *"O my people! I am truly sent to you with a clear warning"* (71:2).

---

## Verse 11:26

> that you should worship none but Allah. I truly fear for you the torment of a painful Day.”

**Every Messenger Opens With the Same Sentence**

The formula recurs with near-identical wording across the Qurʾān, and the recurrence is itself an argument. *"Indeed, We sent Noah to his people. He said, 'O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him'"* (7:59). *"O my people! Worship Allah ˹alone˺. You have no god other than Him"* (23:23). The messengers sent from every direction to the people of al-Ḥijr said the same thing: *"Worship none but Allah"* (41:14). Hūd, addressing the people of ʿĀd, uses the identical clause followed by an almost identical fear: *"Worship none but Allah. I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous day"* (46:21). The consistency is what the sūrah needs. A single messenger with a novel teaching can be dismissed as an eccentric; a line of messengers all opening with the same sentence, in different towns and different centuries, establishes a tradition rather than an opinion. The sūrah will present that line in the verses that follow, and this verse is its first link.

**Pain Attributed to a Day**

The object of the fear is described as *the torment of a painful Day*, and the adjective is attached to the time rather than to the punishment. The construction is a figure — days do not feel pain — and its effect is to make the day itself a bearer of suffering. Everything in it hurts; there is no part of the day that offers relief. The Qurʾān uses the same construction elsewhere, and in one parallel the adjective is even stronger: *a tremendous day* (46:21). The commentators take the phrase to cover both the punishment that would fall on Nūḥ's people in this world and their punishment after it, and the double reference suits the grammar. A day can be the day of the flood and the day of the reckoning, and the warning is not weakened by the ambiguity. What matters for the hearer is that a specific day is coming, that it has been identified, and that its character is pain.

**The Warning Comes Before the Punishment, and That Is the Mercy**

The sūrah named after Nūḥ states the order explicitly: *"Warn your people before a painful punishment comes to them"* (71:1). The warning precedes the punishment, and the precedence is not incidental. A punishment delivered without warning would be an act of force; a punishment delivered after a warning that was refused is a consequence. The Qurʾān treats the interval between the two as the space in which a person's freedom operates, and the sūrah's opening verses have already described that interval as an appointed term. Nūḥ's mission is the longest such interval in the Book, and the sūrah will report how it was used. The verse therefore ends where every prophetic warning ends: with the danger fully named, the exit fully stated, and the decision left in the hands of the people being warned. The response comes immediately, from the notables of the town, and it does not address the demand.

---

## Verse 11:27

> The disbelieving chiefs of his people said, “We see you only as a human being like ourselves, and we see that no one follows you except the lowliest among us, who do so ˹hastily˺ without thinking. We do not see anything that makes ˹all of˺ you any better than us. In fact, we think you are liars.”

**The Notables Answer, Not the People**

The verse identifies who replies, and the identification is the first thing to notice. The disbelieving chiefs — the *malaʾ*, the council, the people whose opinion sets the terms of public speech — answer before anyone else does. The commentators identify them as the leaders of Nūḥ's society, and the same group answers in the parallel accounts: *"But the chiefs of his people said, 'We surely see that you are clearly misguided'"* (7:60). Their priority is not accidental. A message that reaches a town is first assessed by those with the most to lose from it, and their assessment becomes the town's assessment. The verse thus records the mechanism by which a warning is neutralised before most people have heard it. Nūḥ is not being answered by a considered public debate. He is being answered by the people who control what the public will be permitted to consider. The same group appears in the sūrah's own present: the notables of Mekka, who made the same objections to the same demand.

**"A Human Being Like Ourselves"**

The first objection is that the messenger is a man. *We see you only as a human being like ourselves.* The word *only* does the work — not that he is human, which is uncontested, but that he is nothing more. The objection assumes that a message from God should arrive through a being of a different order, and it appears in identical form across the Qurʾān: *"You are no more than humans like us!"* (14:10), and *"This is only a human like you, who wants to be superior to you. Had Allah willed, He could have easily sent down angels ˹instead˺"* (23:24). The assumption underneath is that proximity disqualifies. A man who grew up in the town, whose family is known and whose trade is ordinary, cannot be the bearer of a divine communication, because the people around him have seen him eat and walk in the markets. The objection is not really about ontology. It is about the inconvenience of being addressed by a peer.

**The Followers' Rank Offered as Evidence Against the Message**

The second objection concerns the followers. *No one follows you except the lowliest among us.* The commentators explain that these were poor people employed in trades considered of low standing, and that their social position was the reason they were despised. The parallel account puts the objection as a question: *"How can we believe in you, when you are followed ˹only˺ by the lowest of the low?"* (26:111). The argument is an appeal to the quality of the audience as proof about the message, and it contains a hidden premise: truth is adopted by the eminent. The verse records the objection without answering it, and the sūrah's later verses will show Nūḥ refusing to dismiss these very people at the notables' request.

**"Hastily, Without Thinking"**

The phrase translated as *who do so hastily without thinking* renders an idiom whose sense is that they followed on the surface, without depth of consideration — the outward appearance of a decision rather than a considered one. The Qurʾān records Nūḥ's answer to it in the parallel account, and the answer is a refusal to audit other people's interiors: *"And what knowledge do I have of what they do?"* (26:112).

---

## Verse 11:28

> He said, “O my people! Consider if I stand on a clear proof from my Lord and He has blessed me with a mercy from Himself, which you fail to see. Should we ˹then˺ force it on you against your will

**He Does Not Answer the Social Argument at All**

The notables raised three objections: the messenger's humanity, his followers' low standing, and their hastiness. Nūḥ's reply addresses none of them directly. He does not defend his followers' intelligence, dispute the description of their rank, or argue that he is more than a man. Instead he restates his own position and then asks a question. The omission is a method. To argue about the social standing of the followers would be to accept the notables' premise that standing is relevant, and the whole dispute turns on whether it is. Nūḥ refuses the frame rather than contesting it. The Qurʾān elsewhere instructs the same procedure: when a people argue about rank, respond with the evidence, not with a counter-ranking. The verse therefore enacts the principle it states. A person who holds a clear proof does not need to win an argument about his followers' occupations.

**"Which You Fail to See"**

The clause *and you are blind to it* is short and it carries the whole exchange. Nūḥ does not say the mercy is hidden, mysterious or accessible only to initiates. He says they are blind to it. The verb locates the failure in their perception rather than in the object, and it echoes the sūrah's own parable of the blind and the deaf drawn only four verses earlier. The commentators connect it to the wider Qurʾānic habit of describing those insensible to the message as blind. What makes the clause devastating is its politeness. Nūḥ is not accusing them of lying about the proof; he is telling them they cannot see it, which is a statement about their condition rather than their character. The same formulation is given to this Prophet in identical words: *"Indeed, I stand on a clear proof from my Lord—yet you have denied it"* (6:57). The parallel shows that the sentence is a standard reply, and that the blindness is the standard problem.

**"Should We Force It On You?"**

The rhetorical question that closes the verse is the sūrah's clearest statement of a principle stated elsewhere as a rule: *"Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood"* (2:256). Nūḥ's question puts the same principle in practical terms.

---

## Verse 11:29

> O my people! I do not ask you for a payment for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from Allah. And I will never dismiss the believers, for they will surely meet their Lord. But I can see that you are a people acting ignorantly

**No Payment Asked**

The verse opens by refusing a payment, and the refusal answers an objection that has not been stated in this sūrah. The commentators supply it: the notables had told Nūḥ that if he wanted them to believe, he should send away the people currently following him, because those people were not fit to stand on the same ground as the town's leading families. The refusal of payment comes first because it establishes the messenger's independence. A man who takes money from his audience speaks for his audience. A man who takes nothing can say what he has been given to say. The Qurʾān makes the same statement through Hūd in almost identical words later in this sūrah: *"O my people! I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the One Who created me"* (11:51). The formula is a standard part of the prophetic address, and its function is to remove the suspicion that the messenger has an interest in the outcome beyond the message.

**A Demand Nobody Wanted to Say Aloud**

The second half of the verse responds to a request the sūrah does not quote directly, and the commentators preserve it: dismiss the believers. The reason given was social rather than theological. The notables did not object to the followers' arguments; they objected to their presence. The Qurʾān records the same demand being made to this Prophet in explicit terms, with a warning attached: *"Do not dismiss those ˹poor believers˺ who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. You are not accountable for them whatsoever, nor are they accountable for you. So do not dismiss them, or you will be one of the wrongdoers"* (6:52). The parallel is exact, and it shows that the demand is a recurring feature of every mission rather than a local grievance. In both cases the request is presented as a practical concession that would make the message more acceptable to the people with the most influence.

**"A People Acting Ignorantly" — a Verdict With Restraint**

The verse ends with a judgement, and the judgement is measured. *I can see that you are a people acting ignorantly.* The word *jāhilūn* describes people acting from ignorance rather than from settled malice, and it is the same phrase Hūd uses to his people in an identical construction: *"But I can see that you are a people acting ignorantly"* (46:23). The restraint is worth noting after the notables called him a liar. They accused him of falsehood; he accuses them of not understanding.

---

## Verse 11:30

> O my people! Who would protect me from Allah if I were to dismiss them? Will you not then be mindful

**A Question About Protection, Not About Feelings**

The verse is one line long and it converts the dispute into a question of practical exposure. Nūḥ does not ask whether dismissing the believers would be kind, or whether they would be hurt, or whether the notables would keep their word. He asks who would protect him from God if he did it. The question has a single expected answer: nobody. The notables have no standing with God, no influence over the decree, and no capacity to shield a messenger from the consequences of disobeying his sender. By framing the matter this way, Nūḥ moves the discussion from the social ground the notables chose to the ground where the decision actually sits. The Qurʾān states the same fact as a general principle: *"You can never escape ˹Him˺ on earth, nor do you have any protector or helper besides Allah"* (42:31). The verse is that principle applied to a specific proposal.

---

## Verse 11:31

> I do not say to you that I possess Allah’s treasuries or know the unseen, nor do I claim to be an angel, nor do I say that Allah will never grant goodness to those you look down upon. Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ within them. ˹If I did,˺ then I would truly be one of the wrongdoers.”

**The Treasuries: A Refusal of Wealth**

The first denial addresses a demand the sūrah has already recorded in its Mekkan context, where the disbelievers asked why a treasure had not been sent down to the messenger. Nūḥ's people made a related assumption: that a messenger would arrive with visible provision, and that the absence of wealth was evidence against him. The denial is exact. He does not hold the keys to God's stores and cannot produce provision on demand. The Qurʾān states the corresponding fact about God's giving elsewhere: *"How many are the creatures that cannot secure their provisions! ˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ provides for them and you ˹as well˺"* (29:60). Provision is distributed by God through channels of His choosing, and a messenger is not one of the channels. This is also why the sūrah's sixth verse matters: every moving creature's provision is guaranteed by God, which makes a demand that the messenger produce wealth a demand addressed to the wrong person.

**The Unseen: A Refusal of Knowledge**

The second denial is the most consequential. *Nor do I know the unseen.* A messenger who knew the unseen could answer every question put to him, predict every event and settle every dispute, and the absence of that knowledge was one of the charges brought against the Prophet in Mekka. Nūḥ refuses the claim outright, and the refusal is not modesty. It is a statement about the limits of what was given to him. He was given a message, not an information service. The Qurʾān has this Prophet state the same limit in his own words: *"I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the Will of Allah. If I had known the unknown, I would have benefited myself enormously"* (7:188). The consequence for the audience is practical. They cannot test the messenger by asking him what will happen tomorrow; they can only test the message he has already delivered, and the sūrah's challenge to produce ten sūrahs was framed on exactly those terms.

**An Angel: A Refusal of a Different Nature**

The third denial answers the objection recorded two verses earlier — that he is only a human being like the rest of them. Nūḥ does not dispute the observation. He confirms it: I do not claim to be an angel. The Qurʾān records the same expectation elsewhere, where peoples ask why an angel was not sent instead: *"Had our Lord willed, He could have easily sent down angels ˹instead˺"* (41:14). The refusal matters because it declines the terms of the debate. The notables wanted a messenger of a different order, on the assumption that a peer could not carry a divine message. Nūḥ accepts the premise about his nature and denies the conclusion drawn from it. A man can carry a proof. That is the whole of the claim, and the sūrah's twenty-eighth verse has already stated it.

---

## Verse 11:32

> They protested, “O Noah! You have argued with us far too much, so bring upon us what you threaten us with, if what you say is true.”

**"Bring On What You Threaten"**

The second clause is a demand for the punishment to arrive. This is not courage; it is a wager, and the sūrah has already analysed it. Its eighth verse describes the same response in the present: when the punishment is delayed, people say *what is holding it back?* Nūḥ's people are making the same move in an older town. The demand has a public function. Issued in front of the town, it makes the messenger look powerless and the notables look unafraid, and it transfers the burden of proof onto time. If nothing happens, the threat is exposed as empty; if something happens, the notables have at least been seen to invite it. The Qurʾān records the same challenge put to other messengers, and it treats the challenge as a settled pattern rather than a local outburst. The sūrah's answer is given in the verse that follows: only God can bring it, and when He does, there will be no escape.

---

## Verse 11:33

> He responded, “It is Allah Who can bring it upon you if He wills, and then you will have no escape

**An Answer That Refuses the Framing**

The notables asked Nūḥ to bring the punishment. His reply does not try it and does not explain why he cannot; it relocates the matter completely. *It is Allah Who can bring it upon you.* The emphasis falls on the subject. The messenger is not the agent, has never been the agent, and could not become the agent if he wanted to. One verse earlier he had listed the powers he does not hold — the treasuries, the unseen, an angelic nature — and this verse applies the list to the specific demand in front of him. The reply is therefore not a refusal to cooperate. It is a correction of a factual error. The notables have addressed their challenge to a man who has no part in the thing they are challenging him to produce, and the verse says so in one clause.

**No Escape Once It Arrives**

The second half of the verse shifts from possibility to certainty. *And then you will have no escape.* The word is the same one the Qurʾān uses repeatedly to deny flight from the decree: *"They ask you ˹O Prophet˺, 'Is this true?' Say, 'Yes, by my Lord! Most certainly it is true! And you will have no escape'"* (10:53), and *"O my people! Persist in your ways, for I ˹too˺ will persist in mine. You will soon know who will fare best in the end. Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed"* (6:135). The clause answers the only rational response the notables have left, which is to prepare. If the punishment cannot be escaped, then demanding it is not bravery but a miscalculation of what is coming. The verse states the fact without elaboration, and the flatness is deliberate: a man who has been told there is no escape and continues to demand the thing has stopped reasoning. The clause also answers the question the sūrah raised earlier about the delay. What holds the punishment back is neither weakness nor indecision; it is a term that has already been set. Knowing that there is no escape when the term ends is the only fact here capable of changing behaviour, and it is given without qualification.

---

## Verse 11:34

> My advice will not benefit you—no matter how hard I try—if Allah wills ˹for˺ you to stray. He is your Lord, and to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.”

**Advice That Has Run Out of Use**

The verse is the messenger's own assessment of his work, and it is unsentimental. My advice will not benefit you. He is not describing a temporary setback or a hard audience. He is stating that the instrument he has been using — counsel, warning, patient explanation — has reached the end of its effectiveness for these people. The word *nuṣḥ* covers sincere advice, the kind given by someone who wants the outcome for the hearer rather than for himself. Nūḥ has been giving it for centuries. The verse records the point at which sincerity stops being enough, and it does so without bitterness. A person who has advised a town for nine hundred and fifty years could reasonably conclude that the town is wicked. He concludes instead that the advice has stopped working, and he gives the reason in the second half of the sentence.

**"No Matter How Hard I Try"**

The clause translated as *no matter how hard I try* renders a form that carries intensity and desire: if I wanted to advise you, my wanting would not help. The emphasis is on the messenger's will, and it is emphatic enough to be worth pausing on. Nūḥ is not tired of the work. He is describing a man who still wants to advise them and cannot make the advice land. That distinction separates this verse from resignation. Resignation would say *there is no point*. This verse says *there is every point, and it will not reach you*. The Qurʾān addresses the same situation to this Prophet in direct terms: *"Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their disbelief"* (26:3), and *"We certainly know that your heart is truly distressed by what they say"* (15:97). The distress of a messenger whose counsel is not received is treated throughout the Book as a real and understandable human reaction, and it is answered rather than rebuked.

---

## Verse 11:35

> Or do they say, “He has fabricated this ˹Quran˺!”? Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If I have done so, then I bear the burden of that sin! But I am free from your sinful accusation.”

**The Narrative Interrupted by the Present**

The sūrah has been inside the story of Nūḥ for ten verses. This verse steps out of it without warning and addresses Mekka directly. The shift is abrupt enough to look like an intrusion, and commentators note that the verse concerns the Makkan idolaters and their accusation that the Prophet had fabricated the Qurʾān. The abruptness is the point. The narrative has reached the point where Nūḥ's people have exhausted their arguments and demanded the punishment, and the sūrah seizes the moment to show that the same conversation is happening now, in the same words. The charge against Nūḥ and the charge against this Prophet are not similar; they are identical. By placing the Mekkan verse here, the sūrah makes the reader hear the ancient story as a present event and the present event as an old pattern.

**"I Am Free of What You Do"**

The second clause separates the two cases. *I am free from your sinful accusation.* The word rendered as sinful accusation is *ijrām*, from the same root as the crime he has just accepted responsibility for, and the construction is deliberate. He takes the guilt of fabrication onto himself conditionally, and he disclaims any share in their crime absolutely. The two crimes are not the same and the verse keeps them apart. Fabricating a message from God is one offence; accusing an innocent man of it is another, and the accusers carry the second whether or not the first occurred. The Qurʾān uses the same formula elsewhere to close an argument: *"O my people! Persist in your ways, for I ˹too˺ will persist in mine. You will soon know who will fare best in the end. Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed"* (6:135).

---

## Verse 11:36

> And it was revealed to Noah, “None of your people will believe except those who already have. So do not be distressed by what they have been doing

**A Revelation That Closes a Mission**

The verse records a communication to Nūḥ, and its content is a statement of fact rather than a command. No more of his people will believe. The mission is not being extended, modified or given new instructions; it is being closed. The form of the revelation matters. It is not a prediction offered for comfort or a warning designed to motivate. It is information delivered at the point where information is the only thing left to give.

**Nine Hundred and Fifty Years**

The length of the mission is given elsewhere in the Qurʾān, and the number is what makes this verse extraordinary. *"Indeed, We sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them for a thousand years, less fifty. Then the Flood overtook them, while they persisted in wrongdoing"* (29:14). Nine hundred and fifty years of warning, and the verse here reports the final count of converts as a fixed group that will not grow. The number does two things. It removes the objection that Nūḥ's people were not given enough time, since no people in the Book were given more. And it removes the objection that the messenger gave up too soon, since the closure comes at the end of the longest mission recorded rather than at its beginning.

**"Do Not Be Distressed" Addresses the Messenger's Heart**

The second half of the verse turns from information to consolation. *So do not be distressed by what they have been doing.* The commentators gloss the word as sadness approaching despair, and the address is to a man's feelings rather than to his conduct. The Qurʾān repeatedly speaks to the prophets' human reactions to their trials, and this is one of the clearest instances. Nūḥ is not being rebuked for grief; he is being relieved of a burden he has been carrying. The same address is given to this Prophet: *"Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their disbelief"* (26:3). The pattern shows that a messenger's distress over his people is expected and normal, and that the cure offered is not a command to stop feeling but a statement of fact that makes the feeling unnecessary. Nobody will believe. There is nothing further to be achieved by grieving.

---

## Verse 11:37

> And build the Ark under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes and directions, and do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong, for they will surely be drowned.”

**A Command to Build, Not to Flee**

The instruction is practical and it is addressed to a man who has just been told his mission is over. Nūḥ is not told to leave the town, abandon the people, or wait for rescue. He is told to build. The verb is ordinary — construct, make — and the object is a vessel. The command places the messenger inside the coming event rather than outside it, and it gives him work to do during the interval between the closure of the mission and the arrival of the flood. That interval will be long enough to be publicly ridiculed, as the next verse records. The command therefore shapes the whole narrative. A man building a ship is visible, questionable and mockable, and the sūrah uses the visibility deliberately. The construction becomes the final public statement of the mission, made in wood rather than in words.

**The Prohibition on Pleading**

The second half of the verse forbids something Nūḥ had been doing for centuries. *Do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong.* The commentators read this as a prohibition on interceding for them and on praying that the punishment be withheld. The instruction is a change in the messenger's standing. Up to this point he had been warning them, and warning includes the hope that they will respond. Now the door is closed, and with it the legitimacy of the plea. The verse does not criticise the earlier pleading; it marks the moment after which it becomes inappropriate. That distinction is important, because it defines the limit of a messenger's advocacy. He is authorised to warn and to hope; he is not authorised to negotiate with the decree once the decree has been issued.

---

## Verse 11:38

> So he began to build the Ark, and whenever some of the chiefs of his people passed by, they mocked him. He said, “If you laugh at us, we will ˹soon˺ laugh at you similarly

**Obedience Before Understanding**

The verse opens with an act of obedience that cannot have made sense to the man performing it. Nūḥ begins to build a vessel. The sūrah does not tell us where he built it, and the commentators differ about the location, but every reading agrees on the essential oddity: the Ark was constructed away from water, in a place where a ship had no function. A man following an instruction he cannot justify to his neighbours is in a specific moral position, and the verse is interested in that position rather than in the carpentry. Nūḥ builds because he was told to, and the telling came with the design. The sūrah's whole account of prophethood rests on this kind of obedience. A messenger does not execute the parts of a revelation he finds reasonable and skip the rest; the sūrah's twelfth verse had already ruled out selective delivery. The building is therefore the practical form of the same principle.

**"We Will Laugh at You Similarly"**

Nūḥ's reply is unexpected. He does not defend the Ark, explain the flood, or rebuke them for their insolence. He announces a reversal: if you laugh at us, we will laugh at you in the same way. The symmetry is exact — the same act, returned. The commentators read this as referring to the final state of affairs, when the believers will laugh at the disbelievers as the disbelievers laughed at them. The Qurʾān describes that scene directly: *"Indeed, the wicked used to laugh at the believers"* (83:29), and *"But on that Day the believers will be laughing at the disbelievers, as they sit on ˹canopied˺ couches, looking on"* (83:34–35). The reply is not gloating. It is a statement about the durability of positions. Laughter is a claim about who is right, and the verse says the claim will be tested.

---

## Verse 11:39

> You will soon come to know who will be visited by a humiliating torment ˹in this life˺ and overwhelmed by an everlasting punishment ˹in the next˺.”

**Two Punishments Stated in One Sentence**

The verse names two punishments and keeps them distinct. One is humiliating and belongs to this life; the other is lasting and belongs to what comes after. The commentators identify the first as the flood, which would disgrace Nūḥ's people in front of the world they had been so confident in, and the second as their state of punishment in the Hereafter. The distinction is not decorative. A single punishment would leave the reader unable to tell whether the story ends with the water. Two punishments establish that the flood is not the conclusion but the first instalment, and that the mockery recorded one verse earlier has consequences that outlive the bodies it drowned.

**Humiliation as the Specific Form of the First Punishment**

The adjective chosen for the worldly punishment is *mukhzī* — one that disgraces, exposes, strips a person of standing. That is a precise choice for these people. The chiefs had spent years mocking a man building a ship, and their mockery rested completely on their own security. They had homes, position, and a world that behaved as expected. The punishment announced against them is not only destructive but specifically deflating: the thing they laughed at becomes the instrument, and the position from which they laughed disappears. The Qurʾān describes the same reversal elsewhere in a scene where the wicked who used to laugh at the believers are laughed at in turn (83:29–34). The verse therefore answers the previous verse's exchange directly. Nūḥ said the laughter would be returned; this verse specifies that the return begins in this life and takes the form of disgrace.

---

## Verse 11:40

> And when Our command came and the oven burst ˹with water˺, We said ˹to Noah˺, “Take into the Ark a pair from every species along with your family—except those against whom the decree ˹to drown˺ has already been passed—and those who believe.” But none believed with him except for a few

**A Signal Chosen From an Ordinary Object**

The verse marks the beginning of the flood with an unlikely sign: an oven gushing water. The commentators explain *al-tannūr* in two ways — as the earth in general, or as a particular place, with Syria and Iraq both proposed — and the parallel account in al-Muʾminūn uses the same sign as the trigger for boarding: *"Then when Our command comes and the oven bursts ˹with water˺, take on board a pair from every species"* (23:27). Whatever the location, the choice of object matters. An oven is the most domestic thing imaginable: a household fixture, fired daily, associated with bread and warmth. Water rising from it is not only an oddity; it is an inversion of the ordinary order in the place where the order is most taken for granted. The sign is placed inside a house rather than on a horizon, which means it cannot be dismissed as a distant weather event. The flood is announced at the kitchen floor.

**"And Those Who Believe" — the Third Category**

The third group is listed after the family, and the order is worth noticing. Kinship is named first, then faith, and the two are not merged. The verse does not say *your family, who believe*; it says *your family, except the doomed, and those who believe*. Faith forms its own category alongside blood rather than inside it. That distinction is the sūrah's central point about belonging, and the coming exchange about Nūḥ's son will turn on it. A person is aboard because of what he holds, not because of who he is related to, and the exception clause proves the point from the other side: a relation who does not hold it is excluded. The Qurʾān makes the same separation in the story of Ibrāhīm's dissociation from his father (6:74), and the sūrah will narrate that story later in the same chapter.

---

## Verse 11:41

> And he said, “Board it! In the Name of Allah it will sail and cast anchor. Surely my Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

**An Invitation Rather Than an Order**

The verse records the only words Nūḥ speaks aboard the Ark, and their form is unusual. *Board it.* He does not command; he invites. The people being addressed have already been selected by a divine command, and they are standing in front of a vessel they have watched being built for years under universal ridicule. What Nūḥ says to them is an invitation to enter, and the invitation is the last act of a mission that has been defined throughout by invitation rather than compulsion. The sūrah established that principle in his own mouth: *should we then force it on you against your will?* Here the principle reaches its practical end. A man being saved from a flood is not forced into the ship. He is asked to come in, and the asking preserves the same freedom that made the refusal of his town a real refusal.

**Two Names of Mercy at the End of a Flood**

The verse closes with a note that seems out of place. Nūḥ has just been through the destruction of his entire people, and what he says is: *my Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.* The two names are *ghafūr* and *raḥīm*, and their placement is not consolation for the drowned. It is a statement about the people in the vessel. Everyone aboard has faults, and the Ark is not carrying the sinless. The sūrah has already made the same point in its opening, where forgiveness was placed before reward in the promise to the repentant. Nūḥ's sentence is therefore an accurate description of the situation rather than a pious flourish: a small group of imperfect believers has been saved, and the reason they have been saved is not their perfection.

---

## Verse 11:42

> And ˹so˺ the Ark sailed with them through waves like mountains. Noah called out to his son, who stood apart, “O my dear son! Come aboard with us and do not be with the disbelievers.”

**Waves Like Mountains**

The verse describes the voyage in five words and the image is exact. The commentators take *mountains* as a description of the waves' size, formed by the intensity of the wind, and the comparison does more than convey scale. A mountain is the most permanent object in the vocabulary of an ancient audience; it is what does not move. Describing water as mountains inverts that stability — the solid thing has become liquid, and the liquid thing has taken on the dimensions of the solid. The sūrah uses the same comparison elsewhere for a different purpose, where people caught at sea in waves like mountains call on God alone (31:32). Here the image is directed at the reader rather than at the passengers. A person aboard the Ark is safe; a person watching the water from outside it is looking at a landscape that has become unsurvivable.

**The Father's Last Argument**

This is the final appeal Nūḥ makes to anyone in the narrative, and its register is different from everything he has said to his people. To the chiefs he spoke as a messenger: proof, mercy, disclaimers, warnings. To his son he speaks as a father, and the address is affectionate. The Qurʾān's phrase for it is one of endearment — *my dear son* — the same form Ibrāhīm uses to his son in the sūrah of al-Ṣāffāt (37:102). The appeal contains no argument, no evidence and no reference to the clear proof. It contains only the request and the warning. That economy is worth noting after twenty verses of reasoned exchange. When a man is speaking to his own child in the last minutes before a flood, he does not produce a case. He asks him to come.

---

## Verse 11:43

> He replied, “I will take refuge on a mountain, which will protect me from the water.” Noah cried, “Today no one is protected from Allah’s decree except those to whom He shows mercy!” And the waves came between them, and his son was among the drowned

**A Mountain Set Against an Ocean**

The son's answer is a plan, and the plan is intelligible. Water rises; high ground does not flood; therefore go to high ground. Every part of the reasoning is sound on its own terms, and the whole of it fails because the terms are wrong. This was not a river overflowing its banks. The sūrah describes a flood in which the water rose above the mountains, and the parallel account says the waters of heaven and earth met for a fate already set (54:11–12). The commentators read the mountain spiritually as well: it stands for the false sense of permanence and stability that a person in denial assigns to the world. The reading fits the text exactly. The son is not making a tactical error. He is trusting the world to behave as the world has always behaved, at the precise moment when it has stopped doing so.

**"Except Those He Shows Mercy" — the Door Left Open**

The exception in Nūḥ's sentence is the most important word in it. *Except those on whom He has mercy.* Protection exists, and it exists in one form only: divine mercy. The clause keeps the door open at the moment it is closing, and it identifies what the son is actually refusing. He is not being asked to abandon a reasonable plan for an unreasonable one. He is being told that the only shelter available is a relation with God, and that the mountain is not shelter at all. The sūrah has already established the same principle in its twelfth verse, where guidance was placed in God's hands rather than the messenger's, and in its thirtieth, where Nūḥ asked who would protect him from God. The answer there was nobody. The answer here is the same, with one addition: mercy protects, and mercy is reached by boarding.

---

## Verse 11:44

> And it was said, “O earth! Swallow up your water. And O sky! Withhold ˹your rain˺.” The floodwater receded and the decree was carried out. The Ark rested on Mount Judi, and it was said, “Away with the wrongdoing people!”

**Two Commands Addressed to Two Elements**

The verse ends the flood the way the sūrah began it: with speech. God does not act on the water directly; He addresses its two sources. The earth is told to swallow, the sky is told to withhold, and the flood stops because both obey. The structure recalls the beginning, in which the oven gushed and the command came. Water entered through the ground and through the air, and it leaves through the same two channels, each closed by a single instruction. The commentators note that the two commands denote the end of the flood, and their form is worth pausing on. Elements that have no ears are addressed as though they had them, and they respond. The verse is making a claim about the world: it is not an independent system running on its own laws but a set of things that can be spoken to. The same address appears in the parallel account, where the sky is opened with pouring rain and the earth bursts with springs (54:11–12).

**"Away With the Wrongdoing People"**

The verse closes with an exclamation, and the commentators take it as spoken by God. *Away with the wrongdoing people!* The identical phrase appears after another destruction in al-Muʾminūn (23:41), which marks it as a formula — a standing verdict pronounced over a destroyed people rather than a spontaneous cry. The form is worth noticing because it is not a description of suffering. It is a dismissal. The people are not analysed, their crimes are not enumerated, their punishment is not detailed. They are removed from the account in five words. The sūrah uses the same economy at the end of its later narratives: *so away with Thamūd*, *so away with Midian*. The brevity is the verdict. After nine hundred and fifty years of warning, there is nothing left to say about the town except that it is gone, and the verse says exactly that.

---

## Verse 11:45

> Noah called out to his Lord, saying, “My Lord! Certainly my son is ˹also˺ of my family, Your promise is surely true, and You are the most just of all judges!”

**A Prophet Asks a Question**

The narrative has ended. The water has receded, the Ark has landed, the town has been dismissed in five words. At that point Nūḥ speaks to God, and what he raises is his son. The verse is unusual because it records a prophet questioning a divine act, and the sūrah preserves the question in full rather than summarising it. The commentators explain the ground of the plea: God had promised to save Nūḥ's family, and the promise is what Nūḥ is holding onto. The verse therefore is not a protest against injustice in the abstract. It is an appeal to a specific commitment made at a specific moment, in the command to take the family aboard. A reader who treats the plea as mere grief misses its structure. Nūḥ is citing a text — the promise — against an event, and asking how the two are to be reconciled.

**"Your Promise Is Surely True"**

The second clause is an affirmation, and it is what keeps the plea from being an accusation. Nūḥ does not say the promise failed. He says the promise is true — and the conjunction with the first clause produces the question rather than the charge. The verse is therefore a man holding two things he believes and asking God to resolve the tension. The Qurʾān treats this kind of question as legitimate. It is not listed among the failures of the messengers, and the answer Nūḥ receives corrects his knowledge rather than condemning his faith. The same confidence in the promise appears in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf, where the residents of Paradise declare that they found their Lord's promise to be true (7:44). Nūḥ is stating the same conviction from the harder side of it.

**"You Are the Most Just of Judges"**

The verse ends by naming God as the most just of judges, and the placement is the most striking feature of the plea. A man whose son has just drowned closes his sentence by affirming the perfect justice of the one who decreed it. The Qurʾān uses the same description elsewhere (95:8), and in each case it functions as an anchor rather than a complaint. The clause tells the reader how to take everything before it.

---

## Verse 11:46

> Allah replied, “O Noah! He is certainly not of your family—he was entirely of unrighteous conduct. So do not ask Me about what you have no knowledge of! I warn you so you do not fall into ignorance.”

**"He Is Not of Your Family" — Two Readings**

The central clause admits two readings, and the commentators preserve both. The first is legal: the son was not among the members of Nūḥ's family whom God had promised to save. On this reading the statement is about the scope of the promise, and it answers Nūḥ's question directly — the exception in the boarding command covered him. The second is spiritual: kinship with a prophet is not established by blood but by allegiance, and a son who rejected his father's message was not his family in the sense that matters. The Qurʾān supports the second reading by parallel, in the case of Ibrāhīm's dissociation from his father (6:74). Weighing the two, the stronger position holds both: the promise had a defined scope, and the reason the son fell outside it was his conduct. The two readings are not competing explanations but the legal fact and its ground.

**"Lest You Be Among the Ignorant"**

The closing clause states the consequence of ignoring the instruction, and the word is significant. *Lest you be among the ignorant.* The commentators gloss the ignorance here as sinfulness rather than mere lack of information, and the gloss is supported by the Qurʾān's wider use of the root, in which *jāhilūn* describes people who act wrongly rather than people who lack data. The warning is therefore not that Nūḥ will be misinformed. It is that persisting in a demand whose grounds he has not established would place him among the wrongdoers. That is a serious statement to make to a prophet, and its seriousness is what marks the boundary being drawn. A messenger's standing does not exempt him from the standard he preaches. The sūrah has already made the same point through Nūḥ's own mouth, when he said that claiming powers he did not have would make him one of the wrongdoers.

---

## Verse 11:47

> Noah pleaded, “My Lord, I seek refuge in You from asking You about what I have no knowledge of, and unless You forgive me and have mercy on me, I will be one of the losers.”

**"I Seek Refuge in You" — the Direction of the Refuge**

The verb Nūḥ uses is *aʿūdhu* — I take refuge — and its object is God. The construction is worth isolating because it identifies where the danger lies. He is not seeking refuge from his son's fate, from the flood, or from grief. He is seeking refuge from a way of speaking to God: asking about what he has no knowledge of. The refuge is sought from God *against* an error in his own conduct, and it is sought *in* God. The two are not in tension. A person who recognises a fault in himself and cannot guarantee he will not repeat it has only one place to go, and the verse shows him going there. This is the same posture the sūrah's opening described from the other side, where the call was to seek forgiveness and then turn. Nūḥ performs both in one sentence.

**Words Shared With Adam**

The second half of the verse is not original to Nūḥ, and the commentators note the fact. His words are identical to those uttered by Adam and his wife after the deception became clear to them: *"Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be losers"* (7:23). The shared formula is significant for two reasons. It places the first prophet and the longest-serving prophet in the same position — both made a mistake, both identified it themselves, both used the same words. And it establishes the formula as the standing language of repentance in the Book, not a private expression. The sūrah's third verse had already commanded the same two movements in the same order: seek forgiveness, then turn. Nūḥ is performing the sūrah's own instruction at the moment he most needs it.

---

## Verse 11:48

> It was said, “O Noah! Disembark with Our peace and blessings on you and some of the descendants of those with you. As for the others, We will allow them ˹a brief˺ enjoyment, then they will be touched with a painful punishment from Us.”

**An Order to Leave the Ship**

The verse opens with the practical conclusion of the voyage. Nūḥ is told to come down. The commentators read the instruction as permission to leave the Ark and live on land without fear of perishing, and the plainness of the command is part of its meaning. After a flood that covered the mountains, the survivors are not left to work out whether the ground is safe. The sūrah has been consistent on this point throughout the narrative: the signal to board was given, the design was revealed, the moment to enter was specified, and now the moment to leave is specified. Nothing in the rescue depended on the survivors' judgement about their own safety. The verse closes the episode by returning them to the ordinary conditions of life under the same supervision that carried them through the extraordinary ones.

**Blessings on Descendants Not Yet Born**

The blessing extends past the people in the vessel. It covers Nūḥ and *some of the descendants of those with you*, which means it reaches generations that did not exist at the time it was spoken. The commentators note an elision in the sentence, so that it means: blessings upon you and upon the communities that will arise from those with you, and from those with you are communities whom We will allow enjoyment. The grammatical observation matters because it identifies two branches arising from the same survivors. The blessing is not a blanket promise over a bloodline. It is a promise over a part of what will come from them, and the verse then names the other part. A reader who takes the blessing as covering everyone descended from the Ark's passengers has missed the sentence's structure.

---

## Verse 11:49

> This is one of the stories of the unseen, which we reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. Neither you nor your people knew it before this. So be patient! Surely the ultimate outcome belongs ˹only˺ to the righteous

**"Accounts of the Unseen"**

The phrase *anbāʾ al-ghayb* describes reports of what lies beyond ordinary access — events the recipient did not witness and could not have researched. The commentators gloss it as accounts Nūḥ's story and others like it that the Prophet did not know before the revelation came. The claim is strong and it is offered as evidence. A man delivering detailed narratives about ancient peoples, in a society with no archive of them, is making a claim about his source simply by speaking. The sūrah does not argue for the claim; it states it and leaves the fact of the delivery to carry the weight. This is the same method used in the opening challenge to produce ten sūrahs: a demonstration rather than a proof, addressed to people in a position to check it.

**"So Be Patient" — a Command Placed After the Story**

The command to patience is the verse's practical output, and its placement is deliberate. The commentators read it as addressed both to the task of delivering the message and to the hardship the Prophet was enduring, and they note the implied comparison: be patient as Nūḥ was patient. The comparison is severe. Nūḥ's patience ran for nine hundred and fifty years and ended with a handful of believers and a drowned town. The command is therefore not an encouragement to expect quick results. It is an instruction to continue under conditions that may not improve, given by a Book that has just narrated the longest such case on record. The sūrah makes the same appeal elsewhere in general terms: patient endurance, perseverance and mindfulness as the path to success (3:200).

**"The Outcome Belongs to the Righteous"**

The verse closes with a formula that recurs in the Qurʾān: the ultimate outcome belongs to the God-conscious. It appears in Mūsā's reassurance to his people — *"The ultimate outcome belongs ˹only˺ to the righteous"* (7:128) — and in the description of the eternal Home: *"The ultimate outcome belongs ˹only˺ to the righteous"* (28:83). The word for outcome, *ʿāqiba*, denotes the end result of a sequence, and the formula is a claim about how sequences end. It answers the specific difficulty the Nūḥ narrative has just created. A mission that ran for centuries and produced almost nothing looks, from inside, like a failure. The formula says that the appearance at any given point is not the outcome. The verse pairs it with the command to patience for that reason: patience is only possible for a person who believes the ending differs from the present.

---

## Verse 11:50

> And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him. You do nothing but fabricate lies ˹against Allah˺

**The Second Narrative Opens With the Same Formula**

The verse that begins the story of Hūd uses the same construction that began the story of Nūḥ, twenty-five verses earlier: *We sent to X their brother Y.* The repetition is the sūrah's method made visible. It is not writing a collection of edifying tales; it is building a series, and each item in the series is deliberately aligned with the others so that the differences stand out. A reader who has followed the first narrative will recognise the shape immediately — the sending, the address, the demand for exclusive worship — and will be listening for what is new. What is new here is the third sentence. Nūḥ's opening described his own office; Hūd's opening describes his people's crime. The demand is the same; the diagnosis is sharper, and it arrives one clause earlier than it did in the first story.

**Worship Allah; You Have No Other God**

The demand is four words and it is identical to the one that opened this sūrah and the one Nūḥ made. The commentators note that this is the call made by every prophet in the Qurʾān, and the sūrah's own verse 41:14 records messengers arriving from every direction saying nothing else. The consistency is the argument. A teaching repeated by messengers separated by centuries and sent to unrelated peoples is not the invention of any one of them. Hūd's formulation adds a negative clause — *you have no god other than Him* — which makes the demand exclusive rather than merely additive. He is not asking ʿĀd to include Allah among their gods.

**ʿĀd: Who They Were**

The people addressed here are identifiable, and the sūrah expects the reader to know them. ʿĀd is generally placed in southern Arabia, between Ḥaḍramawt and Oman, a tribal people whose name derives from an ancestor. Traditional sources distinguish two peoples bearing the name — ʿĀd the Former, also called Iram, and a later ʿĀd — and the commentators take Hūd's people to be the former. The Qurʾān refers to both, naming the destruction of the first ʿĀd (53:50) and describing Iram as a people of great stature (89:7). Hūd himself is an Arabian prophet with no biblical parallel, and he is traditionally counted among the descendants of Nūḥ, which is consistent with the previous verse's statement that the coming peoples would descend from the Ark's passengers. The identification matters because it makes ʿĀd a real location in the audience's geography rather than a distant legend.

---

## Verse 11:51

> O my people! I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the One Who created me. Will you not then understand

**The Same Disclaimer in a Different Town**

Hūd's second sentence is almost word for word what Nūḥ said to his people twenty-two verses earlier, and the near-identity is the point. Rāzī notes the equivalence directly: Hūd's assurance that he asks no reward and that his reward lies only with God is the same statement in meaning as Nūḥ's. The sūrah is presenting a fixed element of the prophetic address, and a fixed element can be examined. A person inventing a case adapts it to his audience; a person transmitting a message repeats it. The disclaimer also does its practical work here. ʿĀd is being asked to abandon a religious system tied to its identity and its standing, and the first suspicion in any such negotiation is that the messenger has something to gain. The verse removes the suspicion before it can be raised, and it removes it in the same terms used centuries earlier.

**A Formula Used Across the Prophetic Corpus**

The refusal of reward is not peculiar to these two messengers. The Qurʾān records it repeatedly and in near-identical words: *"I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds"* (26:127), *"I ask no reward of you for this ˹Quran˺—it is a reminder to the whole world"* (6:90), and *"I do not ask you ˹Meccans˺ for a reward for this ˹message˺"* (42:23). The last of those is addressed to this Prophet's own audience, which links the ancient disclaimer directly to the present one. The consistency across unrelated missions is itself evidence, and the sūrah depends on that evidence. A formula repeated by messengers who never met, in towns that never corresponded, is not a local rhetorical device. It is a condition of the office.

---

## Verse 11:52

> And O my people! Seek your Lord’s forgiveness and turn to Him in repentance. He will shower you with rain in abundance, and add strength to your strength. So do not turn away, persisting in wickedness.”

**The Same Two Movements as the Sūrah's Opening**

Hūd's call reproduces, almost exactly, the instruction given at the beginning of this sūrah: seek forgiveness, then turn. The commentators note the parallel explicitly and connect it to the earlier verse. The sequence is the same and so is its logic. *Istighfār* is a request made outward — asking that a debt be cancelled. *Tawbah* is a movement of the will — turning around and walking the other way. A request without the turn costs nothing; a turn without the request settles nothing.

**Rain Promised to a People in Drought**

The reward attached to repentance is specific and material: abundant rain from the sky. The commentators connect this to a drought ʿĀd was experiencing, with one report placing it at three years. The specificity transforms the promise from a generality into an answer. These people were not being offered an abstract spiritual benefit; they were being offered the thing they needed and lacked. The same promise appears in Nūḥ's preaching: *"Seek your Lord's forgiveness, ˹for˺ He is truly Most Forgiving. He will shower you with abundant rain"* (71:10–11). Two messengers, separated by centuries, offering the same remedy for the same need. The verse therefore answers an unspoken objection — that abandoning the ancestral religion would cost the tribe its prosperity — by offering a better source for it. The rain is not a metaphor for blessing here. It is water.

**"Strength Added to Strength"**

The second promise is unusual and deserves attention. Repentance will add strength to their strength. ʿĀd was known for physical power, and the Qurʾān elsewhere describes them as a people of great stature and as the first ʿĀd destroyed (53:50; 89:7). The promise is therefore not that the weak will be made strong but that the already strong will be given more. The commentators read it as increase in bodily power, in wealth, in offspring or in overall capacity, and the readings converge on the same claim: nothing they value will be lost by turning. The verse is meeting an idolatrous people on the ground of what they actually prize. It does not ask them to trade strength for piety. It tells them that the piety is the route to more of what they already have, and that the route runs through a source they have not been using.

---

## Verse 11:53

> They argued, “O Hûd! You have not given us any clear proof, and we will never abandon our gods upon your word, nor will we believe in you

**An Objection That Sounds Like a Demand for Evidence**

The reply opens with what appears to be a reasonable complaint: you have not brought us a clear proof. The word is *bayyina*, the same term the sūrah has used throughout for the manifest evidence a messenger stands on, and its use here gives the objection a respectable shape. A person asking for evidence is engaged in an argument. The commentators note, however, that ʿĀd were unable to see the clear proofs Hūd brought because of their obstinacy, which means the complaint was not a request for more information. It was a declaration that what had been given would not be accepted. The sūrah has described this state twice already — in the people who covered their heads to avoid hearing, and in the parable of the blind and the deaf. The verse records the verbal form of the same condition, and its verbal form is the hardest to identify because it borrows the language of enquiry.

---

## Verse 11:54

> All we can say is that some of our gods have possessed you with evil.” He said, “I call Allah to witness, and you too bear witness, that I ˹totally˺ reject whatever you associate

**The Last Stage of Refusal**

The verse records the point at which ʿĀd stop arguing. Their words are hedged — *all we can say is* — and the hedge is not modesty. It marks the end of what they are willing to offer: not a counter-claim, not a reason, but a diagnosis. Some of our gods have struck you with evil. The commentators explain that this reflects their belief that Hūd was a madman put under a spell by one of the idols, because he had cursed it and called them to stop worshipping it. The move is worth naming precisely, because it is the most efficient way to end a discussion. A person accused of being bewitched cannot defend himself, since any defence he offers can be read as further evidence of the spell. The sūrah has seen the same move in Mekka, where the Prophet was called a magician and, elsewhere, insane (23:70; 81:22).

**"I Am Quit of What You Associate"**

The content of the oath is a disavowal, and the word is strong: I am free of it, innocent of it, clear of it. The commentators note that prophets make the same disavowal elsewhere of the beliefs of the peoples they are sent to (6:19; 6:78). The disavowal is not a statement of disagreement. Disagreement would leave open the possibility that the idols exist and are merely not to be worshipped. This is a declaration of separation from the entire arrangement — the beings, the worship, the practice and the belief. The verse's wording is deliberately incomplete, breaking off before its object is finished, and the next verse completes it. The effect is a sentence that continues past the verse boundary, which mirrors the substance: the disavowal is not confined to one statement but runs through everything that follows.

---

## Verse 11:55

> with Him ˹in worship˺. So let all of you plot against me without delay

**The Verse as the Turning Point of the Narrative**

Structurally this verse closes the argumentative phase of the Hūd narrative. After it, the messenger stops engaging with objections and states his position: trust in God, the universality of divine control, the justice of God's way, and the announcement that the message has been delivered. The dialogue with ʿĀd is over, and the sūrah does not record another word from them. The transition mirrors the Nūḥ narrative exactly, where the demand to bring on the punishment was followed by a statement about who controls the punishment and then by the command to build. In both stories the pattern is the same: the people exhaust their objections, the messenger states the facts, and the narrative moves toward the event. The challenge in this verse is the last thing said to ʿĀd before the facts are set out.

**The Verse and the Sūrah's Mekkan Challenge**

The challenge in this verse belongs to a family of challenges the Qurʾān issues, and comparing them shows a consistent design. The challenge to produce ten sūrahs specified the object, the number and the help available. The challenge to bring on the punishment, made by Nūḥ's people and refused, specified the timing. This challenge specifies the participants and the absence of delay. In each case the terms are set so that failure cannot be reinterpreted, and in each case the terms were never met. The pattern is the sūrah's evidential method: not assertion, but an open offer with defined conditions. A reader who doubts the claim is invited to test it, and the test is one he can conduct. The refusal of every such test, by every people in the chapter, is what the sūrah presents as the argument.

---

## Verse 11:56

> I have put my trust in Allah—my Lord and your Lord. There is no living creature that is not completely under His control. Surely my Lord’s Way is perfect justice

**"My Lord and Your Lord"**

The phrase joins two relations in three words, and the join is the verse's quiet argument. Hūd does not say *my Lord* and leave it there, which would make his trust a private arrangement. He says *my Lord and your Lord*, which places his opponents inside the same jurisdiction they are trying to escape. The construction answers the accusation of the previous verse directly. They claimed one of their gods had struck him; he replies that the being who holds him also holds them. The formula is the standard prophetic statement of shared creaturehood, and it appears in similar form elsewhere in the Qurʾān. Its function here is to remove the possibility of two separate realms — one governed by Allah, another by the idols. There is one governance, and both parties are inside it.

**"My Lord's Way Is Perfect Justice"**

The verse closes with a statement about God's path, and the commentators gloss it in two directions that reinforce each other. God is upon the path of truth; He is just; He does not let wrongdoers escape. And He does not abandon one who relies on Him. The first gloss answers ʿĀd: their plotting will not succeed because injustice does not succeed in the long run. The second answers Hūd's own situation: his trust is not misplaced, because the one he has trusted does not drop what He has taken up. The phrase *straight path* is the same one the Qurʾān's opening sūrah asks to be guided along (1:6), and its use here makes a claim about God rather than a request of Him. The sūrah's earlier verses described the wrongdoers as those who strive to make the path appear crooked. This verse states the actual condition of the path.

---

## Verse 11:57

> But if you turn away, I have already delivered to you what I have been sent with. My Lord will replace you with others. You are not harming Him in the least. Indeed, my Lord is a ˹vigilant˺ Keeper over all things.”

**The Condition Stated Without Pressure**

The verse opens with a conditional that carries no threat in its first clause. *But if you turn away.* Hūd does not say what will happen; he says what he will have done. The conditional is therefore not a lever but a closing statement. He is describing a completed transaction: whatever ʿĀd decide, the delivery has already occurred. The sūrah established the principle in its Mekkan section, where the messenger's duty was limited to conveying the message and guidance was placed elsewhere. This verse is the principle spoken by a messenger at the end of his mission, and its calm is the mark of a man who has understood the limits of his own office. Nothing further can be added by him, and nothing further is needed.

**"My Lord Will Replace You With Others"**

The second clause is the verse's only forward-looking statement, and the commentators gloss it plainly: God will destroy ʿĀd and bring another people who worship Him alone. The Qurʾān makes the same statement elsewhere as a general principle: *"And if you turn away, He will replace you with another people; and they will not be like you"* is the sense carried in several parallel verses (9:39; 47:38). The claim answers an assumption ʿĀd had not stated but clearly held — that they were indispensable. A tribe in possession of land, strength and an ancestral religion naturally supposes that its continuation is necessary to the order of things. The verse denies it. The world will not lack worshippers because this town is removed, and the removal will not leave a gap that cannot be filled.

---

## Verse 11:58

> When Our command came, We rescued Hûd and those who believed with him by a mercy from Us, saving them from a harsh torment

**The Command, and What It Was**

The verse begins with the arrival of the command, and the sūrah leaves the word unexplained here because the parallel accounts supply it. The punishment that came on ʿĀd was a wind, and the Qurʾān describes it in detail elsewhere: a wind that tore people out as though they were uprooted palm trunks (54:20). The commentators connect the wind to the drought described earlier in this narrative, and the connection is what makes the account devastating. ʿĀd had been in drought, Hūd had offered rain as the reward of repentance, and when a cloud finally appeared they said: *"This is a cloud bringing us rain"* (46:24). The next verse answers them: it was the very thing they had asked to be hastened, a wind carrying a painful punishment, destroying everything by its Lord's command until nothing was visible except their dwellings (46:25). A people who mocked a warning were destroyed by the thing they most wanted.

**The Size of the Saved Group**

Some commentators record that those who accepted Hūd's message numbered forty thousand, and the figure, if sound, is striking in comparison with Nūḥ's handful. The sūrah does not give a number, and its silence is consistent with its method: it counts the saved only when the smallness of the number is part of the point, as it was in the flood narrative. Here the emphasis falls on the mercy rather than the tally. What the reader is given is the fact of a rescue and its ground. The Qurʾān's parallel account closes the episode in similar terms: *"So We saved him and those with him by Our mercy and uprooted those who denied Our signs. They were not believers"* (7:72). The two halves of that sentence are the whole of this verse expanded.

---

## Verse 11:59

> That was ’Âd. They denied the signs of their Lord, disobeyed His messengers, and followed the command of every stubborn tyrant

**"That Was ʿĀd" — a Case Closed**

The verse opens with three words that function as a verdict. *That was ʿĀd.* Not *these were the people of ʿĀd*, not *such was their story*, but a flat identification that reads like a file being closed. The sūrah uses the same formula at the end of its later narratives, and its function is always the same: the account is finished, and what follows is the summary of the charge. The abruptness is deliberate. After a narrative containing dialogue, a challenge and a rescue, the reader is given a label. The label reduces a civilisation to a name, and the reduction is the point. A town that occupied nine verses of narrative is disposed of in three words, which is the sūrah's way of stating how much weight it assigns to a people who refused a messenger.

**Three Charges in Sequence**

The verse then lists three things, and the sequence traces a decline. First, they denied the signs of their Lord. Second, they disobeyed His messengers. Third, they followed the command of every stubborn tyrant. The order moves from rejection to disobedience to allegiance, and the third charge is the completion of the first two. A people who deny signs and disobey messengers have created a vacancy in their allegiance, and the verse says what filled it. The commentators note the parallel statement elsewhere that every stubborn tyrant fails (14:15), and they read this verse as meaning that those who follow such people will ultimately be unsuccessful, since God aids His messengers against them. The verse is therefore not describing three separate failures but one failure in three stages.

---

## Verse 11:60

> They were followed by a curse in this world, as they will be on the Day of Judgment. Surely ’Âd denied their Lord. So away with ’Âd, the people of Hûd

**"Surely ʿĀd Denied Their Lord"**

The third clause restates the first charge of the previous verse, and the restatement has a function. After a list of three failures — denying signs, disobeying messengers, following tyrants — the verse reduces them to one: they denied their Lord. The reduction identifies the root. The signs were denied because the one who sent them was denied; the messengers were disobeyed for the same reason; the tyrants were followed because no higher allegiance was recognised. The sūrah's method throughout is to trace behaviour back to a single disposition, and this verse performs the tracing explicitly. It also uses the title *their Lord* — *rabbihim* — which carries the sense of the one who nurtures and maintains. ʿĀd were denying the being who had sustained them, which is what makes the denial more than an intellectual error.

**The Verse as the End of the Second Narrative**

Structurally this verse closes the second of seven narratives, and comparing it with the end of the first shows the sūrah's variation. The Nūḥ narrative ended with an instruction to disembark, a blessing on descendants, and a command to patience. The Hūd narrative ends with a curse and a dismissal. The difference follows the material. Nūḥ's story contained a survivor's question about a promise, and the sūrah had to answer it. Hūd's story contained no such question; his people refused immediately and were destroyed quickly. The sūrah gives each narrative the ending its material requires and moves on. The next narrative begins in the same formula that began this one, and the reader is now trained to hear what the formula means.

---

The curse that follows ʿĀd in this world and on the Day of Resurrection is one the Book records for other destroyed peoples and for Pharaoh's house, on the same pattern: a curse in this life and a curse at the end (28:42). The closing sentence — "So away with ʿĀd, the people of Hūd" — is the sūrah's refrain after each destruction, and its form is a formal dismissal rather than an epitaph, since the chapter repeats it over Thamūd (11:68) and the towns of Lūṭ (11:83, 11:95). The Qur'an preserves the memory of ʿĀd in several places — their pillars at Iram (89:6–8), the wind loosed against them for seven nights and eight days (69:6–7), and the screaming blast of 54:18–20 — which makes the sūrah's verdict the summary of a story its audience already knew.

## Verse 11:61

> And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him. He ˹is the One Who˺ produced you from the earth and settled you on it. So seek His forgiveness and turn to Him in repentance. Surely my Lord is Ever Near, All-Responsive ˹to prayers˺.”

**The Third Narrative, the Same Opening**

The verse that begins the story of Ṣāliḥ uses the formula the sūrah has now used twice: *We sent to X their brother Y.* By the third repetition the reader recognises the shape before the content arrives, and that recognition is the point. The sūrah is not assembling independent stories; it is running a series in which the constants are visible and the variables are what matter. The constants here are the sending, the kinship, the demand for exclusive worship. The variables are the argument Ṣāliḥ adds and the sign he is given. A reader who has followed Nūḥ and Hūd knows what is coming and can attend to what is new, which is exactly how the sūrah wants the material received.

**"My Lord Is Ever Near"**

The verse closes on two divine names, and the first is the sūrah's most intimate. *Qarīb* — near. The commentators gloss it as nearness to those who are sincere in worship and wish to repent, while noting the theological point that God is close to all people, including those who feel distant from Him or deny Him; sincerity is not a condition of His closeness but of awareness of it. The Qurʾān states the same nearness in direct terms: *"When My servants ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Me: I am truly near. I respond to one's prayer when they call upon Me"* (2:186). Placing nearness at the end of a call to repentance is deliberate. A person asked to abandon an inherited religion needs to know that the alternative is accessible, and the verse tells him it is not far away.

---

## Verse 11:62

> They argued, “O Ṣâliḥ! We truly had high hopes in you before this. How dare you forbid us to worship what our forefathers had worshipped? We are certainly in alarming doubt about what you are inviting us to.”

**The Most Personal Objection in the Sūrah**

Every previous reply in this chapter has attacked the messenger's credentials: he is only a man, his followers are nobodies, he has brought no proof, he is bewitched. This reply attacks nothing of the kind. It expresses disappointment. The Thamūd are not disputing Ṣāliḥ's honesty or his sanity. They are telling him he has let them down. The shift in register is the verse's most significant feature, and it makes the objection harder to answer than any of the others. A man accused of lying can produce evidence. A man told that his people had expected better of him has nothing to produce, because the accusation is not about facts. The sūrah places this objection in the third narrative for a reason: it is the objection most likely to move a messenger.

**The Forefathers Again**

The clause about what the forefathers worshipped is the standard objection of every destroyed people in this sūrah, and the Qurʾān records it repeatedly: *"We found our forefathers worshipping them"* (21:53), *"What we found our forefathers practicing is good enough for us"* (5:104). The Book answers it directly elsewhere: *"˹Would they still do so,˺ even if their forefathers had ˹absolutely˺ no understanding or guidance?"* (2:170). The answer identifies the flaw in the appeal: inheritance is not evidence. The Thamūd's formulation is slightly different from the others, because it is embedded in a complaint about Ṣāliḥ rather than stated as a principle. That embedding is what makes it effective. A principle can be examined; a grievance has to be soothed.

---

## Verse 11:63

> He responded, “O my people! Consider if I stand on a clear proof from my Lord and He has blessed me with a mercy from Him. Who could help me against Allah if I were to disobey Him? You would only contribute to my doom

**The Same Formula a Third Time**

Ṣāliḥ's reply opens with the sentence the sūrah has now put in three messengers' mouths: consider if I stand on a clear proof from my Lord, and He has given me a mercy from Himself. The repetition is the sūrah's evidence for the fixed character of the prophetic claim. A man inventing a case adapts it; a man transmitting a message repeats it, and the repetition makes the claim examinable. Ṣāliḥ does not answer the disappointment expressed in the previous verse, does not defend his decision to give up a leadership position, and does not argue about the forefathers. He restates where he stands, which is the only reply available to a messenger facing a social objection.

**"Who Could Help Me Against Allah?"**

The rhetorical question is the same one Nūḥ asked thirty-three verses earlier, and the commentators note the parallel. The form is practical rather than theological: if I disobey Him, who will shield me? The expected answer is nobody. The question converts the town's demand into a question about Ṣāliḥ's own exposure. They are asking him to accommodate their practice; he is telling them that accommodation would leave him undefended. The verse therefore answers the objection without addressing it. A man being asked to compromise is not answering with a principle when he says *who protects me if I do?* He is answering with a fact about consequences, and the fact is not negotiable.

---

## Verse 11:64

> And O my people! This she-camel of Allah is a sign for you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s earth and do her no harm, or a swift punishment will overtake you!”

**A Sign Given in Answer to a Demand**

The she-camel is not introduced as an unsolicited miracle. The parallel accounts show that Thamūd had asked for a sign: *"You are only a human being like us, so bring forth a sign if what you say is true"* (26:154). The commentators record that Ṣāliḥ brought the pregnant camel forth from a large rock in response to that request. The sequence matters for evaluating what follows. This is not a case of a people being given an arbitrary test. They set the terms, the terms were met, and the sign was produced in the form and place they specified. The sūrah's method throughout is to place responsibility with the party that had the choice, and here the choice was explicit. A people who ask for proof and receive it have exhausted their objection.

**"This She-Camel of Allah"**

The possessive construction is the verse's theological centre. The camel is not Ṣāliḥ's, not the town's, and not a wild animal. She belongs to God, and the attribution is repeated in the parallel account: *"this is Allah's she-camel as a sign to you"* (7:73). Naming her as God's property does two things. It removes her from the town's jurisdiction, which is why the command that follows concerns leaving her alone. And it makes any harm done to her an act against the owner rather than against an animal. The verse is therefore not a rule about animal welfare in the abstract. It is a statement about ownership, and the ownership is what makes the coming violation a direct offence rather than a local dispute.

**The Drinking Arrangement**

The parallel accounts supply the other half of the command, which this sūrah leaves implicit. The water was to be divided: *"She will have her turn to drink as you have yours, each on an appointed day"* (26:155), and *"the ˹drinking˺ water must be divided between them ˹and her˺, each taking a turn to drink"* (54:28). The arrangement is precise and it is the practical form of the test. A shared resource with an alternating schedule is the clearest possible measure of whether a people will honour an allocation they did not choose. The camel takes a full day's water, which in a dry region is a substantial cost, and the cost is what the town resents. The verse is not testing their theology in the abstract. It is testing whether they will keep a schedule.

**A Test, Not a Gift**

The Qurʾān describes the camel's function explicitly elsewhere: *"We are sending the she-camel as a test for them. So watch them ˹closely˺, and have patience"* (54:27). The verse is addressed to Ṣāliḥ, and it tells him what the camel is for. She is not a benefit conferred on Thamūd, not a source of milk or labour, and not a reward for anything. She is an instrument of examination. The description explains why the arrangement is costly rather than convenient. A test that costs nothing measures nothing. The town's resentment of the camel is therefore not a misunderstanding of her purpose; it is the correct perception of a burden, and their response to the burden is what the narrative is recording.

---

## Verse 11:65

> But they killed her, so he warned ˹them˺, “You have ˹only˺ three ˹more˺ days to enjoy life in your homes—this is an unfailing promise!”

**The Whole Violation in Two Words**

The verse reports the central act of the narrative in a single verb: they hamstrung her. No account of the decision, no description of the person who did it, no record of a debate. The sūrah has spent two verses establishing the camel's status, the arrangement for her water, and the warning attached to harming her, and then it disposes of the violation in a word. The economy is a judgement. What Thamūd did was not complicated and does not require explanation. They were given a specific instruction about a specific animal, and they killed the animal. Everything else in the narrative — the disappointment, the appeal to the forefathers, the doubt — was preamble to this.

**Why Hamstringing Specifically**

The verb is precise: *ʿaqarūhā*, they cut her leg sinews. Hamstringing is not the quickest way to kill an animal; it is the way to disable one so that it can be finished at leisure. The choice of method is recorded because it removes any reading in which the killing was an accident, a panic or a mercy. It was a deliberate, staged act performed on an animal the town knew belonged to God. The parallel account states the disposition behind it: *"Then they killed the she-camel—defying their Lord's command"* (7:77). The defiance is explicit, and the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf adds that they then challenged Ṣāliḥ to bring on the punishment he had threatened. The sequence — disable, kill, taunt — is the same sequence the sūrah recorded in Nūḥ's narrative and in its Mekkan section.

---

## Verse 11:66

> When Our command came, We saved Ṣâliḥ and those who believed with him by a mercy from Us and spared them the disgrace of that Day. Surely your Lord ˹alone˺ is the All-Powerful, Almighty

**"And From the Disgrace of That Day"**

The extra clause is the verse's distinctive contribution. The believers were spared not only the punishment but the disgrace of the day. The word *khizy* denotes humiliation, exposure, the loss of standing — the same word used in verse 39 for the humiliating torment promised to Nūḥ's mockers. The sūrah has treated disgrace as a distinct category throughout, and its treatment is consistent: destruction is one thing, and being disgraced in the destruction is another. A person can die without being humiliated, and the verse says that this mercy was given to the believers. The addition tells the reader that the rescue was complete in a way that mere survival is not.

**"Your Lord Is the Strong, the Mighty"**

*Qawī* — strong, possessing power; *ʿazīz* — mighty, unconquerable, whose will cannot be thwarted. The two names answer two different doubts. Strength answers the question of capacity: could God have carried out the punishment? Might answers the question of opposition: could anyone have prevented it? The sūrah has addressed both doubts in its doctrinal section, where the wrongdoers were told they could not frustrate God on earth and had no protector besides Him. The verse restates the point at the moment of execution, which is where it has its full force. The punishment did not arrive because the town was weak. It arrived because the one who decreed it is strong, and nobody could intervene.

---

## Verse 11:67

> And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes

**The Punishment Named as a Sound**

The verse identifies what destroyed Thamūd, and the identification is unusual. It was not water, not wind, not fire. It was a cry — *al-ṣayḥa*, a shout, a blast, an overwhelming sound. The commentators connect it to the earthquake recorded in the parallel account: *"Then an ˹overwhelming˺ earthquake struck them, and they fell lifeless in their homes"* (7:78), and they take the two descriptions as referring to one event, an earthquake accompanied by a terrible sound. The naming of the punishment as a sound is significant. It is the least material of all the punishments in the sūrah. A flood can be escaped by height, a wind by shelter, stones by cover. A sound passes through walls, and the verse says it did.

**"Those Who Did Wrong"**

The verse identifies its victims by a moral description rather than by name. Not *Thamūd*, but *those who did wrong*. The choice is deliberate and it does two things. It connects the destruction to the charge rather than to the ethnicity, which means the reader cannot treat the event as something that happened to a particular ancient tribe. And it makes the description transferable. Any group that does what Thamūd did falls under the same label, and the sūrah will apply the same vocabulary to Midian later in this chapter. The verse is therefore a rule stated in the form of a report, which is the sūrah's preferred method throughout its narrative section.

---

## Verse 11:68

> as if they had never lived there. Surely Thamûd denied their Lord, so away with Thamûd

**"As Though They Had Never Thrived There"**

The verse opens by completing the previous one, and the phrase it uses is precise. The Arabic verb behind *lived* carries the sense of thriving, prospering, dwelling in comfort — not merely existing. The Thamūd did not simply occupy their homes; they flourished in them, and the verse says the flourishing left no trace. The commentators note that the identical wording is used for Midian later in this sūrah, which marks it as a formula. The formula's function is to describe a specific kind of erasure. A town that is destroyed but remembered has left something behind. A town described as though it had never prospered has left nothing, and the nothing is what the verse is reporting.

**"Surely Thamūd Denied Their Lord"**

The middle clause repeats the charge the sūrah made against ʿĀd eight verses earlier, in the same words with a different name. The repetition is the sūrah's method of summary: after the narrative, the root failure is named. Denial of the Lord is the origin of everything else — the rejection of the messenger, the appeal to the forefathers, the hamstringing of the camel, the taunt about bringing on the punishment. The verse reduces eight verses of narrative to that single diagnosis, and the reduction is what makes the section portable. A reader who remembers nothing else can remember the charge, and the charge is the same in every case. The title used is *their Lord*, the one who nurtures and maintains, which is what makes the denial more than an intellectual position.

---

## Verse 11:69

> And surely Our messenger-angels came to Abraham with good news ˹of a son˺. They greeted ˹him with˺, “Peace!” And he replied, “Peace ˹be upon you˺!” Then it was not long before he brought ˹them˺ a ˹fat,˺ roasted calf

**A Fattened, Roasted Calf**

The food is described with two adjectives: fat and roasted. The commentators note that the parallel account calls it a fattened calf (51:26). The specificity serves the narrative rather than the appetite. A fattened animal is one kept for an occasion and slaughtered for it; roasting takes time. Ibrāhīm did not offer whatever was at hand. He gave the best available thing, prepared properly, to strangers who had not asked for anything. The sūrah is establishing the standard of the host before it introduces the anomaly of the guests, and the establishment requires detail. A reader needs to know how much was offered to understand what the refusal cost.

**A New Kind of Narrative Begins**

The fourth narrative differs from the three before it in structure. There is no opening formula — no *We sent to X their brother Y* — and no demand for exclusive worship addressed to a town. The verse opens with visitors arriving at a house. The sūrah has spent forty-four verses on the pattern of a messenger sent to a people; here it turns to a domestic scene, and the turn is the point. Ibrāhīm is not being shown preaching.

**"Our Messengers" — Plural and Unnamed**

The verse calls the visitors *our messengers*, and the commentators identify them as a group of angels, with the reports varying on their number. The Qurʾān describes them elsewhere as Ibrāhīm's honoured guests (51:24) and simply as his guests (15:51). Neither their names nor their number is given here, and the sūrah's silence is consistent with its treatment of the unseen throughout: it reports what is needed and omits what is not. What matters for the narrative is that they came from God, that they came with news, and that Ibrāhīm did not initially know what they were. The verse sets up a scene in which a prophet is uncertain about his own guests, and the uncertainty drives everything that follows.

**The Greeting Exchanged**

The visitors say *peace*, and Ibrāhīm answers *peace*. The exchange is short and its form is worth noting: the reply repeats the greeting rather than extending it, and the parallel account records that the reply was given with a reservation in his mind — *"˹These are˺ an unfamiliar people"* (51:25). The two facts together describe a host who is courteous and alert at the same time. He returns the greeting properly and he notices that these are strangers. The sūrah records both because both matter: the courtesy explains what he does next, and the alertness explains what he feels in the following verse. A reader who takes the greeting as mere formality misses the tension the verse is building.

---

## Verse 11:70

> And when he saw that their hands did not reach for the food, he became suspicious and fearful of them. They reassured ˹him˺, “Do not be afraid! We are ˹angels˺ sent ˹only˺ against the people of Lot.”

**The Signal That Something Was Wrong**

The verse turns on a small physical fact: their hands did not reach for the food. Nothing is said, nothing is refused in words, no explanation is offered. The guests simply do not eat. The sūrah records the observation rather than the conclusion, which is a mark of its narrative method — it shows the reader the evidence and lets the reaction follow. The parallel account gives the same sequence: he placed the food before them and asked *"Will you not eat?"*, and when they did not, he grew fearful (51:27–28). The fear is not presented as a failure of perception. It is the correct reading of a signal that, in the culture of the time, meant something specific.

**Fear in the House of a Prophet**

The verse states plainly that Ibrāhīm was afraid, and the statement is worth holding. A prophet is not depicted here as serene, untroubled or supernaturally informed. He is a host who has noticed something wrong and is frightened in his own house. The Qurʾān's account of the same scene in al-Dhāriyāt records that he had already thought them an unfamiliar people (51:25), so the fear had a foundation before the food was refused. The sūrah's willingness to record a prophet's fear is consistent with its treatment of the messengers throughout: Nūḥ distressed, this Prophet's chest constricted, Ṣāliḥ told to watch and be patient. The verse is not diminishing Ibrāhīm. It is describing a man who has been given incomplete information and is responding to what he can see.

**"Do Not Be Afraid"**

The reassurance is immediate and it is the guests' first direct statement about themselves. The commentators note that the reply was prompted by a question Ibrāhīm asked, recorded in the parallel accounts: *"What is your mission, O messengers?"* (51:31; 15:57). The sequence is therefore: fear, question, reassurance, disclosure. The sūrah compresses the question and reports the answer, which keeps the movement fast. The reassurance is not a general comfort. It is a specific correction of a specific misapprehension, and it is followed at once by the information that makes the correction meaningful. The verse does not leave the fear unresolved for long, and the speed of the resolution is part of the narrative's economy.

**"Sent Against the People of Lūṭ"**

The disclosure is blunt. The commentators gloss it plainly: they have come to destroy them. The verse does not soften the phrase, does not explain the reason, and does not describe what will happen. It names the target and stops. The naming is what produces the rest of the narrative, because Lūṭ's town is close enough to Ibrāhīm's concern that he will plead for it. The sūrah's parallel accounts give the same disclosure in almost identical words: *"We have actually been sent to a wicked people"* (51:32; 15:58). The consistency across three sūrahs marks it as a fixed element of the story, and its fixedness suggests that the disclosure itself — not the destruction — is what the narrative is about.

---

## Verse 11:71

> And his wife was standing by, so she laughed, then We gave her good news of ˹the birth of˺ Isaac, and, after him, Jacob

**A Wife Standing, Not Sitting**

The verse notes a posture, and the note is deliberate. Ibrāhīm's wife was standing. The detail places her inside the scene rather than at its edge — present, upright, attending to what is happening rather than withdrawn from it. The parallel account in al-Dhāriyāt describes her differently, coming forward with a cry and clasping her forehead (51:29), which suggests movement across the two tellings. This sūrah's version has her stationary and watching, and the stillness makes the laughter more striking. A person who is standing in a room where strangers have refused to eat, and who then laughs, has drawn a conclusion. The verse does not state the conclusion; it records the sound that came from it.

**Two Names Given in One Verse**

The good news is specific and it names two people: Isḥāq, and after him Yaʿqūb. The naming distinguishes this account from the parallel ones, where the son is described as knowledgeable (51:28; 15:53) without being named in the same breath. The sūrah's choice to give both names does specific work. It is not announcing a birth; it is announcing a line. A son is one generation; a son and a grandson are a beginning. The verse therefore transforms a domestic announcement into a statement about history, and it does so with two proper nouns. The reader is told, in the middle of a scene about frightened hospitality, that two of the prophets in this chapter's lineage are about to exist.

---

## Verse 11:72

> She wondered, “Oh, my! How can I have a child in this old age, and my husband here is an old man? This is truly an astonishing thing!”

**An Exclamation, Not an Argument**

The verse records a woman's first response to an impossible announcement, and the form of the response is informative. It opens with an exclamation — *oh, my* — which is not a word of protest but a sound of overwhelm. The commentators note the traditional identifications: this is Sārah, and the accounts give her age at the time as ninety-nine, with Ibrāhīm at a hundred and twenty. The verse does not state the ages; it states the reaction, and the reaction carries the ages inside it. The sūrah's method here is consistent with its treatment of the whole encounter: it reports what people said and did, and leaves the reader to reconstruct the circumstances. What is recorded is not disbelief but astonishment, and the distinction is what the next verse will confirm.

**The Verse and Other Such Wonders**

The Qurʾān records the same response in a later prophet's case, and the parallel is instructive. Zakariyyā asks how he can have a son when his wife is barren and he has reached old age, and the answer he receives is direct: *"So will it be! Your Lord says, 'It is easy for Me, just as I created you before, when you were nothing!'"* (19:9). The two cases share a structure: an elderly man, a barren or aged wife, an astonished question, and an answer that locates the possibility in creation rather than in biology. The sūrah of Hūd gives the answer in a different form, and the difference is worth noting in the verse that follows. Here the reply is not an explanation of mechanism but a question about the hearer's premise.

---

## Verse 11:73

> They responded, “Are you astonished by Allah’s decree? May Allah’s mercy and blessings be upon you, O people of this house. Indeed, He is Praiseworthy, All-Glorious.”

**A Question Answering a Question**

The reply to the woman's wonder is not an explanation. It is a counter-question: are you astonished at the decree of God? The form is significant. An explanation would have described a mechanism — how a barren woman conceives, how an old man fathers. The question instead addresses the premise of the astonishment. The commentators note the parallel with God's answer to Zakariyyā's prayer (19:9), and the two replies differ in method: that one explains by pointing to creation, this one corrects by pointing to the source. The sūrah chooses the second form because the woman's wonder was not about biology. It was about whether such a thing could be decreed at all, and the counter-question answers exactly that.

**Praiseworthy, All-Glorious**

The verse closes on two divine names, and the pairing answers the woman's astonishment in a third way. *Ḥamīd* — praised, worthy of praise, the one whose acts merit gratitude. *Majīd* — glorious, noble, abundant in honour and generosity. The first name addresses the response owed to the decree: it is to be praised rather than marvelled at. The second addresses its character: what God does is generous and abundant, not grudging or minimal. Together the names tell the hearer how to receive an impossible announcement. The sūrah uses the same device at the end of its other narratives, closing an account with the names that summarise its meaning. Here the names are spoken by guests, which makes them part of the dialogue rather than a narrator's comment.

---

## Verse 11:74

> Then after the fear had left Abraham, and the good news had reached him, he began to plead with Us for the people of Lot

**Two States Named Before the Plea**

The verse describes Ibrāhīm's condition at the moment he begins to intercede, and it names two things. The fear had gone, and the good news had arrived. Both are recorded, and both are conditions of what follows. A man still afraid cannot plead for anyone; his attention is on his own safety. A man who has not received good news has nothing standing behind his confidence. The verse is therefore not simply narrating a sequence.

**He Pleaded for a Town That Was Not His**

The object of the plea is significant. Lūṭ's people were not Ibrāhīm's people. They were not his tribe, his town, or his mission; the commentators note that the plea consisted of speaking to the angels and trying to dissuade them from bringing destruction on them. The parallel account records the substance of the objection: *"But Lot is there!"* — to which the answer came that God knows best who is there and will save him and his family (29:32). The verse is therefore recording an intercession on behalf of strangers, made by a man who had just been told that the destruction was already decided. The sūrah presents this as characteristic of Ibrāhīm rather than as an anomaly, and the next verse will explain why.

---

## Verse 11:75

> Truly, Abraham was forbearing, tender-hearted, and ever turning ˹to his Lord˺

**Ḥalīm — Forbearance**

The first quality is *ḥalīm*, usually rendered forbearing or clement, and the word denotes a person who does not react quickly to provocation. It is the quality of a man who can absorb an offence without answering it in kind. The sūrah has used the same vocabulary elsewhere, and its theological weight is considerable: the name *al-Ḥalīm* is applied to God Himself. Applied to Ibrāhīm, it explains a specific feature of the plea. A man who is quick to anger would have demanded the town's destruction when its people threatened his nephew. A forbearing man keeps looking for a reason it might be spared. The quality is therefore not a soft virtue in this context. It is what makes intercession possible at all, since intercession requires a person to keep arguing for people who have given him nothing.

**The Same Description Elsewhere**

The Qurʾān describes Ibrāhīm in similar terms in another context, and the comparison sharpens the meaning here. In al-Tawbah his prayer for his father's forgiveness is discussed, and the verse closes: *"Abraham was truly tender-hearted, forbearing"* (9:114). The two descriptions share the same core qualities and attach them to two different acts — pleading for a father in one case, pleading for a town in the other. The consistency establishes that these are not situational reactions but settled traits. The sūrah of Hūd adds the third quality, the ever-turning, which the other verse does not name. The addition is what allows the reader to see how the tenderness stays within bounds: the man who pleads is also the man who keeps returning.

---

## Verse 11:76

> ˹The angels said,˺ “O Abraham! Plead no more! Your Lord’s decree has already come, and they will certainly be afflicted with a punishment that cannot be averted!”

**A Command to Stop**

The verse is an instruction, and its form is direct. The commentators take the words as spoken by the angels, and the same expression appears elsewhere in the Qurʾān in a different context: *"O Joseph! Forget about this"* (12:29). The phrase tells a person to drop a matter, and its use here is unambiguous. Ibrāhīm is not being answered on the merits of his plea. He is being told that the pleading has reached its end. The sūrah records the instruction without softening it, and the bluntness is part of the teaching. There is a point beyond which intercession does not merely fail but becomes inappropriate, and the verse marks that point with a command rather than with an explanation.

**"Turn Away From This"**

The wording of the instruction is worth holding. It does not say *your plea is rejected* or *the town deserves this*. It says *turn away from this* — leave the matter, set it down. The commentators gloss the command as a direction to stop the intercession because the decree has been passed. The phrase identifies what the intercessor is to do, not what God has decided. That distinction matters. Ibrāhīm is not being told that his concern was misplaced or that the people were unworthy of it. He is being told that the matter is no longer open. The verse therefore separates two things that are easily fused: the legitimacy of the feeling and the availability of the outcome. The first is affirmed by the previous verse; the second is closed by this one.

---

## Verse 11:77

> When Our messenger-angels came to Lot, he was distressed and worried by their arrival. He said, “This is a terrible day.”

**Handsome Youths, and a Problem**

The commentators record that the visitors came in the form of handsome young men, and that Lūṭ did not know they were angels. The detail explains the distress in this verse. The problem is not that strangers have arrived; a prophet would welcome strangers. The problem is that these particular strangers, in this particular town, are in immediate danger. The sūrah has already described the men of Lūṭ's people as aggressive in pursuing their desires, and the parallel account records them coming to the house rejoicing (15:67). The verse is therefore recording a host who recognises the danger before anyone has done anything, and the recognition is what makes the evening terrible rather than merely difficult.

**"This Is a Difficult Day"**

The exclamation is short and unguarded. The commentators take the day referred to as the day of the guests' arrival, and the word used carries the sense of something hard to bear. The verse preserves the sentence exactly as it would have been spoken — not a prayer, not a statement of faith, but a man saying aloud that the day is hard. The sūrah's willingness to record this is consistent with its treatment of the prophets throughout: Nūḥ questioning God about his son, Ibrāhīm afraid of his guests, this Prophet's chest constricted. The exclamation is not a failure of trust. It is the sound a person makes when he understands his situation completely and has no way out of it yet. The verse ends there, and the next verse brings the people to the door.

---

## Verse 11:78

> And ˹the men of˺ his people—who were used to shameful deeds—came to him rushing. He pleaded, “O my people! Here are my daughters ˹for marriage˺—they are pure for you. So fear Allah, and do not humiliate me by disrespecting my guests. Is there not ˹even˺ a single right-minded man among you?”

**A People Coming in Haste**

The verse describes the arrival of the people with a word that carries speed and compulsion, and the parallel account adds that they came rejoicing (15:67). The sūrah's phrase suggests men driven by something rather than walking toward something, and the commentators connect it to the aggression already noted in the previous verses. The haste is the first evidence in the verse, and it is evidence about them rather than about Lūṭ. A group that arrives at a prophet's house in a rush, at night, having heard that strangers are inside, is not arriving to discuss anything. The verse records the movement before it records the speech, and the movement establishes what the speech will fail to change.

**"They Had Long Been Committing Evil Deeds"**

The clause is placed before the dialogue, and its function is to inform the reader rather than the characters. Lūṭ already knows what his people do; the reader is being told so that what follows can be understood. The word rendered here as evil deeds is the same one used in the previous verse for the wrongdoers generally, and its application here is specific rather than general. The verse is therefore establishing that the demand about to be made is not an aberration or a single drunken impulse. It is the settled practice of the town. The sūrah elsewhere describes the same behaviour explicitly: *"Do you commit a shameful deed that no man has ever done before? You lust after men instead of women!"* (7:80–81). The clause here assumes that background without restating it.

**"They Are Purer for You"**

The comparative is the phrase that gives the offer its moral weight. Lūṭ does not say the daughters are permissible or available; he says they are purer. The commentators connect the word to the taunt his people used against him and his followers: *"They are a people who wish to remain chaste!"* (7:82; 27:56). The same vocabulary of purity is therefore in play on both sides — his people use it as an accusation, and he uses it as an argument. The verse shows a man appealing to a standard his hearers have already rejected by name. The appeal is not naive. It is the last available ground, and the sūrah records it because the refusal of that ground is what the following verses describe.

**"Is There No Right-Minded Man Among You?"**

The final question is the verse's most searching line. Lūṭ is not asking for a favour; he is asking whether a single person of sound judgement remains in the town. The question assumes what the previous clause stated — that the practice is general — and asks whether the generality is total. The sūrah has already answered the question elsewhere: Lūṭ's household contained no believing family except one, and the wife was among those who remained behind (29:33). The verse therefore records a question whose answer the reader already knows, and the effect is to make the refusal that follows more complete. A man asks whether anyone among a whole town can think straight, and the answer given in the next verse is not that one man can. It is that none of them want what he is offering.

---

## Verse 11:79

> They argued, “You certainly know that we have no need for your daughters. You already know what we desire!”

**"You Know We Have No Claim to Your Daughters"**

The commentators gloss the phrase as meaning they have no need of the daughters. The word used carries the sense of a want or a requirement, and its negation is significant. They are not saying the daughters are forbidden to them or that they have no right to marry them. They are saying they do not want them. The verse is therefore recording a refusal at the level of appetite rather than of law. The distinction matters for what follows, because a refusal of appetite cannot be met with a change of terms. Lūṭ offered the lawful route and it was declined not on grounds but on preference. The sūrah preserves the exact form of the refusal so that the reader can see that nothing further was available to say.

**The Verse as the Depth of the Case**

Read in sequence, the verse marks a boundary the sūrah has been approaching. The previous verse contained every available appeal: an alternative, a religious command, a social obligation, and a question about reason. This verse disposes of all four in two clauses. The sūrah's narratives regularly reach this point, where a messenger's arguments are exhausted and what remains is the outcome. The difference here is that the exhaustion happens in a single night rather than over years. Nūḥ argued for nine hundred and fifty; Ṣāliḥ argued until the camel was hamstrung. Lūṭ argues for one evening and is answered with a statement of appetite. The verse is therefore the fastest refusal in the sūrah, and its speed is a measure of how far the town has gone.

---

## Verse 11:80

> He responded, “If only I had the strength ˹to resist you˺ or could rely on a strong supporter.”

**A Wish, Not a Prayer**

The verse records an exclamation of a particular kind. It is not addressed to God and it is not a petition. It is a wish about himself — *if only I had*. The sūrah preserves the sentence in that form, and the form tells the reader where Lūṭ's attention is. He is not asking for rescue; he is measuring his own capacity and finding it short. The verse therefore records a moment of complete clarity about limitation. The man has argued, offered and appealed, and having exhausted those routes he turns to the one remaining question: what force do I actually have? The answer is none, and the verse is the sound of a person arriving at that answer in the middle of the night.

**What the Verse Records About Isolation**

The verse is the sūrah's clearest picture of prophetic isolation. Everything Lūṭ names as missing is missing because of who he is. He has no army because he has no followers. He has no support because his household does not share his faith — the accounts state that his family contained no believer except one, and that his wife was among those left behind (29:33). The verse therefore describes a man whose isolation is produced by his own message. The sūrah has been building toward this throughout: a messenger's truth does not bring him allies, and the absence of allies does not make the truth false. The verse states the cost in one sentence, and the following verse supplies what the cost could not.

---

## Verse 11:81

> The angels said, “O Lot! We are the messengers of your Lord. They will never reach you. So travel with your family in the dark of night, and do not let any of you look back, except your wife. She will certainly suffer the fate of the others. Their appointed time is the morning. Is the morning not near?”

**The Exception of the Wife**

The verse names one exception and then explains it in the same breath. The wife is excluded from the instruction not to look back because she is excluded from the rescue, and the reason given is that she will certainly suffer the fate of the others. The commentators are consistent on her position: she outwardly followed Lūṭ's religion but was inwardly a disbeliever, and she is counted among those who remained behind (7:83; 15:60; 26:171; 27:57). The sūrah has already established the principle in the Nūḥ narrative, where a prophet's son was refused because he was not of the family in the sense that matters. The verse here applies the same principle to a prophet's wife, and the application is stated in a single clause without elaboration.

**"They Will Not Reach You"**

The phrase is absolute, and its placement before the instruction matters. Lūṭ is told he is safe before he is told what to do, and the order is significant. A man who is still afraid cannot follow a complicated instruction; his attention is on the danger. The verse removes the danger first and then gives the direction. The same structure appeared in the Ibrāhīm narrative, where the reassurance preceded the disclosure. The sūrah's method with frightened people is consistent: settle the fear, then give the command. The verse is therefore not only informing Lūṭ. It is putting him in a state where information can be acted on, and the following clauses assume that state has been achieved.

**"Is Not the Morning Near?"**

The closing question is the verse's most striking line. It is asked by the angels, and its function is not to seek information. The morning's nearness is a fact they know and Lūṭ does not, and the question makes him feel the time remaining. The commentators note that the appointed time for the destruction was the morning, and the same timing appears in the parallel account: the blast took them at sunrise (15:73). The verse therefore ends the night's events by pointing at the hour, and the pointing does two things at once. It explains the urgency of the instruction and it states the certainty of the outcome. A reader is left with a household walking out of a town in the dark toward a morning he already knows the content of.

---

## Verse 11:82

> When Our command came, We turned the cities upside down and rained down on them clustered stones of baked clay

**"When Our Command Came"**

The verse opens with the sūrah's fixed formula for the moment of destruction, and its use here connects this narrative to every other in the chapter. The command that came to Nūḥ's people through the oven, to Thamūd at morning, and to Midian through the blast comes here in the same words. The commentators link the command in this case to the blast that seized Lūṭ's people at sunrise (15:73), and the identification matters: the sūrah is describing a single event with two aspects rather than two separate punishments. The formula's repetition across the narratives is what makes the section an argument. Each destruction is the same decision arriving in a different form, and the verse's opening words place this one inside the pattern without needing to explain it.

**The Cities Turned Upside Down**

The commentators report that the people of Lūṭ lived in five cities according to some accounts and seven according to others, with Sodom the greatest of them. The verse describes the reversal plainly: the uppermost was made the lowermost. One reading given by the commentators is that the greatest of the cities became the lowliest. The parallel account uses the same language: *"We turned the cities ˹of Sodom and Gomorrah˺ upside down"* (15:74). The sūrah records the inversion rather than the mechanism, and the inversion is the point. A town that considered itself beyond reach is described in the single image that most completely reverses its position. The verse needs no commentary on the pride of the destroyed; the geometry of the punishment states it.

**Stones of Baked Clay**

The second element of the punishment is a rain of stones, and the description of the material is specific. Baked clay is fired earth — a substance made by human hands, used for building, and here used as the instrument of a town's end. The sūrah describes the same rain in three other places: *"We poured upon them a rain ˹of brimstone˺"* (7:84), and the same phrase in 26:173 and 27:58. The recurrence across four sūrahs marks the detail as fixed. The verse's choice of material is worth noting alongside its choice of method: the cities were overturned from below and struck from above, and the stones that struck them were made of the earth the cities were built from. The sūrah does not comment on this; it reports it.

**The Verse and Its Parallels**

The destruction of Lūṭ's people is narrated in seven places in the Qurʾān, and the accounts differ in emphasis. The version in al-ʿAnkabūt records the dialogue at greater length; the version in al-Qamar is the briefest; the version in al-Ḥijr adds the sunrise timing. This sūrah's account is distinctive in what it omits: it does not describe the people's response to the angels, does not narrate the household's departure, and does not name the cities. The verse is therefore stripped to the punishment itself, and the stripping is deliberate. The sūrah's interest in this narrative has been in the household — the host, the guests, the plea, the exception of the wife. Once those are settled, the destruction needs only a verse and a half.

---

## Verse 11:83

> marked by your Lord ˹O Prophet˺. And these stones are not far from the ˹pagan˺ wrongdoers

**"Marked By Your Lord"**

The verse's first phrase modifies the stones, and the modification changes what they are. Stones that fall are a natural event. Stones that are marked are instruments, and the marking is attributed to God directly. The commentators explain the attribution: each stone fell according to God's judgement. The phrase therefore converts the previous verse's physical description into a statement about intention. The sūrah has used the same device throughout its narratives, where the punishment is never described as a catastrophe but always as an act. The verse is the clearest instance in the chapter, because the marking is on the individual stones rather than on the event as a whole. What fell was not weather. It was a series of decisions with a destination each.

**Stones With Names**

The commentators report a tradition that each stone had inscribed on it the name of the person for whom it was intended. The same wording appears in al-Dhāriyāt: *"marked by your Lord for the transgressors"* (51:34). The verse does not state the inscription itself, and the tradition supplies what the text implies. Whether or not the inscription was literal, the claim being made is precise: the punishment was distributed by person rather than applied collectively. The sūrah elsewhere distinguishes between a punishment that takes a people as a whole and one that reaches individuals; here it makes the individual claim explicit. A reader is being told that no one in those cities was struck by accident, and that none of them escaped by being overlooked.

---

## Verse 11:84

> And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib. He said, “O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him. And do not give short measure and weight. I do see you in prosperity now, but I truly fear for you the torment of an overwhelming Day

**The Sixth and Final Narrative**

This is the last full narrative in the sūrah's cycle, and its position matters. Four destructions have been narrated — Nūḥ's people, ʿĀd, Thamūd and Lūṭ's towns — and the Mūsā account that follows is shorter and differently shaped. The Shuʿayb narrative is therefore the climax of the cycle rather than one more entry in it. The sūrah signals this by giving the narrative the longest dialogue in the section: eleven verses of exchange, against Hūd's eleven and Ṣāliḥ's eight. The reader is being given more speech here than anywhere else in the cycle, and the extra speech is where the sūrah's argument about justice is made. What the previous narratives established as doctrine, this one states as practice.

**"Their Brother Shuʿayb"**

The verse uses the sūrah's standard formula for a prophet's relation to his people, and the commentators add that Shuʿayb is identified by many Muslim authorities with Jethro, the father-in-law of Mūsā, and that he was sent to Midian in northwestern Arabia. The brotherhood is what makes the accusation that follows bearable. A stranger denouncing a town's commerce would be dismissed as an outsider. A brother of the same tribe cannot be dismissed that way, and the people's response in the following verses confirms that they recognise the tie even while rejecting the message. The verse therefore establishes the relation before the charge, and the relation is what gives the charge its force.

---

Shuʿayb is the last of the messengers the sūrah narrates, and his message returns to the two concerns that frame the whole passage: the worship of God alone and fairness in the marketplace. The fuller account in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf adds the people's threat and Shuʿayb's reply, and the sūrah of the Poets repeats the call to give full measure and not to cheat (26:177–183). The Book's linking of the two is deliberate: honesty in trade is treated as part of what the messengers were sent for, and the market is put under the same law as the sanctuary. The two villages addressed are named Midian and the companions of the thicket, so the audience is shown that the pattern extended from the towns of the desert to the trade routes of the north.

## Verse 11:85

> O my people! Give full measure and weigh with justice. Do not defraud people of their property, nor go about spreading corruption in the land

**"Weigh With Justice"**

The word for justice here is *qisṭ*, and the commentators take the phrase to mean weighing with an even balance — neither short nor, by implication, excessive in the other direction. The requirement is precision rather than generosity. The verse is not asking merchants to be lavish; it is asking them to be accurate. The distinction matters because accuracy is testable. A person can check whether his measure is full and whether his balance is level, and the check can be made by the person on the other side of the transaction. The sūrah's use of a measurable standard is deliberate. A charge that can be verified is harder to dismiss than a charge about intention, and the people's response in the following verses shows them trying to dismiss it anyway.

**"Spreading Corruption in the Land"**

The final phrase widens the scope beyond the marketplace. The commentators link it to the general prohibition against working corruption on the earth, and the same phrase appears in the parallel account in al-Aʿrāf (7:85). The word *fasād* denotes disorder, ruin, the breaking of a thing's proper condition. Applied to a society, it describes a state in which ordinary dealings cannot be relied on. The verse is therefore making a claim about the social effect of the practice: systematic cheating does not merely transfer wealth, it destroys the conditions that make exchange possible. The sūrah elsewhere uses the same vocabulary for the effect of rejecting the messengers, and its application here to commerce places trade inside the same category of moral consequence.

---

## Verse 11:86

> What is left ˹as a lawful gain˺ by Allah is far better for you if you are ˹truly˺ believers. And I am not a keeper over you.”

**"What Is Left by Allah"**

The phrase translates *baqiyyat Allāh*, and the word means a remainder or that which remains. The commentators give the primary reading directly: wealth obtained through honest practice and given by God is better than gains acquired through fraud, the remainder being what is left after a person gives full measure and abstains from the unlawful. The formulation is economical. It does not prohibit the gain; it compares two kinds of gain and states which is better. The verse is therefore arguing with a merchant on his own terms. The assumption behind the fraud is that more is better. The verse accepts the assumption and disputes the arithmetic: the smaller lawful amount, being what remains after the unlawful has been left, is the greater of the two.

**The Two Readings of the Remnant**

A second reading runs in another direction, and it deserves equal weight. The remnant may mean what God keeps for a person on the far side of this life — His acceptance, His pleasure, a reward that does not run out. On this reading the comparison is not between two sums but between a sum and something with no equivalent in currency. The sūrah states the same preference in other words elsewhere: what is with Allah is far better and more lasting for those who believe and put their trust in Him (42:36). Both readings follow from the word itself, which means simply what stays behind. What stays behind depends on the horizon a person measures against, and the verse leaves the horizon to the hearer rather than fixing it for him.

---

## Verse 11:87

> They asked ˹sarcastically˺, “O Shu’aib! Does your prayer command you that we should abandon what our forefathers worshipped or give up managing our wealth as we please? Indeed, you are such a tolerant, sensible man!”

**The Mocking Compliment**

The closing sentence praises Shuʿayb, and the commentators are clear that the praise is inverted. They note that even in calling him forbearing and of sound judgement, the people intimate that he has these qualities only in his own estimation. The word rendered *tolerant* here is the same word the sūrah used of Ibrāhīm — *ḥalīm* — and its use as mockery is a measure of how far the people are from taking him seriously. The sūrah preserves the compliment because the preservation exposes them. A person who must mock a prophet's character rather than answer his argument has run out of answers. The verse does not need to say so; the compliment does the work.

**The Verse and the Sūrah's Other Mockeries**

The sūrah has recorded mockery in every narrative, and comparing the instances shows a progression. Nūḥ's notables called him a man gone astray. ʿĀd dismissed Hūd as one had. Thamūd's elite said they would not follow the lowest among them. Lūṭ's people made no argument at all. Midian's mockery is the most sophisticated of the set: it takes the form of a question, includes a compliment, and identifies the source of the demand rather than the demand itself. The verse is therefore showing the most developed form of refusal the sūrah has encountered, and the development makes it more dangerous rather than less. A mockery that sounds reasonable is harder for a listener to see through, which is why the sūrah preserves it in full. What the verse records, then, is a closed mind announcing itself politely — and the politeness is what makes the record useful.

---

The mockery has a subject that explains it: the objection is not to worship but to the effect of the message on their commerce. "Does your prayer command you that we should abandon what our forefathers worshipped, or that we should not do as we please with our wealth?" The Book records the same resistance in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf, where the people of Shuʿayb threaten to drive him out (7:88), and later in the sūrah of the Cave, where the objection of the prosperous man to the believer's company is the same: that the poor and humble should not be listened to. The verse's sarcastic compliments — the forbearing, the rightly guided — are the community's way of dismissing a man whose argument they cannot answer.

## Verse 11:88

> He said, “O my people! Consider if I stand on a clear proof from my Lord and He has blessed me with a good provision from Him. I do not want to do what I am forbidding you from. I only intend reform to the best of my ability. My success comes only through Allah. In Him I trust and to Him I turn

**"A Clear Proof From My Lord"**

The commentators identify the clear proof as the miracles given to prophets to establish their veracity, and they note the parallel with the same phrase in al-Aʿrāf (7:85). The verse's use of the phrase is conditional in form — *consider if I stand on* — and the condition is rhetorical rather than doubtful. Shuʿayb is not wondering whether he has a proof; he is asking his hearers to consider the case in which he does. The formulation is significant because it shifts the burden. The people have dismissed the message by mocking its source. The verse asks them to examine the source rather than the messenger's character, and the asking is done in the form of an invitation rather than an assertion.

**"In Him I Trust and to Him I Turn"**

The final clause combines two movements, and the commentators note that the same words are taught to this Prophet in 42:10. Trust is reliance on God for what one cannot control. Turning is the movement of return described throughout the sūrah as *tawba* — seeking forgiveness and coming back. The pairing describes a person who has both surrendered the outcome and kept the relation. The verse's decision to end here is significant. Having stated his proof, his integrity, his limited intention and his dependence, Shuʿayb closes with the two dispositions that hold the rest together. The sūrah has been recommending exactly these two things since its eleventh verse, and here they appear in the mouth of a prophet rather than in the narrator's instruction.

---

## Verse 11:89

> O my people! Do not let your opposition to me lead you to a fate similar to that of the people of Noah, or Hûd, or Ṣâliḥ. And the people of Lot are not far from you

**Four Peoples Named in One Sentence**

Shuʿayb names four destroyed communities, and the naming is a summary of the sūrah itself. The people of Nūḥ were destroyed by the flood narrated in verses 25 to 48. The people of Hūd by the wind in verses 50 to 60. The people of Ṣāliḥ by the blast in verses 61 to 68. The people of Lūṭ by the overturning in verses 77 to 83. The verse therefore functions as a table of contents for the narrative section, delivered in the mouth of a prophet rather than by the narrator. The effect is to show that the prophets knew each other's histories. Shuʿayb is not citing scripture; he is citing events, and he cites them to people who could not have read them anywhere.

**The Verse and the Danger of Resentment**

The verse makes a claim about human behaviour that is worth stating plainly. It says that opposition to a person can become a reason for action independent of the matter in dispute. The Midianites' grievance is not with the theology; it is with the man who told them their trade was corrupt. The verse identifies that grievance as the operative danger, and the identification is supported by the Qurʾān elsewhere, which warns against letting resentment of a people push one into injustice (5:2, 8). The sūrah is therefore giving a warning about a mechanism rather than about a punishment. The punishment follows the mechanism, and the mechanism is what a person can still interrupt.

---

## Verse 11:90

> So seek your Lord’s forgiveness and turn to Him in repentance. Surely my Lord is Most Merciful, All-Loving.”

**The Same Two Commands as Hūd's**

The verse repeats, word for word, the command Hūd gave his people thirty-eight verses earlier: seek forgiveness from your Lord, then turn to Him. The commentators note the identity, and the repetition is not economy. It establishes that the requirement is the same in every case, whatever the specific sin. ʿĀd were proud builders; Midian are cheating merchants; the command addressed to both is identical. The sūrah is therefore stating that repentance is not tailored to the offence. There is no special repentance for commercial fraud and another for arrogance. There is one movement — ask, then return — and it applies to whatever the case may be. The verse's placement at the end of Shuʿayb's speech gives the claim its final position in the cycle.

**"Most Merciful, All-Loving"**

The speech closes on two divine names, and the second is the distinctive one. *Raḥīm* — merciful — has appeared throughout the chapter. *Wadūd* — loving, affectionate, the one who loves and is loved — appears here for the first time in the sūrah. The commentators take the name to denote God's love for His servants and the love He inspires in them. Its use at this point is significant. The verse is not closing with a name of power or justice; it is closing with a name of affection. A people who have just been told that four nations were destroyed receive, as the final word of the prophet's address, a name that describes God's love. The choice is not sentimental. It is the ground on which the command to return rests.

---

## Verse 11:91

> They threatened, “O Shu’aib! We do not comprehend much of what you say, and surely we see you powerless among us. Were it not for your clan, we would have certainly stoned you, for you are nothing to us.”

**"You Are Nothing to Us"**

The final clause completes the assessment, and the commentators read it as *you have no power over us* or *you are of no account among us*. Either way the claim is that Shuʿayb does not register. The verse is therefore recording a total dismissal: the speech is not understood, the speaker is weak, his survival is accidental, and his standing is nil. The sūrah preserves all four claims in the people's own words, and the preservation is what makes the next verse possible. Shuʿayb's answer will address the third claim specifically, because the third is the one that reveals what the people actually fear. The verse is therefore not merely an insult. It is a confession, and the following verse will show that Shuʿayb heard it as one.

**The Verse as a Statement of Tribal Reality**

Read sociologically, the verse describes a society in which protection is collective and belief is individual. Shuʿayb's clan shields him; his clan does not follow him. The two facts together define his position precisely: safe and alone. The sūrah has described the same position for Lūṭ, whose household contained no believer but one, and for Nūḥ, whose followers were few. The recurrence is the sūrah's account of what a messenger actually occupies — a space between his people's protection and his people's rejection. The verse states the arrangement without commenting on it, and the absence of comment is significant. The sūrah does not treat the tribal protection as a mercy or as a humiliation. It records it as a fact, and the fact is what the next verse turns into an argument.

---

## Verse 11:92

> He said, “O my people! Do you have more regard for my clan than for Allah, turning your back on Him entirely? Surely my Lord is Fully Aware of what you do

**"Turning Your Back on Him"**

The phrase describes the people's relation to God, and the commentators gloss it as casting God behind their backs without fear of Him. The image is physical: a person who puts something behind him has stopped attending to it. The verse is therefore describing not disbelief in God's existence but disregard of His claims. The distinction is central to the sūrah's diagnosis throughout. The peoples it narrates do not argue that God does not exist; they act as though His requirements do not reach their conduct. The phrase captures that condition in three words, and its placement immediately after the question makes the connection explicit. The people who respect a clan more than God have not rejected Him in argument. They have turned away from Him in practice.

**"My Lord Encompasses What You Do"**

The closing statement shifts from the question to a declaration, and the declaration concerns knowledge. The commentators note the same formula elsewhere in the sūrah: God is aware of what people do, and the awareness is stated at the point where people have just acted as though it were absent. The verse's placement of the statement is significant. It follows a question the people cannot answer, and it supplies the reason the question matters. A hierarchy that puts a clan above God is not a private arrangement; it is being recorded. The sūrah has made this point throughout, and here it comes at the moment when the people have stated their hierarchy aloud. The verse therefore tells them that the statement has been heard by the One it concerns.

---

## Verse 11:93

> O my people! Persist in your ways, for I ˹too˺ will persist in mine. You will soon come to know who will be visited by a humiliating torment and is a liar! And watch! I too am watching with you!”

**A Command to Continue**

The verse opens with an instruction that appears, on its face, to be the opposite of a command. Shuʿayb tells his people to act according to their position — to keep doing what they are doing. The commentators note that the same words are taught to this Prophet in other places (6:135; 39:39) and that they serve as both warning and threat. The form is not permission. It is the language of a person who has finished arguing and is stating that both sides will now continue on their current courses. The verse therefore marks the end of the dialogue. Nothing further is offered, nothing further is asked. The sūrah has shown this moment in every narrative, and here it is stated in the most explicit terms available.

**"Who Will Be Visited by a Humiliating Torment"**

The second clause states the two questions the outcome will settle, and the first concerns humiliation. The word describes a punishment that disgraces as well as harms. The sūrah has used the same vocabulary in its thirty-ninth verse, where the punishment that disgraces was said to be coming, and the earlier use establishes that this is not a new threat but the sūrah's standing one.

**"And Who Is a Liar"**

The second question is the more personal of the two. The people have called Shuʿayb a man of forbearance and sound judgement in mockery, and the commentators note that their earlier taunts included calling him bewitched and a liar (26:185–186). The verse takes up the charge without repeating it and states that the outcome will determine which side lied. The formulation is economical. Shuʿayb does not defend himself or produce further evidence. He refers the matter to an event both sides expect. The sūrah's method here is consistent with its treatment of the whole cycle: the truth of a messenger's claim is not established by argument alone but by the arrival of what he said would arrive.

**"Watch — I Too Am Watching"**

The final instruction is shared, and the sharing is the verse's most striking feature. Shuʿayb does not tell his people to watch while he waits. He says watch, and adds that he is watching with them. The commentators note that the same instruction is given to this Prophet in several places (9:52; 10:20, 102; 32:30). The phrase places both parties in the same posture, looking at the same expected event, and the placement removes any suggestion that the messenger holds a private advantage. The sūrah has used the same device with Ṣāliḥ, who told his people to enjoy themselves for three days. Here the waiting is indefinite and mutual, and the mutuality is what makes the verse a challenge rather than a prediction.

---

## Verse 11:94

> When Our command came, We saved Shu’aib and those who believed with him by a mercy from Us. And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes

**"When Our Command Came"**

The verse opens with the sūrah's fixed formula, and this is its fifth and final use in the narrative cycle. The command came to Nūḥ's people through the oven, to ʿĀd through the wind, to Thamūd through the blast, to Lūṭ's towns through the overturning, and now to Midian. The repetition is the sūrah's structural signature: whatever the punishment's form, its timing is described identically. The commentators note that the wording here is identical to the wording used for Thamūd, and the identity is deliberate. A reader who has followed the cycle hears the formula and knows that the dialogue has ended and the decree has arrived. The verse needs no transition because the formula is the transition.

**The Verse and the Sūrah's Fifth Destruction**

This is the fifth destruction the sūrah has narrated, and the verse's use of the standing formula marks it as such. The reader who has followed the cycle has now seen the formula five times and can predict what follows it. The predictability is the sūrah's achievement rather than its weakness. By the fifth instance the narrative has become a demonstration: a pattern is being shown to recur, and each recurrence strengthens the inference that the pattern is not accidental. The verse therefore does less narrative work than the first destruction did and more argumentative work. It is evidence in a case the sūrah has been building since verse 25.

---

## Verse 11:95

> as if they had never lived there. So away with Midian as it was with Thamûd

**"Away With Midian, As It Was With Thamūd"**

The refrain returns for the third and final time, and this instance differs from the previous two. ʿĀd in verse 60 and Thamūd in verse 68 were each dismissed with the same formula. Midian is dismissed with the formula plus a comparison. The sūrah has not earlier linked two destroyed peoples explicitly; here it does, and the commentators give the reason: their fates were so similar. The comparison is the sūrah's own conclusion, delivered in the narrator's voice for the first time in the cycle. A reader who has noticed the identical wording in the previous verse is being told that the noticing was correct, and the telling is the sūrah's only direct comment on its narrative method.

**The Last Use of the Refrain**

The refrain's final appearance is worth marking. It began with ʿĀd in verse 60, repeated for Thamūd in verse 68, and closes here with Midian. Three peoples, one formula, one conclusion. Across the cycle it has done one job: to state that distance from God's mercy is the substance of the punishment. The verse's addition of the comparison gives the refrain its cumulative force. The sūrah does not explain the formula or defend it. It repeats it until the repetition is the argument, and then stops. The stopping is significant: after this verse the narratives end, and the sūrah turns to what the narratives were for.

---

## Verse 11:96

> Indeed, We sent Moses with Our signs and compelling proof

**"Our Signs"**

The plural is significant. Mūsā is described as coming with signs rather than with a sign, and the commentators identify them as the miracles he performed, referred to elsewhere as clear proofs (7:105; 29:39). The sūrah has used the same phrase for the other messengers — Hūd and Ṣāliḥ both spoke of a clear proof from their Lord — and its application here in the plural marks the scale of Mūsā's case. The verse does not enumerate the signs or describe any of them, and the omission is deliberate. A reader familiar with the accounts elsewhere in the Qurʾān supplies the list; a reader who is not still receives the essential fact, which is that the messenger came equipped. The sūrah's economy here is a measure of how familiar the Mūsā narrative already was to its audience.

**Why the Mūsā Account Is Short**

The sūrah devotes four verses to Mūsā where it gave eleven to Shuʿayb and twenty-four to Nūḥ. The compression is not a judgement on Mūsā's importance — he is the most frequently narrated prophet in the Qurʾān. It is a decision about what this sūrah needs from him. The cycle has established its pattern five times, and a sixth full account would repeat what has already been showed. What the Mūsā account adds is different: a case in which the destroyed party had a ruler, and in which the followers' allegiance to that ruler is named as the operative fact. The verse therefore introduces the account at exactly the length its function requires, and the function is stated in the following three verses.

---

## Verse 11:97

> to Pharaoh and his chiefs, but they followed the command of Pharaoh, and Pharaoh’s command was not well guided

**Two Audiences Named**

The verse identifies the recipients of the mission, and the identification is precise. Mūsā was sent to Pharaoh and his chiefs — not to Egypt, not to the people generally, but to a ruler and the group around him. The sūrah has used the same description elsewhere for the elite who answer a prophet's call with mockery, and the recurrence is meaningful. In every previous narrative the decisive refusal came from the notables, and here the sūrah names them from the outset. The verse is therefore locating responsibility where the sūrah has consistently located it: not in a population but in the few who decide for it. The choice of audience determines everything that follows, and the verse states the choice before stating the outcome.

**Why the Verse Blames the Followers**

The verse's grammar assigns the action to the followers and the deficiency to the command, and the assignment is the point. Pharaoh's command is described, not condemned at length; the following is stated as an act the people performed. The sūrah has made the same distribution throughout its narratives, where the destroyed are always described as having done something rather than as having been acted upon. It follows that consistent with the chapter's larger claim, stated explicitly five verses later, that God did not wrong them but they wronged themselves. The claim is prepared here in a single clause: the people followed, and what they followed was unsound. Both halves of the sentence are theirs.

---

## Verse 11:98

> He will be before his people on the Day of Judgment and will lead them into the Fire. What an evil place to be led into

**The Verse and Every Day of Gathering**

The verse locates the scene on the Day of Judgement, and the location matters. The previous narratives ended with destruction in this world — a flood, a wind, a blast, an overturning. This one ends with a scene in the next. The sūrah is therefore adding a dimension the earlier accounts did not supply, and the addition prepares the section that follows, which describes the Day at length. The verse is the hinge between the two: it takes a story about a destroyed ruler and shows that his destruction was not the end of his case. A reader who has followed five narratives ending in this-world punishment is told that a sixth has a further stage, and the further stage is what verses 103 to 108 will describe.

**What the Verse Adds to the Cycle**

The Mūsā account contributes one thing the other five did not: a named leader whose followers are held responsible for following him. ʿĀd's notables are described collectively; Thamūd's elite are a group; Midian's chiefs are not distinguished. Pharaoh is an individual, and the verse makes him a continuing agent after death. The addition gives the cycle its sharpest case against the excuse of obedience. A person who says he did what his leader told him is told that the leader is still in front, still leading, and that the destination is now visible to both. The verse therefore closes the narrative section with the strongest statement of individual responsibility the sūrah makes.

---

## Verse 11:99

> They were followed by a curse in this ˹life˺ and ˹will receive another˺ on the Day of Judgment. What an evil gift to receive

**The Last Destruction Narrated**

This is the final verse of the sūrah's narrative section, and its position gives it a summarising function. Six accounts have been told, and the last one differs from the others in two ways: it concerns a ruler and his chiefs rather than a town, and it extends past this world. The verse's two instalments gather both differences. The curse in this life matches the pattern of the previous five; the curse on the Day exceeds it. The sūrah is therefore closing its narrative section by opening a new horizon, and the opening is what the following verses will fill. A reader arrives at verse 100 having been told that the accounts are not finished, and the following section explains what remains.

**Why the Mūsā Account Ends Here**

The account could have continued — the Qurʾān narrates the confrontation, the magicians, the plagues and the crossing at length elsewhere. The sūrah stops after four verses, and the stopping is a decision about function. What the account was needed for has been delivered: a leader who led, followers who followed, and a punishment in two instalments. The sūrah does not need the rest, and including it would have delayed the transition to the concluding section. The verse therefore marks the point where narration gives way to instruction, and the following verses make the shift explicit by gathering the accounts and describing them as news. The Mūsā account is the last thing the reader is shown before the sūrah turns to address him directly.

---

## Verse 11:100

> These are accounts, We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺, of the ˹destroyed˺ cities. Some are still standing ˹barren˺, while others have been mowed down

**Why the Verse Addresses the Prophet**

The sūrah could have summarised its narratives in the narrator's voice without naming a recipient. It names one, and the naming does two things. First, it identifies the accounts as revelation — news brought to a man who had not read them anywhere. Second, it locates the accounts' first function in the Prophet's own situation rather than in the audience's instruction. The sūrah's fortieth-ninth verse made the same point about the unseen accounts, and the recurrence establishes a principle: the stories are given to sustain a messenger before they are given to warn a people. The verse therefore corrects a reading that treats the narratives as mere illustration. They are sustenance first, and instruction follows.

**"Some Are Still Standing"**

The first category describes towns whose structures remain. The commentators note that the phrase denotes places still visible but emptied of inhabitants — walls standing, fields untended, homes without residents. The description is archaeological in a precise sense: it describes what a traveller on the Arabian trade routes would actually have seen. The sūrah's willingness to make a claim about visible remains is significant. It is not describing a hidden or unverifiable past; it is pointing at ruins its audience could visit. The verse therefore grounds the narratives in observable fact, and the grounding is what makes the following verses' warning available. A people whose destroyed towns are still standing cannot treat the accounts as legend.

---

## Verse 11:101

> We did not wrong them, rather they wronged themselves. The gods they invoked beside Allah were of no help at all when the command of your Lord came, and only contributed to their ruin

**"Their Gods Were of No Help at All"**

The second half of the verse turns to the objects of worship, and the statement is absolute. The commentators note that these gods could not benefit their worshippers in this world, and that the case would be stronger still in the Hereafter. The verse's wording is emphatic: not a little help, no help at all. The sūrah is making a claim about the function of false gods at the moment they are most needed. A god who cannot help when the command arrives has not been shown to be weak; he has been shown not to be a god. The verse therefore uses the destructions as a test of divinity, and the test's result is stated in four words. The sūrah does not argue the point philosophically. It points at the record.

**The Verse and the Sūrah's Doctrine of Responsibility**

The verse states the chapter's central claim about human agency in its plainest form. The sūrah has asserted it repeatedly — that God gives to whomever He wills, that no soul is charged beyond its capacity, that a person's outcome follows his conduct. Here the claim is compressed into two clauses and applied to six nations at once. The compression is significant because it removes any room for the excuse the previous verses discussed: that people followed a leader, or a custom, or a god. The verse assigns the wrong to the peoples themselves, collectively, and the assignment is what the following verse will generalise into a statement about how God deals with societies.

---

The verse's two halves settle responsibility. The first states what the sūrah has been arguing throughout: the destroyed peoples were not wronged, since the warning reached them and the choice was theirs (11:101), the same conclusion the sūrah of the Cattle draws from history (6:131) and the sūrah of the Bee repeats for the towns that were destroyed (16:118). The second states the failure of the false objects of worship at the decisive moment: the gods they called on were of no use when the command of God came. The Book makes the same point in the sūrah of the Cave, where those invoked besides God will deny the worship and be unable to help (18:52), and in the sūrah of the Troops, where the trusteeship of the false deities is acknowledged to belong to God alone (39:44).

## Verse 11:102

> Such is the ˹crushing˺ grip of your Lord when He seizes the societies entrenched in wrongdoing. Indeed, His grip is ˹terribly˺ painful and severe

**"When He Seizes the Societies"**

The word for societies is significant. The verse does not say towns, peoples or nations; it uses a term denoting a settled community in its wrongful state. The commentators note the condition attached — God seizes them while they are doing wrong — and the condition is the verse's most important qualification. The grip is not arbitrary and not indiscriminate. It falls on a community in a specific condition, and the condition is stated. The sūrah elsewhere makes the same qualification explicit, when it states that God would not destroy towns unjustly while their people were reforming. The verse therefore describes a rule with a stated trigger, and the trigger is what makes the rule something a person can act on.

**The Verse as the Narratives' Conclusion**

Structurally this verse closes the sūrah's longest section, and it closes it with a rule rather than with a story. The choice is significant. A narrative section might end with its most dramatic account or with a moral. The sūrah ends with a statement about how God deals with societies, which is the thing the narratives were demonstrating. The verse therefore functions as the section's thesis, and its position immediately after the gathering verse makes the structure clear: the accounts are named, the responsibility is assigned, the rule is stated. Three verses do the work that sixty verses of narrative prepared, and the compression is possible only because the preparation was thorough.

---

## Verse 11:103

> Surely in this is a sign for those who fear the torment of the Hereafter. That is a Day for which humanity will be gathered and a Day ˹that will be˺ witnessed ˹by all˺

**Why Fear Is the Condition**

The verse names fear rather than knowledge, intelligence or scholarship, and the choice is instructive. Knowledge of the accounts does not produce the sign; the sūrah's original audience knew them. What produces the sign is the recognition that the accounts might apply to the reader. That recognition is a form of fear, and it is what the verse requires. The sūrah has described the opposite condition repeatedly: a people who hear the warning and treat it as a story about someone else. The verse identifies the alternative and names it precisely. A reader is not asked to believe more or to know more. He is asked to be afraid in a specific way — afraid that the pattern includes him.

**"A Day for Which Humanity Will Be Gathered"**

The verse's second statement shifts from the narratives to the Day, and the shift is the sūrah's turn toward its final section. The commentators connect the gathering to the Day of Judgement, where all humanity is assembled to be judged, and they note the same description elsewhere in the Qurʾān (42:7; 64:9). The verse's wording is comprehensive: humanity, not a nation or a people. The sūrah has spent eighty verses on particular communities and their particular ends; here it states that all of them, and everyone else, will be gathered. The shift in scale is significant. The destructions were local and historical; the gathering is universal and future. The verse therefore extends the narratives' reach beyond history.

---

The verse gives the city's destruction the status of a sign, and it attaches the sign to fear rather than to information: what benefits from history is the reader who expects the same account for himself. The Day it describes is the day on which humanity is gathered and witnessed — the same characterization the sūrah of al-Shūrā gives when it says the day is not far off (42:7) — and the closes with the warning that God will give no respite. The Book states the same purpose for the stories in the sūrah of Joseph, where the accounts are called lessons for people of understanding (12:111), which is the standard this verse applies to the ruins of the destroyed towns.

## Verse 11:104

> We only delay it for a fixed term

**The Shortest Verse in the Chapter**

The verse is four words in the translation and shorter in the original, and its brevity is deliberate. It follows a verse describing the gathering of all humanity and precedes a verse describing the Day's silence and the division into two groups. Between those two descriptions the sūrah inserts a single statement about timing. The placement gives the verse a function its length would not suggest: it answers the question the previous verse raises. A reader told that a Day is coming asks when, and the verse answers that the delay is bounded. The sūrah does not elaborate, does not give a duration, and does not explain the reason for the delay. It states that the delay has a limit and stops.

**The Fixed Term as a Theological Claim**

The phrase makes a claim about divine action that the sūrah has asserted elsewhere. God's decisions have terms; the terms are set; the setting is not arbitrary. The sūrah has described the same structure in the narratives, where each destruction came at a specified time — the oven, the three days, the morning. The verse extends the structure to the Day itself. A reader who has followed the accounts knows that the terms were kept in every case, and the knowledge applies here. The verse is therefore not offering a reassurance about timing; it is offering a guarantee about reliability. The term is fixed, and the sūrah's record of fixed terms being met is the evidence that this one will be met.

---

## Verse 11:105

> When that Day arrives, no one will dare speak except with His permission. Some of them will be miserable, others joyful

**The Day's First Feature: Silence**

The verse describes the Day's arrival, and the first thing it records is the absence of speech. The sūrah has just spent eighty verses on dialogue — messengers arguing, notables mocking, prophets pleading — and the Day it now describes begins with none of it. The silence is not incidental. It is the first feature named, and the naming makes a claim about the Day's character. Every argument the sūrah has narrated was conducted in speech; the Day on which those arguments are settled will not be. The verse therefore establishes a contrast that gives the narratives their weight. What could be said then cannot be said then, and the reader is being told which of the two moments he is currently in.

**"Except With His Permission"**

The qualification is the verse's central statement, and it establishes whose the Day is. Speech is not prohibited absolutely; it is permitted selectively. The commentators connect the claim to two other passages: that on the Day the Spirit and the angels will stand in rows and none will speak except one whom the Most Merciful permits and who speaks what is right (78:38). That voices will be humbled so that nothing is heard but a murmur (20:108). The verse's wording is the most general of the three, and the generality is what makes it a statement about authority rather than about noise. The Day belongs to God in the sense that nothing is said in it without His leave.

---

## Verse 11:106

> As for those bound for misery, they will be in the Fire, where they will be sighing and gasping

**"In the Fire"**

The verse states the location without elaboration, and the economy is significant. The sūrah has described the punishments of this world in concrete detail — a flood, a wind, a blast, an overturning with stones. Here it names the abode in two words. The commentators note that the description that follows concerns the state of its inhabitants rather than the architecture of the place. The choice is deliberate. The sūrah is not attempting to describe the Fire; it is describing what it is like to be in it. The verse therefore shifts from the physical to the experiential, and the shift is what distinguishes this section from the narratives. What happened in this world was narrated as event; what happens here is narrated as condition.

**What the Description Does Not Say**

The verse does not say why the group is there. The narratives have supplied the reasons — refusal of the messengers, allegiance to custom, cheating in trade, following an unguided command — and the verse does not repeat them. The omission is deliberate and it makes a claim. The assignment has been made and the reasons are behind it; the Day described in the previous verse is not a place where cases are reopened. The sūrah has already established that no one speaks except by permission, and the silence covers the explanation as much as the defence. The verse therefore describes an outcome whose grounds have been given elsewhere, and the reader is expected to supply them from the eighty verses he has just read.

## Verse 11:107

> staying there forever, as long as the heavens and the earth will endure, except what your Lord wills. Surely your Lord does what He intends

**"As Long as the Heavens and the Earth Endure"**

The phrase measures the duration, and the commentators note a reading that takes it beyond ordinary time. Al-Bayḍāwī argues that the period described is neither serial time nor time as it exists in the physical realm. Refers to the heavens and earth as the basic setting of the afterlife, citing the statement that on that Day the earth will be changed into other than the earth, and the heavens too (14:48). The reading matters because it takes the phrase out of the category of a long period and places it in the category of a permanent condition. The sūrah's wording supports the reading: the measure is not a number of years but the endurance of the frame in which the state exists.

**The Verse and the Debate About the Fire's End**

The commentators record that Ibn Taymiyyah read the wording of this verse as indicating that the Fire itself will one day cease to exist, though not the Garden, and that the same position has been held by other Islamic authorities, particularly among the Sufis. The position rests on the exception clause and on the asymmetry between this verse and the next, where the Garden's giving is described as unfailing. The sūrah does not state the position, and the report should be held as a reading rather than as a claim of the text. What the verse supports directly is that the duration is expressed with a qualification here and without a corresponding qualification of the gift in the following verse. The difference is real; the inference drawn from it is a matter of interpretation.

## Verse 11:108

> And as for those destined to joy, they will be in Paradise, staying there forever, as long as the heavens and the earth will endure, except what your Lord wills—a ˹generous˺ giving, without end

**"A Giving Without End"**

The closing phrase is the verse's distinctive addition, and the commentators take it to mean that the gift will not be cut off. The word translated as giving denotes a bestowal, and the phrase describes it as unfailing — not interrupted, not withdrawn, not conditional. The sūrah's use of the vocabulary of gift is consistent with its use two verses earlier, where the curse was ironically called a gift. Here the word is used properly, and the proper use completes the irony. What was called a gift in the case of the miserable was an earned consequence; what is called a gift here is given. The verse therefore makes a claim about the character of Paradise that the narratives have prepared: it is not a wage calculated from deeds but a bestowal.

**Why the Gift Is Described as Unfailing**

The phrase answers a question the exception clause raises. If the duration is qualified by what God wills, a reader may ask whether the Garden's continuation is uncertain. The closing phrase addresses the uncertainty without removing the clause. The gift is unfailing; the clause remains. The sūrah is therefore holding two claims together: the Garden's giving does not lapse, and God's will is not constrained. The commentators' reading — that the Garden will never cease — follows from the first claim, while the exception preserves the second. The verse's ability to hold both is a mark of its precision. A reader is given assurance about the gift and reminded that the assurance is not a limitation on the Giver.

## Verse 11:109

> So do not be in doubt ˹O Prophet˺ about what those ˹pagans˺ worship. They worship nothing except what their forefathers worshipped before ˹them˺. And We will certainly give them their share ˹of punishment˺ in full, without any reduction

**The Verse and the Narratives It Gathers**

Structurally this verse collects the sūrah's material and applies it. Six accounts have been narrated, and the verse identifies the audience as belonging to the same category as three of them. The identification is not made by analogy or by suggestion; it is made by describing the audience's worship in the same words used for the destroyed. The sūrah has been building toward this application throughout, and the verse is where the building completes. A reader who has followed the accounts from verse 25 arrives here already knowing what the identification means. The verse therefore does not need to argue. It states the resemblance and the outcome, and the resemblance supplies the argument.

**The Verse as the Narratives' Application**

This is the verse where the chapter's hundred verses of history become a statement about the audience, and the transition is made without announcement. The verse does not say *you are like them*. It describes the audience's worship in the words the sūrah used for the destroyed, states the ground of that worship, and declares the outcome. The reader makes the identification himself, and the making is what gives the verse its force. The sūrah has been training this response throughout — every narrative was structured so that the audience recognised itself in the refusal. The verse is the last and plainest instance, and its plainness is possible only because the training has been thorough. The verse therefore completes the chapter's case against its audience in three statements, and the completeness is what allows the chapter to turn to instruction. The verse therefore closes the case in three statements, and the closure is what allows the chapter to move from argument to instruction.

---

The verse's answer is that the worship of the pagan Arabs is the worship of what their fathers worshipped, with no claim to knowledge or guidance behind it, and the sūrah has already shown the pattern in the reply of Nūḥ's people and of ʿĀd. The Book states the doctrine in the sūrah of Joseph, where Joseph says that the gods they worship are none but names their fathers invented, and that command belongs to God alone (12:40), and it applies the same reasoning to the Meccans' inherited practice in the sūrah of the Cattle (6:148). The sūrah's closing words give the prophet his own task: his part of the matter is what he has been given, and the reckoning of the worshippers belongs to the Lord.

## Verse 11:110

> Indeed, We had given Moses the Scripture, but differences arose regarding it. Had it not been for a prior decree from your Lord, their differences would have been settled ˹at once˺. They are truly in alarming doubt about it

**A Second Reference to Mūsā**

The verse returns to Mūsā, and the return is not a repetition of the earlier account. That account concerned Pharaoh and his chiefs, their following of his command, and their punishment. This verse concerns something different: the Book given to Mūsā and what happened to it afterwards. The commentators note that the verse is repeated word for word in Sūrah 41, which marks it as a fixed statement. The sūrah is therefore making a claim that appears twice in the Qurʾān in identical words, and the identity suggests that the claim is central rather than incidental. The verse's subject is not the messenger but the reception of what he brought, and the subject shift is what allows the sūrah to make a statement about its own audience.

**"A Prior Decree From Your Lord"**

The verse's second claim explains why the disagreement was not resolved immediately, and the commentators note that the phrase is used throughout the Qurʾān for God's decree (10:19; 20:129; 41:45; 42:14). The wording establishes that something had already been decided before the disagreement arose, and that the decision concerns the timing of the settlement. The verse is therefore making a claim about divine patience rather than divine indifference. The differences would have been settled at once had it not been for a decree already in place. The sūrah has described the same structure throughout its narratives, where the punishment came at a fixed term rather than immediately. The verse applies the structure to disagreement rather than to refusal.

**The Verse and Its Twin in Sūrah 41**

The commentators note the verbal identity with 41:45, and the duplication is worth considering. A statement repeated word for word in two sūrahs is doing work that neither context alone requires. The claim — that scripture was given, that difference arose, that a decree delayed the settlement — is a general statement about revelation's reception. The sūrah of Hūd places it immediately after addressing the Makkans about their worship, and the placement makes its function clear. The audience is being told that disagreement about revelation is not a new phenomenon and not an argument against it. The verse therefore answers an objection the audience has not stated but is certainly thinking.

## Verse 11:111

> And surely your Lord will fully pay all for their deeds. He is certainly All-Aware of what they do

**Full Payment for Deeds**

The verse states a guarantee, and the guarantee concerns payment. The commentators gloss the phrase as God giving the full reward for actions, and the wording is emphatic: fully, for each, for their deeds. The verse is therefore making a claim about completeness. Nothing will be left unpaid, nothing will be overlooked, and nothing will be reduced. The sūrah has made the same claim in the previous verse, where the share was said to be given in full without reduction, and the recurrence within two verses establishes it as a settled point. The verse's function is not to introduce a new claim but to confirm one the narratives have showed and the previous verse has stated.

**Why Payment Is the Right Image**

The vocabulary of payment connects this verse to the sūrah's commercial material. The Midian narrative concerned short measure and defrauded property, and the charge was that people were not giving others what was owed. The verse applies the same vocabulary to God's dealing with deeds, and the application makes a claim by contrast. Human beings short-measure; God pays in full. The sūrah has drawn the same contrast elsewhere, where the remnant left by God was said to be better than the surplus gained by fraud. The verse therefore completes that argument: the One who requires full measure is the One who gives full payment. The image is not decorative. It ties the sūrah's economic teaching to its the doctrine of the Last Day.

## Verse 11:112

> So be steadfast as you are commanded ˹O Prophet˺, along with those who turn ˹in submission to Allah˺ with you. And do not transgress. Surely He is All-Seeing of what you ˹believers˺ do

**The Command the Chapter Was Built Toward**

This is the verse the sūrah's opening identified as the hardest in it. The report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās states that no verse was more difficult for the Prophet than the command to be steadfast as he was commanded, and the tradition is recorded in the introduction to the sūrah by al-Qurṭubī. The verse's position confirms the report: it comes after a hundred and eleven verses of narrative, warning and description, and it is the first direct command to the Prophet in the chapter's concluding section. The sūrah has been preparing this instruction since its first verse. Everything before it — the accounts, the rule, the Day, the two outcomes — is the ground on which the command stands. The verse is therefore not an urging appended to a chapter. It is the chapter's destination.

**The Verse and the Report About Its Difficulty**

The tradition that this verse was the hardest for the Prophet deserves a final consideration. The claim is striking because the verse contains no threat, no description of punishment, and no difficult doctrine. Its difficulty lies in the qualifier — as you are commanded — and in the extension to others. A person is told to hold to a measure he does not set, in company with people he does not choose, without exceeding, while being observed. The tradition records the weight of that combination, and the chapter's hundred preceding verses are what make the weight felt.

## Verse 11:113

> And do not be inclined to the wrongdoers or you will be touched by the Fire. For then you would have no protectors other than Allah, nor would you be helped

**A Prohibition About Inclination**

The verse prohibits an inclination rather than an action, and the distinction is the verse's first feature. The commentators gloss the phrase as meaning that the believers should never approve of the actions of the Makkan idolaters. The prohibition is therefore directed at a disposition, not at a deed. A person can refrain from wrongdoing and still incline toward those who do it — approving, admiring, envying, or simply preferring their company. The verse addresses that space, and the addressing is significant. The sūrah is not content with a community that abstains. It requires a community that does not lean, and the requirement is stricter than the prohibition of the act because it reaches the attitude behind the act.

**"Or the Fire Will Touch You"**

The consequence is stated in a single clause, and the verb is significant. The Fire is said to *touch* — not to envelop, not to consume. The commentators take the touching as a real contact with the punishment, and the word's mildness relative to the sūrah's other descriptions of the Fire is deliberate. A person who inclines is not described as being destroyed; he is described as being reached. The verse is therefore making a graded claim. Inclination is not identical with the wrongdoing it inclines toward, but it is not harmless either. It brings a person within range of the punishment, and the range is what the verse is warning about. The sūrah has described the same proximity in the Lūṭ narrative, where the stones were said to be not far from the wrongdoers.

---

## Verse 11:114

> Establish prayer ˹O Prophet˺ at both ends of the day and in the early part of the night. Surely good deeds wipe out evil deeds. That is a reminder for the mindful

**The Sūrah's Second Command**

The verse gives the second of the three instructions that close the chapter, and it is the only one that names a specific act. The previous verse prohibited an inclination; this one commands a practice. The sūrah's arrangement is significant: after a hundred verses of narrative and warning, the practical instruction given to the Prophet and his companions is prayer at specified times. The verse is therefore identifying what sustains a person under the requirement the previous verses imposed. Steadfastness to a fixed measure, in company, without excess, and without inclining toward the wrongdoers — the verse supplies the practice that makes this possible, and the practice is timed rather than occasional.

**"At Both Ends of the Day"**

The phrase locates the prayers, and the commentators record a range of identifications. The two ends are variously taken as dawn and sunset, dawn and late afternoon, or midday and late afternoon; the early part of the night is taken as sunset and night prayer together, or as the night prayer alone. The commentators note that a similar range of opinion exists about the middlemost prayer in 2:238. The sūrah's wording does not settle the identifications, and the unsettled range is characteristic of its treatment of practice. What the verse states is the structure — the day's two ends and the night's early hours — and the structure is what the identifications elaborate. A reader receives a pattern of coverage rather than a schedule.

**"Good Deeds Wipe Out Evil Deeds"**

The clause is the verse's theological centre, and the commentators give it two readings. The first is specific: performing the daily prayers erases minor sins, and they support the reading with the report that the five prayers, Friday to Friday, and Ramadan to Ramadan expiate what falls between them so long as the major sins are avoided (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 233). The second is general: good action offsets the effect of evil action, as in the report that a person should follow a bad deed with a good one and it will erase it (Sunan al-Tirmidhī 1987). The sūrah's wording supports both, since it speaks of good deeds generally rather than of prayer alone. The verse is therefore making a claim about the relation between acts that applies beyond the practice just commanded.

---

## Verse 11:115

> And be patient! Certainly Allah does not discount the reward of the good-doers

**The Third Command**

The verse gives the last of the chapter's three instructions, and it is the shortest. Be steadfast, do not incline, establish prayer, be patient. The final command is two words in the translation, and its brevity is not a diminishment. The sūrah has been building toward patience since its eleventh verse, where the exception was defined as those who are patient and do good. The command here is the requirement that definition assumed. The verse is therefore not adding a new demand; it is naming the one the whole chapter has assumed. A reader who has followed the accounts has seen patience operating in every prophet — Nūḥ warned for nine hundred and fifty years, Hūd challenged and dismissed, Ṣāliḥ told to watch, Lūṭ alone at a door. The verse names what all of them were doing.

**"Allah Does Not Discount the Reward"**

The second clause supplies the ground of the command, and the wording is emphatic. The commentators note that the same assurance appears elsewhere in the Qurʾān — that God does not neglect the reward of those who do good (3:171; 7:170; 9:120; 12:56, 90; 18:30). The verse's use of the word for discounting or diminishing is precise, and it connects to the sūrah's commercial vocabulary: the share given in full, the remnant left by God, the payment not reduced. The verse is therefore making the same claim in the same idiom. What a person spends in patience is not lost or reduced. The connection is what makes the command bearable: the cost is real, and the payment is exact.

**Why Patience Comes Last**

The order of the three commands is instructive. Steadfastness is the general requirement; non-inclination is its negative counterpart; prayer is its sustaining practice; patience is the disposition that holds all three over time. The verse's final position gives patience the widest scope. A person can be steadfast for a day, avoid inclination for a season, and pray at the appointed hours. Patience is what makes any of these continue. The sūrah has shown the same point in its narratives, where the messengers' distinguishing feature was not eloquence or success but duration. The verse therefore closes the chapter's instructions with the quality that determines whether the others survive contact with time.

## Verse 11:116

> If only there had been among the ˹destroyed˺ peoples before you, ˹O believers,˺ virtuous individuals who forbade corruption in the land—other than the few We had saved ˹from the torment˺. But the wrongdoers ˹only˺ pursued their ˹worldly˺ pleasures, becoming wicked

**"Other Than a Few We Saved"**

The exception is the verse's most severe clause. In each destroyed nation there were some who were saved, and the verse identifies them as few. The commentators connect the saving to the believers who accompanied each messenger — Nūḥ's handful, Hūd's group, Ṣāliḥ's companions, Lūṭ's household minus one, Shuʿayb's followers. The verse gathers them into a single figure and describes them as an exception. The effect is to establish a proportion: in every case the saved were a minority, and the majority contained almost no one willing to object. The sūrah is therefore not describing societies that were evenly divided. It is describing societies in which the objectors were so few that their presence did not alter the outcome.

**The Verse and the Chapter's Social Claim**

The verse makes the chapter's largest claim about the relation between individuals and societies, and it makes it by describing an absence. The destroyed nations lacked upright people who spoke, and the lacking is what the verse identifies as decisive. The claim is not that societies are punished for the sins of their members. It is that societies reach the condition described in verse 102 when the people who could object do not. The verse therefore places a specific responsibility on a specific group — those with merit — and the placement is uncomfortable. A reader who counts himself among the upright is being told that his uprightness is not enough unless it is expressed, and the telling is what the verse is for.

---

## Verse 11:117

> And your Lord ˹O Prophet˺ would never destroy a society unjustly while its people were acting rightly

**The Condition Named**

The condition is that the people are acting rightly — reforming, setting things right. The commentators offer a reading that gives the condition a specific content: God would not destroy a people merely for disbelieving if their dealings and conduct were fair and honest. On this reading the destroyed nations were destroyed because they harmed not only themselves but other people and other creatures. The reading is supported by the sūrah's own accounts, where the charges against the peoples include defrauding others, abusing travellers and working corruption on the earth. The verse therefore ties the outcome to conduct toward others rather than to belief alone, and the tying is what makes the rule something a society can act on.

**Why the Condition Is Social**

The verse's condition concerns a people, not individuals. A society is not destroyed while its people are reforming, and the reforming is collective. The sūrah's previous verse identified what was missing in the destroyed nations — people who forbade corruption — and this verse states the consequence of that absence. The two verses together make a claim about the relation between individual righteousness and communal fate. A society containing upright individuals who do not act is not protected by their uprightness. The verse is therefore not offering comfort to good people living in a bad society. It is telling them that their goodness protects the society only if it is expressed, and the expression is what the previous verse found lacking.

---

## Verse 11:118

> Had your Lord so willed, He would have certainly made humanity one single community ˹of believers˺, but they will always ˹choose to˺ differ—

**A Counterfactual That Establishes a Fact**

The verse opens with a condition that was not fulfilled, and the unfulfilled condition states something about divine power. God could have made humanity a single community. The commentators note that the same claim appears elsewhere in the Qurʾān (5:48; 16:93; 42:18; 2:213). The verse is therefore establishing that the present state of division is not a failure of power. The sūrah's method here is characteristic: it states what God could have done to clarify what He did do. A reader who assumes that division exists because God could not prevent it is corrected by the counterfactual. The division is not an accident; it is the outcome of a will that chose otherwise.

**What the Claim Does Not Mean**

The verse does not say that all differences are equally valid, and it does not say that the search for truth is pointless. The sūrah has just spent a hundred verses distinguishing between people who accepted the messengers and people who destroyed them, and the distinction is not dissolved by this verse. What the verse says is that the difference will persist, not that it is indifferent. The commentators' reading supports this: some people receive mercy and follow the truth while others pursue falsehood. The verse is therefore describing an outcome rather than making a judgement, and the judgement has already been made in the narratives. A reader who takes the verse as relativism has misread its function in the chapter.

**The Verse and the Sūrah's Account of the Messengers**

The claim explains something the narratives have shown. Six messengers were sent, and in every case the majority refused. The sūrah has narrated the refusals without explaining why they were so consistent. The explanation is not a dismissal of the refusals — the sūrah has assigned responsibility to the peoples themselves. It is a statement about the frame within which the refusals occurred. The verse is therefore reconciling two claims the chapter has made: that people are responsible for their refusal, and that the pattern of refusal is not an accident. Both are true, and the verse holds them together.

## Verse 11:119

> except those shown mercy by your Lord—and so He created them ˹to choose freely˺. And so the Word of your Lord will be fulfilled: “I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and humans all together.”

**The Exception That Completes the Claim**

The verse begins by completing the sentence the previous verse left open, and the completion changes its meaning. Humanity will always differ, except those on whom God has mercy. The commentators describe this group as people God has drawn out by His generosity, holding to the foundation of the religion — that God is one, and that every messenger is to be believed along with what he brought. The exception is what keeps the previous verse's claim from becoming a description of universal confusion.

**"The Word of Your Lord Will Be Fulfilled"**

The verse's final statement shifts to a declaration, and the commentators note that the same promise appears in 32:13 and cf. 7:18; 38:85. The wording is a quotation of something already said — the Word — and the quotation establishes that the outcome was announced before it arrived. The sūrah has used the same device throughout its narratives, where a word had already gone forth from God. The verse applies the device to the final outcome, and the application makes a claim about the relation between human choice and divine declaration. The Word will be fulfilled; the previous verses have stated that people choose. The verse holds both claims without reconciling them, and the holding is the sūrah's method with matters beyond human determination.

## Verse 11:120

> And We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ the stories of the messengers to reassure your heart. And there has come to you in this ˹sûrah˺ the truth, a warning ˹to the disbelievers˺, and a reminder to the believers

**A Warning and a Reminder**

The verse's final phrase identifies two audiences and gives each a different thing. The sūrah is a warning to those who disbelieve and a reminder to the believers. The commentators connect the second function to the statement elsewhere that reminding benefits the believers (51:55). The distinction is significant because it describes two different operations. A warning informs a person of a danger he has not accepted; a reminder recalls something he already knows. The verse is therefore identifying what the chapter does to each of its audiences, and the identification explains the chapter's double register. The narratives warn; the commands remind. A reader is told which operation is being applied to him, and the telling determines how he should receive what follows.

**Why the Verse Names the Sūrah**

The phrase *in this* refers to the sūrah itself, and the self-reference is unusual. The chapter names itself as the vehicle of what has come, which makes the verse a statement about the text the reader is holding. The sūrah's willingness to refer to itself is significant because it closes the distance between the revelation and the reader. The verse is not describing a message delivered elsewhere; it is describing this one. A reader encountering the chapter is therefore told, inside the chapter, what the chapter is and what it does to him. The self-reference is the sūrah's way of making its function explicit rather than leaving it to be inferred from the material.

## Verse 11:121

> Say to those who disbelieve, “Persist in your ways; we will certainly persist in ours

**An Instruction to Speak**

The verse is a command addressed to the Prophet, and the command is to say something specific to a specific group. The sūrah's final movement has been a series of instructions — be steadfast, do not incline, establish prayer, be patient — and this verse adds one more. The addition is different in kind. The previous commands concerned the Prophet's own conduct; this one concerns what he is to say to those who reject him. The verse is therefore turning the chapter outward at its close, and the turn is significant. After a hundred and twenty verses addressed inward — to the heart, the prayer, the patience — the sūrah's last real statement is a message to the opponents.

**The Words He Is to Say**

The message is the same one Shuʿayb gave his people thirty verses earlier: act according to your position, we are acting. The commentators note that the same words are taught to this Prophet elsewhere (6:135) and that they function as both warning and threat. The verse is therefore not composing a new statement. It is giving the Prophet a formula already used by a prophet whose people were destroyed. The connection is precise and it does specific work. A reader who has followed the chapter knows what happened after Shuʿayb said these words, and the knowledge is what the verse relies on. The formula is not an argument; it is a statement that both sides will continue, made by a person who knows how the continuation ends.

## Verse 11:122

> And wait! Surely we ˹too˺ are waiting.”

**The Shortest Statement in the Chapter's Close**

The verse is two clauses, and it completes the message the previous verse began. The commentators note that these statements function as warnings and threats against the Makkan idolaters. The verse's brevity is not a diminishment; it is the point. After a hundred and twenty-one verses, the sūrah's final word to its opponents is an instruction to wait and a statement that the believers are waiting too. The economy is what gives the verse its force. A longer statement would be an argument, and the chapter has finished arguing. A statement this short is not an argument; it is a posture, and the posture is what the verse communicates.

**A Shared Waiting**

The verse's most distinctive feature is the word *too*. The opponents are told to wait, and the believers state that they are also waiting. The sūrah has used the same formula in the Shuʿayb narrative, where the prophet told his people to watch and added that he was watching with them. The shared posture removes any suggestion that one side holds an advantage. Both are waiting for the same event, and neither knows its time — the chapter has stated that the Day is delayed for a fixed term and that the term is known only to God. The verse is therefore placing both parties in an identical relation to the future, and the identity is what makes the statement a challenge rather than a threat.

## Verse 11:123

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the knowledge of what is hidden in the heavens and the earth. And to Him all matters are returned. So worship Him and put your trust in Him. And your Lord is never unaware of what you do

**The Chapter's Last Verse and Its Shape**

The verse contains four statements, and their arrangement is the sūrah's final method. First: the unseen belongs to God. Second: all matters return to Him. Third: worship Him and trust Him. Fourth: your Lord is not unaware of what you do. The sequence moves from knowledge to return to command to assurance. The sūrah has used this shape throughout — a statement about God, a statement about the outcome, a command, and a closing ground. The verse is therefore not a summary appended to the chapter. It is the chapter's characteristic structure at its most compressed, and the compression is possible because the hundred and twenty-two verses before it have supplied the material.

**"So Worship Him and Put Your Trust in Him"**

The command has two parts, and the commentators note that the call to trust is echoed throughout the Qurʾān — that God loves those who trust (3:159), and that believers are to trust in God (5:23). The pairing is significant. Worship is the outward act; trust is the inward reliance. The sūrah has commanded both separately earlier — the opening verse commanded worship, and the Shuʿayb narrative closed with a prophet saying *in Him I trust and to Him I turn*. The final verse combines them in a single instruction, and the combination is the chapter's requirement in its shortest form. A reader is given the whole demand in six words, and the six words are the same demand the chapter has been stating since its beginning.
