# Sūrah al-Qiyāmah (Chapter 75) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Self-Accusing Soul and the Radiant Faces of the Vision**

Al-Qiyāmah, "The Resurrection," is the seventy-fifth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 40 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with two colossal, deeply intimate divine oaths: *“I swear by the Day of Resurrection (*Lā uqsimu bi-yawmi al-qiyāmah*), and I swear by the self-reproaching / self-accusing soul (*wa-lā uqsimu bi-l-nafsi al-lawwāmah*)!”* (75:1–2). In moral psychology and Islamic spirituality, Sūrah al-Qiyāmah is universally celebrated for conjoining the macrocosmic reckoning cosmic (*al-Qiyāmah*) with the microcosmic reckoning of the human conscience (*al-Nafs al-Lawwāmah*). Characterized by rapid, dramatic staccato verses that beat like a racing pulse, the sūrah captures the terror of the physical disintegration of death, the reconstruction of the human fingertips, the prohibition of rushing the tongue during revelation, and the supreme, transcendent reward of Paradise: gazing directly upon the Unveiled Face of God (*ilā Rabbihā nāẓirah*, 75:23 — *“looking at their Lord”*).

**The Structural Modulation and the Tangled Legs**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Qiyāmah moves across three dramatic, intensely focused movements:

1. The Awakening of the Conscience and the Reconstruction of Man (1–15): Swears by the Day of Resurrection and the self-accusing conscience (*al-nafs al-lawwāmah*). It asserts that man knows his own moral bankruptcy even as he puts forward excuses (*“Nay, man against himself is a seeing witness, even though he presents his excuses,”* 75:14–15). It describes the cosmic collapse: the vision dazed, the moon eclipsed, and the sun and moon joined together in celestial collision, while man cries on that Day: *“Where is the escape? (*Ayna al-mafarr?*)”* The answer thunders: *“No! There is no refuge! To your Lord, that Day, is the [place of] permanence.”*

2. The Preservation of Scripture and the Two Faces of Eternity (16–25): The divine guarantee of the Qur'an's textual preservation and clarity (75:16–19 — *“Do not rush your tongue trying to memorize ˹a revelation of˺ the Quran It is certainly upon Us to ˹make you˺ memorize and recite it So once We have recited a revelation ˹through Gabriel˺, follow its recitation ˹closely˺ Then it is surely upon Us to make it clear ˹to you˺”*). It rebukes humanity for loving the fleeting, immediate world (*al-ʿājilah*) and abandoning the Hereafter (*al-ākhirah*). It presents the immortal, contrasting spectacle of the two faces: - *The Radiant Faces* (22–23): *“Faces, that Day, will be radiant (*nāḍirah*), looking at their Lord (*ilā Rabbihā nāẓirah*)!”* In authentic hadith (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7434), the Prophet ﷺ confirmed that believers will see their Lord on the Day of Resurrection as clearly as they see the full moon in an unclouded night sky. - *The Gloomy Faces* (24–25): *“And faces, that Day, will be contorted in despair (*bāsirah*), anticipating that there will be done to them a back-breaking calamity (*fāqirah*)!”*

3. The Climax of Death and the Clattering of Shins (26–40): Delivers one of the most chilling, intimate descriptions of human mortality in world literature: the dying soul reaching the collarbone (*al-tarāqī*), the helpless family crying: *“Who can cure him?”*, the dying man realizing that this is the final departure. His legs tangling with one another in the agony of death (*wa-l-taffati al-sāqu bi-l-sāq*)—*“To your Lord, that Day, will be the drive!”* It indicts the arrogant denier who neither believed nor prayed, but denied and turned away, then strutted back to his family in self-admiration (*yatamattā*), greeted by the fourfold death knell: *“Woe to you, and woe! Then woe to you, and woe!”* (75:34–35). The sūrah concludes by challenging human pride with its humble origin: *“Does man think that he will be left purposeless (*sudā*)? Was he not a drop of emitted sperm? Then a clinging clot, and He created and proportioned, and made of him two mates, male and female? Is not that [Creator] able to give life to the dead?”*

## Verse 75:1

> I do swear by the Day of Judgment

**The Oath Falls on the Day Itself**

The sūrah opens with an oath sworn by the resurrection, and it does so before a single line of evidence for the resurrection has been given. God swears by the Day of Judgment; the forty verses that follow unpack what that oath has already declared. Elsewhere the Qur'an swears by created things that a hearer can look at — *“By the sun and its brightness”* (91:1), *“By the night when it covers”* (92:1), *“By the fig and the olive ˹of Jerusalem˺”* (95:1). Those oaths ask the listener to reason from something present to something absent. Here the thing sworn by is absent as well, and the boldness of the verse lies exactly there: a future event is treated as though it were already an object, as settled as the sun overhead. The Makkan audience denied that any such Day was coming. The reply is not an argument built out of premises they would grant; it is an oath resting on a fact they have not conceded, which leaves them either to accept it or to call the Speaker a liar.

**What the Word Qiyāmah Carries**

*Qiyāmah* comes from the root *q-w-m*, to stand. The Day is named for standing: the dead stand up out of the earth, and then every creature stands before its Lord to answer. Both motions are held in the single noun, rising and accounting, and the sūrah will use each of them. Naming it “the standing” rather than “the day” or “the event” builds a claim into the name itself — this is an assembly, a summons, a reckoning conducted face to face, not merely a stretch of time. The Qur'an elsewhere names the same reality by what it does to those who meet it: *“The Inevitable Hour! What is the Inevitable Hour?”* (69:1–2). The one name describes the posture of those who are raised; the other describes the impossibility of escape. Together they rule out the reading the deniers preferred, that death is a door which opens onto nothing.

**An Oath as Argument, Not Ornament**

Short Makkan sūrahs lean on oaths because their hearers understood the form and what it cost. An oath stakes the speaker's credibility on the statement that follows, and in the Qur'an the thing sworn by is never neutral: it is chosen because its evidential weight is already conceded. What is unusual here is the inversion. No visible sign is offered as the ladder; the disputed Day is itself the rung. Against a position held on the strength of custom alone — *“There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time”* (45:24) — an oath speaks as the testimony of a witness.

**A Day Sworn To, and a Day Mocked**

The certainty asserted here is the certainty the Makkan audience ridiculed, and the Qur'an records both sides of that exchange without softening either. Against the oath stands the mockers' question, repeated across the Makkan and early Madinan sūrahs in nearly identical words. Against their laughter stands the flat declaration: *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves.”* (22:7), and the oath-form is used the same way elsewhere, *“Yes—by my Lord, the Knower of the unseen—it will certainly come to you!”* (34:3). The sūrah therefore begins where a preacher would normally end. Nothing is left to be proved at the level of whether; everything that follows concerns what the Day will do when it arrives, and who will be found standing.

## Verse 75:2

> And I do swear by the self-reproaching soul

**Three Souls, in Ascending Order**

The Qur'an names three conditions of the soul, and this verse supplies the middle one. The lowest is the soul that presses a person toward wrongdoing, named in the confession of the Egyptian minister's wife: *“And I do not seek to free myself from blame, for indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil, except those shown mercy by my Lord.”* (12:53). The highest is the soul addressed at the end of the Fajr sūrah: *“O tranquil soul! Return to your Lord, well pleased ˹with Him˺ and well pleasing ˹to Him˺.”* (89:27–28). Between them sits *al-nafs al-lawwāmah*, the blaming soul, which has not arrived at peace but has stopped being at ease with itself. The ordering matters: the middle term is not a stage one passes through and leaves behind so much as a condition of the struggle, the state of a person still in the fight. To swear by it is to swear by the unfinished, arguing, self-accusing human being rather than by the saint.

**What Makes the Blaming Soul Worth Swearing By**

The word *lawwāmah* is an intensive form: one who blames repeatedly, who returns to the charge. The Qur'an locates this inward witness very precisely: *“Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ created humankind and ˹fully˺ know what their souls whisper to them, and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein.”* (50:16). It is the internal counterpart of the accounting that is coming, which is why one report has every soul, pious or profligate, reproaching itself on that Day — the good wondering why it did not do more, the wicked wishing it had stopped. On the Day the sūrah's first verse swears by, the prosecution that began inside a person simply continues in the open. 1, a minority of commentators read it as real negation: “I do *not* swear by the blaming soul.” Their ground is that an oath is normally sworn by something great — God, or a sign of His — and a human soul, however scrupulous, is not in that class.

**The Soul Sworn By in Another Sūrah**

The same oath-object appears in a sūrah that builds its whole argument on it: *“And by the soul and ˹the One˹ Who fashioned it, then with ˹the knowledge of˺ right and wrong inspired it!”* (91:7–8). There the soul is sworn by precisely as a thing made and then informed, which is what gives its self-reproach its authority: the standard it appeals to was put there by the One who fashioned it. A person who blames himself is not inventing the yardstick he is measured against. That is why the blaming soul can be joined, in a single oath, to the Day of Resurrection, and why the sūrah will later say that on that Day a man becomes a witness against himself despite the excuses he offers.

## Verse 75:3
> Do people think We cannot reassemble their bones

**Why Bones**

Bones are the denier's own chosen example, and the Qur'an quotes him choosing it more than once. In Yā Sīn the objector argues with his Creator *“forgetting they were created—saying, ‘Who will give life to decayed bones?’”* (36:78). The same challenge recurs in almost identical phrasing across the Makkan sūrahs: *“When we are reduced to bones and ashes, will we really be raised as a new creation?”* (17:49).

*“When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?”* (37:16); *“When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?”* (56:47); *“even after we have been reduced to decayed bones?”* (79:11). The choice is deliberate on the objector's part. Bone is the part of a body that outlasts everything else, the last thing to go, the remnant that shepherds find in an old graveyard. If even that cannot be reassembled, he reasons, nothing can. The argument is designed to look empirical, and its persuasiveness came from the sight of dry skeletons scattered on a hillside.

**The Grammar of the Denial**

What is denied is not God's knowledge or His will but His power, and the verb makes that explicit: *jamʿ*, gathering what has been scattered. The objector's picture is of parts dispersed into soil, eaten by animals, carried on the wind, and the claim is that no one can collect them again. The Qur'an's counter is not to dispute the dispersal but to re-describe the collection. *“We certainly know what the earth consumes of them ˹after their death˺, and with us is a well-preserved Record.”* (50:4).

**An Objection About Matter, Answered About the Maker**

The deeper flaw in the challenge is that it treats resurrection as a physical process that must be extrapolated from other physical processes, when the claim being made is about the Agent who set those processes running. The Qur'an consistently refuses to argue on the objector's ground. Where he demands a mechanism, it points at the first creation: *“They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time. He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being.”* (36:79), and *“And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him.”* (30:27). The audacity of that reply is worth stating plainly: making a man from nothing in the first place is not presented as easier than remaking him from his own remains, but the second is presented as the smaller task by comparison. *“Have they not seen how Allah originates the creation then resurrects it? That is certainly easy for Allah.”* (29:19). The denier's difficulty was never with the bones; it was with the premise that the world has a Maker who addresses it.

**The Scale of the Claim**

One further consideration closes the gap the objector thought he had found. He imagined the task as large because he counted the bodies. The Qur'an counts differently: *“The creation and resurrection of you ˹all˺ is as simple ˹for Him˺ as that of a single soul.”* (31:28). Multiplicity is what makes collection hard for creatures who must pick things up one at a time; it is not what makes anything hard for the One who sustains every atom at once. The verse so sets up the answer that follows, which will not argue in general but will descend to the smallest particular the objector's own example contained.

## Verse 75:4

> Yes ˹indeed˺! We are ˹most˺ capable of restoring ˹even˺ their very fingertips

**Bānān: the Word and What It Carries**

*Bānān* in Arabic means the fingertips, and by extension the fingers, and lexical authorities also record its use for the extremities generally. The commentators took it here in its fine sense, and the grammar supports them: the object of the sentence is not only the existence of fingers but their *taswiyah*, their being made proportionate and shaped. The verb is the one the Qur'an uses elsewhere for God's act of forming and levelling a creation into its proper measure. So the verse is not saying that God can reattach parts. It is saying that He can reproduce the design — the ridges, the proportions, the exact configuration that makes a hand the hand of one man rather than of another. Religious precision and biological precision meet in that single word, and the claim is more demanding than the objector's, not less.

**Restoring the Form, Not Only the Matter**

The argument also clarifies what resurrection is being claimed. That is why the Qur'an's creation passages insist on the shaping stages and not merely on the raw material: *“then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation.”* (23:14).

**The Maker of the Extremities Elsewhere**

The sūrah is consistent with the rest of the Qur'an about where the evidence lies. Divine power is showed not by the vastness of the heavens alone but by the finish of small things: *“And He created the pairs—males and females—from a sperm-drop when it is emitted.”* (53:45–46); *“˹They were˺ created from a spurting fluid, stemming from between the backbone and the ribcage.”* (86:6–7). A drop too small to see becomes a creature with hearing, sight, and a mind, and the stages are described in detail precisely so that the reader will not treat the minute as beneath the notice of God. The fingertip argument belongs to that same body of reasoning. Whoever formed a human being out of a fluid emitted in the dark has not lost the pattern when the bones are scattered. The argument does not depend on any particular account of what happens to matter after death. The Qur'an never asks its hearer to believe that the particles of a body stay somewhere retrievable, and it never needs to. What it asks is whether the One who originated the form out of no form at all can originate it a second time out of what remains, or out of nothing again.

## Verse 75:5

> Still people want to deny what is yet to come

**The Reversal at the Edge of Death**

What the deferrer cannot imagine is how the same request will sound in his own mouth at the end. The Qur'an records the plea several times, always in the same grammar of belatedness: *“When death approaches any of them, they cry, ‘My Lord! Let me go back, so I may do good in what I left behind.’”* (23:99–100). The answer is a barrier, not a negotiation. Others ask for the smallest of extensions: *“My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while, I would give in charity and be one of the righteous.”* (63:10), and the reply is flat — *“But Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes.”* (63:11). The cruelty is not in the refusal. It is that the plea is identical to the one the same person declined a hundred times while it was still possible to act on it.

**What the Word Means: the Root f-j-r**

The verb *yafjuru* carries a family of senses that all descend from one image: breaking through. Dawn is *fajr* because it splits the dark. *Fujūr* is transgression because it breaks the bound that should hold a person in. From that root the commentators took two readings of this verse. The first: man wants to go on breaking the limit in the time that lies ahead of him, meaning he persists in sin for the rest of his life. The second: he denies the Hereafter that lies before him, spoiling it in advance by refusing it. The first reading has the root's ordinary moral sense on its side; the second has the sūrah's argument on its side, since the whole chapter is about refusing the Day.

**Why Delay Feels Safe**

Nothing in the texture of ordinary life warns a person that the delay is dangerous. The goods keep arriving, the warnings lose their urgency, and the silence of heaven starts to look like approval. The Qur'an names this and refuses to let it be read as a verdict: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise.”* (6:44). Extended time is not a certificate; *“They are only given more time to increase in sin”* (3:178), and the One who grants the extension is the One who said *“I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless.”* (7:183). A person who treats his own survival as evidence that nothing is wrong has misread the situation completely. Delay is the substance he is being tested with, and he is spending it.

**Where the Sūrah Places the Blame**

By naming desire rather than doubt, the verse settles a practical question about how the message is to be delivered. If the obstacle were intellectual, the remedy would be more argument, and the Qur'an would be obliged to keep producing proofs until the last objection was met. Since the obstacle is appetite, the remedy is a warning that breaks the trance of *later*. That is why the sūrah moves at once from the denial to the state of the denier when the Day arrives, and why the door is held open until the breath stops: *“O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53). The offer runs right up to the moment the request becomes impossible.

## Verse 75:6

> asking ˹mockingly˺, “When is this Day of Judgment?”

**The Question Rehearsed Across the Qur'an**

The wording recurs so often, and in such similar form, that it reads as a fixed piece of the Makkan repertoire. *“They ask ˹mockingly˺, ‘When is this Day of Judgment?’”* (51:12). *“˹Still˺ they ask ˹the believers˺, ‘When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?’”* (67:25). The same sentence appears with small variations in 10:48 — *“They ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass if what you say is true?””*, 21:38 — *“They ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass if what you say is true?””*, 27:71 — *“They ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?””*, 34:29 — *“And they ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?””* and 36:48 — *“And they ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?””*. The repetition tells us something about the audience rather than about the Day. This was not an objection raised once by a thoughtful questioner; it was a slogan, repeated in assemblies, whose function was social — to mark the believers as people making claims they could not substantiate. That is why the Qur'an answers it so many times and in so many different ways: not because the question was hard, but because it kept being asked in bad faith.

**Why the Date Is Withheld**

The reply given to this question elsewhere in the Qur'an is consistent and unusually direct: the time belongs to God, and the Messenger was not permitted to disclose it. *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the Hour, ‘When will it be?’ Say, ‘That knowledge is only with my Lord. He alone will reveal it when the time comes. It is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth and will only take you by surprise.’”* (7:187). The same instruction is repeated in 79:42–43 — *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the Hour, “When will it be?””*, and the list of five hidden things includes the Hour alongside knowledge of what the wombs carry and what a soul will earn tomorrow (31:34 — *“Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ has the knowledge of the Hour.”*). If the date were published, the life lived in front of it would be transformed into a countdown, and conduct would be governed by arithmetic rather than by love, fear, and loyalty. The withholding is the condition of the test, not a gap in the revelation. A second wisdom sits behind it. A published date would change conduct, but it would also end the condition in which conduct means anything, since a person who knows exactly when the audit falls can arrange his affairs for the day before it and live as he pleases until then. The hiddenness of the hour is what makes every hour the possible one.

**What Was Actually Being Demanded**

Behind the demand for a date sat a demand for a different kind of proof. The Makkan audience wanted the threat cashed immediately, in this world, on a schedule they could check. *“And if We delay their punishment until an appointed time, they will definitely say, ‘What is holding it back?’”* (11:8). A prophecy that arrives late looks, to someone keeping score, like a prophecy that failed, and the delay was therefore taken as evidence. The Qur'an's response is to re-locate the whole matter: the Hour is not late, it is near in a sense that has nothing to do with clocks, *“Bringing about the Hour would only take the blink of an eye, or even less.”* (16:77). Nearness here belongs to the One who will bring it, not to the interval that remains.

**How the Qur'an Answers a Question It Refuses to Answer**

The sūrah does not date the Day; it describes what the Day will do to the people who asked. A description of the Day addresses the will, which is where this sūrah has already located the problem in v. So they will not be able to avert it, nor will it be delayed from them.”* (21:40).

The impulse behind the question — the demand that God produce His threat on the asker's timetable — is one the Qur'an treats as a recurring disease, and it prescribes patience as its cure: *“And do not ˹seek to˺ hasten ˹the torment˺ for the deniers. On the Day they see what they have been threatened with, it will be as if they had only stayed ˹in this world˺ for a hour of a day.”* (46:35).

## Verse 75:7

> But when the sight is stunned

**The Stare Recorded Elsewhere**

The same fixed gaze is described in several passages, and the details agree. In Ibrāhīm the guilty come forward *“rushing forth, heads raised, never blinking, hearts void.”* (14:43). In al-Qamar, *“With eyes downcast, they will come forth from the graves as if they were swarming locusts, rushing toward the caller. The disbelievers will cry, ‘This is a difficult Day!’”* (54:7–8). In al-Anbiyāʾ the disbelievers are portrayed as *“staring ˹in horror, crying,˺ ‘Oh, woe to us! We have truly been heedless of this.’”* (21:97). The consistency of the detail is instructive. These are not people looking for a way out or scanning the horizon for help; sight has stopped being a tool of action and has become pure reception. What they refused to look at in life is now the only thing in front of them.

**Why the Eye Fails First**

There is a logic in the sequence. The Qur'an's complaint against the deniers was never that they lacked evidence but that they had eyes and did not use them: *“They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with.”* (7:179). The faculty was intact; its employment was refused. The Hour accordingly begins by disabling the instrument that was misused, and the reversal is exact. Sight that would not read the signs is left with nothing to read but the thing it denied, and this time it cannot look away. The punishment fits the fault without any need for a separate machinery of retribution: the organ that was made for recognising the truth is simply handed the truth at a moment when recognition no longer helps.

**A List Without Connectors**

The sūrah strings these signs together with almost no connectors. The clauses fall one after another with the barest of connectors, giving the passage the quality of a list delivered quickly rather than a narrative unfolding in order. The effect is compression: the events are piled rather than sequenced, and the reader is left with the impression of a world coming apart faster than it can be described. Other sūrahs use the same device for the same subject — *“When the sky splits open, and when the stars fall away, and when the seas burst forth, and when the graves spill out.”* (82:1–4). The rhetorical point of the piling is that the distinction between first and next has stopped mattering. For the person living through it, catastrophe is not a series; it is a single event with many edges.

**The Collapse Comes Before the Answer**

The scoffer had asked when. The sūrah answers by describing the state of the man who asked, which is a way of saying that the question has been overtaken by its subject. By the time the date is known, the knowing is no longer information but experience, and the eyes that demanded proof have been rendered incapable of examining anything. The same principle governs what happens to the other faculties on that Day. Hearing, speech and the power to argue are not removed; they are left intact and turned against their owner, because a person who would not use an instrument for its purpose must eventually meet the truth the instrument was built to receive. The Qur'an describes mouths sealed while hands and feet speak (36:65 — *“On this Day We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to commit”*), and ears, eyes and skin testifying to what was done through them (41:20 — *“When they reach it, their ears, eyes, and skin will testify against what they used to do”*). Sight is handled in exactly this way: not destroyed but overloaded, handed at last the full object it spent a life avoiding, and left without the capacity to process what it receives.

## Verse 75:8

> and the moon is dimmed

**Two Lights With Two Different Jobs**

The Qur'an is precise about the difference between the two great lights, and the distinction is repeated wherever they appear together. *“He is the One Who made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases, so that you may know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺.”* (10:5). The sun is a lamp; the moon is a measure. The moon's function is to mark out time — *“As for the moon, We have ordained ˹precise˺ phases for it, until it ends up like an old, curved palm stalk.”* (36:39) — and human beings were given it as a calendar. The two are also described as running on tracks that never interfere: *“It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day. Each is travelling in an orbit of their own.”* (36:40). Order, measure, and reliability are the qualities being emphasised every time.

**The Calendar the Deniers Relied On**

The first audience lived by a lunar calendar. Months were tracked by the moon's phases, and the same moon regulated the times of pilgrimage, the waiting periods of divorce and inheritance, and the season of the fast. A community that ordered its commercial and ritual life by that light could not have missed the force of the image. The thing they treated as the fixed frame of their affairs was itself a creature with an appointed term, and the Qur'an had already said so: the sun and moon are *“each travelling in an orbit”* (21:33), *“each orbiting for an appointed term”* (13:2), *“each orbiting for an appointed term”* (39:5). A term is a thing that ends. The stability people assume when they look up at the sky is the stability of a schedule, not of a necessity. Nor is the point that timekeeping was somehow wrong — the Qur'an itself instructs believers to reckon their appointed times by the moon. The point is that a measured thing is a created thing. Living by the phases is lawful; assuming that the phases guarantee themselves is to mistake a schedule for a necessity.

**The Order Was Never Self-Sustaining**

The religious point underneath the imagery is that the regularity of the heavens is not a machine left running. Every description of the sun and moon in the Qur'an attaches them to a term and to a Will that set them on their courses. The purpose of their regularity is stated as a sign: *“He makes the signs clear so that you may be certain of the meeting with your Lord.”* (13:2). The order in the sky was installed as an argument for the Day that is now arriving, and its dissolution is that argument's conclusion. The One who split the moon as a sign to a generation that demanded one (54:1 — *“The Hour has drawn near and the moon was split ˹in two˺”*) is the One who now puts its light out, and nothing in between was ever held in place by anything but His command.

## Verse 75:9

> and the sun and the moon are brought together

**Two Orbits, One Ending**

The moon was darkened in the previous verse; here the sun is joined to it. *Jumiʿa* is a passive of gathering, and the two bodies that had been kept apart for the whole of created time are brought into one place and one condition. The sūrah does not say they collide, and the commentators did not read it that way — they read it, with the parallel passage in 81:1–2 — *“When the sun is put out and when the stars fall down”*, as an enfolding in which both lights are extinguished and rolled up together. The joining is therefore not a crash but an assembly: two separate things are made to share a single fate. What had been the most reliable pair of objects in the sky becomes, in one clause, a pair of undone things.

**The Order Was Built on Separation**

The Qur'an is emphatic that the two lights are kept apart by command, and it says so in the same terms in several sūrahs. *“It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day. Each is travelling in an orbit of their own.”* (36:40). *“The sun and the moon ˹travel˺ with precision.”* (55:5). The pattern in these verses is always the same: distinctness, measure, and a term. Two bodies on separate tracks, each with its appointed course, never interfering. That arrangement is what the present verse ends. Gathering the two is the exact negation of the description that has been repeated throughout the Book, which is why the image lands as hard as it does.

**Both Lights Are Servants, Not Powers**

Nothing in the Qur'an permits the sun or the moon to be treated as an independent power. They are described repeatedly as *musakhkharāt*, subjected, made to serve by command, and always for a stated purpose: *“He causes the dawn to break, and has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing.”* (6:96). The regularity that impresses an observer is the regularity of something obeying, not of something ruling. This is also why their dissolution requires no contest. Nothing is being defeated here, and no rival power is being overthrown; a servant's commission has simply reached its end. The universe does not end in a battle but in a discharge. The sun's own term is stated in this vocabulary elsewhere: *“The sun travels for its fixed term. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing.”* (36:38), and the same note is struck in the creation accounts, where the sun, moon and stars are created *“all subjected by His command.”* (7:54). A fixed term is the opposite of a necessity. Whatever has been given a term is a thing that stops, and the regularity that looked like independence was only the reliability of obedience. The Qur'an even anticipates the religious use people would make of these bodies: *“Do not prostrate to the sun or the moon, but prostrate to Allah, Who created them ˹all˺, if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺.”* (41:37). Servants cannot shelter anyone.

## Verse 75:10

> on that Day one will cry, “Where is the escape?”

Six verses of cosmic signs end here, and the passage turns without warning to direct speech. Up to this point the sūrah has described what will happen to the sky and the eyes; now a person speaks, and the sentence is a cry rather than a statement. The shift is the point of the whole sequence.

**Al-Mafarr: a Place, Not a Plan**

The word cried out is *al-mafarr*, from a root meaning to flee. The noun is a place-noun: not the act of escaping but somewhere to escape *to*. That distinction carries the desperation of the sentence. The speaker is not asking for advice, a strategy, or a way of buying time, which is what a person in danger normally wants. He is asking for a destination, and the form of the question concedes that flight itself is assumed — only the destination is missing. The same root produces the description of that Day elsewhere, when people are portrayed running from the people they love most: *“on that Day every person will flee from their own siblings, and ˹even˺ their mother and father, and ˹even˺ their spouse and children.”* (80:34–36). Flight is universal there, and it is useless there too.

**A Question With No Addressee**

Nobody is named as the person being asked. The cry is not directed at God, at the angels, or at the other people in the scene, and that anonymity is part of its horror. A question addressed to someone assumes that someone might answer and that an answer would help. This question is addressed to the situation. The Qur'an uses the device elsewhere to show a person who has passed beyond negotiation — the disbelievers detained before the Fire cry out to be sent back (6:27 — *“If only you could see when they will be detained before the Fire!”*). The wrongdoers ask for a short delay (14:44 — *“And warn the people of the Day when the punishment will overtake ˹the wicked among˺ them, and the wrongdoers will cry, “Our Lord!”*), and in both cases the plea is met with the fact that the time for pleading has closed. What distinguishes this cry is that it is not even a plea. The Qur'an addresses the same cry to people who have not yet reached that Day, and the preposition changes everything: *“Respond to your Lord before the coming of a Day from Allah that cannot be averted. There will be no refuge for you then, nor ˹grounds for˺ denial ˹of sins˺.”* (42:47). The word for refuge there is of the same family as the one in the next verse, and the notice is served in advance. Nothing about that Day is presented as a trap sprung on the unprepared; the exits are described as closing long before they close.

**The Escape Routes That Used to Exist**

In ordinary life there is always somewhere to go. A man under pressure can leave a city, change his company, occupy himself with work, or simply refuse to think about the thing that frightens him. The Qur'an treats the availability of these exits as the real content of the test. It names the day they run out in several places: *“The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it.”* (18:53); *“If only you could see when they will be horrified with no escape ˹on Judgment Day˺! And they will be seized from a nearby place.”* (34:51); *“Whatever ˹idols˺ they used to invoke besides Allah will fail them. And they will realize that they will have no escape.”* (41:48). The repetition is deliberate. The Qur'an is training its hearers to recognise that the exits they rely on are borrowed, not owned.

## Verse 75:11

> But no! There will be no refuge

**Wazar: Shelter, Stronghold, and Burden**

The word being negated is worth unpacking, because English *refuge* flattens it. *Wazar* is the place a person runs to when something is after him — a fortress, a mountain's protection, the shelter of a protector's tent. The same root yields *wizr*, the burden of sin a person carries, and *wazīr*, the minister who carries a ruler's weight. The family of meanings hangs together: a refuge is what bears the weight you cannot bear yourself. To say there is no *wazar* is therefore to say something stronger than *there is nowhere to run*.

**Every Exit the Qur'an Has Already Closed**

The denial is not unique to this verse; it is the Qur'an's standard account of that Day, and the consistency is the point. *“The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it.”* (18:53). *“˹Imagine˺ how many peoples We destroyed before them, and they cried out when it was too late to escape.”* (38:3). *“so those who dispute about Our signs may know that they have no refuge.”* (42:35). The same verdict is delivered in 34:51 — *“If only you could see when they will be horrified with no escape ˹on Judgment Day˺! And they will be seized from a nearby place”* and 41:48 — *“Whatever ˹idols˺ they used to invoke besides Allah will fail them. And they will realize that they will have no escape”*. What changes across these passages is the identity of the thing that fails — idols, protectors, distance, time — and what never changes is that the failure is total. The Qur'an is not describing a difficult situation from which the clever might slip.

**Why There Is Nowhere Left to Go**

The refuges of this life are worth naming, because each of them is a good thing in its place and each of them fails here. Time was a refuge: while it lasted, nothing was settled. Company was a refuge: among people who shared his assumptions, a man's account of himself went unchallenged. Status, wealth, and the ability to move were refuges, and so was the simple power of not thinking about something. Every one of these is a created thing being used as though it were independent, and the Day arrives as the removal of that pretence. What is left is the person and what he did, and the Qur'an is insistent that the two cannot be separated. The record is not a file kept somewhere and produced at the hearing; it is what the person has become. *“And We have everything recorded precisely.”* (78:29), and the precision is the problem: nothing small is omitted, because nothing small was ever without weight. Nothing is taken from anyone; the intermediaries simply stop functioning, and the person is left alone with what he did, which is what he had spent his life arranging to avoid.

**The One Direction That Still Works**

The Qur'an does acknowledge a flight, and it is the only one, and its direction is the opposite of what the cry in v. 10 imagines. Of three men who had failed badly and knew it, the text says: *“their souls were torn in anguish. They knew there was no refuge from Allah except in Him. Then He turned to them in mercy so that they might repent.”* (9:118). The preposition does all the work. There is no refuge *from* God except *in* God, which means the flight that succeeds is the one that stops running. That is available now, in the interval the sūrah keeps insisting is closing, and the Qur'an's offer is stated without conditions attached to the size of the offence: *“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53).

## Verse 75:12

> On that Day all will end up before your Lord

**The Destination Is a Person**

What makes the sentence severe is its preposition. The settling is not *in* a place but *to* the Lord: *ilā rabbika*. The Qur'an could have said that everyone comes to rest in the grave, or in the Fire, or in the Garden, and each of those would have been true. It says instead that the terminus is God, which relocates the whole matter from geography to audience. A person's end is not a location he reaches but a Person he meets, and every description of that Day in the Qur'an is arranged around this: the record is opened, the witnesses arrive, and the judgment is pronounced in the presence of the One who was present all along. The horror and the comfort of the verse are the same fact seen from two sides. The same idea is put the other way round in the last verse of the sūrah of the Morning Brightness, where the terminus is named in the same preposition: *“and that to your Lord ˹alone˺ is the ultimate return ˹of all things˺.”* (53:42). A person's life is movement with an end built into it, and the end is not a place on a map but an audience. That is why the Qur'an's descriptions of the Hereafter are saturated with the language of meeting, presence, and being seen. The Garden is not described as a location so much as a nearness, and the Fire is not described as a place so much as a separation from the One before whom everyone has arrived.

**To Your Lord: Why the Prophet Is Addressed**

The possessive is singular and it is addressed to the Messenger. That choice does two things at once. It universalises the statement, since the Lord of the Prophet is the Lord of everyone, and it personalises it, since the one hearing it first is the man who has been told to warn his people. Every human being will come to rest before the Lord whom Muhammad ﷺ proclaimed, which is the sūrah's answer to the mockery in the opening movement.

**Sovereignty Without Intermediaries**

The scene the Qur'an builds for that Day strips away every other claim to authority. *“the Day all will appear ˹before Allah˺. Nothing about them will be hidden from Him. ˹He will ask,˺ ‘Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme!’”* (40:16). *“All authority on that Day is for Allah ˹alone˺. He will judge between them.”* (22:56). Everything that mediated power in this life — tribe, wealth, office, the protection of a patron, the opinion of a crowd — is absent from these descriptions, and the absence is the argument. A creature who has arranged his standing through intermediaries is brought into a court where no intermediary is recognised, and the verse's plainness is deliberate: no advocate is mentioned, no negotiation is described, and no one speaks except by permission.

## Verse 75:13

> All will then be informed of what they have sent forth and left behind

**What Was Sent Ahead**

The first half of the account is what a person sent on before him. The image is of a traveller dispatching his goods ahead to the place he is going, so that they arrive before he does. Every deed in the Qur'an is described as travelling: *“It is certainly We Who resurrect the dead, and write what they send forth and what they leave behind. Everything is listed by Us in a perfect Record.”* (36:12); *“˹then˺ each soul will know what it has sent forth or left behind.”* (82:5). The point of the metaphor is that a deed does not vanish when the moment of doing it passes.

**Nothing Too Small to Record**

Both halves of the account are kept with a precision the Qur'an describes in some detail. Two angels record, one on the right and one on the left, and *“not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺.”* (50:18). Secret conversations are included: *“Or do they think that We do not hear their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks? Yes ˹We do˺! And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording ˹it all˺.”* (43:80). The same precision is stated of creation at large — *“Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge”* (6:59) — which means the record is not a special surveillance installed for human beings but a consequence of the kind of world this is. Nothing is too small to be counted, and the smallest item on the list is what the Qur'an singles out elsewhere: *“So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it.”* (99:7).

**The Record Is Not Separable From the Person**

What is being disclosed is not a file produced from outside. The record is what the person has made of himself, and the Qur'an's language consistently binds the two. *“And We have everything recorded precisely.”* (78:29) is a statement about the keeping, but the content kept is the man's own history, held in a form he cannot now edit. That is why the disclosure is described as a recognition rather than a revelation: the person reads and knows. *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it. They will cry, ‘Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?’”* (18:49). The cry is not about unfairness. It is the shock of completeness.

**Why the Telling Is the Judgment**

Once the disclosure has happened, surprisingly little remains to be done. There is no cross-examination in these scenes, no dispute about mitigating circumstances, no argument about whether the record is accurate. The person's own knowledge of what he did is the evidence, and the Qur'an states the principle directly: *“Read your record. You ˹alone˺ are enough this Day to take account of yourself.”* (17:14). The judgment therefore does not need to be imposed from outside. It is the encounter between what a person is and what he always knew he was, conducted in the open, in front of the One whose knowledge was never in question. The disclosure is also strictly individual. *“˹Consider˺ the Day ˹when˺ every soul will come pleading for itself, and each will be paid in full for what it did, and none will be wronged.”* (16:111), and no soul bears another's load (17:15 — *“No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another.”*). The company a person kept, the crowd whose approval he shared, and the community whose habits he absorbed will all be present, but they will not dilute his account. The convoy he sent ahead contained his deeds and no one else's.

The second is also the heavier claim, and the sunnah supports it: the Prophet said that when a person dies his deeds end except for three — ongoing charity, beneficial knowledge, and a righteous child who prays for him (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1631).

## Verse 75:14

> In fact, people will testify against their own souls

**Why No Interrogation Is Needed**

The Qur'an's scenes of the reckoning dispense with witnesses in the ordinary sense, because the admission comes from the party himself. The limbs supply what the mouth will not: *“When they reach it, their ears, eyes. Skin will testify against what they used to do.”* (41:20). *“on the Day their tongues, hands, and feet will testify against them for what they used to do.”* (24:24); *“On this Day We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to commit.”* (36:65). These are not external informants brought in to defeat a denial. They are parts of the person's own history, and their testimony is simply the record of what was done through them. The confession in this verse is the last step in the same process: the man stops disputing and says it himself.

**Conscience Becomes Evidence**

The Qur'an locates this inward knowledge with precision: *“Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ created humankind and ˹fully˺ know what their souls whisper to them, and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein.”* (50:16). The whisper of the soul is known before it is spoken, which means the self-knowledge described in this verse was never hidden from God; it was hidden from the person, or from the parts of him he permitted himself to consult. The Day is so a removal of the veil rather than an acquisition of evidence. What a person has been carrying inside him all along — the unexamined knowledge of what he has done — is produced, and he hears himself say it.

**Confession Without Coercion**

The confessions recorded elsewhere in the Qur'an have a striking quality: they are volunteered. *“O assembly of jinn and humans! Did messengers not come from among you, proclaiming My revelations and warning you of the coming of this Day of yours?” They will say, “We confess against ourselves!”* (6:130). The same admission is put in the mouths of those whom the angels take at death (7:37 — *“They will receive what is destined for them, until Our messenger-angels arrive to take their souls, asking them, “Where are those ˹false gods˺ you used to invoke besides Allah?””*). Nothing in these passages suggests a confession extracted under threat. What has changed is the situation, not the person's knowledge. In life, denial was inexpensive because the interests that made it attractive were still in place; on that Day those interests have been removed, and the same man who found denial easy now finds it impossible. Two passages fill in how the confession actually arrives. When the enemies of God are gathered, their own skins speak against them, and they turn on their skins in outrage, only to be told that they never bothered to hide anything from their ears, eyes and skin (41:21–22 — *“You did not ˹bother to˺ hide yourselves from your ears, eyes, and skin to prevent them from testifying against you.”*). The reply given to them locates the real failure: *“It was that ˹false˺ assumption you entertained about your Lord that has brought about your doom, so you have become losers.”* (41:23). Their account of themselves was never a dispute about evidence. It was a false assumption about the One who kept the record, and once that assumption collapses, the confession follows on its own. That is the most economical explanation of the confession, and it is the one the Qur'an supports.

**What This Means for the Living**

The practical implication is plain enough to state without embellishment. If the witness at the reckoning is a person's own self, then the thing to attend to now is not the strength of one's arguments but the state of one's self-knowledge. The man who has practised accusing himself, in the manner the sūrah swore by in v. 2, arrives at the hearing with the testimony already given and the account already settled. The Qur'an also records the moment at which denial becomes impossible, and it is not a moment of duress but of unveiling: *“You were totally heedless of this. Now We have lifted this veil of yours, so Today your sight is sharp!”* (50:22). The veil was never a shortage of information. It was the arrangement of interests that made ignoring the information comfortable.

## Verse 75:15

> despite the excuses they come up with

The Arabic term is the plural of an intensive form built on the root for excuse, and its shape suggests something done repeatedly and with effort. The same word appears in the Qur'an on the lips of people in this life, offering reasons to the Prophet for what they had failed to do, and the response they receive is instructive: *“They will make excuses to you ˹believers˺ when you return to them. Say, ‘Make no excuses, ˹for˺ we will not believe you. Allah has already informed us about your ˹true˺ state.’”* (9:94).

**Two Ways of Reading the Clause**

The commentators divided over how this verse attaches to the one before it. On the first reading it is a concession: he testifies against himself *even though* he has his excuses ready, meaning the testimony is so strong that it overrides his defence. On the second it is a rejection: he casts about for excuses, and none of them will be accepted. The first reading gives the verse a rhetorical force, since it measures the strength of the self-witness by what it overcomes. The second gives the excuses their own clause and makes the verse a verdict on pleading. The second is the stronger reading, because the Qur'an says the same thing elsewhere in explicit terms — *“the Day the wrongdoers’ excuses will be of no benefit to them.”* (40:52) — and because the sūrah has already closed every avenue of escape in v. 11. Either way, the outcome is identical: the plea fails.

**The Plea the Qur'an Records**

Scripture is specific about what the pleading sounds like, and the examples are sobering. Some will swear that they were never associators at all: *“Their only argument will be: ‘By Allah, our Lord! We were never polytheists.’”* (6:23). Others will swear to God as they used to swear to people, *“thinking they have something to stand on. Indeed, it is they who are the ˹total˺ liars.”* (58:18). What these scenes share is that the lie is not tactical. A person who has spent decades telling a story about himself has come to believe it, and the exposure of the story is experienced as an assault rather than as a correction. The Qur'an diagnoses the failure at its root elsewhere: the assumption that God did not really know much of what they were doing (41:22 — *“Rather, you assumed that Allah did not know much of what you used to do”*). Fix that assumption and the excuses lose their function.

**Why No Plea Can Work There**

The failure of the excuses is not a refusal to hear them. It is that the conditions which made them plausible have been removed. An excuse works in this life because it is offered to someone who cannot see the whole picture, or who can be worn down, or whose opinion matters more to the speaker than the truth does. None of those conditions get at the reckoning. The Witness is the One whose knowledge was never in question, the record is complete, and the person's own soul is on the side of the prosecution. The Qur'an warns the living about this in the plainest possible terms: *“There will be no refuge for you then, nor ˹grounds for˺ denial ˹of sins˺.”* (42:47). The time for the plea that works is the time before the Day arrives.

## Verse 75:16

> Do not rush your tongue trying to memorize ˹a revelation of˺ the Quran

**What Prompted the Instruction**

The circumstance is preserved in the most reliable collection of the Prophet's sayings. Ibn ʿAbbās reported that when revelation came down, the Prophet used to move his tongue and lips along with it, hurrying to fix the words before they escaped him, and this was hard for him; so God revealed *“Move not your tongue concerning the Quran to make haste therewith.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4927). A second narration in the same collection adds the explanation of what the following verses promise: that God would gather it in his chest and enable him to recite it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5, 4928). The wording of the instruction is therefore precise: the thing prohibited is not effort but haste, and the haste came from fear of loss. God's answer addresses the fear rather than the diligence.

**The Fear Behind the Haste**

The anxiety is completely intelligible. A man receiving scripture in an oral culture, with no written text in front of him and no means of checking what he had heard, would reasonably press to secure every syllable before it slipped. The Qur'an answers that anxiety elsewhere with a promise rather than a technique: *“We will have you recite ˹the Quran, O Prophet,˺ so you will not forget ˹any of it˺, unless Allah wills otherwise.”* (87:6). The guarantee is attached to the Giver, not to the recipient's precautions. The Prophet's haste was not disobedience; it was the last remnant of relying on his own management, and the verse removes it. The report carries a further detail.

**The Reciter's Manner**

The sūrah's instruction also settles a question about how the Qur'an is to be recited. Haste of the tongue is prohibited, and the opposite is prescribed: *“or a little more—and recite the Quran ˹properly˺ in a measured way.”* (73:4). The word there is *tartīl*, recitation that is distinct, unhurried, and given its full measure of sound. A text that is recited slowly is a text the reciter is attending to, and the attention is part of the act. The opposite of rushing is not slowness for its own sake; it is allowing the words to arrive in the order and at the speed at which they were sent. Since the Qur'an describes itself as revealed in stages for exactly this reason — *“˹It is˺ a Quran We have revealed in stages so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace.”* (17:106) — the manner of recitation reproduces the manner of revelation.

**The Student's Discipline**

The early commentators drew out a further application that the wording supports. One who hurries to master the words before their meaning has been settled will form conclusions on partial evidence, and a text revealed over twenty-three years could not be understood in any other way than by waiting for the whole. The instruction is therefore a rule for anyone reading: hold the question open until the passage is complete. The Qur'an makes the same point about itself in several places, insisting that it is a Book whose parts interpret one another and whose verses were perfected and then explained in detail (11:1 — *“Alif-Lãm-Ra. ˹This is˺ a Book whose verses are well perfected and then fully explained. ˹It is˺ from the One ˹Who is˺ All-Wise, All-Aware”*). The rule reaches the listener as well. If the Prophet was told not to move his tongue ahead of the revelation, the obligation on those who hear it is no lighter: attend while it is recited, and let it finish before drawing conclusions. The Qur'an names the opposite practice and complains about it — *“The Messenger has cried, ‘O my Lord! My people have indeed received this Quran with neglect.’”* (25:30) — and it describes the proper response elsewhere as attending silence.

The same instruction is given in Sūrah Ṭā Hā in almost identical terms: *“Do not rush to recite ˹a revelation of˺ the Quran ˹O Prophet˺ before it is ˹properly˺ conveyed to you, and pray, ‘My Lord! Increase me in knowledge.’”* (20:114).

## Verse 75:17

> It is certainly upon Us to ˹make you˺ memorize and recite it

**The Promise of Preservation**

The assurance given here is stated elsewhere as a general promise covering the whole text: *“We will have you recite ˹the Quran, O Prophet,˺ so you will not forget ˹any of it˺, unless Allah wills otherwise.”* (87:6). The exception clause is important, since it prevents the promise from being read as mechanical; the guarantee is personal and continuing, not an once-for-all deposit. The same protection is claimed for the scripture itself: *“It is certainly We Who have revealed the Reminder, and it is certainly We Who will preserve it.”* (15:9). Taken together, the two verses describe a single arrangement in which the preservation of the text and the preservation of the reciter are the same divine act, and neither is left to the resources of the man who received it. The two promises also answer the two forms of loss that a revealed text can suffer. It can be lost through forgetting, which the gathering in the chest addresses, and it can be lost through alteration, which the recitation addresses, since a text that is constantly recited in public by many memorisers cannot be quietly revised by anyone. The Qur'an's own account of its preservation takes in both risks.

**Gathering It in the Chest**

The first of the two tasks is *jamʿuhu*, its gathering, and the early authorities were specific about what was meant. Ibn ʿAbbās explained it as God's promise to gather the Qur'an in the Prophet's breast (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4928), and the same collection narrates him saying that the Messenger used to bear the revelation with difficulty and move his lips with it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5). The image is of scattered pieces being brought into one place inside a man, and it suits a revelation that arrived in fragments over twenty-three years — a few verses at a time, in response to events, questions, and circumstances no one could have scheduled. Two features of that gathering deserve attention. First, it occurred inside a man, not on a page: the primary vessel of the Qur'an in the first generation was the chest, and the writing down of the text ran alongside a memorisation that was never treated as secondary.

**How the Community Understood It**

Muslim practice from the first generation onward has treated this verse as the basis of an institution rather than only as a comfort. If the collection of the Qur'an rests with God, then its preservation among human beings is maintained through human effort rather than in competition with it, and the community organised itself accordingly: recitation from memory, transmission by chain, and the writing down of what had been recited and checked. The Qur'an describes the text as held in honoured, purified pages in the hands of angel-scribes (80:13–16 — *“by the hands of angel-scribes”*), and as *“a well-preserved Record”* (56:78). Those descriptions are not poetry about an ideal scripture. They are the religious account of why the copy in a child's hands and the copy in a museum in Samarkand are recognisably the same book. The practical consequence is that the community has never treated memorisation as a specialism.

The text is not called a book that must then be performed. It is called a recitation, an event of the mouth and the ear, which is why the Qur'an's own self-description insists on its oral character: *“Indeed, you ˹O Prophet˺ are receiving the Quran from the One ˹Who is˺ All-Wise, All-Knowing.”* (27:6).

## Verse 75:18

> So once We have recited a revelation ˹through Gabriel˺, follow its recitation ˹closely˺

**Who Is Doing the Reciting**

The subject of the verb is God: *when We recite it*. The Qur'an attributes the recitation to its Author and treats the angel and the Prophet as the means through which it reaches human ears, and the early exegesis follows the text closely. Ibn ʿAbbās glossed this verse as an instruction to listen and be silent, and then explained that afterwards the Messenger would recite it as Gabriel had recited it to him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4928). The same narration fills in what the listening looked like in practice: whenever Gabriel came, the Prophet would listen, and when he departed he would recite as Gabriel had recited. The discipline described here became a yearly institution. Abū Hurayra reported that Gabriel used to repeat the recitation of the Qur'an with the Prophet once every year. Twice in the year in which he died, and a second narration in the same collection has Gabriel meeting him every night of Ramaḍān to review the Qur'an with him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4998; 6). The review was a listening as much as a reciting, with each party checking the other's reading. That is the discipline of this verse written into the community's calendar, and it explains why the annual revision of the text became a standing practice rather than an occasional one.

**The Pace of the Descent**

The same principle governs how the revelation reached the community. It was not sent down in a single delivery but in stages, and the reason given for that arrangement is the reason given in this verse: *“˹We have sent it˺ as such ˹in stages˺ so We may reassure your heart with it. And We have revealed it at a deliberate pace.”* (25:32). The objectors wanted it all at once, on the ground that a scripture should arrive complete; the reply is that the pace was chosen for the recipient's sake. *“˹It is˺ a Quran We have revealed in stages so that you may recite it to people at a deliberate pace.”* (17:106). The objectors who wanted the whole scripture at once were asking, in practice, for a text they could control — a document complete on delivery, available for consultation, usable on their own timetable. What they were given instead was a recitation that arrived in instalments and had to be followed. Listening is therefore built into the structure of the revelation rather than demanded only of its first hearer.

**A Rule for Reading**

The verse has always been read as a rule for anyone who opens the text, and the rule is simple to state and hard to keep. The opposite practice is the one the Qur'an complains about — a people who received the recitation with neglect (25:30 — *“The Messenger has cried, “O my Lord! My people have indeed received this Quran with neglect.””*) — and its most respectable version is the hurry of the student who reaches conclusions on partial evidence.

## Verse 75:19

> Then it is surely upon Us to make it clear ˹to you˺

**Limits of the Promise**

The guarantee of clarification should not be read as a promise that every question will be answered, and the Qur'an prevents that misreading. Some matters are left undisclosed, and the withholding is presented as mercy rather than as a defect: *“O believers! Do not ask about any matter which, if made clear to you, may disturb you.”* (5:101). Knowledge of the Hour, of what the wombs carry, and of what a soul will earn tomorrow is withheld by name (31:34 — *“No soul knows what it will earn for tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die.”*). What is promised in this verse is therefore not exhaustive exposition but enough clarity: the text will be explained to the extent that its guidance depends on explanation. A reader who treats every silence in the revelation as a gap to be filled by speculation has misunderstood the promise as much as one who treats it as licence to ignore the meaning.

**What Bayān Covers**

The word rendered as clarification is *bayān*, and it carries a wide range: making something distinct, separating it out, and stating it so that it is no longer ambiguous. It covers the meaning of a word, the ruling contained in a verse, and the application of a general principle to a particular case. The early authorities took it in this breadth. Ibn ʿAbbās explained that God would clarify the Qur'an through the Prophet's own tongue (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4928), which assigns the explanation to the same channel as the revelation without collapsing the two into one act. The verse so supports the view that the Prophet's exposition of the text was itself part of what was promised. The Qur'an states that office explicitly: *“And We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Reminder, so that you may explain to people what has been revealed for them, and perhaps they will reflect.”* (16:44).

**The Prophet as Exponent**

The guarantee in this verse is one reason the community has always treated the Prophet's explanation as authoritative rather than merely useful. If the clarification of the text was promised by the One who sent it, then the clarification is not one scholarly opinion among many. The Qur'an describes the Prophet's teaching office in the vocabulary of the same gift: he was sent to recite the revelations, to purify, and to teach the Book and wisdom (3:164 — *“Indeed, Allah has done the believers a ˹great˺ favour by raising a messenger from among them—reciting to them His revelations, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom.”*; 62:2 — *“He is the One Who raised for the illiterate ˹people˺ a messenger from among themselves—reciting to them His revelations, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom, for indeed they had previously been clearly astray”*). The pairing is significant. The text is one thing and the wisdom of its application another, and both are attributed to instruction rather than to independent reasoning. That is the scriptural basis for the weight the sunnah carries in every school of Islamic law.

**Why the Meaning Descends Too**

The verse settles a question that matters more than it first appears. If the text had been preserved but its meaning left to whoever came after, the scripture would have become whatever the most persuasive reader said it was, and the community's disputes would have had no court of appeal. By placing the *bayān* with the One who sent the text, the sūrah establishes that meaning is part of the revelation and not an addition to it. That is why the Qur'an insists on its own internal consistency as a mark of its origin (4:82 — *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies”*), and why its description of itself as guidance is never separated from its description as a clarification. Because the clarification is promised by the Speaker, the text is not at the mercy of its readers' ingenuity, and the Qur'an's repeated challenge to produce a sūrah like it (2:23 — *“And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true”*; 10:38 — *“Or do they claim, “He made it up!”? Tell them ˹O Prophet˺, “Produce one sûrah like it then, and seek help from whoever you can—other than Allah—if what you say is true!””*) is not a challenge about style alone.

## Verse 75:20

> But no! In fact, you love this fleeting world

**The World Is Not Condemned Here**

Nothing in this verse, or in the Qur'an generally, requires contempt for created things. The lawful provisions and adornments God brought forth are declared to be for His servants in this life (7:32 — *“Ask, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Who has forbidden the adornments and lawful provisions Allah has brought forth for His servants?””*), and the sūrah of the Poets warns against the opposite error of withholding them. What is condemned is the ordering: *“Surely those ˹pagans˺ love this fleeting world, ˹totally˺ neglecting a weighty Day ahead of them.”* (76:27). The parallel verse in Sūrah al-Insān makes the structure explicit — loving the hasty thing and neglecting the weighty thing are two descriptions of one movement. A person does not have to hate the world to be ruined by it. He has to love it in the wrong position, ahead of the thing that outlasts it.

**The Address Swings Back**

Four verses were spent instructing the Prophet about how to receive revelation, and now, with the particle *kallā*, the sūrah turns away from him and speaks to people in the plural. The shift is abrupt and it is meaningful. The parenthesis established that God guarantees His text; the accusation that follows is that the hearers do not want what the text offers. The particle *kallā* appears three times in this short sūrah, and each appearance marks a turn in the argument rather than a simple negation. In v. 11 it closed the cry for escape; here it dismisses the whole posture of the person who receives the revelation and ignores it; later it will introduce the moment of death.

**Why Haste Is Fatal**

The danger in the adjective is that the hasty world does not feel temporary. It presses with the urgency of things that need deciding today, and the Hereafter, being absent, never presses at all. That asymmetry is the test. It is not a fair contest between two objects of equal presence, and the Qur'an's remedy is to make the absent thing present through remembrance, revelation, and the constant reminder of death. The sūrah has been doing precisely that for twenty verses — collapsing the sky, opening the record, closing the exits — so that the weighty Day acquires some of the urgency the hasty world has by default. The warning here is the conclusion of that effort: you are being hurried by a thing that is itself hurrying away. The Qur'an states the comparison in the plainest terms available: *“But you ˹deniers only˺ prefer the life of this world, even though the Hereafter is far better and more lasting.”* (87:16–17). A person is not being asked to choose a worse thing out of duty. He is being told that he is choosing the smaller and the shorter, and the sūrah of Iron spells out why the near thing cannot bear the weight put on it: *“this worldly life is no more than the delusion of enjoyment.”* (57:20).

**Reordering, Not Renunciation**

If the disease is misordered love, the cure is not the destruction of the object but the reordering of the heart, and the Qur'an's prescriptions take that shape. Wealth is not abolished; it is put to work — *“You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish.”* (3:92) — and the test is precisely that the thing given is the thing loved.

## Verse 75:21

> and neglect the Hereafter

**The Anatomy of Neglect**

Neglect is an omission rather than an act, and that is what makes it so difficult to detect in oneself. One does not neglect something by attacking it; one neglects it by filling the space it would have occupied with other things. The diary fills up, the obligations multiply, and the question of how one stands before God is never answered because it is never quite reached. The Qur'an describes the same condition from the other end, in the words of those who arrive at the reckoning having left the Day behind them: *“those who took this faith ˹of Islam˺ as mere amusement and play and were deluded by ˹their˺ worldly life.” ˹Allah will say,˺ “Today We will ignore them just as they ignored the coming of this Day of theirs.”* (7:51). The most severe statement of this exchange is put in God's own words: *“So taste ˹the punishment˺ for neglecting the meeting of this Day of yours. We ˹too˺ will certainly neglect you.”* (32:14).

**Why the Hereafter Loses the Contest**

The competition is inherently unequal, and the Qur'an is honest about the reason. The world is present, sensory, and shared by everyone around; the Hereafter is absent and known only by report. Under those conditions, attention will always drift toward what can be seen unless something compensates, and the compensating mechanism in the Qur'an is revelation plus remembrance. That is the practical function of the daily prayers, the recitation, and the constant return to these descriptions. The sūrah's own twenty-verse campaign to make the Day vivid is itself the counterweight being supplied. A person who stops feeding his attention on the absent will find, without any decision having been made, that he has stopped believing in it. The Qur'an describes this outcome as a form of knowledge that is truncated rather than false: *“They ˹only˺ know the worldly affairs of this life, but are ˹totally˺ oblivious to the Hereafter.”* (30:7). The remedy it prescribes is equally concrete, and it is not withdrawal from the world. Wealth and children are called the adornment of this life, *“but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope.”* (18:46).

**What Neglect Costs**

The cost is not only the loss of a future benefit; it is the deformation of the present. A person who never reckons with an end to his account lives without a standard by which to order his desires, and the result is that the nearest desire governs. The Qur'an's analysis of this drift is unsparing: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise.”* (6:44). Prosperity then confirms the neglect rather than curing it, because the neglect is invisible from inside a life that is going well. And the consequence on the other side is stated without softening: *“Undoubtedly, they will be sealed off from their Lord on that Day.”* (83:15).

## Verse 75:22

> On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright

**The Face as Standing**

Honour and disgrace are described on the face throughout the Qur'an, and the passages are consistent enough to fix the meaning. Of the guilty: *“Humiliation will cover them—with no one to protect them from Allah—as if their faces were covered with patches of the night’s deep darkness.”* (10:27). Of the same people in the sūrah of the Overwhelming, *“On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be downcast,”* (88:2). And the two classes are named together in one verse: *“On that Day some faces will be bright while others gloomy.”* (3:106). In each case the face registers what a person is worth in the only court that matters. The Qur'an is not describing cosmetics. It is describing dignity and its loss, visible at last without the disguises that status, wealth, and reputation supply in this life.

**Recognition, Not Transformation**

What happens on that Day is not the alteration of a face into something foreign to its owner. The Qur'an says elsewhere that the radiance is recognisable — *“You will recognize on their faces the glow of delight.”* (83:24) — and that those granted joy show it in the same place where everyone has always shown what he feels. Nothing here suggests a mask fitted to a face at the last moment. The sūrah of the Heights gives the same principle from the other side, where the people of the Garden describe what was removed before they entered: *“We will remove whatever bitterness they had in their hearts.”* (7:43). The removal of what corrupts and the appearance of what flourishes are one event, and the face is where it is legible. The face had been trained all along: by what it looked at, by what it was ashamed of, by the things it lit up at and the things it turned away from. The sūrah of the Night names the training in a sentence: *“But the righteous will be spared from it — who donate ˹some of˺ their wealth only to purify themselves,”* seeking *“the pleasure of their Lord, the Most High. They will certainly be pleased.”* (92:17–21). Spending for the sake of a countenance one has not seen is the discipline, and seeing it is the wage. A person who spent his life brightening at the mention of his Lord arrives with a face that does that naturally, and the Day removes the last thing preventing the correspondence between the inside and the outside.

**Where the Radiance Comes From**

The Qur'an is careful to attribute the brightness to God rather than to the person's own achievement, and the parallel in Sūrah al-Insān states it directly: *“So Allah will deliver them from the horror of that Day, and grant them radiance and joy,”* (76:11). Radiance is granted. That is not a detail of phrasing but the theology of the whole passage: the face is bright because it has been turned toward its Lord and then looked upon favourably, not because it earned a good complexion. The same correspondence is stated from the other direction in the sūrah of the Overwhelming: *“On that Day ˹other˺ faces will be glowing with bliss, ˹fully˺ pleased with their striving, in an elevated Garden.”* (88:8–10). The faces are pleased because the striving was accepted, and the acceptance is God's act throughout. One's deeds are real, and the record is exact, but the radiance is a gift, and the sūrah's next verse will name the gift in a single clause that the commentators have argued over ever since.

The Qur'an elsewhere names three rather than two groups — *“the people of the right, how ˹blessed˺ will they be. The people of the left, how ˹miserable˺ will they be; and the foremost ˹in faith˺ will be the foremost ˹in Paradise˺.”* (56:8–10) — which is a reminder that the division is not coarse.

## Verse 75:23

> looking at their Lord

**The Majority Reading and Its Evidence**

The great majority of commentators understood this as the literal vision of God in the Hereafter, and they did so on the strength of reports that are among the most securely transmitted in the corpus. Jābir's narration is explicit: the Prophet came out to his companions on the night of the full moon and said, *“You will see your Lord on the Day of Resurrection as you see this ˹full moon˺ and you will have no difficulty in seeing Him.”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7436). A longer version in the same collection adds the comparison to the sun and moon when no cloud obscures them and the instruction to guard the prayer before sunrise and before sunset, which ties the vision to the practice of worship (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 554; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 633). The wording of the verse and the wording of the reports converge, and the preposition is doing ordinary work in both.

**The Argument From 6:103 — *“No vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision. For He is the Most Subtle, All-Aware”***

The stronger objection is religious rather than lexical. *“No vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision.”* (6:103) denies that sight understands God, and a reader who treats that verse as definitive must treat this one as figurative. Al-Zamakhsharī argued along these lines, and the discussion belongs to the larger question of how verses about God's attributes are to be handled. The majority answer distinguishes the predicates: the verb in 6:103 — *“No vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision. For He is the Most Subtle, All-Aware”* is *idrāk*, comprehending or overtaking, which is a claim about grasp, whereas the claim here is about sight. The hadith's own simile makes the same distinction, since the companions were not told they would understand the moon — only that seeing it involves no crowding and no doubt. Vision without encompassment is exactly what the reports describe. That reading is stronger, because it honours both texts without weakening either, and because a third passage settles where the vision is *not* available. When Moses asked to see God, the answer was *“You cannot see Me! But look at the mountain. If it remains firm in its place, only then will you see Me.”* (7:143). The mountain was levelled and Moses fell unconscious. A text that denies the vision in this life and promises it in the next is making a distinction the two parties both need: the limitation belongs to the creature in his present state, not to the object seen.

**Correspondence Between the Life and the Sight**

The reward also fits the life that prepared for it, and the fit is the Qur'an's usual way of describing outcomes. Those who acted for the sake of God's countenance rather than for the thanks or repayment of people (76:9 — *“˹saying to themselves,˺ “We feed you only for the sake of Allah, seeking neither reward nor thanks from you”*) are given the countenance itself. The sūrah opened by swearing by the self-reproaching soul, the faculty that keeps a person's account in front of him; it closes this passage with the people who kept that account honestly being brought into the presence of the One who was keeping it all along. The faces are radiant because they are looking at what they had been looking for, and the looking is described as continuing rather than as a single glance. Two conditions are named elsewhere and both fit. The first is awe: *“And whoever is in awe of standing before their Lord will have two Gardens.”* (55:46). The second is soundness of the organ itself: *“Only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺.”* (26:89). A heart that has been trained to look toward its Lord in absence is the heart that will look at Him in presence.

## Verse 75:24

> And ˹other˺ faces will be gloomy

**Two Images: Dust and Darkness**

Other passages supply the detail that this verse abbreviates, and they use two different pictures. One is dust: *“while ˹other˺ faces will be dusty, cast in gloom”* (80:40–41). Dust is what covers a person who has been dragged, and in the Qur'an's phrase it is the mark of defeat and disgrace. The other picture is darkness: *“Humiliation will cover them—with no one to protect them from Allah—as if their faces were covered with patches of the night’s deep darkness.”* (10:27). Darkness is the loss of light rather than the addition of filth. Together the two images say that this face has both lost what lit it and acquired what disfigures it, and neither is applied from outside. The defilement and the darkening are what the person has become. A third passage adds the posture of the same people once the sentence is carried out: *“And whoever comes with an evil deed will be hurled face-first into the Fire. Are you rewarded except for what you used to do?”* (27:90). The face leads, and the question attached to it rules out complaint. What is happening to these faces is what they did, returned to them in a form they can no longer edit.

**What the Face Registers**

A face on that Day is registering a verdict, and the Qur'an is consistent about which verdict it is. These are people who had standing — tribe, wealth, followers, reputation — and none of it has been recognised. *“And all faces will be humbled before the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining. And those burdened with wrongdoing will be in loss.”* (20:111). The humiliation is not an additional punishment imposed on top of a sentence; it is the sentence, seen from the inside. A person who built his worth out of things that do not survive discovers, in a single moment and in public, that he has nothing to stand on, and the discovery is legible on his face before a word is spoken.

**Why the Face and Not the Body**

The Qur'an could have divided humanity by destination, by deed, or by the record placed in the right or the left hand — and elsewhere it does all of these. The choice of the face here is deliberate, because in Arabic usage the face is the part of a person that turns toward something. It is the organ of attention: what you face is what you are for. A life spent turned toward the hasty world produces a face that, when the world is gone, has nothing left to turn to. The Qur'an states the same principle in the language of light and sight elsewhere: the blind and the seeing are not equal, nor are darkness and light, nor shade and heat, nor the dead and the living (35:19–22 — *“nor are the darkness and the light”*).

**The Faces of This Life Cannot Be Managed There**

One of the functions of status in this world is to control what other people see. A man can surround himself with people who will not contradict him, buy the appearance of composure, and ensure that his account of himself is the only one in circulation. Every one of those arrangements depends on an audience that can be managed, and the removal of that audience is part of what the Day does. *“On that Day all will follow the caller ˹for assembly˺, ˹and˺ none will dare to deviate. All voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate.”* (20:108). No crowd is available to be worked, and no face can be held in an expression it does not have. Pleading is removed as well: *“Then the disbelievers will neither be allowed to plead nor appease ˹their Lord˺.”* (16:84), and the witnesses who could have spoken for them are speaking against them (4:41 — *“So how will it be when We bring a witness from every faith-community and bring you ˹O Prophet˺ as a witness against yours”*). The management of appearances was never going to survive an occasion designed to end it, and the Qur'an is careful to say so in advance.

## Verse 75:25

> anticipating something devastating to befall them

**Al-Fāqira: the Back-Breaking Calamity**

The noun is built on a root meaning to break the spine, and it belongs to the vocabulary of catastrophic disaster rather than of ordinary misfortune. The image is of a blow that leaves a person unable to stand, and its application here is to something that will befall the faces rather than merely be seen by them. The spine is what holds a person upright, both physically and in the Qur'an's phrase of dignity, so a blow that breaks the back is a blow that removes the ability to stand. The same people describe their condition once the calamity has landed, and their account of it is instructive: *“My wealth has not benefited me! My authority has been stripped from me.”* (69:28–29).

**Ẓann: Reckoning, Not Conjecture**

The word deserves a note, because in ordinary usage it can mean opinion or suspicion, and a reader might take it to mean that these people are merely worried. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the confidence of the blessed, which settles the sense: when the context is the Hereafter, the verb describes a settled expectation, whether of good or of harm. The people of the record given into the right hand say, *“I surely knew I would face my reckoning.”* (69:20). The same certainty, the same verb, applied in the other direction, is what darkens the faces in this verse. What distinguishes the two groups is not that one knows and the other guesses. It is what each has become certain about.

**The Pain of Knowing Beforehand**

Scripture is candid that the interval is part of the suffering, and it describes the physiological shape of the dread in more than one place: *“Warn them ˹O Prophet˺ of the approaching Day when the hearts will jump into the throats, suppressing distress. The wrongdoers will have neither a close friend nor intercessor to be heard.”* (40:18). A second passage gives the same moment from the standpoint of those who can see where the path ends: *“The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it.”* (18:53). Knowing, in these scenes, is not a mitigation. It is the beginning of the thing known, and there is no longer any route by which the knowledge can be put to use.

**Why Certainty Is Not Mercy Here**

A person might suppose that certainty would be kinder than suspense, since at least the waiting would be over. The Qur'an's answer is that certainty is only a relief when it can be acted on. Knowledge of a verdict one cannot appeal, cannot flee, and cannot bargain with does not shorten anything; it removes the last occupation the mind had, which was the arrangement of arguments and hopes. The faces in this verse have run out of those. *“And warn the people of the Day when the punishment will overtake ˹the wicked among˺ them, and the wrongdoers will cry, ‘Our Lord! Delay us for a little while. We will respond to Your call and follow the messengers!’”* (14:44) — and the reply they receive is a reminder that they had once sworn none of this would happen.

## Verse 75:26

> But no! ˹Beware of the day˺ when the soul reaches the collar bone ˹as it leaves˺

**Tarāqī: the Point of Exit**

The word rendered as collarbones is *al-tarāqī*, the plural of a noun for the bone at the top of the chest, and it means the hollow above the collarbone where the throat meets the torso. The picture is precise: the soul has risen through the body to the point of leaving. The Qur'an uses the same image in the sūrah of the Inevitable Event — *“Why then ˹are you helpless˺ when the soul ˹of a dying person˺ reaches ˹their˺ throat,”* (56:83) — and the two passages share the assumption that dying is a process with a describable anatomy rather than a switch. Nothing in the verse requires the reader to locate the soul physically. The point is the threshold: the moment at which the person is still here and about not to be. The early commentators described this as the point at which the soul is drawn out of the body. They disagreed about the manner of the drawing rather than about its reality: the soul of the believer leaving easily, like water from a waterskin, and the soul of the denier dragged out as a skewer is pulled through wet wool. Those descriptions are reports preserved in the tafsīr literature rather than hadith with secured chains, and they are cited here as such. What they agree on is the verse's own claim: death is a transition that happens to a person, not a state he simply enters.

**Why the Description Is Physical**

There is a reason the Qur'an describes death in this register instead of speaking only of the soul's departure. The angels are described taking the soul at exactly this point in another sūrah — *“When death comes to any of you, Our angels take their soul, never neglecting this duty.”* (6:61) — which places the visible moment and the invisible agent in the same sentence.

**The Moment Denial Becomes Impossible**

What happens at that threshold, on either reading, is the end of the argument the sūrah has been conducting since v. 3. The denier's position depended on the absence of the thing he denied; at death the thing arrives, and the Qur'an describes the arrival as an unveiling rather than as a revelation of new facts: *“You were totally heedless of this. Now We have lifted this veil of yours, so Today your sight is sharp!”* (50:22). Nothing is added to the person's knowledge. What is removed is the arrangement that allowed him to live as though he did not have it.

**The Hour and the Private Hour**

The sūrah's structure now becomes visible. The Qur'an states that there is a barrier set behind the dead until the Day they are raised (23:100 — *“so I may do good in what I left behind.” Never! It is only a ˹useless˺ appeal they make. And there is a barrier behind them until the Day they are resurrected”*), so the period between a person's death and the general resurrection is real, but it is not an interval he experiences as waiting.

## Verse 75:27

> and it will be said, “Is there any healer ˹who can save this life˺?”

**A Second Reading: the Angels Speak**

Several commentators took the sentence differently and read it as the question of the angels rather than of the bystanders, on the basis that the same root can carry the sense of ascending: *who will take his soul up?* The grounds for this reading are lexical and contextual — the next verses describe a departure and a driving to the Lord. The angels are described elsewhere as ascending with the soul. Some specified that the question is asked by the angels of mercy in the case of a believer and the angels of punishment in the case of a denier, which would make the sentence a division between two kinds of death. The reading is coherent, but the majority position is stronger, because the verb as used here is the ordinary word for healing, because the question fits the scene of a deathbed. Because the following verse's certainty about the parting reads more naturally as the dying person's awareness than as an angel's report.

**Everything That Could Be Tried Has Been Tried**

The pathos of the question lies in what precedes it. By the time anyone asks, the medicine, the money, the physicians, and the prayers of the household have all been exhausted. The Qur'an is precise elsewhere about who actually rescues people from the edge: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Who rescues you from the darkest times on land and at sea? He ˹alone˺ you call upon with humility, openly and secretly: “If You rescue us from this, we will be ever grateful.”’”* (6:63). The verse identifies the Rescuer, and it also identifies the pattern — a vow made at the edge and forgotten once the shore is reached. The question in this sūrah is asked at the one edge from which no one is brought back. What distinguishes that edge is not the failure of medicine but the arrival of the agents who take the soul: *“When death comes to any of you, Our angels take their soul, never neglecting this duty. Then they are ˹all˺ returned to Allah—their True Master.”* (6:61–62). Every death is so described as a handing over, and the question in the room is being asked of the wrong party.

**Where the Help Actually Was**

The question is asked at the point where it can no longer be answered, and the Qur'an's purpose in recording it is to relocate the asking. That is the plain sense of the sūrah's warning, and it is why the Qur'an keeps returning to the same instruction while breath remains: *“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53). The verse of Yā Sīn's successors puts the same point as an inventory of what will not help on the day it matters: *“the Day when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit, only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺.”* (26:88–89).

## Verse 75:28

> And the dying person realizes it is ˹their˺ time to depart

**What He Is Certain About**

The object of the certainty is *al-firāq*, the parting — the separation of what has been joined. The commentators took this in two directions, and both are true at once. The dying person knows that he is parting from the world: from the property he assembled, the people he loved, the body he lived in, and the version of himself that existed only among others. He also knows that the parting is the separation of soul from body, which the previous verse located at the collarbones. What makes the parting bitter is also stated plainly: the things being left are the things that were made to look desirable — *“The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people.”* (3:14).

**The Will and the Intellect Finally Agree**

The sūrah traced unbelief in v. 5 to what a person wants, and it has spent twenty-three verses describing the consequences of that wanting. At the deathbed, wanting and knowing coincide for the first time — the man no longer wants to deny, and he no longer can. That convergence is worth noticing, because it shows that the sūrah was never describing an intellectual failure that more evidence could have cured. Evidence arrives here in overwhelming quantity, and what it produces is regret rather than conversion. Faith, in the Qur'an's account, is a thing a person does with the interval he has been given, not a conclusion he reaches when the interval closes. The request made at that moment is always for a little more of it: *“My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while, I would give in charity and be one of the righteous.”* (63:10), and the answer is that no soul is delayed once its appointed time arrives (63:11 — *“But Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do”*). The man in this verse is not asking for more time because he has ceased to believe. He is asking because he believes perfectly well, and has discovered that belief without a remaining interval is not worth having.

**The Same Certainty, the Other Face**

Certainty at death is not always a terror, and the Qur'an gives the counterpart so that the reader does not take this verse as a statement about dying as such. To the tranquil soul the address runs: *“Return to your Lord, well pleased ˹with Him˺ and well pleasing ˹to Him˺.”* (89:28). And of those whom the angels take in a good state: *“Peace be upon you! Enter Paradise for what you used to do.”* (16:32). The same *yaqīn*, the same parting, the same threshold — and the content of the certainty is what a life has made it. The sūrah's next move is to follow the body from the bed to the grave and then to the driving, and it does so without pausing for comment.

## Verse 75:29

> and ˹then˺ their feet are tied together ˹in a shroud˺

**Weighing Them**

The shroud reading should be preferred, and the reason is contextual rather than lexical. Every verse from 26 onward has been tracking the body: collarbones, the question at the bedside, the certainty, and then, three verses later, the driving to the Lord. A description of the corpse being wrapped sits naturally in that series, while a moral abstraction about two hardships arriving together does not. The objection to discarding the second reading completely is that the Qur'an itself uses *sāq* in the sense of a grave and unavoidable matter: *“˹Beware of˺ the Day the Shin ˹of Allah˺ will be bared. The wicked will be asked to prostrate, but they will not be able to do so,”* (68:42). The lexicographers connect that usage to the image of a man rolling up his garment to his shank for a task that has become serious, which is the same phrase the commentators invoked here. That said, the two are not exclusive in practice. The Qur'an frequently uses a physical detail that carries a moral charge, and the binding of a dead man's legs in a shroud is not an image anyone finds neutral. The body is being prepared for the grave at the very moment the sūrah is insisting that the grave is not the end.

**The Body Is Not Discarded**

This is the point the sūrah's argument has been building since v. Resurrection is not the replacement of a body but the reassembly of one. The sūrah said as much at the outset, when it met the objection about crumbled bones with the claim that even the fingertips can be restored. The Qur'an shows the same principle enacted for a man who doubted it: *“And look at the bones ˹of the donkey˺, how We bring them together then clothe them with flesh!”* (2:259).

**Burial, and What Follows Immediately**

The sequence also compresses time in the way the Qur'an does elsewhere. Between the shroud and the driving there is, in ordinary experience, an interval that may last centuries; in the sūrah's account the two are joined by *on that Day*, and the barrier that is set behind the dead until the resurrection (23:100 — *“so I may do good in what I left behind.” Never! It is only a ˹useless˺ appeal they make. And there is a barrier behind them until the Day they are resurrected”*) is not an interval the dead experience. The grammar is doing theology here. A person dies, is wrapped, and is driven to his Lord, and nothing in the sūrah's telling allows the reader to insert a comfortable delay between the grave and the reckoning.

## Verse 75:30

> On that day they will be driven to your Lord ˹alone˺

**The Same Verb for Both Destinies**

What makes the word interesting is that the Qur'an uses it for the saved as well. The disbelievers are *“driven to Hell in ˹successive˺ groups”* (39:71), and the mindful are described in the parallel verse with the same verb: *“And those who were mindful of their Lord will be led to Paradise in ˹successive˺ groups.”* (39:73). The verb is identical in both. It describes the conduct of the soul to its destination and says nothing about which destination that is, which is exactly the point of using it here. The sūrah is not yet saying where this person is being taken. It is saying that he is being taken, and that the taking is not his to arrange. The verb also carries a claim about deserts. The groups in those two passages are not processed anonymously: the disbelievers arrive to opened gates and a question about whether messengers had come to them (39:71 — *“Those who disbelieved will be driven to Hell in ˹successive˺ groups.”*), while the mindful arrive to gates already open and a greeting of peace (39:73 — *“And those who were mindful of their Lord will be led to Paradise in ˹successive˺ groups.”*). The driving is the same; what differs is what the person has made of the interval before it, and the sūrah has spent thirty verses describing how that interval is spent.

**On That Day: the Compression Continues**

The phrase *on that day* refers most immediately to the day of the death just described, and it is the same phrase the sūrah used in v. 12 for the Day of Resurrection. The double reference is not carelessness. The Qur'an states plainly that a barrier is set behind the dead until they are raised (23:100 — *“so I may do good in what I left behind.” Never! It is only a ˹useless˺ appeal they make. And there is a barrier behind them until the Day they are resurrected”*), so the interval between a person's death and the general resurrection is real; but it is not an interval the dead person experiences, and the sūrah's grammar reflects that. For the dying man, the shroud and the arrival are separated by nothing at all. The driving also has agents, and the Qur'an names them: *“Each soul will come forth with an angel to drive it and another to testify.”* (50:21). One conducts and one bears witness, which means the journey described in this verse is neither solitary nor unrecorded. The person being driven is accompanied by the record of what he did, and the two angels who were assigned to him in life are the two who present him at the end. Nothing in the scene is improvised, and nothing about it is being decided on the way.

## Verse 75:31

> This denier neither believed nor prayed

**A Second Reading of the First Verb**

Some commentators took the first verb in its other sense and read the verse as *he did not give in charity*, since the root that means to affirm the truth also means to give alms — the connection being that almsgiving is the verification of faith. The grounds for this reading are strong. The Qur'an uses the root in both senses, and the parallel passage in Sūrah al-Muddaththir names exactly these two failures as the self-description of the people in the Fire: *“They will reply, ‘We were not of those who prayed, nor did we feed the poor.’”* (74:43–44). On that reading the verse pairs worship of God with care for His creatures, which is the pairing the Qur'an uses constantly. The reading is coherent and well supported; the majority held the first sense on the ground that the sūrah's context is denial rather than stinginess, and that is the stronger reading here, though the two converge in practice, since a person who affirms the truth gives. The convergence is visible in the Qur'an's own definitions. Righteousness is described as believing, *“and give charity out of ˹their˺ cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives; who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make”* (2:177). Belief, worship, and giving appear in one sentence because the Qur'an does not treat them as separate departments.

**The Confession of the Inhabitants of the Fire**

The parallel in al-Muddaththir is worth following, because it supplies the full catalogue that this verse abbreviates. The inhabitants of the Fire are asked what put them there, and they answer with four items: *“We were not of those who prayed, nor did we feed the poor. We used to indulge ˹in falsehood˺ along with others, and deny the Day of Judgment, until the inevitable came to us.”* (74:43–47). Neglect of worship, neglect of the poor, absorption in the talk of the crowd, and denial of the Day — and then the arrival of the certainty that ends the argument. The sūrah's two failures are the first two items in that list, and the order is the same in both passages.

**Why Denial Takes This Shape**

It is worth asking why disbelief should be described through worship and charity rather than through doctrine. The answer is that the Qur'an does not treat the Day of Judgment as a hypothesis to be evaluated. It treats it as a claim that, if true, reorganises a life: a person who believes he will be asked about what he owns gives, and a person who believes he will stand before his Lord prays. Where those two are absent, the belief is not present either, whatever the mouth says. The opposite profile is drawn just as concretely, and it is drawn in what a person is not distracted from: *“by men who are not distracted—either by buying or selling—from Allah’s remembrance, or performing prayer, or paying alms-tax.”* (24:37).

**A Profile to Be Checked, Not a Curse**

The verse is addressed to the living, and its purpose is diagnostic rather than merely condemnatory. Nothing in the wording suggests that these failures are beyond repair before death; the sūrah has said the opposite, holding out forgiveness while breath remains (39:53 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet, that Allah says,˺ “O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins. He is indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”*).

## Verse 75:32

> but persisted in denial and turned away

**Takdhīb: Declaring It False**

The first verb is from the root that means to lie and, in this form, to declare something a lie. It is stronger than disbelief and different in kind. A person who does not believe may simply have never considered the claim; a person who declares it false has considered it and passed judgement. That is the significance of the root's constant appearance in the Qur'an's accounts of the messengers' audiences: what was rejected was not an unexamined proposition but a showed one, and the rejection took the form of a counter-claim. *“Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they say, ‘Ancient fables!’”* (83:13). Calling the message old stories is a verdict on it, not an absence of information about it. The Qur'an goes further and states that the verdict can be reached against the person's own conviction: *“And, although their hearts were convinced the signs were true, they still denied them wrongfully and arrogantly.”* (27:14). Denial here is not the absence of belief but its suppression, and the motive named is arrogance rather than doubt. That single verse settles the sūrah's underlying claim: what the denier lacks is not evidence but the willingness to be the kind of person the evidence would make him.

**Tawallā: the Turn of the Back**

The second verb is physical. *Tawallā* means to turn away, to give something one's back, and the Qur'an uses it wherever it wants to describe a deliberate withdrawal rather than a gradual drift. The pairing of the two verbs is fixed in the Qur'an's vocabulary — *“who deny and turn away”* (92:16) — and the order is always the same: a verdict is formed, and then the person turns his back on what he has judged. The image is of a man who has decided and now physically removes himself from the place where the thing is being said. The decision comes first and the departure follows, which is why the Qur'an treats rejection as an act of the will rather than a failure of the evidence. The same point is made to the Prophet in a verse of consolation, and it identifies what was actually being rejected: *“It is not your honesty they question—it is Allah’s signs that the wrongdoers deny.”* (6:33). The objection was never to the messenger's character, which was not in dispute; it was to the claim being made on their lives. Turning away is the physical form of that refusal.

**Why Denial Is Not Ignorance**

The sūrah's whole account rests on this distinction, and it is worth stating plainly because the alternative reading is common. The Qur'an describes the hardening that produces such verdicts as something a person does to himself over time — *“their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit!”* (83:14) — and the staining is the accumulated effect of repeated refusals.

**What Turning Away Looks Like Now**

The description has a contemporary shape that the Qur'an itself supplies elsewhere. A person can be told the same truth repeatedly and move further from it each time: *“We have surely varied ˹the signs˺ in this Quran so perhaps they may be mindful, but it only drives them farther away.”* (17:41). The remedy is not to vary the message more cleverly, since the variation was already there. The obstruction is in the will of the hearer. The sūrah's answer to it is the one it has been giving since the first verse: the Day is coming whether or not he turns his back on it, and turning away does not alter what waits at the end of the road.

## Verse 75:33

> then went to their own people, walking boastfully

**Yatamaṭṭā: the Word for the Walk**

The verb rendered as walking boastfully is *yatamaṭṭā*, built on a root meaning to stretch and draw oneself out, and the classical lexicons gloss it as a swaggering, strutting walk in which a man lengthens his step and stretches his sides. Some commentators took it in the related sense of lounging at ease in luxury, since the same root is used for stretching oneself out in comfort. The first reading has the context on its side: the sūrah is describing a man going out among his people, and the Qur'an's habit is to show a moral condition in a bodily posture. Pride, on this reading, is not described as an opinion the man holds but as the way he walks, which is far more cutting. The Qur'an prescribes the opposite gait in several places, which confirms that the walk is being read as a moral statement: *“And do not turn your nose up to people, nor walk pridefully upon the earth. Surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful.”* (31:18). The instruction is not about etiquette. It is about the body being made to say what the heart has decided, and the prescription is a shortening of the step: *“Be moderate in your pace. And lower your voice, for the ugliest of all voices is certainly the braying of donkeys.”* (31:19).

**Why the Household Matters**

The choice of *ahl* — his own people, his household, his circle — identifies where the performance is aimed. The Qur'an treats this as one of the engines of unbelief, and it describes the same mechanism from the other side: *“When they meet the believers they say, ‘We believe.’ But when alone with their evil associates they say, ‘We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.’”* (2:14).

**The Parallel in Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn**

The same figure appears in a later Makkan sūrah, drawn in more detail and with the same destination. *“wink to one another whenever they passed by, and muse ˹over these exploits˺ upon returning to their own people. And when they saw the faithful, they would say, ‘These ˹people˺ are truly astray,’”* (83:30–32). The three movements are identical: the mockery performed in passing, the return home to laugh about it, and the verdict pronounced on the believers. The repetition across two sūrahs suggests that the Qur'an regarded this as a recurring and identifiable social type rather than as a single man's behaviour. The denier is sustained by a circle, and the circle is sustained by mockery. The reason given elsewhere for the arrogance is physical and it is worth quoting, since it is an argument rather than a rebuke: *“And do not walk on the earth arrogantly. Surely you can neither crack the earth nor stretch to the height of the mountains.”* (17:37). A creature of that size, with that reach, has no material for a swagger. The strut is a claim about importance made by a body that cannot support it.

A further verse supplies the interior state that produces the gait, and it is worth quoting because it names the link the sūrah is relying on: *“For they used to be prideful among their people, thinking they would never return ˹to Allah˺.”* (84:13–14).

The Qur'an's counter-portrait supplies the alternative in the same register: *“The ˹true˺ servants of the Most Compassionate are those who walk on the earth humbly, and when the foolish address them ˹improperly˺, they only respond with peace.”* (25:63).

## Verse 75:34

> Woe to you, and more woe

**Why the Threat Is Not Filled In**

No punishment is described. There is no fire here, no chain, no duration, no description of what the person will undergo, and the absence is deliberate. A specified punishment can be measured against a person's estimate of his own deserts and found excessive; an unspecified one cannot be argued with. The Qur'an uses the same economy elsewhere, and its threats are frequently at their heaviest when they are at their vaguest. A man told that woe is nearer to him than his own next step is left to supply the content, and what he supplies is shaped by everything he knows about what he has done. The Qur'an uses the explicit word for woe elsewhere, and the objects of it are instructive: *“Woe on that Day to the deniers—those who deny Judgment Day!”* (83:10–11). The woe attaches to the denial of the Day rather than to a list of particular sins, which is the same emphasis the sūrah has maintained since its opening oath. The man being threatened here is not a monster. He is someone who declined to believe that this Day would arrive.

**The Answer to the Swagger**

The placement of the threat is exact, and the Qur'an's sense of fitness here should not be missed. The man in v. 33 drew himself up, lengthened his stride, and went home to be admired. The reply tells him that something is nearer to him than his own stride and that it is coming for him. Pride is met with nearness, because the one thing arrogance cannot survive is the collapse of distance. A man can shrug off a punishment he regards as remote; he cannot shrug off one he is told is at the door. The nearness is not a rhetorical flourish invented for this verse. It is the Qur'an's standing description of the situation: *“˹The time of˺ people’s judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away.”* (21:1), and *“The Hour has drawn near and the moon was split ˹in two˺.”* (54:1). Every person alive is closer to his own reckoning than he was yesterday, which is what makes the phrase *nearer to you* literally rather than merely rhetorically true.

**A Threat Is Also an Address**

The hardest thing to hold on to in a verse like this is that it is spoken to someone still alive. A threat delivered to a man in this life is a warning, and a warning assumes that the thing warned against can be avoided. The Qur'an never threatens the already-damned; its threats are always served in advance, and the sūrah has already stated the alternative in the plainest terms available: *“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”* (39:53). The severity of the phrase is the measure of what is at stake, not of what has been decided.

## Verse 75:35

> Again, woe to you, and even more woe

**The Fitness of the Response**

The Qur'an's punishments are always described in terms that correspond to the fault, and the correspondence here is unusually tight. The man in v. 33 was swaggering on his way home to an audience that admired him. What is threatened to him now is the loss of every one of those supports: the woe at Badr removed his standing among his people, the grave removed his audience, and the Resurrection and the Fire removed his self-possession. The principle is stated elsewhere as a rule: *“Whoever does an evil deed will only be paid back with its equivalent.”* (40:40). Nobody is punished for something other than what he did, and the shape of the punishment is the shape of the act.

**How Many Woes Are Contained Here**

The phrase in the previous verse holds two occurrences of *awlā*, and this verse holds two more, which gives four across the two verses. Exegetes who counted them assigned the four to four distinct stages: the battlefield of Badr, the grave, the Resurrection, and the Fire. That assignment is a homiletical reading preserved in the tafsīr literature rather than a report with a chain, and it should be cited as such. What gives the reading its value is not the specific four but the principle underneath it: the punishment is not a single event but a condition that unfolds, and each stage is real rather than symbolic. The second possibility is simpler and should be stated alongside it: in Arabic rhetoric, repetition of this kind is a standard device of emphasis, and the doubled phrase may intend nothing more than to make the threat unmistakable.

**Why the Punishment Is Still Not Described**

The sūrah could easily have named the fire, the chain, or the boiling drink, all of which appear elsewhere in the Qur'an in far more detail. There is a second reason for it, which the Qur'an supplies elsewhere: the thing being threatened is not a penalty imposed from outside but the consequence of the man's own state. *“Undoubtedly, they will be sealed off from their Lord on that Day.”* (83:15) names the punishment as separation, and separation admits no description, because it is the absence of everything rather than the presence of something.

## Verse 75:36

> Do people think they will be left without purpose

**What the Word Means**

The term rendered as without purpose is *sudan*, and it belongs to a root used of a camel left untended, wandering without a herdsman and without a destination. The image is not of something deliberately destroyed but of something carelessly abandoned, and that is exactly the claim being examined. Would God make a creature of this complexity and then leave it to wander? The choice of word places the burden where the sūrah wants it. The alternative to purpose is not hostility but negligence, and a negligent creator is not a credible description of the One whose power the sūrah has spent forty verses describing. The word also carries a social sense in its root usage: a thing left *sudan* is a thing without an owner to answer for it. The claim being tested is therefore not that God is cruel but that He is indifferent, and that is the more dangerous idea, since a cruel god could at least be appeased. An indifferent one offers nothing to do.

**What Being Left Would Entail**

If a human being were truly abandoned in that sense, several things would follow, and all of them are false on the Qur'an's account. There would be no command and no prohibition, since a creature with no purpose cannot be directed. There would be no messengers, since there would be nothing to say. There would be no record, no reckoning, and no return, since an aimless creature cannot be called to account for a journey it was never sent on. The Qur'an poses the same absurdity in a direct question: *“Did you then think that We had created you without purpose, and that you would never be returned to Us?”* (23:115). The two halves are inseparable: purpose and return stand or fall together. The purpose is named elsewhere in a single verse, and it is not a vague invitation to be good: *“˹He is the One˺ Who created death and life to test which of you is best in deeds.”* (67:2). A test requires an end at which the result is read, which is why the same Qur'an that insists on purpose insists with equal force on the reckoning. The sūrah of the Spider puts the consequence of purpose in the form of a question every believer is asked: *“Do people think once they say, ‘We believe,’ that they will be left without being put to the test?”* (29:2).

**The Deniers Had Purposes of Their Own**

There is a subtlety in what is being denied. The people addressed here were not nihilists; they had projects, ambitions, trades, and family obligations, and they pursued them with real energy. What they denied was that any purpose had been *assigned* to them. The verse rejects that division. A creature made by a wise Maker has a purpose whether or not he acknowledges it, and the purpose is not up for negotiation: *“We only created them for a purpose, but most of these ˹pagans˺ do not know.”* (44:39). The ignorance is theirs, not the absence of the thing. The same movement appears in the Qur'an's account of what these people actually said: *“There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time.”* (45:24).

## Verse 75:37

> Were they not ˹once˺ a sperm-drop emitted

**The Vocabulary of the Drop**

Two words carry the verse. *Nuṭfa* means a small quantity of liquid, a drop, and the Qur'an uses it consistently for the beginning of a human being. The second word is a passive participle from a root meaning to pour or emit, and it locates the drop in the moment of its emission. The same vocabulary appears across the Qur'an in the passages describing human origins: from a drop when it is emitted (53:46 — *“from a sperm-drop when it is emitted”*), from a spurting fluid (86:6 — *“˹They were˺ created from a spurting fluid”*), from a drop of mixed fluids (76:2 — *“˹For˺ indeed, We ˹alone˺ created humans from a drop of mixed fluids, ˹in order˺ to test them, so We made them hear and see”*), and, in the most pointed of them all, *“He created humans from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Him˺.”* (16:4). That last verse is the argument of this sūrah in one line: the origin is admitted and the conclusion is refused, and the two cannot both stand.

**The Stages Are the Point, Not the Embryology**

The Qur'an's interest in origins is not scientific curiosity, and its descriptions are given as signs rather than as data. The stages are listed in several sūrahs with slight variations — a drop, then a clinging clot, then a lump, then bones, then flesh, then a new creation (23:14 — *“then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation.”*) — and the purpose of the listing is stated in the passage itself: *“to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you.”* (22:5). The same point is made about the signs generally: *“We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth.”* (41:53). The sūrah is using the origin as evidence of an Agent who acts with intention over time, and the staged character of the process is what makes the intention visible. That intention is spelled out in the account of the same stages in Sūrah al-Sajdah, where the sequence ends with the faculties that make a moral life possible: *“then He fashioned them and had a spirit of His Own ˹creation˺ breathed into them. And He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect.”* (32:9). Hearing to receive the message, sight to read the signs, and intellect to judge between them — a creature equipped in that way has not been left wandering.

**From This Beginning to the Ending in Question**

The argument is still building, and its shape should be visible by now. If a human being comes into existence through a sequence of transformations directed by Someone, then the sequence has not ended with his birth, and the One who directed it has not lost interest in the result. The Qur'an states the principle as a comparison of difficulty: *“And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him.”* (30:27). The Qur'an presses the same comparison from the other direction as well, using the creation of the whole universe as the measure: *“The creation of the heavens and the earth is certainly greater than the recreation of humankind. Most people do not know.”* (40:57), and *“Do they not realize that Allah, Who created the heavens and the earth and did not tire in creating them, is able to give life to the dead?”* (46:33).

Asked who made them, they answered *God*: *“If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created them, they will certainly say, ‘Allah!’”* (43:87), and the same admission is recorded in 29:61 — *“If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon ˹for your benefit˺, they will certainly say, “Allah!””*, 31:25 — *“And if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will definitely say, “Allah!” Say, “Praise be to Allah!” In fact, most of them do not know”* and 39:38 — *“If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created the heavens and the earth, they will certainly say, “Allah!””*.

The Qur'an makes the connection explicit in more than one place: *“Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Us˺?”* (36:77).

## Verse 75:38

> Then they became a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then He developed and perfected their form

**ʿAlaqa: a Word With a Wide Range**

The second stage is named with a word whose meanings do not fit neatly into one English term. *ʿAlaqa* belongs to a root meaning to cling, to hang, to be suspended, and the classical lexicons give it three senses that all bear on this verse: the thick, clotted blood that adheres; the thing that clings to the wall of the womb; and the leech, an animal named for its clinging. The word therefore describes at once the substance, the behaviour, and an object the Arabs knew well. No exegete of the classical period read it as anything other than the early stage of the embryo, and the range of the word is part of the Qur'an's economy: one noun carries a physical description and a functional one.

**The Full Sequence, Given Elsewhere**

This verse abbreviates a process that other sūrahs set out at greater length, and the fuller accounts are worth placing beside it. *“then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation.”* (23:14). Sūrah al-Ḥajj adds the purpose explicitly — *“to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you”* (22:5) — and Sūrah Ghāfir repeats the same sequence with the same conclusion (40:67 — *“He is the One Who created you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then He brings you forth as infants, so that you may reach your prime, and become old—though some of you ˹may˺ die sooner—reaching an appointed time, so perhaps you may understand ˹Allah’s power˺”*). The variations between the lists are not inconsistencies; each passage selects the stages that serve the point it is making, and the sūrah of the Prostration adds the element this sūrah leaves implicit: that the forming is done well by design. *“Who has perfected everything He created. And He originated the creation of humankind from clay.”* (32:7). Perfection here is not an aesthetic judgement but a functional one — each thing fitted to what it is for — and a creature fitted in that way has been fitted for something. The sūrah is about to say that he has not been left without purpose.

## Verse 75:39

> producing from it both sexes, male and female

**A Third Verb, a Third Act**

The sequence has moved from drop to clot to proportioned form, and now a further verb introduces the last stage named in this sūrah: *fa-jaʿala*, and He made. The change of verb is worth noticing, since the sūrah has already used *khalaqa* for bringing into being and *sawwā* for proportioning. *Jaʿala* is the verb of assigning, of making one thing into another, of instituting a function. The two sexes are made *from him* — from that single human source — which is the Qur'an's consistent account of the origin of the pair (4:1 — *“And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties.”*) and the point on which this verse turns.

**The Pairing Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The same point is made repeatedly and in several registers. *“And He created the pairs—males and females—from a sperm-drop when it is emitted.”* (53:45–46). The pairing is extended to the whole created order: *“And We created pairs of all things so perhaps you would be mindful.”* (51:49). It is stated as a sign accompanied by affection rather than only by function: *“And one of His signs is that He created for you spouses from among yourselves so that you may find comfort in them. And He has placed between you compassion and mercy.”* (30:21). And it is given as the origin of human diversity: *“Indeed, We created you from a male and a female, and made you into peoples and tribes so that you may ˹get to˺ know one another.”* (49:13).

**Reproduction as a Standing Sign**

The argumentative value of reproduction is that it puts the proof in front of everyone. A person does not have to accept a report about the beginning of the human race, because the beginning of every human being is observable. *“One of His signs is that He created you from dust, then—behold!—you are human beings spreading over ˹the earth˺.”* (30:20), and: *“And He is the One Who originated you all from a single soul, then assigned you a place to live and another to ˹be laid to˺ rest.”* (6:98). The Qur'an's constant question in these passages is the one it asks in Sūrah al-Naḥl: given that you concede the origin, why do you then argue with the One who originated you (16:4 — *“He created humans from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Him˺”*)?

**The Multiplication Is Not the Difficulty**

One of the underlying assumptions of the objector in v. 3 was that the sheer number of the dead makes resurrection implausible — billions of bodies, dispersed over the whole earth and throughout all of history. The production of the two sexes answers that assumption rather than merely repeating an answer to it. Human multiplication is itself part of the divine arrangement described in this verse, and the God who instituted a mechanism for filling the earth with people is not going to be defeated by the result of His own institution. The Qur'an states the principle in a single line: *“The creation and resurrection of you ˹all˺ is as simple ˹for Him˺ as that of a single soul.”* (31:28). Multiplicity is what makes a task hard for a creature, since a creature must deal with things one at a time. It is not what makes anything hard for a Creator who sustains every particle at once. The sūrah of Yā Sīn makes the same point by asking about the origin of the whole order: *“Can the One Who created the heavens and the earth not ˹easily˺ resurrect these ˹deniers˺?” Yes ˹He can˺!* (36:81 — *“Can the One Who created the heavens and the earth not ˹easily˺ resurrect these ˹deniers˺?” Yes ˹He can˺! For He is the Master Creator, All-Knowing”*).

## Verse 75:40

> Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life

**A Question That Supplies Its Own Answer**

The last verse is a question, and it is the kind of question that supplies its own answer. *A-laysa dhālika bi-qādirin* — is not that One able to give life to the dead? — expects *yes, He is*, and the expectation is part of the grammar rather than an addition to it. The sūrah that opened with an oath by the Day of Resurrection closes by asking whether the Creator can raise the dead, and it leaves the hearer to say the word.

**How the Logic Runs**

The argument constructed over the last five verses is a single syllogism, and it rests completely on premises the audience granted. The One who made a human being from an emitted drop, took him through the stage of the clinging clot, proportioned him, and brought forth from him the two sexes from whom the earth was populated — is that One unable to bring the dead back? The Qur'an states the comparison of difficulty in the same terms elsewhere: *“And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him.”* (30:27). Bringing a person into being from nothing is the greater act; reconstituting him from what remains is the lesser, and the sūrah's closing question is unanswerable on any other premise. The Qur'an adds the guarantee in the plainest terms elsewhere: *“Just as We produced the first creation, ˹so˺ shall We reproduce it. That is a promise binding on Us.”* (21:104). A promise binding on God is not a hope, and the sūrah has built its entire case on a premise the objectors never disputed — that the God who made them is the God who made everything.
