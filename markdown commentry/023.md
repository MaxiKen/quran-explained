# Sūrah al-Muʾminūn (Chapter 23) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Blueprint of Spiritual Prosperity and the Throne of Grace**

Al-Muʾminūn, "The Believers," is the twenty-third chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 118 verses of mid-to-late Makkan revelation. It derives its title from its majestic opening proclamation: *“Certainly, the believers have succeeded (*qad aflaḥa al-mu'minūn*)”* (23:1). In prophetic tradition, the opening ten verses of this sūrah are celebrated as the definitive portrait of spiritual felicity: ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb reported in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī (3173) that when revelation descended upon the Prophet ﷺ, a sound like the buzzing of bees was heard near his face. After a moment of profound ecstasy, he turned to the Companions and recited these opening verses, stating: *“Ten verses have been revealed to me; whoever establishes them will enter Paradise.”* Coming at a time when the early Muslims in Makkah were socially ostracized, economically marginalized, and physically threatened, Sūrah al-Muʾminūn performs a radical transvaluation of values: it declares that true success (*falāḥ*) is not measured by pagan wealth, tribal lineage, or political dominion, but by the cultivation of inward humility in prayer, moral purity, sexual integrity, fidelity to trusts, and steadfastness upon truth.

**The Structural Anatomy of the Sūrah**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Muʾminūn is organized around four clear thematic movements. The opening movement (1–22) establishes the six ethical pillars of the true believer: inward humility and awe in prayer (*khushūʿ*), turning away from futile and vain speech (*laghw*), active purification through zakat and charity, guarding sexual modesty except within lawful marital bonds, honoring trusts and covenants, and punctually guarding the regular prayers, promising that such souls shall inherit *al-Firdaws* (the highest terrace of Paradise). This ethical code is immediately grounded in the biological miracle of human embryogenesis: the clay extraction, the drop of semen (*nuṭfah*), the clinging clot (*ʿalaqah*), the lump of flesh (*muḍghah*), the clothing of bones with flesh. The miraculous fashioning into another creation, concluding: *“So blessed is Allah, the best of creators”* (23:14), followed by the cosmic signs of the seven layered heavens, life-giving rain, and the blessed olive tree that springs from Mount Sinai producing oil and seasoning for the eaters. The second movement (23–50) reviews sacred history through the lens of moral confrontation: Noah building the Ark under the mocking eyes of the chieftains who branded him an ambitious social climber; the successive generation destroyed by the screaming blast (*al-ṣayḥah*); Moses and Aaron confronting Pharaoh's arrogant refusal; and the universal dietary and moral command given to all messengers: *“O messengers, eat from the good things and work righteousness”* (23:51). The third movement (51–92) exposes the splintering of humanity into sectarian factions, each rejoicing in what it has, contrasts the terrified pleas of the dying sinner against the serenity of the mindful, and stages the dramatic reality of the *Barzakh*—the impenetrable spiritual barrier behind deceased souls preventing them from returning to earth to rectify their wasted lives (23:99–100). The closing movement (93–118) provides the Prophet with defensive prayers against demonic whispers (*“My Lord, I seek refuge in You from the incitements of the devils,”* 23:97), portrays the horror of the deniers whose faces are scorched in Hell while the believers are rewarded for their patient endurance. Asks humanity the penetrating existential question of verse 115: *“Did you then think that We created you without purpose, and that you would not be returned to Us?”*, sealing the chapter with the glorious prayer: *“My Lord, forgive and have mercy, and You are the best of the merciful.”*

Sūrah al-Muʾminūn addresses this psychological confrontation directly: it warns the wealthy deniers not to imagine that their abundant wealth and children are a sign of divine favor or a hastening of good (*“Do they think that what We extend to them of wealth and children is because We hasten for them good things? Nay, they perceive not!”*, 23:55–56).

## Verse 23:1

> Successful indeed are the believers

**A Definite Group, Described Immediately After**

The specification follows at once, and that is what keeps the opening from turning into a slogan. Faith is not left as an interior claim that nobody can check. It is given eight outward descriptions — humility in prayer, silence about what does not matter, alms, guarded desire, kept trusts, guarded prayer — and a reader can ask which of them he recognises in himself. The same construction appears twice more, both times with the identical verdict word: *"Successful indeed are those who purify themselves,"* (87:14) and *"Successful indeed is the one who purifies their soul,"* (91:9). Each time the word is followed by the work.

**A Word Taken From the Plough**

The noun behind the verdict, *falāḥ*, does not begin life as an abstraction. Its root f-l-ḥ carries the plain physical sense of cleaving or splitting, and specifically of cutting open soil; from the same root comes *falāḥah*, the work of tilling land. The classical lexicons record both senses side by side, and the second follows from the first without strain: the farmer who opens the ground is labouring toward a yield that has not yet appeared.

That is why *falāḥ* is a stronger word than the English "success" it usually receives. English success can describe a lucky outcome. *Falāḥ* describes the condition of a thing that has been sown, cultivated, and brought to its harvest — growth that has finished its work.

**Success Declared Rather Than Claimed**

Across the Book, *falāḥ* is assigned by God instead of achieved by self-assessment. A community is told to raise up a group that calls to good and forbids evil, and the sentence closes: *"it is they who will be successful"* (3:104). The opening of the Cow describes the guided and lands on the same phrase: *"It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful."* (2:5). In both places the verdict belongs to God, and in both it settles on a described group rather than on a hope.

## Verse 23:2

> those who humble themselves in prayer

**The First Mark Is an Inner State**

The list begins where a person cannot be seen. Not attendance at prayer — the sūrah demands that separately, in its final item — but the condition of the one who is praying. The word is *khāshiʿūn*, and the quality it names is neither effort, nor length of standing, nor fluency in recitation. It is the collapse of the self's claim to importance while standing before God.

Placing this first is a judgement about what prayer actually is. A prayer correct in its movements and empty in the person performing it does not satisfy the description, and the sūrah knows the outward form is the easier half. The same emphasis runs through the Cow, where prayer is called a heavy thing *"except for the humble"* (2:45). The difficulty is not the rite. It is being small enough in one's own eyes to perform the rite as an address.

**A Root About Lowered Eyes**

The root kh-sh-ʿ describes physical lowering. Where the Qur'an applies its derivatives to the deniers on the Day of Judgment, the image is always the eyes: *"With eyes downcast, they will come forth from the graves as if they were swarming locusts,"* (54:7), and *"with eyes downcast, utterly covered with disgrace. That is the Day they have ˹always˺ been warned of."* (70:44). The root also carries the sense of something withered or subdued — land dried out and sunk low.

**The Report About Eyes Lifted to the Sky**

Anas b. Mālik reports that the Prophet asked what was wrong with people who raise their eyes toward the sky during prayer, and that his tone hardened as he said it: they must stop, or their sight would be taken away. The report stands in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 750.

**Humility in Prayer Depends on the Life Outside It**

The spiritual tradition treats *khushūʿ* as something prepared before the prayer begins. Practice of the virtues in the hours between prayers supplies the material of the humility inside them, and the sūrah makes the link structural: the first mark is humility in prayer, and the six that follow are conduct in the world.

The prophets are described with the same word: *"Indeed, they used to race in doing good, and call upon Us with hope and fear, totally humbling themselves before Us."* (21:90). The order there is instructive. Good deeds and supplication come first, and humility is named as the manner of both.

**The Same Word on the Day of Judgment**

Derivatives of this root return at the end of the Book as the description of those who refused to lower themselves here: *"with eyes downcast, totally covered with disgrace. For they were ˹always˺ called to prostrate ˹in the world˺ when they were fully capable ˹but they chose not to˺."* (68:43). The connection is explicit inside the verse — they were called to prostrate and did not. The lowered gaze of the worshipper and the lowered gaze of the condemned share one vocabulary. One is a choice made in time; the other is the same posture, imposed.

## Verse 23:3

> those who avoid idle talk

**The Evidence in the Cups of Paradise**

One verse settles the range of the word by setting it beside a different one. Describing the drink of Paradise, the Book says those there pass it around, *"a drink ˹of pure wine,˺ which leads to no idle talk or sinfulness."* (52:23). *Laghw* and *ithm*, sin, are named as two things. If the first simply meant the second, the sentence would have repeated one idea through two unrelated nouns joined by "or", which Arabic does not do for emphasis.

The same verse supplies the positive contrast without stating it. What fills the cups of Paradise is conversation that means something, and the drink there produces no *laghw* because nothing in that company needs to be said for its own sake. The defect being removed is not intoxication but the speech intoxication produces.

**Turning Away Instead of Answering**

The verb is *muʿriḍūn*, turning aside. It does not describe someone who wins the argument, nor someone who corrects the speaker, nor someone who stays and disapproves in silence. It describes a person who leaves. What is praised is not superior argument but the refusal to enter.

The Book shows the action performed: *"When they hear slanderous talk, they turn away from it, saying, 'We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. Peace ˹is our only response˺ to you! We want nothing to do with those who act ignorantly.'"* (28:55). Every element of that reply is a refusal to continue.

**Passing By Without Stopping**

A second description places the same behaviour among the servants of the Merciful: *"˹They are˺ those who do not bear false witness, and when they come across falsehood, they pass ˹it˺ by with dignity."* (25:72). The verb differs and is slightly stronger. They do not merely turn away; they move past the thing while remaining upright, neither joining it nor stooping to fight it.

The dignity in that verse is what turns avoidance into a virtue rather than a temperament. Many people stay clear of pointless talk because they cannot handle it, or because contempt keeps them out. The believer described in 25:72 stays intact — his bearing does not change — and simply declines to participate. That is why the item belongs in a list of the marks of success. Avoidance that leaves a person bitter has gained nothing; avoidance that leaves him whole has gained the thing this sūrah wants.

**The Absence That Defines Paradise**

Paradise is described negatively with this same word. *"There they will never hear any idle talk—only ˹greetings of˺ peace."* (19:62), and again of the reward: *"never to hear any idle talk or lying in it—"* (78:35). The felicity of the Garden is defined in part by what will not be heard inside it, and what is removed is exactly what the believer has already stopped producing.

## Verse 23:4

> those who pay alms-tax

**The Eight Recipients Named in One Verse**

The Meccan generality of this verse was later given precision. *"Alms-tax is only for the poor and the needy, for those employed to administer it, for those whose hearts are attracted ˹to the faith˺, for ˹freeing˺ slaves, for those in debt, for Allah's cause, and for ˹needy˺ travellers. ˹This is˺ an obligation from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise."* (9:60). The list is exhaustive in form — "only for" — and it closes off the payer's discretion over destination as well as amount. Four of the eight categories are people in a temporary bind rather than permanent poverty: the indebted, the stranded traveller, the administrator, the one being freed. Zakāh in the Qur'an is so not merely relief for the destitute but a standing mechanism for returning people to independence.

**Zakāh Means Growth Before It Means Giving**

The term used here, *zakāh*, is built on a root whose primary sense is increase. To say that something *zakā* is to say that it grew, and that it grew clean — the two ideas are not separated in the root. Agricultural usage keeps this visible: the same family of words describes a crop that comes up well. When the Qur'an speaks of zakāh as something a person gives away, it is naming the part of wealth that must be removed for the remainder to increase.

That is not a figure of speech wrapped around a tax. It is a claim about how property behaves.

**The Same Root Inside the Same Verdict**

Two other places in the Book join this root to the identical verdict word that opened this sūrah. *"Successful indeed are those who purify themselves,"* (87:14) uses *tazakkā*, the same root in its reflexive form. *"Successful indeed is the one who purifies their soul,"* (91:9) uses *zakkāhā*. In all three sentences the formula is the same: *qad aflaḥa*, success has come, followed by a verbal noun from z-k-w.

**The Purification Runs Both Ways**

The direction of the benefit is easy to misread. Because alms reach the poor, it is natural to suppose that the recipient is the one being purified. The Qur'an says otherwise in a verse addressed to the Prophet about the collection itself: *"Take from their wealth ˹O Prophet˺ charity to purify and bless them, and pray for them— surely your prayer is a source of comfort for them."* (9:103). The object of the purification is the payer.

## Verse 23:5

> those who guard their chastity

**The Guard Begins at the Eyes**

The Qur'an sets out the order of this defence explicitly elsewhere, and the order runs from the gaze to the part: *"˹O Prophet!˺ Tell the believing men to lower their gaze and guard their chastity. That is purer for them. Surely Allah is All-Aware of what they do."* (24:30). Two commands, in that sequence, and the first is the precondition of the second.

**A Thing Guarded Is a Thing Under Attack**

The verb is *ḥāfiẓūn*, guarding, and the noun is *furūj*, the plural of *farj*, the private part. The pairing is worth holding on to. Nobody guards a thing that is safe by nature. Guarding implies a target exposed to pressure, an attack expected, and a defence maintained over time rather than settled once. The verse does not describe people who lack desire or who find temptation easy. It describes people who hold a position.

The word choice also refuses abstraction. English translations render the phrase as guarding "their chastity", which sounds like a quality. The Arabic names a part of the body.

**The Requirement Named for Both**

Nothing in this verse is limited to men, and the Book states the parallel requirement in terms that leave no room to argue. A long list of paired qualities ends with the same phrase applied to both sexes: *"and men and women who guard their chastity, and men and women who remember Allah often—for ˹all of˺ them Allah has prepared forgiveness and a great reward."* (33:35).

The grammatical form of 23:5 is masculine plural, which in Arabic includes both men and women unless something restricts it. The explicit pairing in al-Aḥzāb removes any doubt about the intention. It also removes a common evasion — the assumption that a text addressed in the masculine is legislating for men while leaving women's conduct to a separate, private code. Here the same duty, the same word, and the same reward are extended across the list, item by item, to both.

**The Same List in Sūrat al-Maʿārij**

Nearly the whole sequence reappears later in the Book, in the same order and often in the same words. *"and those who guard their chastity"* (70:29), followed by the identical exception and the identical phrase about transgression, followed by *"˹The faithful are˺ also those who are true to their trusts and covenants;"* (70:32) and *"and who are ˹properly˺ observant of their prayers."* (70:34). The portrait is fixed enough to be recognised as a portrait. Two sūrahs, separated in revelation, describe the believer with one set of features, and the stability of the description is the argument: this is not a mood of a particular moment in the Prophet's mission but a standing definition.

## Verse 23:6

> except with their wives or those ˹bondwomen˺ in their possession, for then they are free from blame

**The Exception Names Two Legal States, Not Two Moods**

The carve-out lists two categories and no more: wives, and those whom the right hand has. Both are defined by law rather than by feeling. A wife is a woman joined to a man by a contract with conditions — a dowry, witnesses, rights and duties that survive the marriage and bind his heirs. The second category is a status created by the law of war in the Arabia of the seventh century, and it too carried obligations: maintenance, protection, and the prohibition of coercion that another verse makes explicit.

Naming both, and only both, is what gives the prohibition its edge. The verse does not say "with those you love" or "with those who consent".

**What "Those in Their Possession" Referred To**

The second category needs stating plainly rather than glossing over. Captives taken in war entered the household as bondwomen, and the Qur'an legislated for that existing institution instead of pretending it away. The ruling in an-Nisāʾ shows how the category worked in practice: married women are forbidden *"except ˹female˺ captives in your possession"* (4:24), which means the law recognised the captivity and regulated its consequences rather than leaving them to the owner.

One of those regulations is recorded in a report about the captives of Awṭās. Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī relates the Prophet's instruction: do not approach a pregnant woman until she has given birth, nor a woman who is not pregnant until she has menstruated once. The report is in Sunan Abī Dāwūd, 2157, and al-Albānī graded it ṣaḥīḥ in Irwāʾ al-Ghalīl, 187. The rule establishes lineage and prevents a woman from being treated as available the moment she is captured — a limit imposed on the owner, in his own household, by a law he did not write.

**The Text's Direction Is Toward Freeing**

The institution described in this verse is set inside a Book that repeatedly pushes the other way. Emancipation is named as the steep path: *"It is to free a slave,"* (90:13). A master asked to release a bondsman is commanded to help it and to contribute from his own wealth: *"And if any of those ˹bondspeople˺ in your possession desires a deed of emancipation, make it possible for them, if you find goodness in them."* (24:33). One of the eight categories entitled to alms-tax is precisely those being freed from bondage (9:60).

**The Prophet's Own Case Was Treated Differently**

The sūrah of al-Aḥzāb lists the Prophet's permissions at length and then closes them with a sentence that shows how exceptions function in this legal system: *"˹this is˺ exclusively for you, not for the rest of the believers."* (33:50). A permission granted to one person by name is marked as his alone, and the general rule for everyone else is stated separately in the same verse — *"We know well what ˹rulings˺ We have ordained for the believers in relation to their wives and those ˹bondwomen˺ in their possession."* (33:50). The contrast makes the shape of 23:6 clearer. Where the Book intends a particular allowance, it says so and restricts it; where it states a rule for believers, it leaves the list closed.

## Verse 23:7

> but whoever seeks beyond that are the transgressors

**The Word Describes Crossing a Line**

*ʿĀdūn* comes from the root ʿ-d-w, whose basic sense is to pass over, to go beyond, to cross from one side of something to the other. A transgressor in this vocabulary is not mainly a sinner in the sense of one who has failed a standard of excellence. He is a man who was told where the boundary ran and stepped over it. The word contains its own geography: there is a line, and he is now on the wrong side of it.

**No Third Category Is Left**

The force of the sentence comes from what it excludes structurally. Verse five states the rule, verse six gives the exception, and verse seven declares everyone else outside. There is no intermediate status, no tolerated grey zone, no category of the regrettable but permitted.

**Where the Jurists Took This Verse**

These two verses became a standard proof-text in a dispute about temporary marriage. Jurists of the mainstream schools argued that a fixed-term contract does not make a woman a wife in the sense 23:6 requires, since the relation is not intended to be permanent and does not carry the full set of marital rights; on that reading, seeking such a relation is seeking "beyond that". Imāmī jurisprudence disagrees, holding that a fixed-term contract is a genuine marriage contract and reading the relevant permission in 4:24 as covering it.

The disagreement is worth reporting with its grounds rather than as a list of names. The first side is arguing from this verse and from the nature of the marital bond the Qur'an describes: a bond meant to be lasting, carrying inheritance, maintenance and lineage, none of which a contract that expires on a date can support. The second side answers that 4:24 speaks of seeking women with wealth in a legal marriage and that the earliest community practised fixed-term marriage before it was prohibited, so the question is what 23:6-7 actually excludes. What both sides accept is the premise this verse establishes: the permitted field is closed, and anything claimed inside it must be shown to belong to a named category.

## Verse 23:8

> ˹the believers are also˺ those who are true to their trusts and covenants

**The Same Pair in the Same Order Elsewhere**

The identical formulation appears in the sūrah of the Ascending Ways: *"˹The faithful are˺ also those who are true to their trusts and covenants;"* (70:32). Same two nouns, same order, same verb. Two sūrahs describe the believer with the same phrase, and the phrase is not a general commendation of honesty — it names the two specific categories of owed things.

The Qur'an sets out the weight of the word *amānah* in a verse that is deliberately strange: *"Indeed, We offered the trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, but they ˹all˺ declined to bear it, being fearful of it. But humanity assumed it, ˹for˺ they are truly wrongful ˹to themselves˺ and ignorant ˹of the consequences˺,"* (33:72).

**Trusts Are Owed to People, Not Only to God**

The legal material in the Book makes the horizontal application explicit. *"Indeed, Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners; and when you judge between people, judge with fairness. What a noble commandment from Allah to you!"* (4:58). The command to return what has been placed with you is issued alongside the command to judge honestly, and both are addressed to the dealings of people with each other.

The warning side is just as direct: *"O believers! Do not betray Allah and the Messenger, nor betray your trusts knowingly."* (8:27). The verse places betrayal of a trust beside betrayal of God and His Messenger in a single prohibition, which is not a rhetorical escalation but an equation of seriousness. A deposit kept back, a confidence sold, a duty quietly neglected — these are not small failures of character in the Qur'an's accounting. They are breaches of the same order as apostasy in kind, if not in degree.

The stability of the formulation is the point. Where the Qur'an repeats a description in the same words, it is marking that description as a fixed feature of the portrait rather than an incidental detail of one passage. Trusts and covenants belong to the definition of a believer in the same way that humility in prayer does, and a person who is careless about deposits and promises has failed one of the marks on the list, not merely one of the courtesies.

**The Question That Comes With the Pledge**

The Book attaches an audit to the second term. Of the orphan's property and of undertakings generally: *"And honour ˹your˺ pledges, for you will surely be accountable for them."* (17:34). Accountability is attached to the pledge itself, not only to its performance — a promise made lightly will be asked about as a promise made lightly.

## Verse 23:9

> and those who are ˹properly˺ observant of their prayers

**The List Opens and Closes With Prayer**

The second verse described believers as humble in their prayers; this one describes them as guarding them. Prayer is the frame of the whole list. Everything between — silence about what does not matter, alms, guarded desire, kept trusts — sits inside a structure whose first and last items are the same act of worship.

**Guarding Means Keeping the Appointed Times**

*Yuḥāfiẓūn*, from ḥ-f-ẓ, means to keep watch over a thing so that it is not lost. Applied to prayer, the commentators read it first of all as punctuality: each prayer performed inside its appointed window, not deferred until the next one absorbs it. The Book states the rule directly: *"When the prayers are over, remember Allah—whether you are standing, sitting, or lying down. But when you are secure, establish regular prayers. Indeed, performing prayers is a duty on the believers at the appointed times."* (4:103).

The emphasis on the time is not bureaucratic. A prayer made up afterwards is not the same act as a prayer made on time, because the time is part of what is being given. Anyone who has watched his own day will recognise the mechanism: a prayer pushed to its last moments is a prayer the day has already consumed, and the person who arrives at it arrives late in every sense. The command in the Cow is put in the same terms — *"Observe the ˹five obligatory˺ prayers—especially the middle prayer—and stand in true devotion to Allah."* (2:238) — with observation and standing in devotion joined.

**The Same Word in al-Maʿārij**

The closing item of this list reappears verbatim in the sūrah of the Ascending Ways: *"and who are ˹properly˺ observant of their prayers."* (70:34). The same word, the same position at the end of the sequence, the same bracketing of trusts and covenants before it.

**A Middle Prayer Left Unnamed**

The command in the Cow singles out one prayer for extra emphasis — *"especially the middle prayer"* (2:238) — and does not say which of the five it is. The commentators have argued for the dawn prayer, the afternoon prayer, and others, and no reading has settled the matter.

## Verse 23:10

> These are the ones who will be awarded

**A Pointer That Gathers Eight Descriptions**

*Ulāʾika hum* — these are the ones. The verse contains no new content at all. It is a demonstrative pointing back at the eight descriptions just completed, and it does the work of collection: eight separate qualities, each stated in its own verse, are now gathered into a single company and given a single outcome.

That collecting move matters more than it looks. A list of virtues can be read as a menu, from which a person takes the items he manages and leaves the rest. The demonstrative refuses that reading. It does not say "whoever does some of these"; it says *these* — the whole set, as one description of one kind of person. What is being described is a shape of life, and the reward attaches to the shape.

The demonstrative also does something to time. Eight verses have described behaviour spread across a lifetime — prayers at their hours, wealth given out year after year, trusts returned whenever they come due.

**Inheritance Is Not a Wage**

The Book uses both frames, and the difference between them is the whole difference between two ways of understanding the Hereafter. Elsewhere the same reward is described as earned: *"This is Paradise awarded to you for what you used to do."* (7:43). Here it is inherited.

**The Same Promise Given to the Righteous**

The language of inheritance is not local to this passage. A decree recorded in the Scriptures is quoted: *"Surely, following the ˹heavenly˺ Record, We decreed in the Scriptures: 'My righteous servants shall inherit the land.'"* (21:105). And the righteous themselves will say on the Day: *"Praise be to Allah Who has fulfilled His promise to us, and made us inherit the ˹everlasting˺ land to settle in Paradise wherever we please."* (39:74). The second of these is spoken from inside the reward, and it uses the same verb this verse uses. The promise made here is the promise those verses describe as already kept — which is what the perfect tense of an inheritance always implies: the estate has been settled, and the only question left is who turns up to take possession.

## Verse 23:11

> Paradise as their own. They will be there forever

**Firdaws: The Word Behind the English "Paradise"**

The Arabic word used here is *firdaws*, and it is not an ordinary word for garden. Arabic already has *jannah* for that, a term built on the idea of something covered and hidden, which is why it fits a garden dense with foliage. *Firdaws* is a borrowing, and its history is shared with the English word "paradise". Both descend from an Old Persian compound, *pairi-daēza*, meaning a walled enclosure — a park enclosed by a wall, which passed into Greek as *paradeisos* and into Arabic in this form. Lexicographers of Qur'anic vocabulary have long traced the word along that route, and the older Arabic authorities agreed that it means a garden while differing over what kind.

**The Highest Garden, Named in a Ḥadīth**

The Prophet gave the word a location in the geography of the Garden. He said that Paradise has a hundred grades prepared for those who strive in God's cause, that the distance between two grades is like the distance between heaven and earth. Then: when you ask God, ask Him for al-Firdaws, for it is the best and highest part of Paradise — the sub-narrator adding that he believed the Prophet also said that above it is the Throne of the Most Merciful, and from it the rivers of Paradise flow. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 2790.

**The Garden Promised to the Devout**

The same estate is promised elsewhere in the Book with the same economy, and the condition is stated in the same register of inner disposition: *"That is Paradise, which We will grant to whoever is devout among Our servants."* (19:63). Devotion there and humility in prayer here name the same orientation of a person, and both are followed by a list of outward conduct rather than a list of feelings.

## Verse 23:12

> And indeed, We created humankind from an extract of clay

**Clay, and What the Book Says About It**

Elsewhere the same origin is described with more specificity, and the variations are informative rather than contradictory. Human beings are made *"from sounding clay moulded from black mud"* (15:26), and *"from ˹sounding˺ clay like pottery,"* (55:14). The creation is *"originate[d]... From clay"* (32:7). Elsewhere it is simply clay, or dust, or a base fluid.

**Adam, or Every Human Being**

The commentators divide over the referent of "humankind" here. Some take it as Adam alone, the first man formed directly from clay, and read the following verses as a shift to his descendants. Others take it as the species, in which case the clay is the remote origin of every person. A third position, recorded by al-Rāzī, holds that the clay is a reference to Adam himself rather than to the material.

The general reading is the stronger, and the reason is structural. The verse that follows describes a drop placed in a secure lodging, which is not how Adam was made but how every human being since has been made. If verse twelve were only about Adam, the sequence from twelve to sixteen would switch subjects mid-sentence without warning.

**The Material Answer to an Old Objection**

The objection this origin invites is recorded in the Book in the mouth of Iblīs: *"Should I prostrate to the one You have created from mud?"* (17:61). The argument is not new to the Qur'an's Meccan audience; it is the argument of the first objector, and it takes the same form as the argument against resurrection — that a thing made from low material cannot be worth much, and certainly cannot be raised to anything.

The verse answers it by owning the premise. There is no try to elevate the material or to deny that it is mud. The human being is from an extract of clay, and the honour lies not in the clay but in what was done with it. That is the same answer the sūrah will give about resurrection: the material is not the issue, because the material never made anything by itself. Whoever drew the extract out of the earth and made it into a person who could deny Him can take the same substance back and make it again.

**Dust, Clay, Mud and Pottery**

The Book returns to this origin with a persistence that suggests the point is being pressed rather than illustrated. *"He is the One Who created you from clay, then appointed a term ˹for your death˺ and another known only to Him ˹for your resurrection˺ —yet you continue to doubt!"* (6:2), where the material and the two appointed times are joined in one sentence. And again in the address to the angels: *"I am going to create a human being from clay."* (38:71). The pairing in 6:2 is the one that matters here: origin, term, return, and then the accusation of doubt. That is exactly the four-part structure this sūrah is now building, verse by verse.

## Verse 23:13

> then placed each ˹human˺ as a sperm-drop in a secure place

**Nuṭfah: The Smallest Word for a Whole Person**

The word is *nuṭfah*, a drop — specifically a drop of seminal fluid. Arabic uses the term for any small quantity of liquid, and the Qur'an uses it repeatedly for the beginning of a human being. The choice is deliberate in its diminishment: the account of human origins does not begin with anything impressive, and the word chosen is the word for a quantity too small to notice.

The Book makes the diminishment explicit wherever it wants to argue from it. *"He created humans from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Him˺."* (16:4). And in the sūrah of the Rising: *"Were they not ˹once˺ a sperm-drop emitted?"* (75:37). Both verses set the smallness of the beginning beside the arrogance of the later creature, and both use the exclamation to mark the disproportion. The man arguing with his Maker is a man who began as a drop, and the argument he is making is about his own importance.

**A Fixed and Fortified Lodging**

The place is described with two words: *qarār*, a settled place of rest, and *makīn*, firmly established, from a root meaning to be firmly placed and to have standing. Together they describe more than a container. A *qarār* is where a thing comes to rest and stays; a *makīn* place is one that holds its position and cannot be easily disturbed.

That is an accurate description of the womb in its function. It is enclosed, protected, supplied, and fixed within a body that moves while the thing inside does not. The verse in the Pilgrimage uses the same idea with a different emphasis: *"˹Then˺ We settle whatever ˹embryo˺ We will in the womb for an appointed term, then bring you forth as infants, so that you may reach your prime."* (22:5). There the settling is joined to a term, and the emphasis falls on the fact that the secure place is secure for a measured period and no longer.

**Secure, But Not Permanent**

Elsewhere the Book spells out the term attached to the process: *"then He brings you forth as infants, so that you may reach your prime, and become old— though some of you ˹may˺ die sooner—reaching an appointed time, so perhaps you may understand ˹Allah's power˺."* (40:67). The security of the womb is the first of a series of provisions, each of which ends.

The phrase "so perhaps you may understand" at the end of that verse states the purpose of the account.

**The Same Sequence Repeated Seven Times**

This account is not a local detail. The stages from drop to birth appear in the Pilgrimage, in Fāṭir, in the Believer, in the Rising, in the Sent Forth, in ʿAbasa, and in al-Insān — seven places, with variations of emphasis but never a contradiction. The Sent Forth asks: *"Did We not create you from a humble fluid,"* (77:20), and ʿAbasa states: *"He created them from a sperm-drop, and ordained their development."* (80:19). A theme returned to that often, in that many different registers of argument, is not ornament. It is the Qur'an's standing evidence for the claim that the one who began this can begin again.

*"Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop, then— behold!—they openly challenge ˹Us˺?"* (36:77).

## Verse 23:14

> then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation. So Blessed is Allah, the Best of Creators

**ʿAlaqah: The Clinging Thing**

The second stage is *ʿalaqah*, from a root meaning to cling, to hang, to be suspended. The word is used for a leech and for a blood clot, and the two senses are connected by the idea of attachment. The verse in the Pilgrimage gives the fuller description: *"then ˹developed you˺ into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh —fully formed or unformed"* (22:5), where the addition "fully formed or unformed" acknowledges that this stage may or may not proceed.

That qualification is significant. The Qur'an does not describe an automatic process running to completion every time. It describes a sequence that can stop, and it says so in the one verse that sets the stages out for the express purpose of answering doubt. The Rising states the sequence more briefly and adds a making and a perfecting: *"Then they became a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then He developed and perfected their form,"* (75:38). Between the two accounts the reader has both the stages and the fact that they are governed.

**Bones, Then the Flesh That Covers Them**

The Believer gives the same order in a longer account: *"then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then He brings you forth as infants"* (40:67), compressing the middle stages but keeping the sequence intact. The clothing image is the important element here. A garment is put on something that already exists and has a shape; it is not generated from it. The verse is describing a construction in which the structure comes first and the visible body is fitted to it afterwards — which is exactly the order that makes the human form possible, and exactly the order no one arranges.

**"Another Creation": Three Readings Weighed**

The first reading is the strongest, for a reason internal to the verse. The five preceding stages are all transformations of matter, and the phrase "another creation" marks a break in kind rather than a further step in the same series; if it meant merely another stage of growth, the word *ākhar* would be doing nothing. The Book describes the inbreathing elsewhere in exactly these terms: *"then He fashioned them and had a spirit of His Own ˹creation˺ breathed into them. And He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect."* (32:9). The second and third readings are not excluded by this; birth and maturity are further instances of the same break. But the primary sense is the moment at which the thing in the womb stopped being tissue and became someone.

**The Same Stages Set Out for the Doubter**

The Pilgrimage states the purpose of the sequence openly: *"O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop..."* (22:5), and closes the argument with *"That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth, He ˹alone˺ gives life to the dead, and He ˹alone˺ is Most Capable of everything."* (22:6). The stages are evidence, not anatomy. This sūrah uses them the same way, and the reader who treats the passage as a description of embryology alone has missed the argument it was written to make.

## Verse 23:15

> After that you will surely die

**The Address Turns to You**

The three verses before this one spoke about "him" — the drop, the clot, the thing brought into being as another creation. This verse turns and speaks to the listener directly: you will die. The shift from third person to second is not a stylistic variation. It moves the account out of the category of information and into the category of address.

**The Flattest Sentence in the Passage**

Nothing is added. There is no comfort, no explanation of why, no reminder of what comes after — the next verse handles that, and this one is left bare. The sūrah could have written "you will die and then be raised" in a single breath, and instead it separates the two, letting the first stand alone for a moment.

The Book elsewhere refuses to soften the same statement. *"You ˹O Prophet˺ will certainly die, and they will die too."* (39:30), addressed to the one person whose death the hearers least wanted to face, and paired with theirs in the same sentence. *"Every soul will taste death. And We test you ˹O humanity˺ with good and evil as a trial, then to Us you will ˹all˺ be returned."* (21:35). The word "taste" is the notable one in that verse: death is not something that happens around a person but something he experiences, in the first person, once.

**Death Placed Between Making and Raising**

The position of this verse inside the sequence is the argument. Creation, death, resurrection — three stages, in that order, with nothing else inserted. The sūrah of the Heights puts the same three in God's mouth as a decree about the human being: *"In it you shall live, and in it you shall die, and thence shall you be brought forth."* (7:25).

**The Reward Held Back Until the Day**

The bareness of this verse is answered by a statement elsewhere about when the account is settled: *"Every soul will taste death. And you will only receive your full reward on the Day of Judgment. Whoever is spared from the Fire and is admitted into Paradise will ˹indeed˺ triumph, whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment."* (3:185). Death is not the payment. It is the closing of the period during which anything could still be changed.

---

## Verse 23:16

> then on the Day of Judgment you will be resurrected

**The Day Named for Standing**

*Yawm al-qiyāmah* — the Day of Rising, or of Standing. The noun comes from the same root as the verb in this verse, and the two are deliberately matched: the day takes its name from what happens on it. It is not called the Day of Judgement here, though judgement is what occurs; it is called the Day of Standing Up.

**The Claim the Denier Actually Makes**

The verse is short because the position it answers is short. The Book records the denial in its plainest form: *"They insisted, 'There is nothing beyond this worldly life and we will never be resurrected.'"* (6:29). Two claims, and both are answered by the sequence in verses twelve to sixteen without a word of argument.

**What Is Raised Is What Died**

The verse keeps the second person from the verse before it. The same "you" that dies is the "you" that is raised, and the sūrah does not introduce a new subject or a new body. That continuity is what makes the promise meaningful.

**The Return to the True Master**

Elsewhere the Book describes the same event as a return rather than a beginning: *"Then and there every soul will face ˹the consequences of˺ what it had done. They all will be returned to Allah—their True Master. And whatever ˹gods˺ they fabricated will fail them."* (10:30). The word for returned implies that the soul was somewhere before, and belonged somewhere.

## Verse 23:17

> And indeed, We created above you seven levels ˹of heaven˺. We are never unmindful of ˹Our˺ creation

**Ṭarāʾiq: Layers Laid One Over Another**

The word translated as levels is *ṭarāʾiq*, the plural of *ṭarīqah*. Most readers meet this word as meaning paths or ways, and that is one of its senses. But the same root carries the idea of laying one thing on top of another: the verb *ṭāraqa* means to superimpose, and a *ṭarīqah* is a layer, a stratum, a band laid over what is beneath it. The commentators who read the verse as describing the seven heavens take the word in this second sense.

That reading is supported by the way the Qur'an describes the heavens elsewhere. Nūḥ asks his people: *"Do you not see how Allah created seven heavens, one above the other,"* (71:15), using a phrase that means literally in layers, one upon one. The sūrah of al-Mulk says the same: *"˹He is the One˺ Who created seven heavens, one above the other. You will never see any imperfection in the creation of the Most Compassionate."* (67:3). If the heavens are described as stacked, then a word meaning layers is the natural description of them here.

**Seven Heavens as a Standing Description**

The number is fixed across the Book and never varies. *"He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you. Then He turned toward the heaven, forming it into seven heavens."* (2:29). *"So He formed the heaven into seven heavens in two Days, assigning to each its mandate."* (41:12). *"Allah is the One Who created seven heavens ˹in layers˺, and likewise for the earth."* (65:12).

**The Second Sentence Carries the Point**

The verse could have ended with the seven. It does not: *"We are never unmindful of ˹Our˺ creation."* That clause is the reason the picture of the heavens is here at all.

The statement answers a specific worry, which is the worry of anyone who has looked at the size of the sky and then at his own life. A structure of that scale invites the inference that its maker is occupied elsewhere. The verse refuses the inference in the plainest possible terms — not unmindful, in the sense of a person who has ceased to attend to something he once knew about. The negation is stronger than "God knows about creation"; it denies the specific possibility of neglect.

**Never Unaware: A Repeated Assurance**

The phrase is not unique to this place. After describing hardening of the heart, the Cow closes: *"And Allah is never unaware of what you do."* (2:74). Hūd closes with the same assurance directed at the Prophet himself: *"And to Him all matters are returned. So worship Him and put your trust in Him. And your Lord is never unaware of what you do."* (11:123).

The repetition shows what the phrase is for. It appears where a person might reasonably suppose that his situation has been overlooked — a hardening community, a prophet opposed by his own people, an individual believer inside a structure of seven heavens. The assurance is not information about God's knowledge in the abstract. It is a specific denial that anything is too small, too distant or too long-delayed to have been noticed.

## Verse 23:18

> We send down rain from the sky in perfect measure, causing it to soak into the earth. And We are surely able to take it away

**Bi-Qadar: A Measured Descent**

The rain comes *bi-qadar*, by measure. The word *qadar* covers quantity, proportion and appointed degree, and the Qur'an uses it as a general principle of creation: *"Indeed, We have created everything, perfectly preordained."* (54:49). Applied to rainfall it says something precise: the water that falls is not a random quantity but a calibrated one.

The parallel verses make the calibration explicit and give it a moral reason. *"And ˹He is the One˺ Who sends down rain from the sky in perfect measure, with which We give life to a lifeless land. And so will you be brought forth ˹from the grave˺."* (43:11), where the measured rain is made the image of resurrection. And in the Consultation the measure is explained: *"Had Allah given abundant provisions to ˹all˺ His servants, they would have certainly transgressed throughout the land. But He sends down whatever He wills in perfect measure."* (42:27). The restraint is not a limitation on generosity. It is the form the generosity takes.

**Water Stored in the Ground, Not Just Falling on It**

The second clause is easily passed over: the water is caused to soak into the earth. The verse does not stop at precipitation. It follows the water after it lands, into the soil, where it is held.

That is a specific and unusual thing for a seventh-century text to single out. The sūrah of al-Ḥijr makes the same point by denying human control over it: *"We send fertilizing winds, and bring down rain from the sky for you to drink. It is not you who hold its reserves."* (15:22).

**The Capacity of the Valleys**

Another verse describes the same descent with attention to what receives it: *"He sends down rain from the sky, causing the valleys to flow, each according to its capacity."* (13:17). The measure is in the sending and the capacity is in the receiving, and the two are matched.

**The Threat in the Last Clause**

The verse ends by naming a capacity rather than a fact: *"And We are surely able to take it away."* Nothing has been withdrawn. The rain is still falling, the ground is still holding it, and the sentence says only that removal is possible.

Two other passages put the same possibility as a question. *"Say, 'Consider this: if your water were to sink ˹into the earth˺, then who ˹else˺ could bring you flowing water?'"* (67:30). And: *"Have you considered the water you drink? Is it you who bring it down from the clouds, or is it We Who do so? If We willed, We could make it salty. Will you not then give thanks?"* (56:68-70). All three do the same work: they take the most taken-for-granted provision in human life and show that it is a provision rather than a property. The clause is placed at the end of a verse about blessing, where it changes the reader's relation to the blessing without changing the blessing.

The same descent is described elsewhere with its outcome attached: *"And We send down blessed rain from the sky, bringing forth gardens and grains for harvest,"* (50:9).

## Verse 23:19

> With it We produce for you gardens of palm trees and grapevines, in which there are abundant fruits, and from which you may eat

**Two Trees Named Before All the Rest**

The water described in the verse before produces *jannāt*, gardens, and two plants are named: the date palm and the vine. Both were the staples of the Ḥijāz, and both were what a garden in that landscape was measured by. A plot with palms and vines was a productive holding; a plot without them was land.

The Book names the same pair repeatedly when it wants to describe cultivated bounty. *"And We have placed in it gardens of palm trees and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth in it,"* (36:34). *"And on the earth there are ˹different˺ neighbouring tracts, gardens of grapevines, ˹various˺ crops, palm trees—some stemming from the same root, others standing alone. They are all irrigated with the same water, yet We make some taste better than others."* (13:4). The pairing is not botanical taxonomy. It is shorthand for a settled, irrigated, productive life — the thing a nomadic society aspired to and a mercantile one traded in.

**The Tree the Prophet Compared to the Believer**

The palm carries a further weight in the tradition. Ibn ʿUmar reports that the Prophet once said there is a tree among trees whose leaves do not fall and which is like a Muslim, and asked the company to name it. People began guessing at the trees of the desert; the answer occurred to Ibn ʿUmar, but he was the youngest present and held his tongue while Abū Bakr and ʿUmar stayed silent. When nobody answered, the Prophet said: it is the date palm. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 61, and in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, whose wording supplies the detail about the two elders.

**Fruits Beyond the Two Named**

Between the two trees and the closing clause the verse inserts a general statement: in them there are many fruits. The garden is not restricted to what was named, and the two plants are given as the frame rather than the inventory.

That structure is deliberate. A list of every crop would be a catalogue and would date itself to one region and one climate. The same method appears in the Bee, where the list is longer and the closing clause is the point: *"With it He produces for you ˹various˺ crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and every type of fruit. Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect."* (16:11).

**The Same Two Trees in the Reward**

The reward repeats the bounty in compressed form. Of the successful: *"Gardens, vineyards,"* (78:32), and of what the earth will bring forth for them, *"as well as grapes and greens,"* (80:28). The vocabulary of provision here and of reward there is the same vocabulary.

That continuity makes the verse's blessing harder to dismiss. The believer is not asked to despise the garden to want the Garden. He is asked to notice that the fruit in his hand comes from the same arrangement — measured water, receptive ground, a Creator who is not unmindful — and that the arrangement has a second, permanent version.

*"Would any of you wish to have a garden with palm trees, grapevines, and all kinds of fruits with rivers flowing underneath and as they grow very old with dependent children, a fiery whirlwind hits the garden, burning it all up?"* (2:266).

## Verse 23:20

> as well as ˹olive˺ trees which grow at Mount Sinai, providing oil and a condiment to eat

**A Tree Named by Its Mountain**

The list moves from a category of gardens to a single tree with a location attached. The olive is identified by the place it grows — Mount Sinai — and the identification is the verse's most striking feature. Nothing else in this passage is tied to a geography.

The reason for the tie is that the mountain carries memory. It is where Mūsā received the law, and the Qur'an swears by it: *"and Mount Sinai,"* (95:2). A tree described as growing there is not being given a botanical habitat so much as a lineage. The sūrah has been listing provisions — measured rain, stored water, palms, vines — and here it lists one that arrives with a covenant attached to it. The olive of Sinai is the same tree whose oil lit the lamp in the parable of light, and the hearers who knew the story of Mūsā would have heard the name and thought of the law before they thought of the fruit.

**The Olive Elsewhere in the Book**

It appears too in the list of what the measured rain produces: *"With it He produces for you ˹various˺ crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and every type of fruit."* (16:11), where it is named before the palm. The two uses are different in kind — one is provision, the other is an image — and together they explain why this sūrah gives the olive a verse of its own. It is the only plant in the Book that serves both as food and as the standard illustration of light.

**Why One Tree Gets a Location**

The structure of verses nineteen and twenty is worth reading closely. The first describes gardens in general, with palms and vines named as the two principal plants and other fruits left open. The second describes one tree, and attaches a place name to it.

**A Blessed Tree Neither East Nor West**

The parable in an-Nūr adds a detail that has been read for centuries as the key to the image: the tree is *"neither to the east nor the west"* (24:35). Commentators have taken this to mean a tree on open ground, unshaded by anything, receiving the sun from its rising to its setting, so that its oil is at its purest.

## Verse 23:21

> And there is certainly a lesson for you in cattle, from whose bellies We give you ˹milk˺ to drink, and in them are many other benefits for you, and from them you may eat

**Anʿām: The Eight Animals Named Elsewhere**

The word *anʿām* covers the grazing livestock of Arabia — camels, cattle, sheep and goats — and the Book identifies them precisely when it wants to. An-Niʿām sets out the pairs: *"˹Allah has created˺ four pairs: a pair of sheep and a pair of goats"* (6:143), and then *"a pair of camels and a pair of oxen"* (6:144). Eight animals, four kinds, male and female of each.

That specificity matters for reading this verse. *Anʿām* is not a poetic word for animals in general; it names the herd, the property a household's survival rested on. The verse is therefore not making a general observation about nature. It is addressing the largest single asset its hearers owned, and telling them that what comes out of it did not come from them. The lesson is placed where the wealth is.

**A Drink Taken From Between Two Other Things**

The sūrah of the Bee describes the same provision with a physiological detail: *"And there is certainly a lesson for you in cattle: We give you to drink of what is in their bellies, from between digested food and blood: pure milk, pleasant to drink."* (16:66). Milk appears between two things that are not drinkable, and comes out as neither.

That is the observation the verse is drawing attention to. An animal eats dry fodder and produces blood; from between those two a third substance emerges that is neither, and it is fit to drink. The verse does not explain the mechanism — it has no interest in that — but it insists on the strangeness of the result. Anyone who keeps animals has watched this happen daily and stopped seeing it. The verse restores the sight.

**"Many Other Benefits" Left Unitemised**

The middle clause is deliberately open: in them are many benefits. Nothing is listed. The hearers knew what the list contained — wool, hair and leather; dung for fuel; horns and bone; the animal's labour in the field — and the verse leaves them to supply it.

The openness has a rhetorical function. A list would have been an inventory, and an inventory invites the reply that some items matter and others do not. The same method appears in the sūrah of the Forgiver: *"It is Allah Who made cattle for you so that you may ride some and eat others. Also, you find in them ˹other˺ benefits."* (40:79-80).

**Warmth, Food and a Sight at Evening**

The sūrah of the Bee opens its account of the same animals with the two benefits a desert household would have named first: *"And He created the cattle for you as a source of warmth, food. ˹many other˺ benefits."* (16:5), and then adds a benefit with no use at all — *"They are also pleasing to you when you bring them home and when you take them out to graze."* (16:6).

That last clause is worth keeping beside this verse. The provision is not only useful; it is agreeable to look at, and the Book counts the pleasure of it as part of the gift. The lesson this verse points to is not simply that the cattle are useful but that they are useful, productive and beautiful, and none of the three was arranged by their owner.

The report is in Sunan al-Tirmidhī, 3455.

## Verse 23:22

> And you are carried upon ˹some of˺ them and upon ships

**Two Carriers Named in a Single Clause**

The verse joins two things that have nothing in common except their function. An animal and a ship; a living creature that feeds itself and a built object that does not move without wind or muscle. Both are described with the same passive verb: you are carried.

**The Ship as a Standing Sign**

The Qur'an returns to ships more often than to almost any other human artefact, and always in the same role. The long list of signs in the Cow includes *"the ships that sail the sea for the benefit of humanity"* (2:164) beside the alternation of night and day and the reviving rain. Luqmān puts it as a question: *"Do you not see that the ships sail ˹smoothly˺ through the sea by the grace of Allah so that He may show you some of His signs? Surely in this are signs for whoever is steadfast, grateful."* (31:31).

The reason for the recurrence is that a ship is a visible demonstration of dependency. A building sits on the ground; a ship is held up by something that could stop holding it at any moment, and everyone aboard knows this. The sūrah of ar-Rūm adds the commercial motive — *"and that ships may sail by His command, and that you may seek His bounty"* (30:46) — so the sign is not only physical but economic: the trade the hearers lived by ran on a medium they did not control. Yūnus describes the moment when the sign stops being an observation and becomes a fact: *"And it so happens that you are on ships, sailing with a favourable wind, to the passengers' delight. Suddenly, the ships are overcome by a gale wind and those on board are overwhelmed by waves from every side, and they assume they are doomed. They cry out to Allah ˹alone˺ in sincere devotion, 'If You save us from this, we will certainly be grateful.'"* (10:22).

**Ships Like Mountains**

Ar-Raḥmān gives the image its fullest form: *"To Him belong the ships with raised sails, sailing through the seas like mountains."* (55:24). The comparison is of scale — a vessel that looks like a piece of landscape moving across water — and the verse opens with the possessive: they belong to Him.

Az-Zukhruf turns the same scene into an instruction: *"so that you may sit firmly on their backs, and remember your Lord's blessings once you are settled on them, saying, 'Glory be to the One Who has subjected these for us, for we could have never done so ˹on our own˺.'"* (43:13).

## Verse 23:23

> Indeed, We sent Noah to his people. He declared, “O my people! Worship Allah ˹alone˺. You have no god other than Him. Will you not then fear ˹Him˺?”

**The Refrain Every Messenger Repeats**

The sūrah turns from provision to history with the first of its prophetic narratives, and the first words it puts in the messenger's mouth are words the Book uses again and again. *"Indeed, We sent Noah to his people. He said, 'O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him.'"* (7:59). Hūd says it to ʿĀd: *"O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him."* (11:50). Ṣāliḥ and Shuʿayb say it in the same sūrah, in the same words, to their own peoples.

**Nine Hundred and Fifty Years of It**

The length of Nūḥ's mission is stated elsewhere with unusual precision: *"Indeed, We sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them for a thousand years, less fifty. Then the Flood overtook them, while they persisted in wrongdoing."* (29:14). Nine hundred and fifty years, and the verse closes on the fact that they persisted.

That number reframes the address in this verse. A man who spent nine and a half centuries saying the same sentence to the same community, and who watched generation after generation refuse it, is not a figure of rhetorical patience. The sūrah is describing the longest sustained rejection recorded in the Book, and it opens the story with the sentence that was said throughout. The reader meets the words before he learns how long they were said, and the length makes them heavier.

**Three Moves in the Address**

The speech has three parts and no more. An address — *my people*. A command — worship God. A question — will you not then fear Him. There is no argument in it, no evidence offered, no threat detailed, no appeal to consequence.

The economy is worth noticing. A messenger opening his mission to a community that has never heard him might be expected to establish his credentials first. Nūḥ does none of that. He names the relationship between himself and his hearers, states the single demand, and asks whether they will respond to it.

The opening words, *yā qawm*, are the smallest of the three and the easiest to miss. He does not say "you people" or "O community of such-and-such"; he claims membership in the group he is addressing.

**The Same Sentence From a Different Mouth**

Hūd's version of the refrain carries one addition that shows how stable the core is: *"O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him. You do nothing but fabricate lies ˹against Allah˺."* (11:50). The first two sentences are identical to Nūḥ's; the third is a charge against his particular people's practice.

---

## Verse 23:24

> But the disbelieving chiefs of his people said ˹to the masses˺, “This is only a human like you, who wants to be superior to you. Had Allah willed, He could have easily sent down angels ˹instead˺. We have never heard of this in ˹the history of˺ our forefathers

**The Chiefs Answer Before the People Do**

The reply does not come from Nūḥ's people. It comes from *al-malaʾ* — the notables, the assembly of leading men — and the verse specifies that they are speaking to the masses. The crowd has not yet said anything; its position is being formed for it by the people whose standing the message disturbs.

The word *malaʾ* is used throughout the Book for exactly this group: the chiefs of Pharaoh's court, the council of the Queen of Sheba who are summoned with the words *"O chiefs! Indeed, a noble letter has been delivered to me."* (27:29), the notables of Quraysh who say *"Carry on, and stand firm in devotion to your gods."* (38:6), the king's advisers in the story of Yūsuf addressed as *"O chiefs! Tell me the meaning of my dream"* (12:43). It is a term of office rather than of character, and the sūrah puts the rejection in their mouths first because that is where the rejection originates.

**"A Human Like You"**

The first objection is about the messenger's species. Hūd's people put it the same way: *"We see you only as a human being like ourselves."* (11:27). The messengers as a group are answered with it: *"You are no more than humans like us!"* (14:10). And of the Prophet himself: *"What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺?"* (25:7).

**"Had Allah Willed, He Could Have Sent Angels"**

The second objection is religious in form. If God had wanted to send a messenger, He would have sent an angel; He did not, so He has not. The demand appears repeatedly in the Book: *"If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner,"* (25:7), and the same request put to the Prophet in 6:8 and elsewhere.

The argument fails on its own terms, and the Book says why. An angel would not be a messenger to human beings in any sense they could act on; a being of a different order, unable to be imitated, would establish nothing about how a person should live. The sūrah of al-Anʿām answers the request directly: if an angel were sent and the matter decided, they would be given no respite (6:8). The chiefs are proposing a test whose result they have not thought about, and the objection is a way of refusing the message while appearing to defer to God's power.

**The Demand for an Angel, Answered**

The Book answers the angel objection in a way that turns it into an argument for the messenger's humanity. The messengers themselves say to their peoples: *"Is there any doubt about Allah, the Originator of the heavens and the earth? He is inviting you to forgive your sins, and delay your end until your appointed term."* (14:10) — and then report the reply: *"You are no more than humans like us!"* The messengers do not deny being human.

The sūrah of al-Furqān records the same exchange and answers it by describing what the Prophet is: a warner who eats and trades, sent to a people who need a warner they can follow.

---

## Verse 23:25

> He is simply insane, so bear with him for a while.”

**The Charge Put to Every Messenger**

Sabaʾ reports the same accusation in a disjunction that exposes its function: *"Has he fabricated a lie against Allah or is he insane?"* (34:8). The two options are alternatives offered by people who have not decided which they believe, and the verse comments on the speakers rather than on the charge: those who do not believe in the Hereafter are bound for torment and have strayed furthest from the truth.

**A Word That Means Both Possessed and Mad**

The Arabic is *bihi jinnah* — in him is a *jinnah*, from the same root as *jinn* and related to *majnūn*, the common word for mad. Both terms carry two senses at once: possession by an unseen being, and insanity with no supernatural explanation. Classical usage does not force a choice between them, and the Qur'an uses the family of words for both.

The ambiguity is useful to the speakers. An accusation of possession places the man outside the community's moral order — he is not wrong, he is inhabited, and nothing he says needs to be answered on its merits.

Ad-Dukhān records the version directed at Mūsā: *"then they turned away from him, saying, 'A madman, taught by others!'"* (44:14) — madness and instruction by someone else, two charges that do not sit well together. At-Ṭūr dismisses both against the Prophet: *"For you, by the grace of your Lord, are not a fortune-teller or a madman."* (52:29). The consistency across these passages is the point. Every messenger is met with the same word, and the word is never supported by anything.

**The Diagnosis That Requires No Argument**

Notice what the verse does not contain. No point of Nūḥ's message is addressed. His claim that there is no god but God is not examined, and his call to fear God is not answered. The man is categorised and the discussion is closed.

There is also a social mechanism at work.

**The Accusation Reversed**

Sabaʾ turns the charge back on those who make it. After recording the two options — fabricated lie, or insanity — the verse continues: *"In fact, those who do not believe in the Hereafter are bound for torment and have strayed farthest ˹from the truth˺."* (34:8).

---

## Verse 23:26

> Noah prayed, “My Lord! Help me, because they have denied ˹me˺.”

**The Shortest Prayer in the Story**

The prayer is five words in Arabic. There is no praise, no confession, no list of grievances, no request for a specific outcome. My Lord, help me, for they deny me.

The brevity is not stylistic. A longer prayer would have been about Nūḥ — his suffering, his endurance, his righteousness. This one is about a fact: the denial. He states the situation and asks for help with it, and leaves the rest to the one he is asking. The sūrah of al-Qamar records a version of the same cry that is even more stripped: *"So he cried out to his Lord, 'I am helpless, so help ˹me˺!'"* (54:10). Between the two accounts the reader has the prayer in its plainest form — a statement of inability and a request.

**What a Messenger Does When Persuasion Ends**

The verse records a transition that the rest of the Book treats as normal rather than exceptional. Messengers argue, warn, invite and answer objections; at some point they stop, and what they do then is pray. Yūnus preserves the same moment in Nūḥ's own words, addressed to the people who are about to kill him: *"If my presence and my reminders to you of Allah's signs are unbearable to you, then ˹know that˺ I have put my trust in Allah. So devise a plot along with your associate-gods—and you do not have to be secretive about your plot—then carry it out against me without delay!"* (10:71). He adds that he has never asked them for payment, and that his reward is only from God (10:72).

**"I Am Helpless, So Help Me"**

The parallel in al-Qamar gives the prayer its fullest form and its context: *"So he cried out to his Lord, 'I am helpless. Help ˹me˺!' So We opened the gates of the sky with pouring rain, and caused the earth to burst with springs, so the waters met for a fate already set."* (54:10-12). The words *mughtaẓ*, overcome, describe a man at the end of his resources.

Read beside this verse, the two accounts show the same shape: the messenger reduced to a single sentence, and the response arriving as an event rather than an argument. The sūrah of al-Qamar adds the closing comment — *"We certainly left this as a sign. So is there anyone who will be mindful?"* (54:15) — which is the purpose of the whole narrative, and the reason this short prayer is preserved in the text at all.

The sūrah of al-Qamar makes the sequence explicit: the cry of helplessness, then the opening of the gates of the sky (54:10-11).

---

## Verse 23:27

> So We inspired him: “Build the Ark under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes and directions. Then when Our command comes and the oven bursts ˹with water˺, take on board a pair from every species along with your family—except those against whom the decree ˹to drown˺ has already been passed. And do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong, for they will surely be drowned.”

**Built Under Watchful Eyes**

The answer to the prayer is an instruction, and the instruction begins with a phrase that appears again in the account: *bi-aʿyuninā*, under Our eyes. Hūd gives the same formula — *"And build the Ark under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes and directions"* (11:37) — and al-Qamar describes the finished vessel as *"sailing under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes"* (54:14).

The phrase does two things. It tells Nūḥ that he is not working alone, which matters for a man about to construct something enormous in front of a community that has just called him mad. And it tells the reader that the construction was supervised — the dimensions, the materials and the method were given, not invented. The words "and directions" make that explicit: the building is a revealed act, not an engineering project.

**The Exception Inside the Rescue**

The family is included, and then subtracted: except those against whom the decree has already been passed. The verse does not name them. Hūd does, later, when Nūḥ calls to a son standing apart from the others and the son refuses to board (11:42).

The exception is placed before the reader knows who it refers to, which is a deliberate arrangement. The reader learns first that kinship will not save anyone, and only afterwards discovers whose kinship was at issue. When Nūḥ then asks about his son, the answer is a correction rather than a comfort: *"O Noah! He is certainly not of your family—he was completely of unrighteous conduct. So do not ask Me about what you have no knowledge of!"* (11:46). The category that matters is not blood.

**The Prohibition on Intercession**

The last clause forbids a prayer: do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong. The instruction is given before Nūḥ makes the plea, and it is repeated in Hūd at the same point (11:37).

After it became clear that the polytheists were bound for the Fire, the Prophet and the believers were told it was not proper to seek forgiveness for them, even close relatives (9:113). And Ibrāhīm's prayer for his father is explained as the keeping of a promise, withdrawn once the enmity was plain (9:114). Nūḥ's people had made theirs over nine centuries.

**Planks and Nails**

Al-Qamar describes the vessel in the plainest terms available: *"We carried him on that ˹Ark made˺ of planks and nails,"* (54:13). Two materials, no ornament, no dimensions.

The description belongs beside this verse because it is the answer to the instruction. Nūḥ was told to build under supervision, and what he built was a wooden box fastened with nails — the simplest thing that floats. The sūrah then closes its own account of the event with the reason the story is told at all: *"We certainly left this as a sign. So is there anyone who will be mindful?"* (54:15). The narrative is preserved not for its detail but for the question at the end of it.

Hūd records the same event with the same word (11:40), and neither account explains it, which suggests the hearers knew what was meant.

---

## Verse 23:28

> Then when you and those with you have settled in the Ark, say, “All praise is for Allah, Who saved us from the wrongdoing people.”

**The First Words Spoken in Safety**

The verse dictates what Nūḥ is to say at a precise moment: once he and his company are settled aboard. Not when the command came, not when the last animal was loaded, not when the water receded. The words are assigned to the interval between boarding and departure, when the escape is complete in fact and not yet tested by the journey.

**A Formula Given Before the Event**

The instruction is delivered before the rescue happens. Nūḥ is told what he will say before he has anything to say it about, and the words include a completed act — *Who saved us* — spoken by a man who is still on dry land.

**What the Formula Assumes**

Saying "Who saved us from the wrongdoing people" commits the speaker to a description of those he has just left. It calls them wrongdoers, in a sentence of praise, and it does so at the moment when the charge is being vindicated rather than argued.

**The Rescue Described in al-Aʿrāf**

The sūrah of the Heights records the outcome in a single sentence: *"But they rejected him, so We saved him and those with him in the Ark, and drowned those who rejected Our signs. They were certainly a blind people."* (7:64).

---

## Verse 23:29

> And pray, “My Lord! Allow me a blessed landing, for You are the best accommodator.”

**The Root of Landing**

The word for landing comes from n-z-l, the root of *nuzūl*, descent, and of *manzil*, a place where one alights or is lodged. The same root gives the word for a guest's provision and for a lodging arranged by a host. The request is therefore not simply "let me come down safely" but "give me a place, and let the place be good".

**"The Best of Those Who Give a Place"**

The closing phrase names God as the best of those who provide a landing — *khayr al-munzilīn*, the best of those who cause to descend or lodge. The superlative implies that others do it: hosts lodge guests, cities receive travellers, families take in relatives.

**The Landing Named in Hūd**

The answer to this prayer is recorded in the sūrah that tells the same story: *"And it was said, 'O earth! Swallow up your water. And O sky! Withhold ˹your rain˺.' The floodwater receded and the decree was carried out. The Ark rested on Mount Judi, and it was said, 'Away with the wrongdoing people!'"* (11:44).

The verse gives the landing a name. Whatever al-Jūdī is precisely — a mountain in the region of the upper Tigris, in the traditional identification — the text specifies a place, and that specification is the fulfilment of the request made here. The prayer asked for a blessed lodging; the narrative reports where the lodging was, and closes with the same phrase about the wrongdoers that the prayer of praise had used.

---

## Verse 23:30

> Surely in this are lessons. And We ˹always˺ put ˹people˺ to the test

**Signs Placed in a Story**

The narrative stops and comments on itself. Two clauses, and neither of them continues the account: there are signs in this, and We test. The story of Nūḥ is not told for its interest, and the verse says so before the reader has a chance to treat it as a tale.

Al-Qamar makes the same move with more force: *"We certainly left this as a sign. So is there anyone who will be mindful?"* (54:15). The question at the end of that verse is what the comment in this one exists to raise. A sign is not a proof that compels; it is a thing left in place for someone to notice, and the noticing is the response being asked for. The verse therefore addresses the reader directly, in the middle of a story about people long dead, and treats the reader as the one under examination.

**Testing as Part of the Design**

The Book states the principle without reference to any particular people: *"˹He is the One˺ Who created death and life to test which of you is best in deeds. And He is the Almighty, All-Forgiving."* (67:2). Testing is not an emergency measure or a response to human failure; it is given as the purpose of the arrangement in which life and death exist at all.

Al-ʿAnkabūt puts the same principle as a rebuke to an expectation: *"Do people think once they say, 'We believe,' that they will be left without being put to the test? We certainly tested those before them. And ˹in this way˺ Allah will clearly distinguish between those who are truthful and those who are liars."* (29:2-3). The second verse of that pair repeats exactly what this verse says — the testing of those before — and supplies the reason: to separate the truthful from the liars. A test with no distinction in its results would not be a test.

**The Test Is Not the Punishment**

While Nūḥ was speaking, his people could have answered differently, and the verse's placement says that the open period is the significant one.

Hūd makes the open period explicit in the narrative itself: while the ark was being built, the chiefs walked past and mocked (11:38).

**Tested With Fear and Famine**

The Cow describes what the test consists of in concrete terms: *"We will certainly test you with a touch of fear and famine and loss of property, life, and crops. Give good news to those who patiently endure—who, when faced with a disaster, say, 'Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return.'"* (2:155-156).

---

## Verse 23:31

> Then We raised another generation after them

**How Many Generations After Nūḥ**

The sūrah of the Night Journey puts the number in quantity rather than names: *"How many a generation have We destroyed after Noah! And enough is your Lord, about the sins of His servants, as Aware and Seeing."* (17:17). The verse follows an account of the Israelites' two periods of corruption, and it invokes Nūḥ as the fixed point from which the count runs.

**The Word That Measures History**

*Qarn* is one of the Qur'an's standard units for talking about the past. It appears in a long series of places — in al-Anʿām, in Yūnus, in Hūd, in Maryam, in al-Qaṣaṣ, in al-Aḥqāf, in Qāf — almost always in the same construction: how many generations We destroyed before them. The word does the work that a list of names would do in another literature.

**Which People? The Verse Does Not Say**

The tries are natural and the failure to settle them is not a defect in the text. The sūrah of al-Qaṣaṣ does the same thing with a different people: *"Indeed, We gave Moses the Scripture—after destroying earlier nations—as an insight for the people, a guide, and mercy so perhaps they would be mindful."* (28:43). The earlier nations are unnamed there too. What the account needs is not the name of the people but the shape of their story, and the shape is given in the verses that follow.

The same verse in al-Isrāʾ adds that God is enough, about the sins of His servants, as Aware and Seeing — which puts the replacement in its proper frame.

---

## Verse 23:32

> and sent to them a messenger from among themselves, ˹declaring,˺ “Worship Allah ˹alone˺. You have no god other than Him. Will you not then fear ˹Him˺?”

**A Messenger From Among Themselves**

The phrase *min anfusihim*, from among themselves, is the whole of the verse's new material. Everything else in it repeats what Nūḥ said. The sūrah chooses to specify the messenger's origin at precisely the point where the previous narrative showed that origin being used as an objection — a human like you, who eats what you eat.

The sūrah could have written "and he said to them what Nūḥ had said". It quotes the sentence again instead, and the repetition is deliberate.

**The Unchanged Core Across the Messengers**

The core appears in the same form everywhere it is recorded. *"Indeed, We sent Noah to his people. He said, 'O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him.'"* (7:59). The wording is stable enough that a reader can recognise it as a formula on first hearing and know what is coming.

**The Addition Each Messenger Makes**

The refrain has a fixed core and a variable part, and the variable part is aimed at the local error. Hūd's version appends a charge: *"You do nothing but fabricate lies ˹against Allah˺."* (11:50). Ṣāliḥ's and Shuʿayb's versions, in al-Aʿrāf and Hūd, carry their own specific accusations against their peoples' practice.

This verse gives the core alone, with nothing added, which suits an unnamed people whose specific idolatry the sūrah does not describe. What the reader is shown is the irreducible content of every mission — one God, no other, and a question about whether that changes how you live. Everything else in the prophetic record is commentary on that sentence.

---

## Verse 23:33

> But the chiefs of his people—who disbelieved, denied the meeting ˹with Allah˺ in the Hereafter, and were spoiled by the worldly luxuries We had provided for them—said ˹to the masses˺, “This is only a human like you. He eats what you eat, and drinks what you drink

**Luxury as a Cause, Not a Context**

The third description is a causal clause: those to whom We had given luxury in this life. Their ease is presented as one of the reasons they said what they said, not as an incidental fact about them.

The Book makes the same connection elsewhere. Al-Isrāʾ describes how destruction reaches a society through its elite: *"Whenever We intend to destroy a society, We command its elite ˹to obey Allah˺ but they act rebelliously in it. So the decree ˹of punishment˺ is justified, and We destroy it utterly."* (17:16). Hūd says of the destroyed peoples that the wrongdoers pursued their pleasures and became wicked (11:116). The claim is not that wealth makes a person wicked. It is that a settled, comfortable position gives a person a reason to want nothing to change, and a message about being answerable is a message about change.

**"A Human Like You" Again, With a New Detail**

The objection repeats the one Nūḥ's chiefs made, and then supplies evidence for it: he eats what you eat and drinks what you drink. The addition is the verse's contribution. The earlier account said the messenger was human and wanted superiority; this one says he is human and shares your appetites.

The same objection is recorded against the Prophet in more detail: *"What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺? If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner,"* (25:7). The market and the meal are the two proofs offered in both cases, and both are facts that no one disputes. The argument does not depend on denying anything about the messenger. It depends on treating ordinary human life as incompatible with carrying a message.

**The Luxurious Are Named First in Destruction**

The pairing of luxury with rejection runs through the sūrah's later material and through the Book generally. Al-Anbiyāʾ records the taunt directed at a destroyed people: *"Do not run away! Return to your luxuries and your homes, so you may be questioned ˹about your fate˺."* (21:13). The homes and the comforts are named as the things they will be asked about.

---

## Verse 23:34

> And if you ˹ever˺ obey a human like yourselves, then you would certainly be losers

**A Conditional Threat Aimed at the Crowd**

The verse is addressed to the people, not to the messenger, and its form is a warning about consequences: if you obey him, you will be losers. The chiefs have finished describing the prophet and have moved on to what will happen to anyone who listens to him.

**Who Actually Loses**

The verse states a prediction that the sūrah will reverse. The people the chiefs describe as losers if they obey are, in the Book's accounting, the only people who do not lose; and the ones who refuse are the ones described with the same word.

Al-Kahf puts the reversal in the form of a question: *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Shall we inform you of who will lose the most deeds? ˹They are˺ those whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life, while they think they are doing good!'"* (18:103-104). The description fits the chiefs exactly: they are working hard, they believe they are protecting their people, and the work comes to nothing. The verse in al-Muʾminūn records the charge being made, and the Book elsewhere identifies who it actually applies to.

**The Word for Losers**

*Khāsir* and its plural appear throughout the Qur'an as the standard description of those who misjudge the terms of their own existence. It is used of the people whose scale is light on the Day: *"But those whose scale is light, they have doomed themselves for wrongfully denying Our signs."* (7:9) — the verb there is from the same root, and it is reflexive: they have lost themselves.

**The Real Losers Named Elsewhere**

The sūrah of al-Kahf's question has an answer attached to it, and the answer is not about the afterlife alone. Those who lose most are the ones whose effort in this life was wasted while they believed they were doing well (18:104). The loss is realised here, in the uselessness of the work, before it is realised anywhere else.

---

## Verse 23:35

> Does he promise you that once you are dead and reduced to dust and bones, you will be brought forth ˹alive˺

**The Second Objection Is About the Hereafter**

The chiefs move from the messenger's person to his content, and the content they choose is resurrection. The objection is put as a question addressed to the crowd: does he really promise you this?

The same question appears throughout the Book in nearly identical words. Al-Isrāʾ records it as mockery: *"When we are reduced to bones and ashes, will we really be raised as a new creation?"* (17:49). Al-Wāqiʿah preserves the fuller form: *"When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?"* (56:47). An-Nāziʿāt has it in its shortest version: *"even after we have been reduced to decayed bones?"* (79:11). The formula is stable enough to be recognisable, and this verse is one instance of a standing objection rather than a local quibble.

**Dust and Bones: The Two Stages Named**

The verse names two states, and they are not redundant. Dust is what remains when the body has completely disintegrated; bones are what remain while it has not. Between them they cover the whole process of decay, from the fresh corpse to the unidentifiable powder.

Naming both is a rhetorical device. The objector is not describing a particular corpse but the full range of what happens to corpses, and the range is what makes the promise seem absurd. A body that is still recognisable might conceivably be restored; powder cannot. The objection takes the worst case and presents it as the general case, which is what an effective objection does. The Book's answer, given elsewhere, is that the one who produced them the first time knows every created being (36:79) — an answer aimed at the powder rather than at the bones.

The two words also map onto the sūrah's own account of origins.

**The Question Posed as Ridicule**

The form matters. The chiefs do not say the promise is false; they ask whether he really promises it, in a tone that expects the crowd to laugh. Al-Isrāʾ marks the tone explicitly with the word "mockingly" (17:49), and al-Wāqiʿah does the same (56:47).

The efficiency of ridicule is also its limitation. A crowd that has been made to laugh has not been convinced of anything, and the position it holds is unstable — it lasts only as long as the mood does. The sūrah knows this, which is why the narrative moves straight from the mockery to the creed: underneath the performance there is an actual belief, and the belief is what the following verses have to answer.

**The Same Question From the Quraysh**

The parallel verses are not about ancient peoples. They record the objection as it was put to the Prophet in Mecca, in the same words and with the same tone: *"And they argue with Us— forgetting they were created—saying, 'Who will give life to decayed bones?'"* (36:78). The clause "forgetting they were created" is the Book's answer in miniature.

That clause is what the chiefs in this verse have also forgotten. Their objection is built completely on what happens at the end of a body's history, and it never mentions the beginning. The sūrah has already answered it eleven verses earlier, in its account of the drop, the clot and the lump; the narrative of the messengers then shows the objection being made anyway, by people who had the same evidence available to them.

---

## Verse 23:36

> Impossible, simply impossible is what you are promised

**The Distance the Word Names**

*Hayhāta* is a word about space. It means far, remote, out of reach, and it is used of something that will not come near. The rejection is therefore framed not as falsehood but as remoteness: what you are promised is so far off that it is not worth accounting for.

**Hayhāta Hayhāta: A Word Said Twice**

The Arabic is *hayhāta hayhāta* — an interjection meaning "far away!", used to reject something as remote from possibility. It is not a sentence with a subject and a predicate. It is an exclamation, and the verse reproduces it exactly as it would have been shouted.

The word also carries an implication the speakers may not have noticed. Something that is far away is not something that does not exist; it is something that has not arrived. The strongest form of the objection would have been a denial, and the chiefs did not make one. Their chosen word concedes the possibility of the promise while dismissing its urgency, and the sūrah's reply addresses exactly that concession.

---

## Verse 23:37

> There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die, others are born, and none will be resurrected

After four verses of objection, the chiefs finally state a position. It is short, and it is the most consequential statement in the whole exchange: there is nothing but this life.

**"We Die and Others Live"**

The middle clause is compressed, and the commentators read it two ways. One reading takes it as describing the succession of the species: we die, and others live after us — the world continues without us, and that continuation is all there is. The other takes it as a statement about the alternation itself: some die while others are alive, and the pattern exhausts what happens.

**The Same Words in the Mouths of the Quraysh**

Al-Anʿām records the identical creed, with no middle clause: *"They insisted, 'There is nothing beyond this worldly life and we will never be resurrected.'"* (6:29). The word translated "insisted" carries the sense of holding a position against evidence, and the two halves of the sentence are the same two halves given here.

**A Creed That Cannot Be Argued With on Its Own Terms**

That is why the Book answers it the way it does. Rather than arguing for the afterlife, it points at the beginning: you were a drop, and you are arguing. The sūrah of Yā-Sīn puts the answer in one sentence — *"They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being."* (36:79) — and the answer works only on someone who concedes that he was produced. The chiefs' creed removes that concession, which is why the sūrah spends twelve verses establishing it before it reaches this point.

---

## Verse 23:38

> He is no more than a man who has fabricated a lie about Allah, and we will never believe in him.”

**The Third Charge: Fabrication**

The chiefs' speech ends with an accusation about the messenger's honesty. Having dismissed the message in the verse before, they now account for it: he has made it up and attributed it to God.

The charge is the standard one across the Book. Hūd's people are told by their own prophet that they do nothing but fabricate lies against God (11:50), and Sabaʾ reports it as one of the two options offered about a messenger: *"Has he fabricated a lie against Allah or is he insane?"* (34:8). Between those two accusations the objectors cover the whole field — the man is either dishonest or unwell — and neither option requires them to engage with what he said.

**"We Will Never Believe"**

The lock has an effect on the people listening too. A crowd that hears its leaders swear they will never believe has been told what believing would cost them socially, and the cost is separation from the men who hold the community together. The verse records a decision and, in the same breath, the pressure that makes the decision stick.

**The Sequence of the Three Charges**

The chiefs have now made three accusations across four verses, and they do not sit well together. He wants to be superior to you; he is insane; he has fabricated a lie against God. The first is a claim about ambition, the second about his mind, the third about his honesty.

**The Charge Answered by the Message Itself**

The Book answers the fabrication charge by pointing at the character of what was said. A man inventing a religion for advantage does not produce a message whose first demand is that he holds no authority, that he asks no payment. That the outcome belongs to God alone — and Nūḥ states exactly that in Yūnus: he has never demanded a reward, and his reward is only from God (10:72).

The answer is not a proof, and the sūrah does not offer it as one. It is an observation about the shape of the message: it costs the messenger everything and gives him nothing, which is an unusual arrangement for a fraud. The chiefs' accusation is recorded in full, and the refutation is left standing in the other sūrahs where the messenger's own words are preserved.

---

## Verse 23:39

> The messenger prayed, “My Lord! Help me, because they have denied ˹me˺.”

**The Same Sentence Recorded Twice**

This verse is word for word the verse Nūḥ prayed earlier in the sūrah. The same address, the same request, the same reason given. Nothing has been varied to suit a different prophet, a different people or a different century.

**What the Repetition Establishes**

If the prayer appeared once, a reader could treat it as Nūḥ's personal response to a personal situation — the reaction of a man worn down by nine centuries of refusal. Recorded a second time, in the mouth of a messenger whose name is not given and whose circumstances are barely described, it becomes something else.

**The Prayer as the Turning Point of Every Narrative**

The pattern holds across the Book's prophetic narratives. Yūnus records Nūḥ's prayer in fuller form — he is rejected, so he calls for judgement between himself and them, and asks to be saved with the believers (26:117-118) — and the outcome follows immediately. The prayer is not a pause in the narrative. It is the mechanism by which the narrative passes out of human hands, and this verse occupies that position for the second time in one sūrah.

**The Prayer Before the Answer**

Ash-Shuʿarāʾ preserves Nūḥ's version with its second half: *"Noah prayed, 'My Lord! My people have truly rejected me. So judge between me and them decisively, and save me and the believers with me.'"* (26:117-118). The request has two parts — a judgement and a rescue — and both are granted in the narrative that follows.

---

## Verse 23:40

> Allah responded, “Soon they will be truly regretful.”

**The Answer Is Not What Was Asked For**

The messenger asked for help, and the reply does not mention help. It states an outcome: in a little while they will be regretful. Nothing is promised to the prophet directly, and nothing is threatened to the people in the form of a command.

**Regret as the Predicted State**

The word is *nādimūn*, those who regret — and the choice of state rather than event is deliberate. The answer does not say they will be destroyed, or seized, or punished, though the narrative will describe all of that. It says they will regret.

Regret is the specific suffering of a person who has understood something too late to act on it. It is not pain inflicted from outside; it is the internal consequence of a decision, arriving after the decision can no longer be revised. The Qur'an uses the word for the man who killed his brother and then watched a crow show him what he should have done: *"He cried, 'Alas! Have I ˹even˺ failed to be like this crow and bury the corpse of my brother?' So he became regretful."* (5:31). The regret there is total and useless, and that is the state this verse predicts.

**The Regret That Comes Too Late**

The sūrah of ash-Shuʿarāʾ records the same outcome for a different people in one word: *"But they killed her, becoming regretful."* (26:157). The sequence there is compressed to its elements — the act, and then the regret — with nothing between them, and nothing after the regret.

---

## Verse 23:41

> Then the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook them with justice, and We reduced them to rubble. So away with the wrongdoing people

**Ghuthāʾ: Dross, or Flood-Wreck**

The verb translated as reduced them to rubble comes from *ghuthāʾ*, and the commentators give it two senses. One is the scum and debris a flood leaves behind — the useless matter deposited when the water withdraws. The other is dried plant rubbish, the kind blown about a deserted place.

**"So Away With the Wrongdoing People"**

The verse ends with an exclamation: *fa-buʿdan li-l-qawmi ẓ-ẓālimīn*, away with the wrongdoing people. This is the second time the sūrah has used this formula; the first came after the flood of Nūḥ, and there it named the wrongdoing people as those who disbelieved.

**The Hour Was Specified**

Al-Ḥijr gives the destruction a time of day. Of one people: *"So the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook them at sunrise."* (15:73). The punishment arrived at the start of a day, while people were beginning their ordinary business.

The timing here is that mechanism at its end — the last morning of a community that had decided nothing was going to happen, and that was right up to the moment it was not. Nothing in the account suggests the people were doing anything unusual that morning, and the ordinariness is the point.

**Four Punishments, Four Peoples**

The sūrah has now described four judgements in sequence: the drowning of the people of Nūḥ, the blast that took the people of the cry, and the destructions named around them. Each has its own mechanism and its own word, and each ends with the same shape of outcome.

Of another: *"But the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook them in the morning,"* (15:83).

---

## Verse 23:42

> Then We raised other generations after them

**The Same Verb the Sūrah Has Used Twice**

The word for raised is from the root n-sh-ʾ, and the sūrah uses it earlier for the raising of the generations after Nūḥ and for the stages of a human being's formation. In both of those uses the verb describes something brought into being that was not there before, and in both the emphasis falls on the bringing rather than on the thing brought.

The recurrence matters for how the verse should be read. These generations were not survivors who carried on; they were raised, which is to say they were made. The verb belongs to the same vocabulary as the sūrah's account of creation, and its use here places the succession of communities inside the same order as the succession of the stages of a single life.

That placement is a claim rather than a stylistic choice. A people is a creature with a beginning and an end, formed and then finished, and the word chosen to describe its arrival is the word used for a human being's arrival. The sūrah opened with an account of how a person is made in stages, and it now describes communities with the same verb, which suggests that the pattern it is tracking operates at both scales.

---

## Verse 23:43

> No people can advance their doom, nor can they delay it

**The Same Statement Elsewhere**

The Qur'an states the rule in other places, and the statements agree. Al-Aʿrāf: *"And every nation has ˹its˺ ordained term. So when their term arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor can they advance it."* (7:34). The wording is nearly identical, and the context is the same — the succession of communities and the messengers sent to them.

**Ajal: The Term Fixed at Both Ends**

The word is *ajal*, an appointed term, and the verse states that it cannot be moved in either direction. The two negatives are not a rhetorical doubling. Each excludes something different, and each excludes something a community in that position would plausibly try.

Advancing the term is what a people does when it wants the matter settled — when it calls for the punishment to be brought, as this sūrah's own opponents do elsewhere in the Qur'an. Delaying it is what a people does when it wants more time, and the desire for more time is the more common of the two.

Al-Anfāl adds the historical dimension, describing the nations destroyed before as destroyed when their term came, and stating that God does not withdraw a favour from a people until they change what is in themselves (8:53). Between them the verses describe a term that is fixed and a conduct that is not, which is precisely the distinction this verse draws.

**The Appointed Term in Other Places**

Yūnus states the rule in the context of a challenge: *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Have you considered: if His torment were to overtake you by night or by day, what ˹part˺ of it would the wicked be ˹eager˺ to hasten?'"* (10:50). The question assumes that the hastening is something people ask for, and the answer is that the asking changes nothing.

---

## Verse 23:44

> Then We sent Our messengers in succession: whenever a messenger came to his people, they denied him. So We destroyed them, one after the other, reducing them to ˹cautionary˺ tales. So away with the people who refuse to believe

**Aḥādīth: The Word for Stories**

The verse says they were reduced to *aḥādīth*, and the word means accounts, narratives, things that are told. A destroyed people becomes material for a story, and the story is told by people who were not there.

There is a second edge to the word. A story is told by the survivors, and the survivors are the people who came after — the ones the previous verse described as raised. The destroyed community does not tell its own account, and the account it gets is shaped by the fact that it is not there to give one.

**The Example in Those Destroyed Before**

Al-Anfāl draws the same lesson from the same material, describing the destruction of earlier peoples as the precedent for what follows, and stating that God would not punish a people while its messenger is among them (8:33). The verses go on to describe the Sacred House and those who hinder others from it.

Al-Anfāl adds the condition that suspends it: the punishment does not fall while the messenger is still among them. The interval is therefore not merely fixed in length but protected in a specific way, and the protection ends when the messenger goes.

**What the Word Means and How the Verse Uses It**
The verse says that God sent the messengers one after another and that each community denied its messenger, and that God made them follow one another in ruin, and He made them into tales. The word for tales, *aḥādīth*, is the plural of the word for a thing that is told, and the sentence means that a people with a history became a story told by others. The Sūrah of the Poets states the same outcome for the nations it names, and the Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān tells the Prophet that he is not the first messenger to be denied (3:184).
**What the Verse Teaches**
The Book's sentence carries a warning about the difference between being a people and being remembered, and it places the destruction in the middle of the sentence about the denial. The sūrah of the Prophets tells the same story with the sentence that the communities perished and the people who came afterwards were the ones who inherited their places (21:11), and the verse in front of us gives the last stage of the process: a ruin with a name attached.
**Where the Sūrah Goes**
The verse is followed by the command to the Prophet to ask about the messengers and by the answer that the Book is a warning. The passage belongs to the sūrah's long middle section on the founding of communities, which begins with the story of Noah and ends with the account of the son of Maryam (23:50). The sūrah's purpose is stated in its first verses, where it describes the believers who are successful, and the passages on the nations are placed in the middle to show where the other road ends.

---

## Verse 23:45

> Then We sent Moses and his brother Aaron with Our signs and compelling proof

**Two Brothers Sent Together**

The verse names two messengers, and the naming of the second is the first thing to notice. Aaron is described as his brother, which establishes the relation before the mission, and the mission is given to both of them together rather than to one with the other attached as support.

**Āyāt and Sulṭān Mubīn: Two Things, Not One**

The verse distinguishes between the signs and the compelling proof, and the distinction should be held on to. *Āyāt* are the signs — the things shown. *Sulṭān mubīn* is a clear authority, and the word *sulṭān* carries the sense of a warrant, a proof that establishes a claim.

The two together describe the messengers' equipment completely. The signs are what can be seen; the authority is what makes the seeing mean something.

**The Manner of the Address Was Specified Too**

The mission is described here by what the two men carried; Ṭā-Hā describes it by how they were told to speak. *"Go, both of you, to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺. Speak to him gently, so perhaps he may be mindful ˹of Me˺ or fearful ˹of My punishment˺."* (20:43-44).

**Nine Clear Signs**

The number is given in al-Isrāʾ: *"We surely gave Moses nine clear signs. ˹You, O Prophet, can˺ ask the Children of Israel. When Moses came to them, Pharaoh said to him, 'I really think that you, O Moses, are bewitched.'"* (17:101). The nine are listed across several sūrahs — the staff, the hand, the years of shortage, the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs, the blood, and the parting of the sea.

Al-Aʿrāf records the sending itself: *"Then after them We sent Moses with Our signs to Pharaoh and his chiefs, but they wrongfully rejected them. See what was the end of the corruptors!"* (7:103). The verse names the chiefs as addressees alongside Pharaoh, which is the same arrangement this sūrah gives in the verse that follows.

The charge of bewitchment in 17:101 is not a claim that the events did not happen; it is a claim about their cause, made by a man who had every opportunity to establish the cause and did not. The word *sulṭān* in this verse is what answers that charge — a warrant, not a trick.

---

## Verse 23:46

> to Pharaoh and his chiefs, but they behaved arrogantly and were a tyrannical people

**The Chiefs, Not Only the King**

The verse names two addressees, and the second is the significant one. The mission went to Pharaoh and to his *malaʾ*, his chiefs — the council of notables around him, the men whose standing depended on the arrangement that was already in place.

**Istakbarū: Arrogance as a Refusal to Look**

The verb is *istakbarū*, and it describes a specific behaviour rather than a general attitude. The root is k-b-r, greatness; the form means to make oneself great, to hold oneself above. The verse says the chiefs behaved arrogantly, and it does not say that they examined the signs and disagreed with them.

**Pharaoh's Command Followed**

The sūrah of al-Qaṣaṣ describes the same regime from the other side: *"Indeed, Pharaoh ˹arrogantly˺ elevated himself in the land and divided its people into ˹subservient˺ groups, one of which he persecuted, slaughtering their sons and keeping their women. He was truly one of the corruptors."* (28:4).

The division into graded groups is the mechanism of the tyranny this verse names, and the group singled out for persecution is the one Mūsā belongs to. The parallel supplies the social detail that this verse compresses into a single adjective.

---

## Verse 23:47

> They argued, “Will we believe in two humans, like ourselves, whose people are slaves to us?”

**Whose People Are Slaves to Us**

The second half is new, and it is the bluntest statement in the sūrah. The chiefs do not dispute the messengers' sincerity, their evidence or their conduct. They state that the messengers' people belong to them.

**Two Humans, Like Ourselves**

The first half of the objection is the one the sūrah has already recorded twice. Nūḥ's chiefs said he wanted to be superior to them. The next generation said that obeying a human like themselves would make them losers.

The word for slaves here is *ʿabīd*, and the claim is one of ownership rather than of rank. It is the same objection in an unmuffled form: we own them, and therefore what they say has no standing before us.

**The Charge in the Mouth of Pharaoh Himself**

Ash-Shuʿarāʾ records the same exchange with Pharaoh's own words and at greater length. Mūsā states that he is a messenger from the Lord of all worlds; Pharaoh turns to those around him and says, *"Did you hear ˹what he said˺?"* (26:25). Mūsā adds that He is their Lord and the Lord of their forefathers, and Pharaoh answers: *"Your messenger, who has been sent to you, must be insane."* (26:27).

The sequence shows the two objections in their working order. Pharaoh's first move is not to answer but to appeal to his audience — did you hear that? The second is the charge of insanity.

Neither move engages with what Mūsā said, and neither requires the signs to be examined. The sūrah of al-Muʾminūn gives the ownership charge in a single sentence and reserves the madness charge for its Meccan section later, where the same pair appears in the same order.

Al-Qaṣaṣ states the principle in general terms: *"But it was Our Will to favour those who were oppressed in the land, making them models ˹of faith˺ as well as successors;"* (28:5).

---

## Verse 23:48

> So they rejected them both, and ˹so˺ were among those destroyed

**Why the Sūrah Hurries Past the Greatest Story**

The choice to compress this narrative is the sūrah's clearest statement about what it is for. Al-Aʿrāf, Hūd, Ṭā-Hā, Yūnus, ash-Shuʿarāʾ and al-Qaṣaṣ all tell the story of Mūsā at length. This sūrah gives it four verses and moves on.

The reason is that this sūrah is building an argument about the messengers as a class rather than about Mūsā in particular. Length would have made the account stand apart from the others; compression brings it into line with them.

**"They Rejected" and the Three Earlier Refusals**

The verb is the same one the sūrah used in its summary verse, where every messenger's people denied him. The account of Mūsā is not an exception to that rule; it is an instance of it, and the verse places it inside the rule by using the rule's own verb.

The verse removes the supposition in a single clause. The greatest power of the age is entered in the same list, with the same verb, and the entry is no longer than any other. Scale is not a variable in the sūrah's account of refusals.

---

## Verse 23:49

> And We certainly gave Moses the Scripture, so perhaps his people would be ˹rightly˺ guided

**A Gift to a Free People**

The connection between the deliverance and the Scripture is stated nowhere in the verse, and it does not need to be. The order of the verses supplies it, and the order is an argument.

The sūrah has spent its middle section on communities that refused their messengers, and each account ends with a destruction. This verse breaks the sequence in a specific way: it describes what was given rather than what was taken away.

The break is not presented as a reward for the deliverance. The deliverance came first, and the Book came after, to a people who had done nothing yet to earn either. The sūrah's sequence is provision, then obligation, and the obligation is the guidance the purpose clause names.

**The Same Purpose Elsewhere**

The Qur'an states the purpose of revelation in similar terms in several places, and the formulations vary in their confidence. Al-Baqarah describes the Book as guidance for the mindful (2:2), without a condition. Al-Aʿrāf says it was revealed so that the Prophet might warn by it (7:2), describing a function rather than an outcome.

This verse's formulation is the least assured of the three, and it is the most accurate to what the sūrah has just been describing. A reader who has read four accounts of refused messengers is in no position to accept an unqualified statement that revelation produces guidance.

The verse gives him the qualified one, and the qualification is what makes the earlier accounts intelligible rather than inexplicable.

---

## Verse 23:50

> And We made the son of Mary and his mother a sign, and gave them refuge on high ground—a ˹suitable˺ place for rest with flowing water

**One Sign Standing for Two People**

The word for sign is singular — *āyah* — and it covers both of them. The son of Mary and his mother are made one sign together, not two signs separately, and the singular form is the verse's most consequential feature.

**Mary Withdraws to a Remote Place**

Sūrah Maryam gives the sequence in full: *"So she conceived him, then withdrew with him to a remote place."* (19:22). The withdrawal comes before the birth, and the sūrah describes the labour, the palm tree, the stream beneath it, and the instruction to eat and drink and be at ease.

The parallel supplies the detail this verse compresses into two adjectives. The remote place of Maryam is the high ground of al-Muʾminūn; the stream beneath the palm is the flowing water.

Between the two sūrahs the refuge is described twice, in the same terms, and neither account mentions anyone who provided it except God. The verse in al-Muʾminūn states the provision in a single clause and calls the whole of it a sign, which is the largest claim the sūrah makes about the pair.

---

## Verse 23:51

> O messengers! Eat from what is good and lawful, and act righteously. Indeed, I fully know what you do

**Ṭayyibāt: The Good Things**

The word for what they are told to eat is *ṭayyibāt*, the plural of *ṭayyib* — good, wholesome, agreeable, clean. The Qur'an uses the word especially for provisions, and it uses it in commands: *"O believers! Eat from the good things We have provided for you. And give thanks to Allah if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺."* (2:172).

The word is not a legal category here. It is a description of what has been given, and the command is to take it. Al-Māʾidah answers a question about what is permitted with the same term: *"Say, 'What is good and lawful.'"* (5:4). The consistent use of *ṭayyibāt* across these verses makes the point that the provision is not a concession or a burden. It is good, and it is to be eaten.

The word also carries a sense of wholesomeness rather than mere permissibility. A thing can be lawful and still be disagreeable; *ṭayyib* describes what is good to take, and the command is framed around the quality of the provision rather than around a rule about it.

**"Indeed, I Fully Know What You Do"**

The verse closes with a statement rather than a command, and the statement is about knowledge. Not "I will reward you" or "I am watching to punish". Simply: I know what you do.

It also removes any incentive to perform. A messenger who knows his work is seen by someone who sees the inside of it has no reason to make the outside impressive, and the sūrah's account of the messengers is consistently unimpressive in exactly that way — no armies, no wealth, no status among the men they were sent to.

**The Messenger's Own Table**

The Qur'an makes the same point in the mouth of the objectors, who treat it as a scandal: *"We never sent any messenger before you ˹O Prophet˺, who did not eat food and go about in market-places."* (25:20). The objection and the command are about the same fact. What the chiefs offered as a disqualification, this verse gives as an instruction.

**The Command to Eat, With a Limit Attached**

Ṭā-Hā gives the same command to the Israelites with the boundary attached: *"˹saying,˺ 'Eat from the good things We have provided for you, but do not transgress in them, or My wrath will befall you. And whoever My wrath befalls is certainly doomed.'"* (20:81). An-Naḥl adds the response the provision calls for: *"So eat from the good, lawful things which Allah has provided for you, and be grateful for Allah's favours, if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺."* (16:114).

The limit in Ṭā-Hā is attached to the eating itself, not to something else. What makes the provision dangerous is not its quantity but the manner of taking it, and the warning is placed inside the command rather than after it.

Al-Aʿrāf puts the refusal as a question: *"Who has forbidden the adornments and lawful provisions Allah has brought forth for His servants?"* (7:32).

---

## Verse 23:52

> Surely this religion of yours is ˹only˺ one, and I am your Lord, so fear Me ˹alone˺

**One Ummah**

The word is *ummah*, and the verse makes it singular: this community of yours is one community. The claim is not that all the messengers' followers have been one group in history. It is that what they were given is one thing.

The sūrah of al-Anbiyāʾ states the same fact with a nearly identical sentence: *"˹O prophets!˺ Indeed, this religion of yours is ˹only˺ one, and I am your Lord, so worship Me ˹alone˺."* (21:92). The two verses differ in one word, and the near-identity is itself the evidence for the claim: a sentence about the unity of the message appears twice in the Book in the same form, addressed both times to the messengers as a group.

**"So Fear Me" Instead of "So Worship Me"**

The one word that differs between this verse and its parallel in al-Anbiyāʾ is the command at the end. There it is *faʿbudūn*, so worship Me; here it is *fattaqūn*, so fear Me, or be mindful of Me.

The two sūrahs therefore give the same claim with a different emphasis, and the difference tracks the material that follows in each. Al-Anbiyāʾ goes on to describe the return; this one goes on to describe the sects.

**The Same Sentence in al-Anbiyāʾ, and What Follows It**

The parallel verse in al-Anbiyāʾ is followed by the same observation this sūrah makes: *"Yet the people have divided it into sects. But to Us they will all return."* (21:93). The two sūrahs give the unity, the division and the return in the same order, with almost the same words.

---

## Verse 23:53

> Yet the people have divided it into different sects, each rejoicing in what they have

**The Division Follows the Gift**

The sequence is the verse's sharpest point. The people divided *it* — the religion just described as one — and the division happened after the giving, not before it.

The Book makes the same observation repeatedly and treats it as a pattern rather than an accident. Āl ʿImrān: *"Certainly, Allah's only Way is Islam. Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute ˹among themselves˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them."* (3:19). Ash-Shūrā gives the motive and the timing together: *"They did not split ˹into sects˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them."* (42:14). Al-Bayyinah says the People of the Book became divided only after the clear proof came to them (98:4). In every case the division is downstream of the revelation, and the cause named is not ignorance but rivalry.

The pattern removes the most sympathetic explanation available. A community that splits because it never received the message is a community to be instructed; a community that splits because it did is a community with a different problem.

**"Each Rejoicing in What They Have"**

The second clause describes the emotional state of the factions, and it is the diagnosis rather than the description. They are not only divided; each party is pleased with its portion.

Ar-Rūm records the same condition with almost the same words: *"˹like˺ those who have divided their faith and split into sects, each rejoicing in what they have."* (30:32). The rejoicing is what makes the division stable. A split that produced grief would be a split people tried to repair; a split that produces satisfaction is a split that will last. The verse identifies the pleasure as the mechanism, and the pleasure is not in the truth of the portion but in having a portion that is one's own.

**The Prophet's Distance From the Sects**

Al-Anʿām states the consequence for the messenger: *"Indeed, you ˹O Prophet˺ are not responsible whatsoever for those who have divided their faith and split into sects. Their judgment rests only with Allah. And He will inform them of what they used to do."* (6:159).

That verse belongs beside this one because it draws the line the sūrah implies. The messenger is not answerable for the fragmentation, and he is not authorised to resolve it. The verse in al-Muʾminūn describes the division without prescribing a remedy, and the reason is given in al-Anʿām: the matter has been referred. A reader looking for an instruction about how to reunite the sects will not find one here; he will find a description of what they have done and a statement of where the judgement sits.

**Do Not Be Like Those Who Split**

Āl ʿImrān turns the observation into a prohibition addressed to the believers: *"And do not be like those who split ˹into sects˺ and differed after clear proofs had come to them. It is they who will suffer a tremendous punishment."* (3:105).

---

## Verse 23:54

> So leave them ˹O Prophet˺ in their heedlessness for a while

**An Instruction, Not an Abandonment**

The verse tells the Prophet to stop, and the stopping is a command rather than a concession. He is not permitted to withdraw out of exhaustion; he is instructed to withdraw at a specified point.

**Parallel Instructions Elsewhere**

The Book gives the same instruction in other places and with the same structure. Al-Anʿām records it twice: *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Allah ˹revealed it˺!' Then leave them to amuse themselves with falsehood."* (6:91), and, *"We turn their hearts and eyes away ˹from the truth˺ as they refused to believe at first, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance."* (6:110).

Al-Anʿām describes the wandering as a result rather than a punishment imposed from outside. The refusal produces the inability to hear, and the inability to hear makes the refusal permanent. The verse in al-Muʾminūn describes the end of that loop from the messenger's side.

**What the Prophet Is Relieved Of**

Al-Anʿām states the relief explicitly: the Prophet is not responsible for those who divide their faith and split into sects (6:159). The instruction here is the same relief in a different form. The messenger's commission is to deliver, and once the delivery has been refused for long enough, the remaining part of the matter is not his. The verse gives him permission to stop, and the permission is a mercy to him rather than a judgement on them.

**Leave Them to Their Falsehood**

The parallel in al-Anʿām names what they are left with: *"Then leave them to amuse themselves with falsehood."* (6:91). The word for amuse is the significant one — the falsehood is not something they hold solemnly but something they play with.

That detail belongs beside the heedlessness in this verse. The people being left are not described as searching or struggling; they are described as occupied with something that entertains them. The verse in al-Muʾminūn says they are submerged, and al-Anʿām says what they are doing in the water. The two descriptions together are the sūrah's account of a community that has stopped being reachable: not hostile, not searching, but absorbed.

The sūrah's own audience knew the type. The chiefs who called the Prophet a fabricator were not ignorant of the accounts he recited; they had heard them and gone back to what they were doing.

---

## Verse 23:55

> Do they think, since We provide them with wealth and children

**A Verse That Deliberately Does Not Finish**

The incompleteness is unusual enough to be worth naming. Qur'anic verses routinely end mid-thought, but usually where a clause boundary falls naturally. Here the suspension is inside a single construction: the verb of thinking has no object.

**Why These Two Things, Here**

The verse arrives immediately after the instruction to leave the heedless in their heedlessness. The connection is the reason the two things are named.

A community left to itself does not usually fall into visible misery. It prospers, and the prosperity is read as confirmation that the leaving was justified. The sūrah has described the mechanism before: warnings are ignored, then everything desired is given, and the recipients conclude that the silence of heaven is approval (6:44). The verse names the two items that make the conclusion feel inevitable, and it names them at the point where the reader is most likely to draw it.

Al-Anʿām describes the sequence in full: warnings ignored, then everything desired given, then pride, then seizure. The verse here names the middle stage, which is the stage that feels like evidence of favour.

**The Question Addressed to the Reader**

Holding the question is the intended outcome. A person who has stopped reading his circumstances as a verdict has not acquired new information; he has lost a habit, and the loss is what the verse is after.

**Provision Given to Whoever He Wills**

Az-Zumar states the principle that answers the assumption: *"Do they not know that Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills? Surely in this are signs for people who believe."* (39:52). The same sūrah describes the misreading in a single scene: *"When one is touched with hardship, they cry out to Us ˹alone˺. Then when We shower Our blessings upon them, they say, 'I have been granted all this only because of ˹my˺ knowledge.' Not at all! It is ˹no more than˺ a test. But most of them do not know."* (39:49).

---

## Verse 23:56

> that We hasten to ˹honour˺ them ˹with˺ all kinds of good? No! They are not aware

**The Same Word Used for the Opposite Case**

The verb in the question — *nusāriʿu*, We hasten — is worth holding on to, because the sūrah uses the same idea for the believers a few verses later. There the haste is real: they race toward good deeds. Here the haste is imagined.

**The Answer in One Word**

*Kallā* — no, not so, far from it. The particle is not a simple negation; it is a rejection of the premise, used where the statement being denied should not have been thinkable in the first place.

**Provision as a Test, Not a Verdict**

The Book states the principle that the verse assumes. Ash-Shūrā explains why provision is measured rather than abundant: *"Had Allah given abundant provisions to ˹all˺ His servants, they would have certainly transgressed throughout the land. But He sends down whatever He wills in perfect measure. He is truly All-Aware, All-Seeing of His servants."* (42:27).

The principle also runs the other way, and the sūrah means it to. A person given little has not been rated low; he has been given what he can carry. Both readings of circumstances as verdict are removed by the same sentence, and the verse removes the second only by implication.

**The Mechanism of Being Led On**

Al-Anʿām describes what the provision does to a people who refuse the warnings: *"When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair!"* (6:44).

---

## Verse 23:57

> Surely those who tremble in awe of their Lord

**Mushfiqūn: Fear Mixed With Tenderness**

The word is *mushfiqūn*, from a root whose sense is fear combined with weakness and tenderness — the anxiety of someone who cares about the outcome and is not certain of it. Al-Rāzī describes it as fear with softness in it, and records the reading that takes this awe as the highest form of the fear of God, because it is produced by vigilance rather than by terror.

**The Fear That Comes From Vigilance**

The commentators' point about the origin of this awe is the key to the verse. It is described as the result of persistence in obedience — a fear that grows out of practice rather than out of a single overwhelming experience.

**The Same Word for the Angels**

The Qur'an uses the same word for the angels' relation to God: *"He ˹fully˺ knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them. They do not intercede except for whom He approves, and they tremble in awe of Him."* (21:28). The beings furthest from disobedience are described with the word for careful fear.

**Awe as the Condition of Everything Else**

Az-Zumar describes the physical effect of the same state: *"˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ has sent down the best message—a Book of perfect consistency and repeated lessons—which causes the skin ˹and hearts˺ of those who fear their Lord to tremble, then their skin and hearts soften at the mention of ˹the mercy of˺ Allah."* (39:23).

---

## Verse 23:58

> and who believe in the revelations of their Lord

**Āyāt: The Word With Two Objects**

The word is *āyāt*, and it is one of the Qur'an's most consequential terms precisely because it has two referents. An *āyah* is a verse of the Book, and an *āyah* is a created thing that points to God. The same word covers both, and the commentators take it here in the second sense — the created signs, all of which indicate their Maker.

The double sense is not an ambiguity to be resolved but a claim being made. A revelation and a mountain are the same kind of thing in the Qur'an's vocabulary: both are signs, and both are read. The verse describes people who believe in the signs, and the description covers the Book and the world at once, because the Book is a sign about the world and the world is a sign of what the Book says.

**The Signs Already Listed in This Sūrah**

An-Naḥl closes its catalogue of provision with the same statement: *"And He has subjected for your benefit the day and the night, the sun and the moon. And the stars have been subjected by His command. Surely in this are signs for those who understand."* (16:12). And on the rain: *"And Allah sends down rain from the sky, giving life to the earth after its death. Surely in this is a sign for those who listen."* (16:65).

**The Signs That Require a Reader**

Ar-Raʿd puts the same point as a series of questions: *"Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, 'Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth?' Say, 'Allah!' Ask ˹them˺, 'Why ˹then˺ have you taken besides Him lords who cannot even benefit or protect themselves?' Say, 'Can the blind and the sighted be equal? Or can darkness and light be equal?'"* (13:16).

---

## Verse 23:59

> and who associate none with their Lord

**The Negation Stated Flatly**

The item is a simple negative: they do not associate partners with their Lord. No qualification, no explanation, no reference to the idols of any particular people. The verse states the condition in the barest form available.

**The Command Not to Associate**

Al-Anʿām states the prohibition at the head of its list of commands: *"Say, ˹O Prophet˺, 'Come! Let me recite to you what your Lord has forbidden to you: do not associate others with Him ˹in worship˺.'"* (6:151). The prohibition comes first, before the honour owed to parents, before the prohibition on killing, before the rest.

That ordering matches the position of this item in the sūrah's list. The refusal to associate is not one command among many; it is the condition that makes the others meaningful. The sūrah of al-Muʾminūn states it as a description of a kind of person, and al-Anʿām states it as the first thing God has forbidden. The two formulations are the same claim made in different registers.

The same chapter states the prohibition again at the end of its list of commands, closing with the path itself: *"Indeed, that is My Path—perfectly straight. So follow it and do not follow other ways, for they will lead you away from His Way."* (6:153). The plurality of ways is the danger named, and the plurality is what the sects have produced.

---

## Verse 23:60

> and who do whatever ˹good˺ they do with their hearts fearful, ˹knowing˺ that they will return to their Lord—

**The Deed and the Heart Named Together**

The verse describes a deed and the state of the person doing it in the same clause. Whatever they give — or whatever good they do — they do it while their hearts are trembling. The two halves are inseparable in the sentence.

**"Knowing That They Will Return"**

The reason for the fear is given, and it is not the fear of punishment. It is the knowledge of the return — *ilā rabbihim rājiʿūn*, to their Lord they are returning.

The phrasing matters. A person who fears punishment fears an outcome; a person who knows he is returning fears an encounter. The verse describes the second. What makes the heart tremble is not the prospect of a penalty but the prospect of being fully known by the one the deed was done for. Al-Baqarah gives the same formula in the mouth of the patient at the moment of disaster: *"Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return."* (2:156). The verse here applies it at the moment of a good deed, which is the harder application.

**The Return That Settles the Matter**

Al-Anbiyāʾ ends its account of the divided communities with the same statement: *"Yet the people have divided it into sects. But to Us they will all return."* (21:93). The return is offered there as the answer to the division, and here it is the reason for the trembling heart.

---

## Verse 23:61

> it is they who race to do good deeds, always taking the lead

**The Sentence That Completes the List**

The verse finishes what the previous verse began. The four descriptions — awe, belief in the signs, refusal to associate, deeds done with trembling hearts — were left without a predicate, and this verse supplies it. Those people are the ones who race toward good deeds and are foremost in them.

The sūrah has already described haste in this passage, and it described it as a delusion. The deniers believed good was being hurried to them. Here the haste is real, and it belongs to the people doing the hurrying.

**Sābiqūn: The Foremost**

Racing describes effort; leading describes position. A person can exert himself and still be behind, and the verse says these people are both exerting and ahead. The commentators connect the word to a rank rather than to a quantity of deeds — the *sābiqūn* are those at the highest level, and the term appears elsewhere with that sense.

Al-Wāqiʿah states the connection plainly: *"and the foremost ˹in faith˺ will be the foremost ˹in Paradise˺. They are the ones nearest ˹to Allah˺,"* (56:10-11). The precedence in this world is the precedence in the next, and the verse in al-Muʾminūn describes the first of the two.

**The Foremost in al-Wāqiʿah**

At-Tawbah uses the same word for a specific group: *"As for the foremost—the first of the Emigrants and the Helpers—and those who follow them in goodness, Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him."* (9:100). The term there names people, and it names them by their precedence in a particular event.

---

## Verse 23:62

> We never require of any soul more than what it can afford. And with Us is a record which speaks the truth. None will be wronged

**The Same Principle Across the Book**

The formula recurs, and its recurrence establishes it as a rule rather than a reassurance. Al-Baqarah: *"Allah does not burden any soul with more than it can bear."* (2:286). Al-Anʿām states it inside a list of commands about measure, weight and speech (6:152). Al-Aʿrāf states it inside a description of the residents of Paradise (7:42).

The sūrah of al-Muʾminūn states it after describing the believers' deeds, which is where a reader is most likely to wonder whether the standard can be met. The verse answers before the question is asked.

The recurrence also removes the suspicion that the principle is a softening applied to difficult cases. It appears in al-Baqarah's summary of belief, in al-Anʿām's commercial ethics and in al-Aʿrāf's description of Paradise, and it is the same sentence each time.

**A Record That Speaks the Truth**

The second clause introduces a book, and the description given to it is unusual: a record that speaks with truth, or by truth. The Book is not described as complete or as sealed but as truthful, which is a claim about its character rather than its contents.

Al-Jāthiyah uses almost the same phrase: *"This record of Ours speaks the truth about you. Indeed, We always had your deeds recorded ˹by the angels˺."* (45:29). The two verses agree that the record's distinguishing property is accuracy, and the accuracy is what makes it a record rather than an accusation.

Truthfulness is also the property a record most needs. A complete record could still be inaccurate; an accurate one that omits nothing is what the two verses describe between them, and the sūrah's emphasis falls on the accuracy.

Al-Kahf states the same thing at the moment the record is opened, and it states it as the final clause of the scene: *"And your Lord will never wrong anyone."* (18:49).

**The Record From Which They Read**

Al-Kahf describes the scene at length: *"And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it. They will cry, 'Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?' They will find whatever they did present ˹before them˺. And your Lord will never wrong anyone."* (18:49).

The parallel supplies the detail this verse compresses. The record is laid open; the wicked read it; their reaction is described in their own words; and the assurance about injustice closes the scene exactly as it closes the verse here. The sūrah of al-Muʾminūn gives the principle, and al-Kahf gives the moment it is applied.

The two verses also agree on where the assurance sits. Al-Kahf places it after the wicked have read their record and cried out; this verse places it before any deed is mentioned. The same sentence does the same work at both ends of the accounting.

---

## Verse 23:63

> But the hearts of those ˹who disbelieve˺ are oblivious to ˹all of˺ this, and they have other ˹evil˺ deeds, opposite to this, in which they are engrossed

**Ghamrah Again: The Submerged Heart**

The word is the same one the sūrah used for the heedlessness it told the Prophet to leave alone. There it described a people; here it describes hearts. The image is identical — water deep enough to cover a person — and the shift from the community to the organ is the verse's contribution.

**Engrossed: The Verb of Occupancy**

Al-Kahf describes the same condition from the other side: *"We have certainly cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend this ˹Quran˺—and deafness in their ears."* (18:57). The veils and the deafness are what make the occupancy possible; the occupancy is what keeps the veils in place.

The two verses describe a closed circuit, and the circuit is the sūrah's account of how a person becomes unreachable. Nothing outside it can get in, because everything that would have carried meaning has been displaced by the other deeds.

**Hearts Covered Elsewhere**

Al-Jāthiyah describes the condition in its fullest form: *"Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god? ˹And so˺ Allah left them to stray knowingly, sealed their hearing and hearts, and placed a cover on their sight."* (45:23).

The verse supplies what this one leaves implicit. The other deeds are the desires taken as a god, and the sealing is the result rather than the cause. The sūrah of al-Muʾminūn describes the occupancy; al-Jāthiyah describes what the occupancy eventually produces, and both agree that the person inside it does not notice.

---

## Verse 23:64

> But as soon as We seize their elite with torment, they start to cry for help

**Mutrafūn: The Ones Given Ease**

The word is *mutrafūn*, from a root meaning to be given ease or luxury, and it describes people whose comfort has become their condition. The verse does not name the punishment's cause; it names the people it reaches first.

Sabaʾ records the same group making the same refusal: *"Whenever We sent a warner to a society, its elite would say, 'We truly reject what you have been sent with.'"* (34:34). The verse there names the elite as the ones who speak, and this verse names them as the ones seized.

**The Cry for Help**

The sūrah distinguishes the two elsewhere. Nūḥ's cry came after centuries of refusal by others, and it was a request for a decision. This cry comes after the decision has been made, and it asks for the decision to be reversed.

**The Elite in Sabaʾ**

The parallel supplies the social detail this verse compresses into a single word. The *mutrafūn* are not an abstract class; they are the men whose comfort made the messenger's message inconvenient, and whose inconvenience became the community's position. The verse in al-Muʾminūn names them and then describes what happens when the comfort stops.

---

## Verse 23:65

> ˹They will be told,˺ “Do not cry for help today. Surely you will never be saved from Us

**"Today"**

The word does the most work in the sentence. Today — the day the cry is being made — is distinguished from the days before it, and the distinction is the whole of the verse's argument.

The sūrah has spent its length on the interval: the term that cannot be moved, the time the messengers were to be left, the period during which the hearts were submerged. All of that was "before today". The verse names the day on which the arrangement changes, and it names it from inside.

That is what makes the word severe. A reader outside the day can still act on it. A reader being addressed as living inside it has been told that the period in which his cry would have been heard has closed, and the telling comes too late to be acted on.

**The Reversal of the Cry**

The sūrah has recorded cries before, and the reversal here is exact. Nūḥ cried for help and was answered; the people destroyed by the blast were overtaken by a cry; the elite here cry and are told to stop.

---

## Verse 23:66

> Indeed, My revelations were recited to you, but you used to back away ˹in disgust˺

**Āyāt Recited, Not Given**

The verse specifies the medium. The revelations were *recited* — spoken aloud, in the hearing of the people addressed. They were not written on a tablet, not left in a place to be found, not delivered to someone else to summarise.

The specification is the ground of the charge that follows. A person who was never told cannot be accused of turning away; a person who heard and moved back can. The sūrah has used the same word for the signs given to Mūsā, where the signs were shown rather than spoken, and the difference is deliberate: this community's evidence was audible.

The verb is in a form that indicates repeated or customary action. They *used to* turn back — not once, not sometimes, but as a settled practice.

**Recitation as the Medium**

Al-Isrāʾ describes what happens during the recitation: *"When you ˹O Prophet˺ recite the Quran, We put a hidden barrier between you and those who do not believe in the Hereafter. We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to understand it—and deafness in their ears."* (17:45-46).

---

## Verse 23:67

> boasting of the Sacred House, and babbling ˹nonsense about the Quran˺ by night.”

The verse names what the boasting was about, and the object is the Sacred House. The people addressed were the custodians of the Kaʿbah, and their standing in Arabia rested on that custody.

**Sāmir: The Night Gathering**

The word translated as babbling is *sāmir*, and it describes people gathered at night talking — a specific social practice, not a general habit of speech. The commentators identify the gathering precisely: the idolaters met in the environs of the Kaʿbah after dark.

The place is the sharpest detail. The assembly met beside the House they were boasting about, which means the contempt was produced at the site of the authority the contempt was defending. The verse names the irony by naming the location.

**Talk in Paradise**

At-Ṭūr describes the converse scene: *"They will pass around to each other a drink ˹of pure wine,˺ which leads to no idle talk or sinfulness."* (52:23). The absence of idle talk is named as a property of the reward, and its naming is a direct answer to this verse.

---

## Verse 23:68

> Is it because they have never contemplated the Word ˹of Allah˺? Or ˹because˺ there has come to them something that did not come to their forefathers

**The Word**

The verse calls the Qur'an *al-qawl*, the Word or the Saying, and the choice of word is worth holding. The sūrah has used *āyāt* for the revelations that were recited; here it uses a term whose ordinary sense is speech, something said by someone.

That ordinary sense is what the question depends on. A person does not contemplate a phenomenon; he contemplates a statement, and a statement can be examined for consistency, tested against what he already knows, and checked against itself.

An-Nisāʾ makes the same invitation and gives the test: *"Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies."* (4:82). The verse offers the Word as an object capable of failing an examination, which is what makes the invitation to examine it meaningful.

**Tadabbur: Contemplation**

The verb is from a root meaning to consider the end or the consequence of a thing — to follow it through. It is not the word for quick reading or for listening, and it is not the word for believing.

**"That Which Came Not to Their Forefathers"**

Yā-Sīn supplies the context: the Prophet was sent *"so that you may warn a people whose forefathers were not warned, and so are heedless."* (36:6). The absence of earlier warning is the reason the warning is being given now, and the verse there treats it as the ground of the mission rather than as an objection to it.

---

## Verse 23:69

> Or ˹because˺ they failed to recognize their Messenger, and so they denied him

**Not a Stranger**

The verse proposes an explanation for the denial and immediately makes it implausible. Did they not know their Messenger? The possessive is the point — *their* Messenger, a man who belonged to the community that was denying him.

**Al-Amīn**

The epithet recorded in the biographical tradition is *al-Amīn*, the Trustworthy, and it was given to him by the same people who later called him a fabricator and a madman. The title is not a Muslim attribution; it is what his own community called him.

**Knowledge Without Recognition**

The verse distinguishes between knowing a person and recognising what he carries. The chiefs knew the man; the question is whether they recognised the message, and the verse's grammar connects the two with a consequence: they did not know him, *and so* they denied him.

The commentators read the connection as ironic. The men who denied him knew him better than anyone, and the denial was not a failure of information. The verse proposes ignorance as an explanation to close it off.

That is the same method the previous verse used with contemplation. Did they not contemplate the Word? The question is not asking for information; it is eliminating an excuse.

**The Trustworthiness Recorded Elsewhere**

The Qur'an makes the same appeal through the mouths of earlier messengers. Hūd said to his people: *"I am truly a trustworthy messenger to you."* (26:107), and the same phrase appears in the accounts of Nūḥ, Ṣāliḥ, Lūṭ and Shuʿayb in that sūrah.

---

## Verse 23:70

> Or ˹because˺ they say, “He is insane?” In fact, he has come to them with the truth, but most of them are resentful of the truth

**Majnūn: The Fourth Explanation**

The verse gives the last of the four explanations the sūrah examines, and it is the bluntest. The charge is *majnūn* — had, mad, a man acted on by something other than himself.

The sūrah has already recorded the same charge twice in the mouths of earlier peoples. Nūḥ's chiefs said of him: *"This is only a human like you, who wants to be superior to you,"* (23:24), and then: *"He is simply insane, so bear with him for a while."* (23:25). Pharaoh said of Mūsā: *"Your messenger, who has been sent to you, must be insane."* (26:27).

**"He Has Come to Them With the Truth"**

The sūrah has spent the preceding verses eliminating every alternative explanation. The Word was recited and not contemplated. The novelty was turned into the reason for the message. The messenger was known and trusted. What remains after those eliminations is the assertion, and the assertion stands on the elimination.

That method is unusual and worth naming. The verse does not prove the truth of the message; it removes the reasons for denying it, and then states the message's character as what is left.

**Kārihūn: Resentful of the Truth**

The closing clause names the actual reason, and the word is *kārihūn* — people who are averse, who find the thing distasteful. The commentators specify the causes: jealousy, and attachment to inherited custom.

That diagnosis explains everything the sūrah has recorded. Men who find the truth distasteful will produce explanations for it, and the explanations will be the ones available: novelty, madness, magic, poetry. The sūrah has now listed four and closed all four.

The commentators' two causes — jealousy and attachment to custom — cover both directions. Jealousy is about the messenger; custom is about the community. Between them they account for the personal and the social halves of the refusal.

**The Charge Elsewhere**

Sabaʾ puts the two charges together in a single question: *"Has he fabricated a lie against Allah or is he insane?"* (34:8). Ad-Dukhān records the same pair: *"then they turned away from him, saying, 'A madman, taught by others!'"* (44:14).

The three sūrahs give the same two accusations in the same order, and the pairing is instructive. Fabrication implies intent; insanity removes it. The two charges cannot both be true, and the objectors offer both, which is the clearest available evidence that neither is being put forward as a considered judgement.

The sūrah of al-Muʾminūn adds the third charge from the same repertoire — taught by others, or a fabricator — and the three together form a set. Each is available, none is evidenced, and any of them will do.

---

## Verse 23:71

> Had the truth followed their desires, the heavens, the earth, and all those in them would have certainly been corrupted. In fact, We have brought them ˹the means to˺ their glory, but they turn away from it

**Desire as Chaos**

The reasoning behind the conditional is worth stating, because the verse does not state it. Human desires are not only various; they conflict. What one person wants excludes what another wants, and a reality arranged around wanting would be arranged around contradiction.

Al-Anbiyāʾ makes the same argument about the divine order: *"Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted."* (21:22). The structure of the two verses is identical — a plurality of wills produces corruption, and the existing order is evidence against it.

Corruption is the word both verses use, and it is stronger than disorder. Disorder can be rearranged; corruption is a thing losing the properties that made it what it was. The heavens and the earth in the conditional are not only badly run — they are undone.

The phrase also names what has been done for them. The reminder was brought; the bringing is complete; and the turning away described in the clause that follows is theirs.

**What the Verse Answers**
The verse is one of the sūrah's answers to the suspicion with which the community's opponents held the message: had the truth followed their own wishes, the heavens and the earth and everything in them would have fallen apart. The Book uses the same argument in the Sūrah of the Story about the wish of the people for a sign of their own choosing (28:80-82), and in the Sūrah of the Flood, where the people's demand for the punishment is answered by the statement that God did not make the world to satisfy their appetites. The argument rests on the idea that the order of the world is not a committee decision.
**The Word for Desire**
The Book speaks of *ahwāʾ*, the plural wishes a person carries, and the contrast it draws is between a world built on desire and a world built on truth. The Sūrah of the Cave warns against following one's own inclination in the matter of guidance (18:28-29), and the Sūrah of the Worshipers describes the opposite disposition, where a person gives up his own wish for the sake of God. The verse in front of us states the consequence of the other arrangement in physical terms: the sky and the earth would come apart, which is the same picture the Book uses in the Sūrah of the Prophets when it describes the sky being rolled up like a scroll on that day (21:104).
**Where the Passage Is Standing**
The verse before it describes the community that was given the Book and the men who hold to the prayer, and the verse after it asks the Prophet to call people to the way of God and to stand by it as he was commanded. The sūrah has just finished the long passage on the qualities of the believers and the account of the messengers, and the sentence about desire is placed at the hinge where the sūrah turns from the signs to the duty.

---

## Verse 23:72

> Or ˹is it because˺ you ˹O Prophet˺ are asking them for tribute? But the reward of your Lord is best, for He is the Best Provider

The second clause states what the Prophet has instead of a payment, and the statement is comparative rather than absolute. His Lord's reward is *better* — better than what any audience could give.

**The Fifth Question**

The verse continues the series of questions, and it introduces a new kind of objection. The previous four concerned the message, its novelty, the messenger's identity and his sanity. This one concerns money.

That makes the question rhetorical in the strictest sense. It proposes an explanation that the people proposing it know to be false, and the proposing is a way of closing off the explanation before anyone can use it.

**No Payment: The Messengers' Standing Refusal**

The refusal of payment is one of the Qur'an's most repeated statements, and its repetition is the point. Hūd says to his people: *"O my people! I do not ask you for a payment for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from Allah."* (11:29). The same sentence appears in the mouths of Nūḥ, Ibrāhīm's people's messengers, Lūṭ and Shuʿayb in ash-Shuʿarāʾ (26:109 and four further places).

Sabaʾ puts it as a conditional: *"Say, 'If I had ever asked you for a reward, you could keep it. My reward is only from Allah. And He is a Witness over all things.'"* (34:47). Ṣād adds a second denial beside it: *"I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹Quran˺, nor do I pretend to be someone I am not."* (38:86).

The consistency across so many messengers establishes that the refusal is part of the office rather than a personal choice, and the verse here states it for the Prophet in the same terms.

**Why the Refusal Matters**

A messenger who is paid can be dismissed as an interested party. The refusal removes that dismissal, and the sūrah's question is aimed at exactly this.

Yā-Sīn turns the refusal into a criterion for the hearers: *"Follow those who ask no reward of you, and are ˹rightly˺ guided."* (36:21). The verse there makes the absence of payment a mark to look for, which means its presence would be a mark against.

Yā-Sīn's formulation is the sharpest version of the point. It makes the absence of payment something to look for in a guide, which means its presence would be a disqualification. The verse here relies on the same standard without stating it.

**"The Reward of Your Lord Is Best"**

The clause also answers the objection from the other direction. A man seeking wealth would seek it from the wealthy, and the Prophet's refusal of the wealthy men's money is evidence about where he expects his return to come from.

---

## Verse 23:73

> And surely you are calling them to the Straight Path

**Ṣirāṭ Mustaqīm: The Straight Path**

The phrase is the one every Muslim recites daily in the opening sūrah: *"Guide us along the Straight Path,"* (1:6). The verse here states that the Prophet is calling to it, which connects his mission to the prayer the community makes.

That description answers an objection the sūrah has been addressing. A message requiring special knowledge or special status could be dismissed as belonging to somebody else. A straight, wide road is available to anyone standing on it or beside it.

**Straightness as the Defining Property**

The adjective is what the verse insists on, and straightness has a specific implication. A straight path does not require interpretation to follow; it requires only that a person not leave it.

Al-Fātiḥah's continuation describes the path by whose path it is: *"the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray."* (1:7). The straight path is identified by the people on it, and the sūrah of al-Muʾminūn has spent its length describing both groups.

The identification by company rather than by rule is worth noting. The path is not defined by a list of requirements but by the people walking it, and the sūrah has described those people in detail — the ones who tremble, who read the signs, who race toward good.

**The Path in Other Places**

Al-Anʿām states the same claim in the form of a command: the straight path is God's, and the Prophet is told to follow it and not to follow other ways, because they lead away from His (6:153). The plurality of other ways is contrasted with the singularity of the straight one.

The verse in al-Anʿām also states that the other ways lead away from God's way, which is what the following verse in al-Muʾminūn describes as deviation. The two sūrahs give the same account in different registers — one as a command, the other as an observation.

---

## Verse 23:74

> but those who disbelieve in the Hereafter are certainly deviating from that Path

**Disbelief in the Hereafter as the Cause**

The specific item named is disbelief in the Hereafter, and the choice is precise. Of all the things the audience disputed, this is the one the verse identifies as decisive.

The reasoning is not spelled out, and it does not need to be. A person who does not believe the matter will be settled has no reason to leave a comfortable position. The path is straight and visible, as the previous verse says; leaving it requires a reason, and the absence of a Hereafter supplies one.

The sūrah has described this disbelief elsewhere in the passage. The barrier placed during the recitation is tied to disbelief in the Hereafter in al-Isrāʾ (17:45), and Sabaʾ says of those who make the charges of fabrication and madness that they do not believe in the Hereafter (34:8).

**The Verse's Address to the Sects**

The passage began with a description of communities that divided the religion into portions, each rejoicing in what it had. The verse here describes deviation from a single path, and the two descriptions are related.

The two accounts answer each other. The sects are described as rejoicing in what they have; the deviators are described as leaning away from a single road. Both descriptions are about people who have substituted something of their own for what was given.

**Disbelief in the Hereafter Elsewhere**

Sabaʾ links the same disbelief to the same charges: *"Has he fabricated a lie against Allah or is he insane?" In fact, those who do not believe in the Hereafter are bound for torment and have strayed farthest ˹from the truth˺."* (34:8).

The parallel is close enough to be a companion verse. It gives the charges this passage has just examined, and it names the same underlying disbelief as the cause. The two sūrahs agree that the accusations are downstream of a position about the Hereafter, and that the position is what produces them.

Al-Isrāʾ makes the same connection during the recitation itself, describing the barrier as falling between the Prophet and those who do not believe in the Hereafter (17:45). The three verses describe one condition from three angles.

---

## Verse 23:75

> ˹Even˺ if We had mercy on them and removed their affliction, they would still persist in their transgression, wandering blindly

**A Mercy That Would Change Nothing**

The verse describes a hypothetical act of mercy and states its result in advance. The affliction is removed; the people persist. The mercy is real, the relief is real, and the outcome is unchanged.

The commentators take the affliction as hunger and worldly hardship, or as trials such as warfare. Both readings support the same point, and the verse's wording does not require a choice.

**Az-Zukhruf: The Same Sequence**

The Qur'an records the sequence as an event elsewhere: *"But as soon as We removed the torments from them, they broke their promise."* (43:50). The verse there is past tense and describes a people who were relieved and then reneged.

The two verses belong together. Az-Zukhruf supplies the precedent; this verse states what would happen again. A reader who has read both understands that the counterfactual is not speculative — it is a description of a pattern that has already been observed.

Applying it requires no special knowledge. The audience had seen the affliction lifted and had watched what followed, which is exactly the sequence az-Zukhruf records for an earlier people.

**Wandering Blindly**

The closing phrase describes the manner of the persistence: wandering in confusion, or blind wandering. The commentators connect it to a state of spiritual blindness, and the phrase appears in several places with that sense.

Al-Baqarah uses it of the mockers: *"Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance."* (2:15). Al-Anʿām uses it of those whose hearts and eyes are turned away (6:110). In both cases the wandering follows a refusal rather than preceding it.

**Humility in Affliction**

Al-Anʿām describes the purpose affliction is meant to serve: *"Indeed, We have sent messengers before you ˹O Prophet˺ to other people who We put through suffering and adversity ˹for their denial˺, so perhaps they would be humbled. Why did they not humble themselves when We made them suffer? Instead, their hearts were hardened."* (6:42-43).

The two verses describe the same arrangement from opposite ends. Affliction is sent so that people may be humbled; this verse says the humility would not come, and that relief would not produce it either. Between them they describe a condition in which neither hardship nor ease changes the outcome.

---

## Verse 23:76

> And We have already seized them with torment, but they never humbled themselves to their Lord, nor did they ˹submissively˺ appeal ˹to Him˺

**The Torment Already Sent**

The verse is past tense, and the tense is the point. The affliction described in the previous verse as a hypothetical has already happened. The seizure is not a warning about the future; it is a record.

**They Did Not Become Humble**

The verb is *yataḍarraʿū*, from a root meaning to humble oneself, to make oneself small. The verse says they did not do it, and the negation is attached to a specific response rather than to belief in general.

Humility here is not a feeling. It is a posture toward God produced by hardship, and the sūrah has already described the purpose affliction serves: *"so perhaps they would be humbled"* (6:42). The verse reports that the purpose was not achieved.

Al-Anʿām continues with the consequence: *"Instead, their hearts were hardened, and Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them."* (6:43). The two verses describe the same failure and the same result, and the sūrah of al-Muʾminūn stops at the failure.

**The Report About Thumāmah**

Some commentators connect the verse with a particular episode from the Prophet's own time. Thumāmah ibn Uthāl, the chief of Banū Ḥanīfah, accepted Islam and afterwards stopped the food supply reaching Mecca, which produced real hardship in the city. The Quraysh appealed to the Prophet to have the restriction ended, arguing that it breached the agreement made at Ḥudaybiyyah, and he agreed to end it.

**Humility in Affliction Elsewhere**

Al-Aʿrāf states the principle in its general form: *"Whenever We sent a prophet to a society, We afflicted its ˹disbelieving˺ people with suffering and adversity, so perhaps they would be humbled."* (7:94).

The verse supplies the purpose this verse reports as unfulfilled. Affliction is sent with an aim, and the aim is humility rather than destruction. The sūrah of al-Muʾminūn records that the aim was missed, and the next verse describes what follows a missed aim.

---

## Verse 23:77

> But as soon as We open for them a gate of severe punishment, they will be utterly desperate

**A Gate, Not a Door**

The word is *bāb*, a gate, and the image is of something large and hinged — a thing that opens and cannot be shut again from the inside. The verse describes the severe punishment as arriving through a gate, and the gate belongs to God.

The commentators give the gate three referents: the gates of Hell, the conquest of Mecca, and the Muslim victory at Badr. The three differ in time and in kind, and the verse's wording allows all of them.

**Mubsilūn: Utterly Desperate**

The sūrah has already used the same word for the people destroyed by the blast, in al-Anʿām's account: they were seized by surprise and fell instantly into despair (6:44). The parallel is exact, and it places this verse inside the same sequence.

The verse is written so that all three readings produce the same instruction.

**Despair Elsewhere**

Al-Anʿām describes the same ending for an earlier people: *"But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair!"* (6:44).

The parallel supplies the detail this verse compresses. The pride comes first, the seizure follows, and despair is the immediate result. The sūrah of al-Muʾminūn gives the sequence in two verses — the refused humility and the opened gate — and al-Anʿām gives it in one.

The compression in al-Anʿām makes the suddenness visible. Pride, seizure, despair — three states in a single sentence, with nothing narrated between them.

---

## Verse 23:78

> He is the One Who created for you hearing, sight, and intellect. ˹Yet˺ you hardly give any thanks

**Three Faculties, One Order**

The verse lists hearing, sight and hearts, and the order is not arbitrary. Hearing comes first, and the sūrah's audience had a specific reason to notice that: the revelation came to them by recitation, through the ear.

The three are named together in several places, and the grouping treats them as a single set of instruments. Al-Anʿām asks: *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Consider! If Allah were to take away your hearing and your sight and seal your hearts, which god other than Allah could restore them to you?'"* (6:46). As-Sajdah describes them as given: *"Then He proportioned him and breathed into him of His Spirit. And He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect—yet you hardly give any thanks."* (32:9).

**The Faculties Can Be Sealed**

The commentators note that the same three faculties are described elsewhere as subject to sealing, covering and blinding. Al-Isrāʾ describes it during the recitation: *"We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears."* (17:46).

The possibility of sealing is what gives the verse its edge. Faculties that cannot fail are not a charge against anyone. Faculties that can be lost, and that are lost through use, are — and the sūrah has described the loss repeatedly.

**"Little Do You Give Thanks"**

The closing clause is short and quantitative. Not "you do not give thanks" but "little do you give thanks" — the gratitude exists and it is insufficient.

The clause also names the response the provision calls for. The sūrah described the messengers as told to eat the good provision and act righteously, and an-Naḥl attaches gratitude to the eating: *"be grateful for Allah's favours, if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺"* (16:114). The verse here states that the gratitude has not kept pace with the gift.

**The Same Statement in al-Mulk**

Al-Mulk gives the verse in almost identical words: *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'He is the One Who brought you into being and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect. ˹Yet˺ you hardly give any thanks.'"* (67:23).

Al-Mulk adds the bringing into being, which places the faculties inside the larger act of creation. The sūrah of al-Muʾminūn begins with the same material in its opening verses, and the two sūrahs describe the same sequence from the same starting point.

The shared closing clause is the significant part. Both sūrahs end the statement with the same complaint about insufficient thanks, which suggests the formulation was fixed and that the complaint is the point the faculties are listed to support.

---

## Verse 23:79

> And He is the One Who has dispersed you ˹all˺ over the earth, and to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered

**Dharaʾa: Scattered and Multiplied**

The verb is *dharaʾa*, and the commentators give it two related senses: to create, and to spread out or scatter. The verse uses a word that carries both, and the doubling is the point.

Humanity is not only made; it is made in dispersion. The commentators draw the contrast explicitly — scattered across the earth in this life, gathered on the Day of Resurrection even from its farthest corners.

The gathering is described with a direction: to Him. The verse does not describe a place or an assembly; it describes a movement toward a person.

**The Gathering Elsewhere**

The Qur'an states the gathering repeatedly, and the statements agree on its universality. Al-Anʿām: *"And to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered."* The phrase appears as a closing formula across many sūrahs, and its function is always the same — to mark the end of the dispersion.

Al-Kahf describes the scene: *"And ˹remember˺ the Day We will cause the mountains to disappear, and you ˹O humanity˺ will see the earth exposed. And We will gather them all together, leaving none behind."* (18:47). The verse there supplies what this one compresses: the completeness of the gathering.

---

## Verse 23:80

> And He is the One Who gives life and causes death, and to Him belongs the alternation of the day and night. Will you not then understand

**Night and Day in the Passage**

The verse's mention of the night is not incidental to the passage. Two verses earlier the sūrah described the idolaters gathering at night beside the Kaʿbah to produce their account of the Qur'an, and it treated the timing as part of the charge.

The sūrah has described the believers' deeds as done with trembling hearts and the disbelievers' talk as done at night. The verse that governs both time-frames is this one, and it assigns both to the same owner.

**"Will You Not Then Understand?"**

The closing question is the sūrah's direct address, and it follows three statements rather than preceding them. The faculties were given; humanity is scattered and will be gathered; life and death and the alternation belong to Him. The question asks what the reader has made of all of it.

The particle *thumma* — then — marks a sequence. The understanding is not asked for in the abstract; it is asked for after the statements, as the next step. The verse treats understanding as something that follows from what has just been said.

---

## Verse 23:81

> But they ˹just˺ say what their predecessors said

**"The Like of What Was Said"**

The phrase is *mithla mā qāla al-awwalūn* — the like of what the earlier ones said. The word for like allows some variation while insisting on the substance, and the sūrah has recorded enough variation to show what it means.

**A Refusal With a Precedent**

The verse's claim is short and devastating. The objection the Meccans are making is not new. It has been made before, in the same words, by people the sūrah has already described being destroyed for making it.

Nūḥ's chiefs said he wanted superiority. The next generation said obedience to a human like themselves would make them losers. Pharaoh's court said the messengers' people were their slaves. Three formulations, one objection.

The verse follows the three summary verses about the faculties, the dispersion and the alternation, and it follows the question "will you not then understand?" The refusal comes immediately after the invitation.

**The Same Charge Elsewhere**

The accusation that the revelation is nothing but ancient fables appears repeatedly, and the repetitions are the evidence for this verse's claim. Al-Furqān: *"And they say, '˹These revelations are only˺ ancient fables which he has had written down, and they are rehearsed to him morning and evening.'"* (25:5). Al-Qalam: *"whenever Our revelations are recited to him, he says, 'Ancient fables!'"* (68:15).

The two verses give the charge in slightly different settings, and the wording varies. The substance does not. The verse in al-Muʾminūn says the Meccans are saying what the earlier peoples said, and the parallel verses show the charge circulating in the same form across the revelation.

---

## Verse 23:82

> They said, “Once we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected

**The Same Objection Across the Book**

The denial appears in at least eight places, and the wording is remarkably consistent. Al-Isrāʾ: *"When we are reduced to bones and ashes, will we really be raised as a new creation?"* (17:49). Aṣ-Ṣāffāt: *"When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected? And our forefathers as well?"* (37:16-17).

**The Question Quoted in Full**

The verse quotes the objection rather than describing it, and the quotation is the point. The reader is given the actual words, with their incredulous tone intact, and is left to hear what they sound like.

The sūrah has used the same method throughout its account of the chiefs. Their speeches are recorded verbatim — shall we believe in two humans whose people are slaves to us, he is simply insane, these are fables of the ancients. The recording is the argument.

Here the quoted words are a question, and the question's form carries the disbelief. It is not asking for information. It is expressing astonishment that anyone could think so, and the astonishment is directed at the messenger.

Yā-Sīn puts it as a challenge: *"Who will give life to decayed bones?"* (36:78). Qāf states it as a verdict: *"˹Will we be returned to life,˺ when we are dead and reduced to dust? Such a return is impossible."* (50:3). An-Nāziʿāt: *"even after we have been reduced to decayed bones?"* (79:11).

**The Objection's Actual Content**

Stripped of its tone, the objection is an argument from irreversibility. A body that has decomposed cannot be reassembled, and the objection treats the reassembly as the claim being made.

The sūrah answers it by redirecting. The following verses do not argue about decomposition; they ask who owns the earth and the heavens and the dominion of everything. The answer to the objection is not a mechanism but an ownership.

The dissolution is more complete than a refutation would be. A refuted objection can be restated in a new form; an objection that turns out to rest on a false assumption about ownership cannot, because the assumption is what the following verses remove.

**"Will We Really Be Resurrected?"**

The particle of emphasis in the question — *a-innā la-mabʿūthūn* — carries the incredulity, and the sūrah preserves it. The objection is not stated flatly; it is stated with the stress that marks it as absurd in the speaker's own ears.

The tone is also evidence. A person with a reasoned doubt states it plainly; a person expressing contempt stresses it. The verse preserves the stress, and the stress is what the following questions are aimed at.

---

## Verse 23:83

> We have already been promised this, as well as our forefathers earlier. This is nothing but ancient fables!”

**The Appeal to the Forefathers, Reversed**

The objection has two halves, and the second is the significant one. The warning is not new; it was given to their fathers as well. The speakers present this as a reason to dismiss it.

**"We and Our Forefathers"**

That has a consequence the speakers do not seem to notice. If the warning was given to the forefathers, then the forefathers are inside the same situation as the speakers, and the sūrah has already described how that situation ended for them.

**Asāṭīr: Legends of the Ancients**

The word is *asāṭīr*, and the commentators take it as tales or fables — stories written down and repeated, with no claim to truth. The charge is that the revelation is literature.

The consistency across these verses is the evidence for the previous verse's claim. The charge circulates in the same form, and the sūrah says so because it has already shown the same charge in the mouths of the earlier chiefs.

**The Charge as a Way of Not Reading**

The sūrah has described the same avoidance earlier. The people turned away from the recitation; they gathered at night to produce an account of it; they did not contemplate the Word. The legend charge is the output of that process.

---

## Verse 23:84

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “To whom belong the earth and all those on it, if you ˹really˺ know?”

**"If You Know"**

The conditional at the end is not a genuine condition. It is a formula that puts the burden on the person addressed, and the sūrah uses it where the answer is obvious.

The phrase appears again in the third question of the series, and both times it functions the same way. The questioner does not doubt that the answer is known; he is establishing that it is known before the answer is given.

The formula also carries a quiet challenge. To say "if you know" to people who are certain they know is to invite them to show it, and the demonstration turns out to be a single word they cannot refuse to say.

**Why the Earth Comes First**

The order of the three questions is worth noting. The earth and its inhabitants; the seven heavens and the Throne; the dominion of everything. The sequence moves from the near to the far to the total.

---

## Verse 23:85

> They will reply, “To Allah!” Say, “Why are you not then mindful?”

**"Will You Not Then Be Mindful?"**

The follow-up is not a new question but a challenge to draw a conclusion. *Afalā tadhakkarūn* — will you not take heed, or remember, or be mindful?

The method also explains why the sequence has three rounds. A single challenge could be treated as rhetoric; three challenges following three identical answers establish a pattern, and the pattern is what the final round names.

**Tadhakkur: Remembering as a Religious Act**

The verb from the root dh-k-r appears throughout the Qur'an, and its sense is consistently active. Remembering is not a passive state; it is an operation performed on something already had.

The sūrah has described the same failure elsewhere, in the verse asking whether they had contemplated the Word. Contemplation and remembrance are the two operations the audience is described as not performing, and both are operations on material they already have.

That is why the verse asks for the operation rather than for belief. Belief in God's ownership is already present; what is missing is the act that connects it to anything, and the act is what the challenge names.

---

## Verse 23:86

> ˹And˺ ask ˹them˺, “Who is the Lord of the seven heavens and the Lord of the Mighty Throne?”

**The Second Question**

The sequence moves from the earth to the heavens, and the shift is deliberate. The first question was about something the audience stood on; this one is about something they could see but not reach.

The sūrah has used the seven heavens before, in its account of the stages above the earth. The verse returns to the same image and asks about its Lord, which connects the ownership question to the picture of the heavens the sūrah has already described.

**"Lord of the Great Throne"**

The Throne is named alongside the heavens, and the adjective given to it is *ʿaẓīm*, great or immense. The pairing places the Throne above or beyond the seven heavens, and the sūrah does not elaborate.

Al-Anbiyāʾ names the same Throne in its argument about the divine order: *"Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted. So Glorified is Allah, Lord of the Throne, far above what they claim."* (21:22).

The verse uses *man*, who, rather than a possessive construction, and the change of form matters. The first question asked "to whom does the earth belong?" This one asks "who is the Lord?".

---

## Verse 23:87

> They will reply, “Allah.” Say, “Will you not then fear ˹Him˺?”

**The Same Answer, a Different Challenge**

The audience's reply is identical to the one in the earlier verse — to Allah — and the verse notes the identity by repeating the word. What changes is the follow-up.

The first challenge asked whether they would be mindful. This one asks whether they will be reverent, using a verb from the root w-q-y that carries the sense of guarding oneself, being careful, standing in awe.

The order matters. A person cannot be reverent toward something he has not noticed, and the first challenge had to come before this one. The sequence is graded, and the grading is deliberate.

**Ittaqū: The Verb of the Believers' List**

The word used here is the same one the sūrah applied to the believers in its earlier list — those who tremble in awe of their Lord. The challenge is therefore not a general call to piety; it is a call to the specific disposition the sūrah has already described and recommended.

That connection is the verse's sharpest feature. The audience is being asked whether they will become the kind of people the sūrah described fifty verses earlier, and the asking follows immediately on their own admission about the Throne.

---

## Verse 23:88

> Ask ˹them also,˺ “In Whose Hands is the authority over all things, protecting ˹all˺ while none can protect against Him, if you ˹really˺ know?”

**Malakūt: The Intensive Form**

The word for dominion is *malakūt*, and the commentators identify it as an intensive form of *mulk*, sovereignty. The intensification is not only emphatic; it means the inner reality of the sovereignty rather than its outward appearance.

Al-Ṭabarī's reading takes it further, interpreting *malakūt* as the treasuries of things — the stores from which provision and events are issued. The interpretation connects the word to the sūrah's earlier material on measured provision, where rain was sent down in due measure.

Al-Mulk opens with the same vocabulary: *"Blessed is the One in Whose Hands rests all authority. And He is Most Capable of everything."* (67:1). The parallel establishes the word as a term for total sovereignty, and the sūrah of al-Muʾminūn uses it in the same sense.

**The Question's Scope**

This question covers everything — *kull shayʾ*. The earth and its inhabitants were the first; the heavens and the Throne the second; all things the third. The expansion is complete, and the final question includes the dust and bones the objection was about.

---

## Verse 23:89

> They will reply, “Allah.” Say, “How are you then so deluded?”

**"How Are You Deluded?"**

The word translated as deluded or bewitched is *tuʾfakūn*, from a root meaning to be turned away or diverted. The commentators take it as being misled or fooled, and connect it to Satan.

The verb is passive, and the passivity is significant. The audience is not described as choosing error; it is described as having been turned. The question asks how that has happened to people who have just stated the correct premise three times.

The sūrah has used the same charge elsewhere in this passage. The chiefs said the Prophet was had; this verse asks the audience how it is that they have been turned aside. The reversal is exact and deliberate.

The reversal also relocates the problem. The charge against the messenger placed the defect in him; the question places it in the people making the charge, and it places it there after they have stated the correct premise three times.

---

## Verse 23:90

> In fact, We have brought them the truth, and they are certainly liars

**The Questions End, the Statement Begins**

The verse closes the sequence of questions and does so with a flat assertion. No further question is asked, no premise is established, no answer is invited. The truth was brought; they are liars.

The sūrah has used the same method elsewhere. The five questions about the refusal were followed by the statement that the Prophet calls to a straight path. Here the three questions about ownership are followed by the statement that the truth has been brought. In both cases the questions exhaust what can be asked and the statement supplies what remains.

**"We Brought Them the Truth"**

The verb is the same one used earlier for the reminder — brought. The truth was not offered at a distance or left to be found; it was brought to them, and the bringing is described as complete.

The commentators take the truth as the religion God sent through His Prophet. The definite article matters: not a truth among others but the truth, and the sūrah has been describing its delivery throughout.

---

## Verse 23:91

> Allah has never had ˹any˺ offspring, nor is there any god besides Him. Otherwise, each god would have taken away what he created, and they would have tried to dominate one another. Glorified is Allah above what they claim

**Two Denials in One Sentence**

The verse denies two things, and the two are connected. God has not taken a child; and there is no god alongside Him. The first answers a specific Meccan claim, and the second states the general principle behind the answer.

The order is also significant. Offspring comes first, because that is the claim actually being made; the denial of partner gods follows as the general principle. The verse answers the specific before stating the universal.

**Al-Anbiyāʾ: The Same Argument**

The Qur'an gives the argument elsewhere in a form close enough to be a companion verse: *"Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted. So Glorified is Allah, Lord of the Throne, far above what they claim."* (21:22).

The two verses share structure and conclusion. Both posit a plurality, both describe the corruption that would follow, and both close with a declaration of God's exaltation above the claim.

**"Glorified Is Allah Above What They Claim"**

The sūrah uses the same closing formula in the next verse, and the repetition marks the end of the religious section. Both verses end with exaltation, and the doubled ending is the sūrah's way of closing the subject.

---

The verse's argument is the one the sūrah of the Prophets states in a single line: if there were other gods besides God, they would seek a way against the Lord of the Throne (21:22), and the sūrah of the Night Journey puts the same challenge about their claimed gods' ability to remove harm (17:56). The reasoning here is stated in the form of a consequence: if there were several creators each would take away what he had created and some would overpower others. The sūrah closes the sentence by stating what the disbelievers attribute away from Him, which is its usual way of turning the denial back on the claimant.

## Verse 23:92

> ˹He is the˺ Knower of the seen and unseen. Exalted is He above what they associate ˹with Him˺

**ʿĀlim al-Ghayb wa-sh-Shahādah**

The verse gives God a title, and the title covers two domains: the unseen and the witnessed. The pairing is exhaustive, and the verse's brevity rests on that exhaustiveness.

The title appears throughout the Qur'an — in al-Anʿām, at-Tawbah, ar-Raʿd, as-Sajdah, Sabaʾ, az-Zumar, al-Ḥashr, al-Jumuʿah and at-Taghābun. Its recurrence across so many sūrahs establishes it as a fixed designation.

That refusal is the verse's method.

The word for associate here is *yushrikūn*, and it names the act rather than the objects. The previous verse named the objects — offspring, partner gods.

**The Title Elsewhere**

Al-Ḥashr gives the title in a list of the divine names: *"He is Allah—the Creator, the Inventor, the Shaper. He ˹alone˺ has the Most Beautiful Names. Whatever is in the heavens and the earth ˹all˺ glorifies Him. And He is the Almighty, All-Wise."* (59:24), with the knower of the unseen and the seen given earlier in the same passage (59:22).

The parallel places the title inside a fuller enumeration, and the enumeration shows what the title is doing. Knowledge of the unseen and the witnessed is not one attribute among many; it is the ground on which the others operate.

The sūrah of al-Muʾminūn gives the title alone, at the end of a passage about claims made without knowledge, and the placement is the point.

Giving it alone also makes it final. A title inside a list can be passed over; a title standing by itself at the close of a section cannot, and the sūrah uses the isolation deliberately.

---

## Verse 23:93

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “My Lord! Should You show me what they are threatened with

**The Prophet Told to Pray**

The verse opens with the imperative and then gives the words of a supplication. The Prophet is not composing this prayer; he is being given it, and the giving is recorded.

The recording also makes the words available to be used. A prayer given as instruction is a prayer intended to be said, and the verse supplies the words for anybody in the same position.

**"What They Are Threatened With"**

The sūrah has described the same separation throughout. The messenger delivers; the people respond; the outcome belongs to the responders. The prayer is the messenger's part in that arrangement, stated in his own words.

The separation also explains the prayer's form. A man who is not responsible for the outcome cannot pray about it; he can only pray about his own place, and that is exactly what the verse records.

The limitation is not a defect in the prayer. It is the shape a prayer takes when the person making it has done everything he was commissioned to do, and the verse gives that shape without apology.

---

## Verse 23:94

> then, my Lord, do not count me among the wrongdoing people.”

**The Prayer Completed**

The verse finishes the supplication the previous verse began, and the completion is short and specific. Do not place me among the wrongdoing people. Nothing is asked beyond that.

The brevity is significant. The Prophet does not ask to be spared the sight of the punishment, or to be removed from the city, or to be given a sign. He asks not to be counted with a particular group, and the asking is about classification rather than comfort.

The sūrah has been concerned with classification throughout. The believers were listed; the sects were described; the audience was named as liars. The prayer asks where the one making it stands in the same accounting.

That the messenger asks about his own classification is the verse's most pointed feature. The accounting includes him, and he does not claim an exemption from it.

The absence of a claim is the verse's argument. A man who believed his commission guaranteed his standing would not have asked, and the asking is recorded so that the audience can see that he did not believe it.

---

## Verse 23:95

> We are indeed able to show you what We have threatened them with

**The Showing as a Sign**
The verb for show is the same root the sūrah used for the signs given to the messengers. The punishment, when shown, is a sign — something that makes a thing visible.
The sūrah has described signs throughout: the stages of creation, the rain, the gardens, the nine signs given to Mūsā. The verse places the punishment in the same category, and the placement is a claim about what the punishment is for.
It is not only a penalty. It is a showing, and the showing is what the sūrah's narratives have been describing four times over.
That is why the verse can affirm the capability without specifying the date. A sign's value lies in what it makes visible, and the making visible is God's to time.
The verse therefore does two things at once. It answers the prayer by confirming the threat, and it reframes the threat as a showing rather than merely a penalty, and the reframing is what the following command depends on.

**The Assurance Given in the Middle of Trouble**
The verse is one of the sūrah's consolations, and it is stated as a fact about God's power rather than as a promise about timing: We are able to show you what they are promised. The sūrah has just told the Prophet to repel the bad deed with the better one (23:96), and then to seek refuge from the whispers of the devils when they come (23:97-98). This verse stands between those two instructions and the passage about death and what follows it, and it answers the question a messenger under pressure would ask: will he live to see justice done? The Book says elsewhere that whether God shows the punishment in this life or takes the messenger before it comes, the return of the wrongdoers is to Him in any case (40:77; 10:46; 43:41-42).
**The Patience the Verse Is Asking For**
The point of the sentence is not that the Prophet would see the punishment. It is that the punishment does not depend on his seeing it. Justice in the Book is not carried by the messenger's survival or by the community's success in his lifetime; it is carried by the One who made the appointment, and the verse places that fact directly beside the human instinct to want the matter settled now. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt gives the Prophet the same instruction after a list of the nations that were destroyed: be patient, and God is with those who are mindful of Him.

## Verse 23:96

> Respond to evil with what is best. We know well what they claim

**A Command at the Point of Maximum Provocation**

The verse arrives immediately after the establishment that the punishment is real and that God is able to show it at any moment. The audience has been called liars; the threat has been confirmed; and the command given to the messenger is to respond to their evil with what is best.

The placement is the verse's sharpest feature. A command to respond well would be unremarkable in a passage about patience. Given here, after the charge and the confirmation, it is a demand made at the point where retaliation would be most intelligible.

**"With What Is Best"**

The phrase is *allatī hiya aḥsan*, that which is better or best, and the comparative form matters. The command is not to respond with good in the abstract; it is to respond with the better of the available options.

**Fuṣṣilat: The Same Command**

The Qur'an gives the command elsewhere in nearly identical words: *"Good and evil cannot be equal. Respond ˹to evil˺ with what is best, then the one you are in conflict with will become a close friend."* (41:34).

The parallel supplies the outcome the verse here leaves unstated. Responding with what is best can change the relationship, and the sūrah of Fuṣṣilat says so explicitly.

Al-ʿAnkabūt gives the same instruction in a specific context: *"And do not dispute with the People of the Scripture except in the best manner,"* (29:46). The three verses establish the principle as general rather than situational.

---

## Verse 23:97

> And say, “My Lord! I seek refuge in You from the temptations of the devils

**Hamazāt: The Incitements**

The word is *hamazāt*, and the commentators give it two related senses: the whispering of the devils to the soul, and their goading of an enemy to cause harm. Some describe the incitements in choking or throttling.

The two senses cover the inside and the outside. A person under pressure from his own thoughts and a person under pressure from hostile people are experiencing the same thing from two directions, and the word covers both.

The sense of constriction is worth holding. The commentators' image of choking describes what the incitement feels like — a narrowing of the field in which a person can think. The command to respond with what is best requires a wide field, and the prayer asks for it.

The two senses also cover the sūrah's description of the audience. A man under pressure from his own thoughts is distracted from remembrance; a man goaded by hostile people is pushed toward a response he would not otherwise make. The verse asks for protection from both at once.

---

## Verse 23:98

> And I seek refuge in You, my Lord, that they ˹even˺ come near me.”

**"Lest They Be Present With Me"**

The phrasing is worth holding. The request is not that the devils be destroyed or driven from the world; it is that they not be present with the one praying.

The limitation is exact and modest. The prayer does not ask for a change in the general order of things. It asks about a specific relation — their presence with him — and the modesty is characteristic of the sūrah's prayers.

The same modesty appears in the earlier supplication. Do not count me among the wrongdoers. Neither prayer asks for anything beyond the position of the one making it.

The modesty is also what makes the prayers sayable. A person cannot ask for the world to be reordered; he can ask about his own relation to it, and the verse gives the form of that asking.

The form is worth keeping. Both prayers in this passage are limited to the position of the speaker, and the limitation is what allows them to be used by anybody rather than only by a prophet facing a particular city.

---

The prayer is part of the pair that begins in the previous verse — the refuge from the whisperings of the devils and from their presence with the one who prays — and the same request is made in the sūrah of the Heights, where the command is to seek refuge with God from the prompting of the devils (7:200), and in the sūrah of the Bee before recitation (16:98). The commentators took the wording as a lesson in the limit of the petition: what is asked is not the destruction of the enemy but protection from him, since the servant's part is to keep his distance and God's part is to keep the harm away.

## Verse 23:99

> When death approaches any of them, they cry, “My Lord! Let me go back

**"Until": The Long Interval Closing**

The verse opens with a particle that the commentators connect back to the dismissive words of the disbelievers recorded much earlier: they will persist in that rejection until death comes. The interval between the two verses is the whole of the sūrah's middle section.

**The Verse After the Prayers**

The arrangement is also an instruction. The prayers are available now; the cry is not. The verse describes what remains for a person who did not pray.

The adjacency is what gives the prayers their weight. Read alone, they are devotional; read next to the cry, they are the difference between two ways of meeting the end of the interval.

**The Cry Elsewhere**

The Qur'an records the same request in several places, and the recordings agree. Al-Munāfiqūn sets it at the moment of dying: a person is told to give from what he has been provided *"before death comes"* to him, and he then cries, *"My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while"* so that he could give in charity and join the righteous (63:10).

As-Sajdah gives it after the Fire has been seen: *"Our Lord! We have now seen and heard, so send us back and we will do good."* (32:12). The parallel supplies what this verse leaves implicit — the seeing and the hearing that produce the request.

The repetitions establish the request as a fixed feature of the Qur'an's account of death, and the sūrah of al-Muʾminūn gives it in its shortest form.

---

## Verse 23:100

> so I may do good in what I left behind.” Never! It is only a ˹useless˺ appeal they make. And there is a barrier behind them until the Day they are resurrected

**The Reason Given, and the Refusal**

The verse completes the request and then refuses it in a single word. The speaker wants to return so that he may do good in what he left behind — and the answer is *kallā*, no, far from it.

The commentators note a reading in which the phrase means "these are words he could not but speak" — that is, the request is involuntary, produced by the situation rather than by a settled intention. The reading makes the refusal easier to understand: a plea produced by sight of the Fire is not the same thing as a change of heart.

**Barzakh: The Barrier**

The verse names what stands between the speaker and any return, and the word is *barzakh* — a barrier, or an isthmus between two things. The commentators take it as the truth that the dead do not return to the life of this world.

The fixed end matters. A barrier with no terminus would make the verse a statement about permanence; a barrier that lasts until a named day makes it a statement about waiting, and the sūrah has been about waiting throughout.

---

## Verse 23:101

> Then, when the Trumpet will be blown, there will be no kinship between them on that Day, nor will they ˹even care to˺ ask about one another

**The Trumpet and the End of the Barrier**

The verse names the event that ends the barzakh described in the previous verse. The barrier lasted until the Day they are resurrected; this verse gives the signal that closes it.

The Trumpet appears throughout the Qur'an — in al-Kahf, Ṭā-Hā, an-Naml, Yā-Sīn, az-Zumar, Qāf, al-Ḥāqqah, al-Muddaththir and an-Nabaʾ. Its recurrence establishes it as a fixed feature of the Qur'an's account of the end.

**Kinship Broken**

The verse states that there will be no kinship between people on that Day, and the statement is about the relations the sūrah's audience relied on.

Al-Ḥajj describes the same breaking: *"On the Day you see it, every nursing woman will forget what she nurses."* (22:2). ʿAbasa gives it more starkly: *"That Day a man will flee from his brother."* (80:34). The three verses agree that the closest bonds fail first.

**"Nor Will They Ask One Another"**

The second clause is the verse's most discussed feature, because other verses describe people questioning one another on that Day — in al-Aʿrāf, aṣ-Ṣāffāt and aṭ-Ṭūr.

Al-Rāzī resolves the apparent contradiction by noting that the Day is a long period composed of many states. Some of those states are so overwhelming that people cannot attend to anyone else; others allow attention beyond one's own situation.

The resolution is worth holding, because it changes the verse from a flat statement into a description of one phase. The sūrah is describing what happens when the Trumpet is blown, not what happens throughout the whole Day.

That method runs through the whole sūrah.

---

## Verse 23:102

> As for those whose scale is heavy ˹with good deeds˺, it is they who will be successful

**The Scales Introduced**

The verse introduces the weighing, and it does so by describing one of two outcomes. The scale is heavy, and the people with it are the successful.

The word for successful is *al-mufliḥūn*, the same word the sūrah used in its opening verse about the believers. The return of the word closes a circle that has run the length of the sūrah.

**Al-Qāriʿah: The Same Image**

The Qur'an gives the image at greater length elsewhere: *"As for those whose scale is heavy, they will be in a life of pleasure. But as for those whose scale is light, their home will be the pit."* (101:6-9).

The parallel supplies what this verse leaves unstated — the destination of each group. Al-Muʾminūn says the heavy are successful and the light have lost their souls; al-Qāriʿah names the pleasure and the pit.

**Success as the Sūrah's Subject**

The word *falāḥ* has been the sūrah's subject from the first verse. The believers were named as successful, and everything between that naming and this verse has been an account of what the success consists in and what stands in its way.

---

## Verse 23:103

> But those whose scale is light, they will have doomed themselves, staying in Hell forever

The verse gives the other side of the weighing, and the structure mirrors the previous verse exactly. The scale is light; the people with it have lost themselves and will remain in Hell.

**"They Will Have Doomed Themselves"**

Stating it at the weighing rather than earlier is deliberate. A reader who has just been told that the scale is heavy or light needs to know whose doing the weight was, and the verse supplies the answer in the same breath as the outcome.

**Al-Qāriʿah: The Pit**

The parallel verse in al-Qāriʿah names the destination differently: *"But as for those whose scale is light, their home will be the pit."* (101:9). The word for home is significant — the pit is described as a dwelling rather than a place of passage.

The two accounts agree on the permanence. Al-Muʾminūn says abiding forever; al-Qāriʿah says home. Both describe a settled state rather than a temporary one.

The sūrah of al-Muʾminūn adds the self-destruction, and the addition is what al-Qāriʿah leaves out. Between them the two accounts give both the agency and the destination.

---

## Verse 23:104

> The Fire will burn their faces, leaving them deformed

**The Face as the Site of Punishment**

The verse specifies where the Fire touches, and the specification is the verse's whole content. Not the body in general, not the soul in abstraction — the face.

The commentators note that the verb can mean to burn or to scorch, and that the grimace results from the disfigurement of the lips by the flames. The description is physical and specific.

The Qur'an repeatedly locates punishment on the face. The commentators list al-Anfāl, Yūnus, Ibrāhīm, al-Isrāʾ, al-Kahf, al-Furqān, an-Naml, al-Aḥzāb, al-Qamar and al-Mulk as containing such descriptions.

**The Grimace as Disfigurement**

The verse says the Fire leaves them deformed, and the word carries the sense of a face drawn or twisted. The commentators connect it with the disfigurement of the lips.

That is the loss the verse names. A person reduced to a disfigured face has lost the conduct that the sūrah spent its opening verses praising, and the reduction is described in a single clause.

The clause is the sūrah's shortest description of the Fire and its most specific. What the believers were praised for is gone, and what remains is a face the Fire has made.

---

## Verse 23:105

> ˹It will be said,˺ “Were My revelations not recited to you, but you used to deny them?”

**A Question Put to the Punished**

The verse records a question addressed to the people in the Fire, and the question is about the revelations. Were they not recited to you? And you denied them.

The form is a rhetorical question expecting the answer yes. The recitation happened; the denial happened; the question establishes both before anything further is said.

Returning to it as a question rather than a charge matters. The addressees are not being told what they did; they are being asked to confirm it, and the confirming is what makes the reckoning theirs rather than God's assertion alone.

The asking is the last use the sūrah makes of the recitation. Earlier the signs were described, quoted, and appealed to; here they are simply referred back to the people who heard them, and the reference requires nothing further.

That economy is what makes the question final. Nothing is added to the history and nothing is taken from it. The addressees are asked to hold what they already know, and the holding is the whole of the reckoning.

---

## Verse 23:106

> They will cry, “Our Lord! Our ill-fate took hold of us, so we became a misguided people

**The Answer Given**

The verse records the reply to the question, and the reply is an admission. Their wretchedness overwhelmed them, and they became a people astray.

The admission is complete. The addressees do not dispute the recitation or the denial; they explain how the denial happened, and the explanation is that something took hold of them.

**"Took Hold of Us"**

The verb carries the sense of taking possession or dominion over something, and the commentators note the strength of the word. The wretchedness did not influence them; it owned them.

The sense of ownership is significant. A person influenced can resist; a person owned cannot, and the addressees are describing the second condition.

Their naming it is significant. The sūrah's earlier descriptions could be resisted as a messenger's characterisation; the addressees' own words cannot, and the verse supplies those words.

The words are also the strongest available. Possession is not influence or pressure; it is ownership, and the addressees choose the strongest term to describe what happened to them.

---

## Verse 23:107

> Our Lord! Take us out of this ˹Fire˺. Then if we ever return ˹to denial˺, we will truly be wrongdoers.”

**A Request Made to Be Sent Back**

The verse records a second speech from the people in the Fire, and this one is a request. Take us out, and if we return to denial, we will truly be wrongdoers.

An earlier passage gives the same plea. The request to return at the moment of death asked for a second interval; this request asks for the same thing from inside the Fire rather than at its edge.

**The Promise and Its Structure**

The addressees offer a conditional promise: if we ever return to denial, we will truly be wrongdoers. The promise accepts the classification the sūrah has been applying throughout.

The acceptance is significant. The addressees do not dispute the term *wrongdoers*; they use it themselves, and the using is an admission that the classification is correct.

The previous verse gives the same acceptance. The addressees said they became a people astray; here they say they would be wrongdoers if they returned. Both statements use the sūrah's own vocabulary against themselves.

**Requests Like It Appear Elsewhere**

The Qur'an records the same request in several places. As-Sajdah: *"Our Lord! We have seen and we have heard; so send us back that we may work righteousness."* (32:12). Al-Munāfiqūn gives it at the moment of death rather than from the Fire (63:10).

The repetitions establish the request as a fixed feature of the Qur'an's account, and the sūrah of al-Muʾminūn gives it twice — once at the barrier and once from the Fire.

---

## Verse 23:108

> Allah will respond, “Be despised in there! Do not ˹ever˺ plead with Me ˹again˺

**"Do Not Speak to Me"**

The commentators connect the phrase with the Qur'an's statements about God not speaking to certain people, understood as both a sign of disapproval and a punishment in itself. Al-Baqarah gives the idea elsewhere.

The double character of the phrase matters. The refusal is not only a denial of the request; it is the withdrawal of the relation that made the request possible. The addressees lose the capacity to address God, and the loss is itself the punishment.

The same withdrawal appears earlier. The sealing of the faculties was described as something done to people who refused to use them; the verse here describes the closing of the channel of prayer.

The parallel is exact. What was not used is taken away, and the taking away is not an additional penalty but the completion of the refusal. The verse gives the completion in four words.

Both statements are verdicts rather than arguments, and neither is developed. The sūrah reserves development for the descriptions of what happens; the verdicts are given in a clause and left standing.

---

## Verse 23:109

> Indeed, there was a group of My servants who used to pray, ‘Our Lord! We have believed, so forgive us and have mercy on us, for You are the best of those who show mercy,’

**The Prayer Quoted**

The verse records the words of the group, and the words are a prayer. Our Lord, we have believed; forgive us; have mercy on us; You are the best of those who show mercy.

The prayer is complete in a single verse. It states belief, asks for forgiveness, asks for mercy, and names God as the best of the merciful. Nothing is left out, and nothing is added.

The correspondence is what makes the verse the sūrah's summary of prayer. Everything the opening list described about the believers' inward condition is gathered into one sentence, and the sentence is given as something said rather than something felt.

**"The Best of Those Who Show Mercy"**

The phrase appears elsewhere in the Qur'an — in al-Aʿrāf, Yūsuf, al-Anbiyāʾ, and at the end of this sūrah. Its recurrence establishes it as a fixed form of address.

The form is a superlative, and the superlative does specific work. It does not merely call God merciful; it places His mercy above every other instance of mercy, and the placing is what the prayer needs.

---

The prayer quoted is the one the mockers are being shown: a group of God's servants asked forgiveness and mercy, and the verse states that they are the ones who will inherit the Garden. The Book records the same petition in the sūrah of the Heifer, where the believers ask for pardon and mercy and receive the answer that God does not burden a soul beyond its capacity (2:286). The passage's contrast is the sūrah's closing argument: the mockers' word and the servants' prayer are set side by side in the same frame, and the outcome of each is stated in the verses that follow (23:110–111).

## Verse 23:110

> but you were ˹so busy˺ making fun of them that it made you forget My remembrance. And you used to laugh at them

**The Mockery Named**

The verse describes what the addressees did to the group named in the previous verse, and the description is specific. They mocked them, and the mockery made them forget the remembrance of God.

Naming the consequence is what makes the verse more than a complaint. The mockery is not presented as an injury done to the group but as a harm the mockers did to themselves, and the harm is specified as forgetting.

**Mockery as a Recurring Trait**

The Qur'an repeatedly describes mockery as a trait of those who refuse the messengers. The commentators list al-Baqarah, an-Nisāʾ, al-Māʾidah, al-Anʿām, Luqmān and al-Jāthiyah as containing such descriptions.

A reader who has been mocking can hear himself described, and the description is given without elaboration. No motive is supplied and no excuse is recorded, which leaves the behaviour standing on its own.

The absence of a motive is deliberate. Mockery explained would become understandable; mockery named remains what it is, and the verse chooses the second.

---

The mockery is described with its consequence: the laughing filled the mockers' time and made them forget the remembrance of God, so that the prayer of the servants became a joke rather than a reminder. The Book states the same charge against the people of the Garden and the signs in the sūrah of the Kneeling, where their deeds are lost and they took God's signs and messengers in mockery (45:33–35), and the sūrah of the Cave attaches the same end to mockery (18:106). What the verse adds is the mechanism: the memory of God is what mockery displaces, and the loss described in the next verse is the loss of what they replaced it with.

## Verse 23:111

> Today I have indeed rewarded them for their perseverance: they are certainly the triumphant.”

**"They Are the Triumphant"**

The word is *al-fāʾizūn*, and the commentators connect it with *fawz* — attainment of felicity in the Hereafter as well as victory over the world in a spiritual sense. The word carries both senses at once.

The double sense matters here. The group was mocked and laughed at; the verse calls them triumphant. The triumph is not a reversal of their worldly position but a statement about what that position turned out to be worth.

Naming it matters, because the group could not have seen it. While the laughter was happening, the triumph was invisible; the verse supplies the word that makes the invisible outcome speakable.

The word is also the sūrah's own. Success was claimed at the opening and confirmed at the weighing; triumph is the same reality given its final name, and the naming is spoken rather than reported.

**The Word Returns**

The word *fāʾiz* belongs to the same root as *falāḥ*, the success named in the sūrah's opening verse and at the weighing. The group described as triumphant here is the group described as successful there.

---

## Verse 23:112

> He will ask ˹them˺, “How many years did you remain on earth?”

**"On Earth"**

The phrase specifies the location, and the specification matters. The question is not about time in general; it is about time on earth, in the interval the sūrah has been describing.

The interval is what the verse is measuring. The sūrah has described it throughout — the fixed term, the period inside which conduct matters, the time the messengers were to be left. The question asks how long it seemed.

The asking is the verse's method. A person inside the interval experiences it as long; a person outside it will describe it differently, and the verse sets up the difference.

Setting it up as a question rather than a statement matters. The addressees will supply the answer themselves, and an answer supplied by the speaker carries more weight than a figure given to him.

**The Question and the Mockers**

The continuation is significant. The addressees were told not to plead with God; they are now being asked a question by Him. The channel is not completely closed — it is closed in one direction and open in the other.

---

## Verse 23:113

> They will reply, “We remained ˹only˺ a day or part of a day. But ask those who kept count.”

**"A Day or Part of a Day"**

The phrase is an phrase for a very short time, and the sūrah uses it without explanation. The addressees are not describing a literal day; they are saying that the whole of their earthly life now seems brief.

The Qur'an uses the same phrase elsewhere. The commentators connect it with passages in Yūnus, al-Isrāʾ, al-Kahf, ar-Rūm, al-Aḥqāf and an-Nāziʿāt, all of which describe the life of this world as seeming short from the perspective of the Hereafter.

The reply form is the sūrah's variation. Elsewhere the shortness is stated; here it is estimated by people who have lived it, and the estimating is less precise and more convincing.

**"But Ask Those Who Kept Count"**

The second half of the reply deflects, and the deflection is significant. The addressees do not stand behind their estimate; they refer the question to people who kept count.

The second half is the more severe. Losing track of time inside the interval is ordinary; being unable to reconstruct it afterwards means the time has no handle left, and the verse describes exactly that.

---

## Verse 23:114

> He will say, “You only remained for a little while, if only you knew

**The Verse After the Deflection**

The verse follows the reply that deflected to those who kept count, and the correction does not engage with the deflection. The addressees referred the question to others; the verse answers it directly.

The non-engagement is significant. The deflection was an admission of ignorance; the correction confirms the ignorance without commenting on it. The sūrah gives both and moves on.

An answer is given, a deflection is made, and a correction is stated in a single sentence. Nothing is elaborated, and the lack of elaboration is the sūrah's method at its most economical.

The economy also refuses the addressees a way back into the exchange. A comment on their deflection would have invited a reply; a bare correction closes the matter, and the closing is immediate.

After this verse the addressees are not quoted again. The sūrah moves to the question about purpose and then to its declarations, and the silence is what marks the end of their part.

---

## Verse 23:115

> Did you then think that We had created you without purpose, and that you would never be returned to Us?”

**"Without Purpose"**

The word carries the sense of play or frivolity, and the Qur'an repeatedly denies that creation was made in play. The commentators connect it with passages in al-Anbiyāʾ, ad-Dukhān, al-ʿAnkabūt, ar-Rūm, az-Zumar and Āl ʿImrān, all of which assert that the world was created in truth rather than in vain.

The recurrence establishes the denial as a fixed claim of the Qur'an, and the sūrah of al-Muʾminūn puts it as a question rather than a statement. The form makes the addressees answer it themselves.

The question form is also more effective than a statement. A reader who denies that creation was purposeless has committed himself to the return, and the verse makes him say so.

Binding the two claims together is the verse's real work. Purpose and return are asserted separately elsewhere in the Qur'an; here they are made inseparable, so that conceding one concedes the other.

**"And That You Would Not Be Returned to Us"**

The Qur'an repeatedly asserts that human beings will return to God, and that all things return to Him. The verse here gives the return in the form of a question the addressees cannot answer otherwise.

---

## Verse 23:116

> Exalted is Allah, the True King! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the Lord of the Honourable Throne

**"The True King"**

The phrase can be read as the Sovereign who is Truth, or the True Sovereign, and both readings carry the same claim. The kingship is real, and it is identical with truth.

The word *malik* connects directly with *mulk* (sovereignty) and *malakūt* (dominion), the latter of which the sūrah used in the question about who holds the dominion of everything. The verse returns to that question and answers it with a title.

**"Lord of the Honourable Throne"**

The verse ends with a title, and the title returns to the Throne mentioned in the question about the seven heavens. The audience was asked who is the Lord of the seven heavens and the Lord of the Great Throne; the verse names Him as the Lord of the Honourable Throne.

The change from Great to Honourable is worth noting. The question used the adjective of magnitude; the declaration uses the adjective of nobility. The sūrah gives both, and the giving is deliberate.

---

## Verse 23:117

> Whoever invokes, besides Allah, another god—for which they can have no proof—they will surely find their penalty with their Lord. Indeed, the disbelievers will never succeed

**"His Reckoning Is with God"**

The phrase states that the accounting belongs to God rather than to any human being. Al-Ghāshiyah puts the same point in the divine voice (88:26), and Nūḥ says the equivalent in ash-Shuʿarāʾ when he hands the accounting of his people back to his Lord (26:113).

**Burhān: The Proof**

The word is *burhān*, and the commentators gloss it as a demonstration resting on firm foundations — evidence too clear to be argued away. The same term appears in al-Baqarah, al-Anbiyāʾ, an-Naml and al-Qaṣaṣ.

The same absence runs through the sūrah. The chiefs had no proof for their standing; the nightly assembly had no proof for its conclusions; the appeal to the forefathers had no proof behind it. The verse names the absence.

The recurrence establishes the principle as fixed. The reckoning is not a human task; the messenger does not carry it out; the verse states that it belongs to God alone.

Denying him that role is also a protection. A messenger who carried out the reckoning would be a judge with an interest in the outcome; the verse removes the interest by removing the role.

**"The Disbelievers Will Not Succeed"**

The verse ends with a statement about success, and the word is the same root the sūrah used for the believers at the opening and at the weighing. The disbelievers will not attain *falāḥ*.

The return of the root closes the sūrah's central contrast. The believers were named as successful; the disbelievers are named as not successful. The two statements frame the whole sūrah.

---

## Verse 23:118

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “My Lord! Forgive and have mercy, for You are the best of those who show mercy.”

**One Final Command to the Prophet**

The verse ends the sūrah with an imperative and a prayer, and the form is the same one the sūrah used for the sequence of questions and for the two earlier supplications. Say — and then the words.

That method runs through the whole sūrah. It gives instructions rather than conclusions, and the final instruction is a prayer.

**"The Best of Those Who Show Mercy"**

The phrase returns, and it is the same phrase the group of servants used two verses earlier. The Prophet's prayer and the group's prayer end with the same words.

The sharing is deliberate. The sūrah has described two groups — the mockers and the servants who prayed. The final verse gives the Prophet the words the servants used, and the giving places him with them.

The sharing is what makes the phrase usable. A reader who has heard it in three other sūrahs does not need it explained; he needs it given, and the verse gives it as the sūrah's last instruction.
