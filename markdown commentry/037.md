# Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt (Chapter 37) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Structural Architecture and the Great Sacrifice**

The architecture of Sūrah aṣ-Ṣāffāt moves from angelic astronomy to Last-Day reunions, and from prophetic sacrifices to the final doxology of transcendence. The opening movement (1–39) establishes the absolute unicity of the Lord of the heavens, earth, and the Eastern horizons (*Rabb al-Mashāriq*), depicts the shooting stars defending the celestial realm, and stages the mockery of the deniers turned into horror on the Day of Reckoning, when they are herded with their spouses to the path of Hell and questioned: *“What is [the matter] with you that you do not help one another?”* (37:25). The second movement (40–74) presents an intimate, touching portrait of Paradise: the righteous reclining on thrones facing one another, drinking from cups of pure spring wine that causes no drunkenness or headache, accompanied by pure companions with modest gazes. One of them looks down through a window into the depths of Hell, recognizes an old earthly friend who had mocked his belief in resurrection, and exclaims with trembling relief: *“By Allah, you almost ruined me! Were it not for the favor of my Lord, I would have been of those brought [into Hell]!”* (37:56–57). The third movement (75–148) is the grand prophetic suite:

- Noah (75–82): Crying out in distress, answered by the Best of Responders, leaving his memory blessed among subsequent generations: *“Peace be upon Noah among the worlds!”*

- Abraham and the Sacrifice (83–113): Smashing the idols of his people, cast into the fire, emigrating for God, and praying for a righteous son. When the boy reaches the age of running and laboring with him, Abraham confesses his visionary dream: *“O my son, indeed I see in a dream that I must sacrifice you. See what you think!”* The boy answers with sublime, unhesitating surrender: *“O my father, do as you are commanded; you will find me, if Allah wills, of the patient.”* The sūrah captures the ultimate moment of faith: *“And when they had both submitted (*aslamā*) and he put him down upon his forehead...”*—whereupon divine voice intervenes, declaring the trial fulfilled, and ransoming the child with a magnificent, momentous sacrifice (*wa-fadaynāhu bi-dhibḥin ʿaẓīm*, 37:107).

- Moses and Aaron (114–122): Favored and delivered from great distress.

- Elijah / Ilyās (123–132): Rebuking his people for worshipping the idol Baal and forsaking the Best of Creators.

- Lot (133–138): Delivered with his household while the remaining towns were buried.

- Jonah (139–148): Fleeing to the laden ship, drawing lots and being among the losers, cast into the churning sea, swallowed by the whale while blameworthy, saved because he was of those who perpetually glorified God, and cast upon the barren shore where a gourd vine (*shaJaratan min yaqṭīn*) shaded his exhausted body, before returning to guide a nation of over one hundred thousand souls. The closing movement (149–182) delivers the devastating argumentative cross-examination about the alleged daughters of God, culminating in the immortal three-verse doxology that closes countless Muslim prayers: *“Exalted is your Lord, the Lord of Might, above what they describe! And peace be upon the messengers! And all praise is for Allah, Lord of the worlds”* (37:180–182).

It takes its name from its breathtaking, rhythmic opening oaths: *“By those [angels] aligned in ranks, and those who drive [clouds] firmly, and those who recite the Reminder—indeed, your God is One!”* (37:1–4).

In prophetic tradition, ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar recorded that the Prophet ﷺ would command the imams to shorten the prayers, yet he himself would lead them reciting Sūrah aṣ-Ṣāffāt (Sunan al-Nasā'ī 826), so sublime was its spiritual elevation.

## Verse 37:1

> By those ˹angels˺ lined up in ranks

**An Oath Opens the Sūrah Without Naming Its Subject**

The sūrah does not begin with a claim. It begins with witnesses. Three oaths stand at its door — by those lined up in ranks, by those who drive, by those who recite the Reminder — and only then comes the sentence they were summoned to support: “Surely your God is One!” (37:4). Something weighty is coming, and servants of the unseen world are called to the stand before it is announced. Early commentators call verse 4 the answer of the oath, the *jawāb al-qasam*, the verdict toward which the first three verses advance. The arrangement teaches before the doctrine does: the unity of God arrives not as one opinion among others but as a proclamation with heaven's order ranked behind it. Such staging also fixes how the next two verses must be heard.

**Ranks Point First to Angels Standing Before Their Lord**

Most early commentators read the ranks as angels drawn up in worship, and they read all three oaths the same way: companies of angels defined by service — standing, driving, reciting. The sūrah itself later puts the same words into angelic mouths: “We are indeed the ones lined up in ranks ˹for Allah˺.” (37:165). That self-description nearly settles the question, because the speakers of verse 165 can only be the angels answering the pagans' claims about them. Their ranks are ranks of worship — standing, bowing, prostrating — performed without fatigue and without dissent. Human prayer rows then appear as an imitation from below: believers line their shoulders on earth as angels line theirs above, and a well-known verse praises those who stand firm in God's cause in ordered rows (61:4). Nothing here suggests the angels intercede or share in divinity. They are exhibited precisely as servants, magnificent in obedience, and their ordered ranks become the visible shape of a government that runs on command rather than confusion.

**Wings and Birds Offer a Second Reading Worth Weighing**

A minority reading hears birds rather than angels. The same participle *ṣāffāt* describes birds spreading their wings in another verse, where the flight of birds becomes a sign of divine holding (24:41), and a similar image of birds in poised ranks appears elsewhere (67:19). Classical commentators record the possibility that the oath covers wings spread in flight — angelic wings or birds across the sky. Each side has its reason. The birds reading draws on genuine Qur'anic usage of the exact word, which counts heavily in exegesis. The angels reading draws on context: the two companion oaths concern driving and reciting, tasks the Book elsewhere assigns to angels, and verse 165 shows angels claiming the ranks for themselves. Context wins here. An isolated word-match cannot outweigh the sūrah's own identification of the speakers, and the oath companions all turn out to be servants of the same unseen government. The birds remain a legitimate echo — creation also stands ordered — but the primary referent is angelic, and the commentary tradition settled there with good cause.

The sequence from verse 11 onward shows the target: hearers who mock the message, ask how dust and bones could rise, and demand their forefathers with them (37:11–17).

## Verse 37:2

> and those who diligently drive ˹the clouds˺

**Clouds Give the Driving Its Most Natural Object**

The translation fills the gap with clouds, following the widespread reading that these drivers are angels herding winds and rain across the sky. Much supports this choice. The Qur'an elsewhere swears by winds sent in gusts and by clouds heavy with measure (77:1–3; 51:1–4), and rain arrives in the Book as mercy dispatched after despair (42:28). A desert audience knew exactly what driven clouds meant: life arriving on schedule from a sky no chief could command. Angels fit the role without strain, since the Book assigns them the management of natural affairs under God's leave. On this reading the oath moves from worship to provision. The same Lord whom ranks adore sends water to valleys that never thanked Him, and the drivers are the couriers of that unthanked generosity. Objections exist — the verse names no clouds, and translators mark their addition with brackets for that reason — but the reading coheres with the sūrah's later stress on a governed sky. It keeps the oath-companies parallel: angels standing, angels driving, angels reciting.

**The Same Verb Also Means Rebuking and Restraining**

*Zajr* in Arabic covers more than herding. It means driving back, rebuking, scolding away from a course — the shepherd's shout as much as the shepherd's staff. Early commentators therefore heard a second sense: angels who drive devils away from human beings, or who rebuke souls leaning toward disobedience. The sūrah itself strengthens this hearing a few verses on, where the devils strain to overhear heaven and fail: “They cannot listen to the highest assembly ˹of angels˺ for they are pelted from every side,” (37:8). Pelting from every side is driving in its fiercest form, and the agents of that repulsion stand plausibly among today's *zājirāt*. Other verses describe the devils repelled from the guarded sky and pursued when they snatch a fragment (37:9–10). Here the oath moves from worship to protection. The ranks adore; the drivers defend. Both senses of *zajr* — herding clouds and repelling rebels — may stand together, since the verse's silence about the object reads less like an omission than an invitation to include every driving the angels perform.

**Rain and Restraint Are Two Faces of One Mercy**

Provision and protection sound different, yet the Qur'an treats both as mercy wearing different clothes. Driven rain feeds the obedient and the defiant alike; driven devils shield the heedless from whispers they never detect. Neither gift waits for gratitude, and neither can be purchased or forced. This pairing answers something deep in the Makkan quarrel. An oath by drivers corrects both halves at once: heaven's agents are near enough to steer clouds over Makkah, yet none of them freelances, intercedes, or improvises. Later in the sūrah the angels will disown the worship offered to them, confessing fixed stations and constant praise (37:164–166).

**The Prayer Reading Turns Driving Into Seeking Refuge**

One elegant proposal relocates all three oaths into the daily prayer. On this view the ranks are believers standing for worship, the reciters are the same believers reciting the Qur'an, and the drivers are those believers repelling Satan with the refuge formula uttered before recitation begins (16:98). Al-Rāzī records this reading with evident appreciation, and its devotional fruit is genuine: every prayer rehearses standing, repelling, and reciting. As exegesis of the oath's primary sense, though, the proposal strains. Verse 165 lets angels claim the ranks for themselves, which fixes the first company above the human plane, and parallel structure then lifts the other two companies with it. A secondary, homiletic sense may remain — worshippers do imitate the drivers whenever they seek refuge — but the oath's witnesses are stronger read as angels. The prayer's echo of heaven enriches the verse without replacing its speakers.

## Verse 37:3

> and those who recite the Reminder

**The Third Oath Gives the Unseen a Voice**

After stillness and motion comes speech. The oath-companies have stood in ranks and driven under orders; now they recite, and heaven acquires a voice that human ears can receive. The Arabic repeats the established hammer-blow pattern — feminine plural participle *tāliyāt* braced by its verbal noun *dhikran* — so the recitation, like the ranking and the driving, is complete and sustained. Sound crowns the sequence for a reason. Ranks can be unseen, driving can pass unnoticed, but recitation demands a hearer; it crosses the last frontier between the hidden government and the prophet who must convey its word. Early commentators therefore heard this verse as the Qur'an's own arrival described from above: before the Reminder reaches Makkah, it is carried, voiced, and honored among those who never disobey.

**Angels Recite Before Humans Ever Hear**

The reciters are best read as angels, matching the two companies beside them. Revelation descends through angelic hands: Gabriel brings it down, and the Book elsewhere honors its bearers as noble and dutiful couriers (80:15–16). To swear by reciting angels is to swear by the channel of every warning the sūrah contains. Human reciters enter as a secondary reading — anyone who voices God's Book joins the recitation — and the proposal has warmth: each Qur'an reader lends an earthly tongue to a heavenly office. Yet the oath's witnesses should be definite, and the parallel with standing and driving companies keeps all three above the human plane. The deniers, for their part, demanded a different kind of book altogether, challenging the Prophet over scripture on their own terms: “Then bring ˹us˺ your scripture, if what you say is true!” (37:157). Heaven's answer precedes their challenge. The Reminder already moves through ranks of reciters; what Makkah lacks is not revelation but reception.

**Three Oaths Trace a Descent Toward the Hearer**

Read together, the oaths stoop by stages. Ranks belong wholly to heaven, absorbed in worship no eye has seen. Drivers work the boundary, steering clouds and repelling devils where the unseen touches the seen. Reciters cross it, putting revelation into speech that enters human ears. The movement is deliberate: each company approaches nearer than the last, until the final company speaks the hearer's own language about the hearer's own fate. Such staging refutes the pagan sense of a far-off Allah buffered by intermediaries. Nothing buffers this chain; angels carry the Reminder downward without editing, withholding, or freelance addition, and the Prophet receives exactly what was sent. The sūrah's later angel-passage confirms the pattern, showing servants fixed at stations and constant in praise (37:164–166). Worship, service, speech — the three labors ascend in nearness while the three companies stay one in obedience.

**A Reminder Only Warns Those Willing to Remember**

Recitation guarantees delivery, not reception. The same sūrah will show the Reminder striking sealed ears: “When they are reminded, they are never mindful.” (37:13). The verb and the noun share a root, sharpening the verdict — the *dhikr* arrives, but no *tadhakkur* follows. Scoffing replaces pondering, and signs become theater for mockery rather than summons to awe. This failure is moral before it is intellectual. Remembering means admitting a claim: that life runs toward judgment, that gods of stone answer nothing, that forefathers can err. The deniers refuse the admission and then blame the Reminder, calling its verses sorcery and its bearer had (37:15; 37:36). Against that refusal the oath stands as preemptive testimony. Long before Makkah heard a syllable, the Reminder was voiced in holier assemblies; rejection on earth cannot un-recite it in heaven. Human hardness changes nothing above. It only decides where the hearer stands when the recited warnings arrive.

## Verse 37:4

> Surely your God is One

**“Your God” Confronts People Who Already Named Allah**

The possessive matters as much as the predicate. *Ilāhukum* — your God — addresses hearers who already used the name Allah, acknowledged Him as high Creator, and then worshipped others beside Him. Makkan pagans asked about the Maker of heaven and earth named Allah without hesitation (29:61), yet directed prayer, sacrifice, and hope toward intermediaries. Their error was never atheism but association: a supreme God affirmed and then fenced with partners. The verse therefore strikes precisely at the fence. It does not say a god exists, which no party disputed, but that the God you already confess is One — sole in worship as He is sole in creation.

**Oneness Denies Partners, Children, and Intercessors Together**

A single One sweeps several idols off one shelf. The sūrah will confront pagans who assigned daughters to God while preferring sons for themselves: “Ask them ˹O Prophet˺ if your Lord has daughters, while the pagans ˹prefer to˺ have sons.” (37:149). It will confront idol worship, jinn given kinship with heaven, and angels treated as divine offspring. Each error looks different, yet all share one root: dividing among many what belongs to One — worship, begetting, intercession, control. Verse 4 condemns the root before naming the branches, which is why the later passages read as applications rather than new topics. The idolater, the angel-worshipper, and the believer in divine-jinn kinship all stand answered in advance by five words. Such economy marks the Qur'an's argument throughout: establish the center once, then show each deviation measured and found wanting against it. Refutation gains its sharpness here; the rest of the sūrah is the blade drawn out.

**The Prophet Confirms the Same Oneness Earlier Messengers Brought**

Makkah's pagans treated Muḥammad's message as an innovation and its bearer as a poet gone astray. The sūrah answers by folding his mission into a single ancient line: “In fact, he came with the truth, confirming ˹earlier˺ messengers.” (37:37). The truth he carries is first of all this verse — one God, sole worship — and every messenger from Noah to Jonah carried the same charge in its essence. Noah confronted a drowned world with it, Abraham smashed idols for it, Moses faced Pharaoh armed with it, and the sūrah will parade each witness in turn. Confirmation runs both directions: the earlier messengers vindicate Muḥammad, and Muḥammad's fidelity vindicates them. Against the charge of novelty, continuity is the defense; against the charge of poetry, the defense is older scripture singing the same note. Verse 4 therefore anchors more than theology. It anchors the Prophet's legitimacy, setting his five-word creed inside a prophetic consensus no Makkan poet could fabricate.

**Five Words Carry the Weight of the Whole Sūrah**

Count what hangs on this sentence. Short creeds often prove hardest to live inside: "One" denies every backup god, every inherited idol, every flattering intermediary a frightened heart might hire. That is why the verse provoked arrogance rather than argument — men who heard "no god but God" drew themselves up and refused (37:35).

## Verse 37:5

> ˹He is˺ the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, and the Lord of all points of sunrise

**Heavens, Earth, and Everything Between Leave No Gap**

The domain comes in three strokes: heavens above, earth below, and everything between. That middle term matters most, for it sweeps in whatever a divider might except — clouds, winds, rain, birds, the descending angels and ascending deeds. Nothing hangs ownerless between the levels of creation; every drift of air answers to the same Master as the stars. The Qur'an reasons elsewhere that fashioning heavens and earth outweighs fashioning humankind (40:57), and the comparison serves resurrection: hands that built the larger house can surely rebuild its smallest tenant. Against that scale the idols shrink to absurdity. No pagan claimed his god hung the sky or stuffed clouds with rain; the great works stayed unanimously Allah's even in pagan mouths. The verse simply refuses to let the small works — a complaint heard, a need met — be assigned anywhere else. One government overhead means one direction for prayer below.

**The Plural Sunrises Map a Year of Dawns**

Then the verse narrows from the whole domain to a single daily proof: the Lord of the sunrises. *Mashāriq*, plural of the east, counts every point along the horizon where the sun mounts through the year — each dawn a slightly different gate, summer and winter sliding the hinge. The root *sh-r-q* joins rising and east in one idea: the east is the place of rising, and its plural marks time's own orderliness. Elsewhere the Book varies the number for effect, speaking of the two easts and two wests of solstice extremes (55:17) and swearing by the Lord of all easts and wests (70:40). The shifts are never chaos; the sun never rises from the west on a whim or skips a season. A desert people who steered by dawns felt this argument in their bones. Sunrise cannot be flattered, bribed, or hurried by any idol, yet it never fails — managed, evidently, by One who never sleeps.

**Order in the Sky Argues Against Partners on Earth**

Regularity is the verse's silent witness. Heavens hold, earth bears, dawns rotate through their appointed gates, and no rival's fingerprint smudges the routine. Had many gods managed the sky, the Qur'an argues elsewhere, each would have pulled its creation aside and the order would have fractured (23:91). Instead the same sun serves every valley, the same rain answers every season's turn, and the cosmos runs like a household with one master rather than a council of squabbling heirs. Pagans sensed this too, which is why they never assigned the sun's course or the rain's timing to their idols; the big machinery stayed God's, with small gods hired only for errands. The verse exposes the inconsistency. A universe managed singly at the top cannot be worshipped plurally at the bottom — the errands and the machinery belong to one administration. Cosmic order testifies against cultic disorder, and the testimony never adjourns.

**The Maker of the Greater Can Surely Raise the Smaller**

Lordship over heavens and earth carries a further edge the sūrah will soon sharpen: power to resurrect. The deniers' objection stands recorded in their own incredulous words: “When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?” (37:16). Verse 5 answers before the question is even asked. A Lord who raised the heavens without pillars and wheels the sun through yearly gates will not be strained by reassembling bones. The sūrah makes the comparison explicit later, asking which stands harder — raising men or the greater marvels already made: “So ask them ˹O Prophet˺, which is harder to create: them or other marvels of Our creation? Indeed, We created them from a sticky clay.” (37:11). Creation from clay already happened once; repeating it asks less, not more. Resurrection so hides inside lordship as a conclusion inside its premise. Who grants the heavens must grant the Return, and denying the Return quietly denies the Lord.

## Verse 37:6

> Indeed, We have adorned the lowest heaven with the stars for decoration

**Beauty Is Presented as a Divine Act, Not an Accident**

Adorning is deliberate work. The verse opens with *zayyannā* — We adorned — a verb of beautifying that assigns the night sky's splendor to a Beautifier. The plural pronoun carries majesty, not number, the royal We of a sovereign describing His own craft. *Indeed* (*inna*) fronts the sentence because someone denied the implication: beauty this lavish must have an Author, yet Makkah admired the sparks while ignoring the Hand. Modern ears, dulled by streetlight, must work to recover what desert eyes knew — a sky crowded with brightness so dense it cast shadows. The verse asks for that recovery. Before arguing from the stars, it names their arrangement as arrangement, gift as gift. Contemplation begins where accident ends, and the verse ends accident in its first two words.

**The Nearest Heaven Is the One Human Eyes Can Read**

The adornment hangs in the lowest heaven, *al-samāʾ al-dunyā*, the nearer sky. *Dunyā* means lower or nearer, and the commentators hear both senses: this heaven is lowest in rank among the seven and nearest in reach of human sight. Everything visible overhead — sun, moon, planets, the wheeling constellations — belongs to this first story of the cosmic house. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the same furnishing, telling how the lowest heaven was fitted with lamps and set under guard (41:12), and how those lamps double as missiles against intruders (67:5). Such verses map an universe with depth: beauty on display below, mysteries reserved above. A pagan might have imagined the sky as a ceiling daubed with fire-dots; the verse corrects the imagination by degrees. This ceiling is a heaven — structured, governed, layered — and its lowest tier alone outshines every palace. What eyes can read already overwhelms; what lies beyond the reading exceeds it.

**Stars Serve Worship Before They Serve Navigation**

Decoration comes first in the verse's order, and the order instructs. Stars do guide — desert caravans and sea captains steered by them, and the Book acknowledges their guidance among its signs (16:16). But guidance is their second office, not their first. First they are beauty, hung to be beheld, drawing eyes upward before ever directing feet onward. This ranking quietly rebukes two errors. Astrologers made stars governors, reading decrees in their conjunctions and selling fear of their movements; the verse demotes them to décor, lovely and unthreatening, commanding nothing. Utilitarians made stars tools, valued only for direction; the verse insists that loveliness needs no use to justify its hanging. Both errors steal what the stars were hung to give. Between compulsion and instrument lies the Qur'anic middle: signs. A sign points without pushing, and these burning points direct attention past themselves to the Adorner. Travelers may steer by them, yet steering is the lesser gift.

**Adornment Answers the Lordship Just Claimed**

The previous verse named God “the Lord of all points of sunrise” (37:5), and today's verse exhibits the exhibit. A Lord of dawns should own a worthy sky, and here it hangs — jeweled, ordered, immense.

## Verse 37:7

> and ˹for˺ protection from every rebellious devil

**Protection Proves the Message Arrived Intact**

The guard carries an defensive edge the sūrah will sharpen. Makkah accused the Prophet of sorcery, poetry, possession — every origin except heaven. A guarded heaven answers all three at once: devils cannot deliver what devils cannot approach. The Book states elsewhere that no devil brought the Qur'an down, since hearing itself lies beyond their reach (26:210–212). Consider the logic's neatness. If the message came from below, its purity is unexplained; if from above through an unguarded stair, its protection is unexplained. Only a guarded descent explains both the Book's loftiness and its safety. This also dignifies the Prophet's persecuted listeners. Believing cost them comfort, kinship, sometimes blood — and the verse assures them the purchase was genuine.

**The Guard Protects Revelation, Not Real Estate**

What exactly is being guarded? Not territory — God needs no square footage — but tidings: the decrees and discourse passing among the angels. Devils covet these dispatches because a scrap of genuine heaven-news, salted into fortune-telling, buys credibility for a hundred lies. The jinn themselves later confessed the blockade, reporting a heaven found packed with mighty guards and shooting flames (72:8–9). Their confession is the verse's best commentary: professionals admitting the vault defeated them. What hung in the balance was guidance itself. Had devils freely tapped the channel, every soothsayer could have salted guesswork with stolen truth, and simple folk could never have distinguished revelation from static. The guard therefore protects hearers as much as heaven. It keeps the Prophet's message distinguishable — this, and only this, came down the guarded stair. Purity of channel guarantees purity of Book.

**Eavesdropping Once Worked, Then the Door Closed**

Commentators describe a tightening across time. Before Muḥammad's mission, jinn stationed themselves in the lower reaches and carried down fragments; with the revelation's dawn, the guard multiplied until the sky bristled. The Book depicts devils positioned at listening posts, then driven off by visible flames (15:16–18). Whether the earlier access was wider permission or weaker enforcement, the present fact stands: the channel is sealed. Wisdom shines in the timing. A final, universal message required a demonstrably clean line, so heaven's security peaked exactly when heaven's speech went public. The age of oracles ended where the age of revelation began — not by human skepticism but by angelic sentries. Pagans still paid soothsayers, but the supply of stolen truth was drying at its source. What remained was guesswork wearing revelation's old clothes. The verse so dates itself to mercy's schedule: protection perfected precisely when humanity needed certainty most. Security and revelation rose together, and neither has relaxed since.

## Verse 37:8

> They cannot listen to the highest assembly ˹of angels˺ for they are pelted from every side

**Eavesdroppers Find the Door Bolted From Within**

The rebels do not merely fail to enter; they fail even to listen. *Lā yassammaʿūn* — they cannot give ear — describes incapacity at the threshold of perception itself. Hearing is the easiest theft, requiring no touch and leaving no trace, yet heaven denies devils even this. The verb's form suggests straining: they stretch to catch, they press to overhear, and the sounds arrange themselves beyond reach. Effort is granted; success is not. This precision matters. A guard that barred bodies but admitted ears would guard nothing, since tidings travel on sound. So the verse seals the sense itself, not just the space. Devils may haunt the lower air, may whisper to willing hearts below, may rage at their exclusion — but the assembly's discourse proceeds in a quiet they cannot crack. Impotence this specific is itself a sermon. Powers that seduce empires cannot bug a single angelic council. Their ears work everywhere except where they hunger most.

**The Highest Assembly Is Heaven's Commanding Council**

*Al-malaʾ al-aʿlā*, the highest assembly, gathers the angels in their deliberative dignity — chiefs of heaven receiving decrees and rendering praise. *Malaʾ* elsewhere means notables whose word fills a city; exalted to heaven, the word fills the cosmos. What passes among them no creature learns uninvited: even the Prophet knew of their disputation only when God disclosed it (38:69–70). That precedent humbles every claim to inside knowledge. If the beloved Messenger waited to be told, what thief expects to overhear? The assembly's height is rank as well as altitude — nearness to the Throne expressed as elevation above intriguers. Devils strain upward from their lower station and find the distance moral before it is spatial. Holiness itself is the first wall; flames are only the second. Guarded speech among the pure stays pure precisely because purity cannot be eavesdropped, only granted. No ladder of cunning reaches a council convened in light.

**Pelting From Every Side Leaves No Angle of Approach**

Exclusion is enforced, not merely declared: the intruders are pelted *min kulli jānib*, from every side. No blind spot, no undefended quarter, no midnight window left unlatched. Here the earlier promise stands fulfilled, the “protection from every rebellious devil” (37:7) converted from policy into projectiles.

## Verse 37:9

> ˹fiercely˺ driven away. And they will suffer an everlasting torment

**Driving Turns Fierce When Theft Persists**

The pelting of verse 8 hardens into *duḥūr* — driving away, forceful and contemptuous. Thieves first pelted now find themselves hunted off, expelled with disgrace from precincts they kept testing. The escalation is deliberate: “pelted from every side” (37:8) described repulsion at the perimeter, while driving describes pursuit beyond it. Heaven does not merely hold its line; it clears its approaches. Translators add *fiercely* to catch the verb's edge, marking the addition honestly with brackets. Fierceness here is judicial, not temperamental — measured force answering repeated intrusion. Devils that would not take denial for an answer receive expulsion for one. The sequence teaches how heaven handles persistence in evil: patience first, then perimeter defense, then pursuit. Each stage warned; each warning was spurned. Driving is what remains when warnings are exhausted, and the verse lets the exhausted patience show. Warnings spurned become wounds earned; heaven keeps count.

**Everlasting Torment Closes the Door They Circle**

Present driving opens onto future dwelling: *ʿadhāb wāṣib*, a torment unrelenting, perpetual, without furlough. *Wāṣib* pictures pain that stays — settled, abiding, never commuting its sentence. Today's flames sting and disperse; tomorrow's hold and remain. The verse joins two horizons of justice: the skirmish above the sky and the settlement below it, repulsion now and residence then. Human rebels share the horizon, promised their own tasting of pain: “You will certainly taste the painful torment,” (37:38). Devils and deniers, seducers and seduced, converge where their roads always pointed. The everlasting quality answers a secret hope — that exclusion might be temporary, probation rather than verdict. It is verdict. Doors barred in time stay barred in eternity for those who chose the siege over surrender. What circles heaven now will find no orbit then, held fast where no side offers approach. The circling ends; the holding begins; the contrast is the punishment's point.

**The Devils' Fate Warns Their Human Clients**

Thieves rarely work without fences, and devils found theirs in soothsayers and consulters. Men who bought stolen syllables — paying coin or credence for whispers from the stair — joined the theft they funded. The verse's torment therefore casts a shadow longer than its grammar: seducers sentenced, and the seduced warned. The sūrah will show the partnership's end, when misleaders and misled discover their shared destination: “Surely on that Day they will ˹all˺ share in the punishment.” (37:33). Sharing is the partnership's last honesty — accomplices in deception become companions in consequence. This is why the Book everywhere severs the client relationship: no consulting the stars' pretended brokers, no purchasing certainty from thieves.

## Verse 37:10

> But whoever manages to stealthily eavesdrop is ˹instantly˺ pursued by a piercing flare

**One Thief in a Thousand May Snatch a Fragment**

The blanket ban admits a needle's eye: *illā man khaṭifa al-khaṭfah* — except one who snatches a snatch. *Khaṭf* is lightning theft, a grab mid-flight, and the cognate pairing makes it singular: one snatch, one fragment, one syllable of heaven-news. Against the rule — “They cannot listen to the highest assembly” (37:8) — the exception reads almost tenderly precise. Cannot listen, yet may once snatch: the ban holds absolutely, and absolutely means admitting the hairline case. Such honesty strengthens rather than weakens the guard's claim. A fortress that confesses one breached pebble proves it inspects its walls; a guard that reports its one failure proves it watches. Commentators stress the rarity: tries number in thousands, successes in ones, and even the one success buys only a fragment. Heaven's vault loses a sliver while keeping the treasury. The exception, stated before skeptics could allege it, becomes evidence of candor.

**Pursuit Is Instant, Inescapable, and Visible**

The snatch triggers pursuit: *fa-atbaʿahu shihābun thāqib* — a piercing flame follows him. *Fa* joins consequence to crime without interval; the translation's *instantly* catches that immediacy. *Shihāb* is a shooting flame, a meteor's blade; *thāqib* pierces, penetrating whatever it strikes. No thief outruns this hunter. The Book elsewhere names the stars as lamps turned missiles against intruders (67:5) and describes the eavesdropper trailed by a manifest flame (15:18). Pagans nightly watched these pursuits streak the dark without knowing what they witnessed — heaven's police blotter written in fire across the sky. Visibility is part of the sentence. A secret guard might deter devils, but a seen one instructs humans: look up any clear night and watch theft fail publicly. The flare that pierces the thief also enlightens the watcher. Judgment above becomes lesson below, and the night sky doubles as courtroom transcript.

## Verse 37:11

> So ask them ˹O Prophet˺, which is harder to create: them or other marvels of Our creation? Indeed, We created them from a sticky clay

**Harder to Create Weighs Humanity Against the Vastness**

*A-shaddu khalqan* — stronger, harder, more formidable in creation: which is it, them or the marvels already made? The comparison sets frail deniers on one pan and the furnished universe on the other — heavens, angels, earlier peoples, all that God's creating has produced. Commentators divide the referent: some hear the angels and the heavens, others hear bygone generations mightier than Makkah. The verse's vagueness is deliberate strength; whatever the greater creation includes, it dwarfs the objectors. Their own confession supplies the standard, for they grant God the greater works — “the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between” (37:5) names a domain they never disputed. To concede the cosmos yet balk at the corpse is incoherence wearing confidence. The question lets that incoherence introduce itself. No answer is recorded, because none exists.

**Sticky Clay Recalls Humanity's Humble Starting Point**

*Ṭīn lāzib* — clay that clings, viscous mud sticking to the hand. The adjective embarrasses: man's stuff is ditch-material, adhesive and lowly, the opposite of the star-stuff his pride imagines. The Book elsewhere varies the recipe — dust (3:59), molded mud (15:26), clay like pottery (55:14) — stages of one shaping rather than rival accounts. Each stage humbles differently: dust for commonness, mud for malleability, stickiness for ignobility. Yet the same verses that lower man's matter raise his Maker: fingers that shaped clinging clay can reshape scattered dust. Arrogance about origins is therefore doubly absurd. The denier boasts against resurrection while standing on the evidence of creation — himself, walking loam. Clay that learned to argue will find its arguments weighed. What was shaped once, with affection and breath, can be shaped again. Lowliness of matter never limited loftiness of ends.

**The Objection This Logic Crushes Is Stated Ahead**

The deniers' incredulity rings through the sūrah in their own words: “When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?” (37:16). Dust and bones — the reduction is meant to disgust, to make revival sound grotesque. Verse 11 answers before the question, dismantling the disgust with scale. If fashioning heavens and earth outweighs fashioning men (40:57), then reassembling men asks less than their first assembly did. Repetition costs the Almighty nothing; bringing back stands easier than bringing forth (30:27). The objectors sense none of this arithmetic. They stare at graves and call them final, forgetting they once stared at nothing and were made. Graves are fuller of promise than voids ever were — dust at least was fingers once. The question of verse 11 so precedes their question to preempt it. Scale answers sarcasm, and sarcasm has no reply.

**Our Creation Stretches Wider Than Makkah Imagined**

*Mā khalaqnā* — what We created, whom We created — refuses to narrow. The relative pronoun opens its arms to everything the deniers grant God: ranked angels, wheeling heavens, driven clouds, and peoples of greater might who rose and fell before Makkah laid a stone (40:82). Each item on the unspoken list dwarfs the objectors further. Angels outrank men in strength and obedience; heavens outspan them in measure; mighty nations outbuilt them in pillars and power, yet all lie under the same creating hand. Makkah's scale of difficulty assumed man the summit; the verse reveals him a foothill.

An older contempt echoes here: Iblīs himself scorned the clay-made man, pleading fire against mud (38:76).

## Verse 37:12

> In fact, you are astonished ˹by their denial˺, while they ridicule ˹you˺

**The Prophet Marvels at a Denial That Defies Sense**

Most commentators hear the marvel aimed at their disbelief. After the creation-argument — “which is harder to create” (37:11) — denial persists unwounded, and the Prophet wonders at the woundlessness. How do men concede the cosmos yet contest the corpse? Astonishment here is reason's own protest, the mind's flinch before incoherence flaunted. Context favors this reading strongly. Verse 11 posed an unanswerable question; verse 12 notes the question bounced off; verses 13–14 detail the bouncing — unmindful of reminders, mocking of signs. Marvel at their denial stitches the passage whole. It also humanizes the Messenger. He is not a stone reciter but a man staggered by what he must carry: truth this plain, refusal this merry. Wonder becomes testimony that the message is clear — clear enough that rejection itself amazes. Clarity this bright makes blindness itself the marvel.

**Ridicule Is the Denier's Substitute for Argument**

Against wonder the deniers field no counterproof — only *sukhriyah*, ridicule. Mockery is argument's white flag dyed to look like victory: unable to answer which is harder to create, they laugh at the asker instead. The sūrah samples their witlessness later: “Should we really abandon our gods for a mad poet?” (37:36). Poet, had, magician — the labels multiply as the answers vanish. Each jeer confesses the question's force; men answer strong arguments with weak laughter when strength fails them. Ridicule also recruits. Lone doubters might ponder, but crowds laughing together dare not defect — mockery polices the tribe's borders. The Prophet so faces not refutation but theater, performed to keep waverers from wondering. Wonder asks questions; ridicule punishes asking. Between them every hearer must choose his party. Laughter that fears questions testifies against itself.

**Wonder Honors the Message While Mockery Indicts the Mocker**

The two reactions are not moral equals. *ʿAjab*, wonder, is the soul's proper posture before divine things — prophets wonder, angels wonder, and wonder ripens into worship. *Sukhriyah* runs the opposite road: the Book treats mockery of God, His verses, and His Messenger as a graver matter than its jesters guess (9:65). Asymmetry rules the verse. Marveling elevates the marveler, aligning him with truth's magnitude; scoffing degrades the scoffer, shrinking him to his jeer's size. This is why the verse can commend without commanding — it displays wonder beside mockery and lets the contrast preach. No threat is uttered yet; the ugliness of laughter at revelation needs no caption. Hearers sort themselves by reflex. Those who wonder at denial join the Prophet's party; those who laugh at warning join a crowd the sūrah will follow to its end. Reflexes reveal allegiances faster than arguments do.

## Verse 37:13

> When they are reminded, they are never mindful

**Reminding Arrives but Remembrance Never Follows**

The circuit breaks between verb and verb. *Dhukkirū* — they were reminded, passively, by a mercy outside themselves — meets *lā yatadhakkarūn* — they do not take heed, actively, by a refusal inside. Same root, severed current: reminding arrives like rain, remembrance never rises like growth. The passive voice dignifies the effort spent on them. Prophets warned, verses descended, signs multiplied — heaven did the reminding with unfailing patience. The active refusal indicts the response. Not ignorance but inattention, not inability but indisposition: the ears worked, the wills declined. Arabic grammar so preaches the whole verse before lexicons open. A reminded man who will not remember has converted mercy into evidence — evidence tendered on the day when reminders are read back to the reminded. Every reminder spurned becomes a witness summoned. The reminded will meet their reminders again as witnesses.

**The Reminder They Spurn Is the One Angels Recite**

Heighten the refusal by recalling the Reminder's rank. This is no tribal memo but the heaven-voiced *dhikr* — the same companies that “recite the Reminder” (37:3) above carry it to a prophet below, and he carries it to men who yawn. The chain descends through pure hands into stopped ears. Angels recite without weariness; Makkah hears without waking. Contrast indicts. Beings of light treat these words as worship's matter, while beings of clay treat them as noise — the higher order reveres what the lower dismisses. What the deniers call tedious, heaven calls liturgy. The verse so measures refusal against heaven's reception: spurning looks small only until set beside cherishing. Men who knew what angels do with these words would tremble to yawn at them. Ignorance of the chain's height deepens the shame of breaking it. Yawns at liturgy accuse the yawner, never the liturgy.

Reminders never leave hearts unchanged; they soften or they sear. The sūrah exhibits the finished product: men who hear heaven's speech and file it under fraud — “This is nothing but pure magic” (37:15).

**The Qur'an Keeps Reminding Those Who Keep Forgetting**

Why does heaven persist with the unmindful? Because reminding serves justice as well as mercy. Mercy hopes the next reminder lands; justice ensures no denier pleads unheard on the Day of accounting. Noah preached the better part of a millennium to dwindling returns (29:14), yet every sermon counted — for the few who boarded and against the many who drowned. Persistence also protects the remindful minority. Each public reminder feeds believers even as deniers shrug; the same rain greens one field while running off another's rock. So the verse's bleakness is not the whole story.

## Verse 37:14

> And whenever they see a sign, they make fun of it

**Sight Joins Hearing in the Docket of Refusal**

Verse 13 tried the ears; verse 14 tries the eyes. *Kullamā* — whenever, every time — multiplies the occasions: not one sign spurned but a standing policy toward all. *Āyah* spans the range — recited verse, cosmic wonder, prophetic miracle — every showing heaven stages. Seeing should succeed where hearing failed; eyes outrank ears in the deniers' own court, for *seeing is believing* governs the skeptic's creed. Yet the same men unmindful of reminders prove unmoved by sights. The parallelism convicts. Two senses, two mercies, one refusal — the defense of *we never saw* collapses beside *we never heeded*. Refusal this comprehensive leaves the refusers nowhere to stand. Heaven appealed to every faculty, and every faculty was returned unopened. Two witnesses, hearing and sight, will testify as one. Faculties offered become faculties testifying.

**The Moon Split and They Called It Showmanship**

Tradition anchors *whenever* in occasions the first hearers knew — notably the splitting of the moon, a rent heaven displayed and deniers dismissed (54:1). The sign could not have been more public: night's own lamp divided, crowds beneath it, no laboratory conditions to dispute. Yet publicity avails nothing against policy. Men already committed — “they are never mindful” (37:13) — processed the wonder through the same refusal that filtered reminders. The episode teaches signs' limits. Miracles persuade the persuadable and harden the hardened; no spectacle, however cosmic, converts a will voted shut. This is why the Book increasingly declines to multiply spectacles on demand. Demands for *another* sign usually mean refusal of *this* one, and heaven will not haggle. The moon stands split in memory; the splitters of hairs stand condemned by it. Spectacles do not unlock wills; wills unlock spectacles.

**Signs Harden Hearts That Refuse Their Summons**

Each mocked sign raises the stakes. Pharaoh watched staffs swallow sorcery and seas split asunder, yet every wonder thickened his defiance. Thamūd received a she-camel plainly marked and hamstrung it. The pattern sobers: signs are mercy with a tripwire — received, they elevate; refused, they aggravate. This explains heaven's restraint in sending more. The Book states plainly that nothing held back further signs except the ruinous precedent — earlier peoples saw and still denied (17:59). Multiplying spectacles for committed scoffers would multiply only their guilt. Wisdom therefore redirects: fewer cosmic fireworks, more recited verses, the abiding miracle of a Book that outlives its mockers. Deniers mistake the restraint for poverty. It is policy — mercy refusing to forge heavier chains for necks already stiff. Restraint this merciful will itself testify on the Day.

## Verse 37:15

> saying, “This is nothing but pure magic

**The Verdict on the Signs Is Spoken Aloud**

Mockery finds its voice. After gestures and laughter comes *qawl* — saying, the slogan that packages dismissal for delivery. *This*, the verse's opening finger, points at whatever sign stands on display: the recited verses, the rent moon, the Prophet's person. Vagueness is the slogan's strength; one *this* files every wonder under a single verdict. Speaking the verdict matters. Silent scorn might still be pondering; uttered scorn commits. Tribes cohere around sentences, and this sentence will license every future refusal — no need to examine *this*, for *this* has been classified. The verse so catches denial mid-utterance, preserving the words for the record that will be read back.

**Pure Magic Means Deliberate Fraud, Not Mere Wonder**

*Siḥr mubīn* — sorcery manifest, magic plain and open. *Mubīn* does double duty: the magic is allegedly obvious (anyone can see the trick) and openly declared (no subtlety, just fraud). The charge is not awe but accusation — not *how wonderful* but *how dare he*. Sorcery implies confederates: a magician works with hidden powers, deceiving crowds for gain. So the slogan smuggles a whole conspiracy — the Prophet as charlatan, the Qur'an as script, the believers as dupes. Against men already committed — “they make fun of it” (37:14) — the label lands pre-approved. Mockery prepared the ears; *magic* fills them. Yet the very plainness claimed will undo the claim. Manifest things invite inspection, and inspection is what the slogan fears most. Call it obvious, and the obvious will be examined. Slogans fear inspection the way night fears dawn. Inspection is the enemy of every slogan.

**The Charge Carries a Long Pedigree of Rejection**

No novelty adorns this accusation; it is denial's heirloom. Pharaoh's court called Moses' staff sorcery when it swallowed their ropes (10:76); priesthoods called Jesus' healings the same; Makkah's predecessors labeled every warner a magician in turn. The Book preserves the pattern across generations: truth arrives with wonders, interests tremble, and *sorcerer* flies like a shield. Walīd's sneer at the Qur'an — lovely verses filed as transmitted magic (74:24) — shows the template applied to this very Book. Pedigree matters. A charge leveled identically against every prophet indicts its levelers, not its targets — the same reflex, the same threatened portfolios, the same refusal to examine. Deniers imagine themselves hard-headed realists; history files them as repeaters. The slogan's age is its shame: three thousand years, and no new objection. Repeaters inherit verdicts, not arguments.

**Magic Accuses the Prophet of What Devils Cannot Do**

The charge collapses on the sūrah's own prior showing. Sorcery traffics with devils — whispered syllables, stolen news, dark confederates. But this sūrah has spent five verses exhibiting devils pelted, driven, and flamed: thieves “pursued by a piercing flare” (37:10) cannot staff a prophetic ministry. The alleged confederates are heaven's hunted fugitives. Consider the contradiction's shape. Either devils deliver the Qur'an — impossible, for they cannot approach its source — or no devils are involved, and *sorcery* names nothing. The slogan sawed the branch it sat on before the sitting started.

## Verse 37:16

> When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected

**Dust and Bones Reduce Man to Refuse**

*Turāb* and *ʿiẓām* — dust and bones — are the objectors' chosen portrait of man. The vocabulary is curated for disgust: not *bodies*, which might keep dignity, but the grave's leavings, crumbled and scattered. Reduction is the argument's engine. Paint man as refuse and resurrection sounds like rubbish reassembled — grotesque, absurd, unworthy of God. But the portrait selects its evidence. These same mouths never describe living men as animated dust, though dust they lately were; decay disgusts them only when doctrine needs disgusting. Graves stand open in every town, and the objectors point theatrically: *there* lies your palace-dweller. Theater works on crowds. Yet the sūrah will answer theater with arithmetic — the Builder of heavens unstrained by bones — and disgust will prove no barrier to power. Refuse reassembled is still reassembled.

**The Question Mark Masks a Period**

*A-idhā* — when? What? — frames denial as inquiry, but no inquirer waits for the answer. This is the interrogative of scorn, *istifhām inkārī*, a question mark worn where a period belongs. *Will we really be resurrected?* means *we certainly will not* — the grammar asks, the tone testifies. Such questions recur wherever prophets preach rising: the same dust, the same bones, the same sneer. A lone believer in gardens ahead will recall a companion who jeered identically: “When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be brought to judgment?” (37:53). Stock objection, stock wording — denial shops from one shelf. Recognizing the mask matters. Arguments disguised as questions cannot be answered with information, since information was never requested. Only verdicts answer sneers, and the verdict is coming.

**First Creation From Clay Answers Decay to Dust**

The answer already stands, one verse behind the question. Men who find dust unpromising forget they began as mud: “Indeed, We created them from a sticky clay.” (37:11). Clay to man to dust to man — the circle needs no new power, only the old one applied again. Symmetry dismantles the disgust: if mud could become a disputant, dust can become a defendant. Another passage stages the same exchange, where the skeptic fingers a rotted bone and asks who revives it — and heaven answers that He who produced bones the first time will produce them again (36:78–79). First production is the precedent; second production is the inference. Deniers accept the precedent while rejecting the inference, which is logic's own definition of special pleading. Dust differs from clay only in dryness. Moisture is not the miracle; the Miracle-worker is. Precedent admitted, inference follows; denial must break its own logic to escape.

**The Objection Repeats Across a Dozen Passages**

Familiarity should have warned them. The dust-and-bones sneer echoes through the Book — raised in one sūrah after another by objectors who never met yet phrase identically (17:49; 23:82; 27:67; 56:47). Repetition on this scale proves the doubt is native to the untaught heart, not original to Makkah. Every generation rediscovers the grave's apparent finality and mistakes it for an argument. The Book answers each generation separately, not from poverty of reply but from mercy of pedagogy: new hearers need the old answer in their own hearing. Universality of objection also universalizes the verdict. What refutes Makkah refutes every age that parrots Makkah — including ages armed with microscopes instead of proverbs. Instruments change; the sneer abides; the answer stands. Dust was never an argument, only an attitude.

## Verse 37:17

> And our forefathers as well?”

**Forefathers Cut Both Ways in the Deniers' Mouths**

Ancestors serve denial twice, in opposite directions. When tradition is challenged, forefathers authorize: we follow what our fathers followed, and innovation is treason. The sūrah diagnoses the loyalty bluntly — “they found their forefathers astray” (37:69) — yet the deniers rush the ancestral ruts regardless. When resurrection is preached, the same forefathers embarrass: our mighty fathers, dust these centuries, rising? Reverence flips to ridicule depending on doctrine's needs. Such double duty exposes the opportunism. Forefathers are shields when shields are wanted and exhibits when exhibits are wanted — venerated, then weaponized. Consistency would require one posture: either ancestors matter, in which case their errors warn, or they do not, in which case their dust proves nothing. The objectors want both postures at once. Heaven will answer both: the astray warn, the dust rises, and selective ancestry stands indicted. Shields by day, exhibits by night — ancestry serves every shift of denial.

**Longer Decay Makes the Same Point Louder**

Time is the objectors' unspoken ally. Fathers dead a lifetime, grandfathers dead generations — decay compounded, scattering completed, nothing left to point at. Fresh corpses might conceivably stir; ancestral dust seems past even conceiving. The Book answers time's argument with time's own reversals. A traveler dead a full century rose to find his food fresh and his donkey's bones reassembled before his eyes (2:259). Centuries collapsed into an afternoon; decay reversed like a recited verse. If a hundred years yield to one command, what do a thousand add? Duration intimidates only the time-bound. Eternity reassembles Tuesday's dead and Adam's with identical ease, for ease admits no degrees. The oldest dust answers as promptly as the newest. Antiquity awes archaeologists; it does not encumber the Almighty.

**The Question Quotes the Previous Verse's Logic**

Verse 17 cannot stand alone; it leans its whole weight on verse 16. The forefathers-clause extends a sentence already spoken: “When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?” (37:16). Grammar mirrors dependence — a conjunction and a noun-phrase, no verb of its own. Rhetoric mirrors it too: the second sneer borrows all its force from the first. This leaning is tactically shrewd. By chaining the harder case to the easier, objectors make one answer serve two denials — refute grandparents and grandchildren fall together. But chains bind both ways.

**Love for Ancestors Excuses No Rebellion**

Yet affection cannot acquit. Forefathers rise as defendants, not as character witnesses; lineage shields no one when each soul answers singly. Noah's own son drowned outside the ark's mercy, told plainly that blood without belief is no bond (11:46). Tribes that pled *our fathers* will watch their fathers plead for themselves. The objectors' question so boomerangs: yes, the forefathers — standing beside their grandsons in the same dock. Ancestry dignifies the living; it cannot justify them. The sustained objection now receives its sustained answer, as the next verse will show.

## Verse 37:18

> Say, “Yes! And you will be fully humbled.”

**Three Verses of Objection Earn One Word of Answer**

*Qul naʿam* — Say: Yes. Three verses of slogan, sneer, and extension receive two syllables in reply. The disproportion is the point. Heaven does not haggle with arithmetic it authored; it pronounces, and pronouncement ends discussion. *Qul* puts the verdict in the Prophet's mouth — a mortal voice carrying immortal certainty — so Makkah hears the answer from the man it mocked. *Naʿam* admits no footnote. Not *yes, but* or *yes, perhaps*; the particle of full affirmation, unmodified and final. Brevity here is not curtness but sovereignty.

**Yes Confirms the Resurrection Without Negotiating It**

The *Yes* lands directly on their question: “will we really be resurrected?” (37:16). Question quoted, question answered — no reframing, no softening, no partial concession. Certainty meets certainty: they were sure it could not be, heaven is sure it shall. Such directness dignifies the doctrine. Resurrection needs no apology, no gradual introduction, no *in a sense*. The dead rise — bodies, forefathers, all — and the verse lets the scandal stand unsoftened. Negotiation would imply heaven doubts its own verdict; the bare *Yes* implies the doubters should doubt their doubts. Faith begins where the verse begins: assent without amendment. What God affirms in one word, men may spend lifetimes unfolding — but unfolding never revises. Yes means yes.

**Humbling Is Added Because Mockery Preceded**

Affirmation alone would answer the question; humiliation answers the tone. *Wa antum dākhirūn* — while you are utterly humbled — appends disgrace to the verdict. The addition is calibrated. These mouths did not merely doubt; they laughed, labeled, and performed superiority. Contempt going out returns as contempt coming down — measure for measure, the Book's unfailing exchange rate. The humbled will join ranks the sūrah previews: “they will be ˹fully˺ submissive” (37:26). Submission then will be involuntary, the body's surrender after the heart's long refusal. Contrast today's swagger: shoulders back in the marketplace, faces down at the Blast. Postures reverse on schedule. Mockery's wages are paid in the currency of mockery — smallness, publicly felt. Exchange rates in heaven never fluctuate.

**Dākhirūn Means Smallness Felt From the Inside**

*D-kh-r* roots the humiliation in littleness. The *dākhir* is one made small — diminished, despised, shrunk in his own estimate. Outward defeat could still leave inner defiance; *dukhūr* collapses the inside too. Arrogance inverts exactly: men who *acted arrogantly* at the call to one God (37:35) will feel microscopic at the call to account. The reversal is psychological before it is penal. Pride's pleasure was bigness — standing tall among applauding peers; humiliation's pain is smallness — standing exposed among silent witnesses. Such inwardness makes the sentence inescapable.

## Verse 37:19

> It will only take one Blast, then at once they will see ˹it all˺

**Resurrection Needs No More Than a Single Cry**

*Zajrah wāḥidah* — a single cry, one shout. Resurrection's entire machinery compresses into a solitary sound, and the sound suffices. No mustering over weeks, no gradual waking ward by ward; one Blast, and graves empty like startled barracks. The singularity rebukes the objectors' arithmetic. Men who counted corpses and despaired of logistics forgot logistics bind only the limited. For unlimited power, one and a billion cost identically nothing. *Fa-idhā* — then behold! — slams consequence onto cause without interval: cry, then sight, no comma between. Speed is the sentence's signature. What took lifetimes to deny collapses in the time of hearing. The humbling now gains its mechanism: “you will be fully humbled” (37:18). Cry first, smallness after. Startled barracks empty faster than drilled ones; terror drills its own.

**Two Blasts Frame One Judgment in the Wider Telling**

Elsewhere the Book details two soundings: a first Blast at which creation swoons and falls, a second at which all stand for account (39:68). Today's verse sounds the second — the rising-cry, not the felling one. Another passage confirms the oneness of the final summons: a single cry, and behold, they surface to the plain (79:13). Harmony, not contradiction: the sūrah spotlights the Blast that answers the deniers, since felling never troubled them — rising did. Commentators divide the overlap precisely, assigning cosmic collapse to the first sounding and courtroom assembly to this one. Precision serves piety. Believers learn the Day's program in order: unmaking first, remaking after — and remaking is the mercy toward which unmaking tends. The cry of verse 19 is creation's second morning. Mornings after unmaking outshine mornings after sleep.

**Suddenness Is Part of the Sentence**

*Fa-idhā* — behold! — the particle of the unforeseen. Warnings preceded for years; the event itself grants no preamble. Another passage shows the Blast seizing disputants mid-argument, lives interrupted mid-sentence (36:49). Interruption is justice's style: the heedless are taken heedlessly, as they lived. Suddenness also exposes preparation's absence. Packed bags need packing time; the Day grants none, revealing who packed by faith and who postponed by scorn. Believers live packed — five prayers a day rehearsing readiness — while deniers live unpacked, certain no journey comes. The cry finds both as they lived. Postponement is the only luggage scorn ever packs. Mercy and menace share one suddenness; readiness decides which name it wears.

## Verse 37:20

> They will cry, “Oh, woe to us! This is the Day of Judgment!”

**Mockery Collapses Into Lament in One Verse**

*Qālū* — they said, they cried. The verb that introduced their sneers now introduces their wailing; same mouths, opposite music. Mockery's confidence required distance from the Day; “they will see ˹it all˺” (37:19) closed the distance, and lament rushed into the vacuum. *Yā waylanā* — O our woe! — the cry of loss recognized too late. No argument remains, no slogan survives; the *magic* file-labels burned with the filing cabinet. Collapse this total is itself testimony. Strength that evaporates at one Blast was never strength, only noise at safe remove. The sūrah records the wail without gloating. Heaven states facts; the facts themselves supply the pathos. Laughers become lamenters between two verses, and the hinge is sight.

**Woe Is the Vocabulary of Irreversible Loss**

*Wayl* — woe, ruin — voices loss beyond remedy. The Book reserves the cry for thresholds crossed too late: risers startled from graves asking who roused them (36:52), the heedless confessing wasted warnings (21:97), the misled cursing fatal friendships (25:28). Each *wayl* shares one tense: past opportunity, present horror. Self-pity flavors the cry but cannot sweeten it; *our* woe personalizes what *the* Day universalizes. No helper is summoned — and none could answer. Woe differs from repentance precisely here: repentance turns to Mercy while doors stand open, woe bewails Mercy after doors shut. The vocabulary is honest at last. Deniers who named truth *magic* now name their state *woe* — the first accurate label they ever applied. Accurate labels applied too late decorate verdicts, not defenses.

**Confession Under Compulsion Earns No Credit**

Too-late faith fills the Book as warning. Pharaoh believed as waters closed, crying his submission to the God of Israel — and heaven answered with the famous rebuke: now, after lifelong defiance? (10:90–91). Mechanics are identical here. Sight compels assent, but assent compelled by sight is not the trust heaven rewards; faith means certainty before the Blast, not after. Doors granted this logic all along: warnings while breath lasts, verdicts when breath returns. The coming admission — “we will certainly taste ˹the punishment˺” (37:31) — will confess consequence without escaping it. Tasting cannot be untasted by tasting-words. Mercy had its office in Makkah's markets; the plain of rising hosts justice alone. Pity the timing, not the verdict: every late confessor was an early refuser. Early refusers make late confessors; the sequence never reverses.

**Woe Answers Wonder Across the Sūrah's Arch**

Two astonishments frame the passage. In verse 12 the Prophet marveled at denial; in verse 20 denial marvels at judgment. Wonder that warned meets woe that confesses — the same Day viewed from opposite sides of faith. Symmetry preaches. Every marvel at truth refused becomes, in time, a wail at truth revealed; amazement migrates from the righteous to the ruined. The Prophet's *ʿajab* hoped to prevent this *wayl*; rejection of the first guaranteed the second.

---

## Verse 37:21

> ˹They will be told,˺ “This is the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision which you used to deny.”

**Heaven Answers the Wail With a Title**

Lament gets its reply at last. The deniers named the Day through tears — “This is the Day of Judgment!” (37:20) — and heaven answers with a title of its own. *They will be told*: the passive veils the speaker in majesty, as though the whole court intoned the words at once. No angel is named, no herald introduced; the voice heard is authority's own. Reply completes the dialogue that verse 20 began: man's cry first, then God's caption. Symmetry like this sobers every reader who traces it. Every human word on that Day receives its heavier divine counterpart, and wailing earns its naming. The told cannot untell what the court has pronounced over them. Titles spoken from the bench adhere to prisoners forever. What follows the telling is the title itself — Decision, final, denied too long. Wailing asked for pity; naming answers with precision instead. Precision wounds deeper than pity could ever heal.

**Decision Means Division, Not Mere Verdict**

*Yawm al-faṣl* — the Day of Decision, of Division, of Sorting out. *Faṣl* cuts: truth from falsehood, believer from denier, garden-bound from fire-bound. Verdicts declare, but divisions separate — and this Day does both together. It judges first, then parts the judged into companies that never remix. Other passages name the same Day Decision and press hearers to grasp its weight (77:13–14). Appointment defines it: the Day was dated before any denier doubted it (78:17). Division also answers the crowd's false unity in error. Together they mocked, but decision scatters them into kinds forever. Each soul gets filed exactly where its deeds already filed it. Cutting is final by nature; what decision severs, eternity never sutures. Separation completes what sentencing begins. The Day decides, divides, and dismisses — in that order, without appeal. No reunion follows this parting. Eternity keeps the sorted order.

**Two Titles for One Day: Judgment and Decision**

Verse 20 cried *Judgment*; verse 21 answers *Decision*. *Dīn* stresses requital: debts paid in full, wages rendered exactly. *Faṣl* stresses separation: kinds parted, ranks divided forever. Together the titles complete the Day from both sides. First comes what gets settled, then who gets sundered. The denied content behind both titles was always one question — “will we really be resurrected?” (37:16). Arrival has now answered what argument could not. Titles multiply because the Day overflows every single name. The Book elsewhere adds still others, each catching another facet. Plenitude of names signals magnitude in the thing Named. One Day, many titles, no escape for anyone. Judgment pays every wage, Decision parts every company. Both titles remember denial, and both repay it. Memory in heaven never blurs or bargains. What was denied together gets settled together. Settlement and sundering proceed without pause.

---

## Verse 37:22

> ˹Allah will say to the angels,˺ “Gather ˹all˺ the wrongdoers along with their peers, and whatever they used to worship

*Iḥshurū* — gather, assemble — opens the scene with a command, and the command lists three sets of prisoners: the wrongdoers, their spouses, and whatever they worshipped besides God. The third set carries the lesson. The gods of the idolaters are rounded up with the men who served them, not to plead for them but to walk in the same procession, so that the worshippers see their "lords" as fellow prisoners on the road to the Fire. Some early readers took *azwāj* here as "their likes" rather than "their wives": the companions and accomplices who shared the same practice and the same road, which is how the verse reaches business partners and drinking companions as well as households. Either reading makes the same point about companionship: the closest human bond is not a defence on that Day, and no crowd walks to the Fire alone. The next verse names the destination, and the ones that follow record the quarrel among the marchers.

---

## Verse 37:23

> instead of Allah, then lead them ˹all˺ to the path of Hell

*Fa-hdūhum* — show them the way — is a bitter pun: the verb that ordinarily means guiding someone to the truth is used here for the parade to the Fire. The phrase *ṣirāṭ al-jaḥīm*, "the path of Hell," occurs nowhere else in the Book; everywhere else a path leads to God, to the Garden, or to the straight way (37:118), and the sūrah reserves the word for this one road. The verse follows the gathering of 37:22 and states the destination before any interrogation begins, which is the scene's logic: the questions that follow are not held to find out where the prisoners are going. The gods are made to walk in the line, the spouses are made to walk in it. The men who trusted both discover that the only route out of the crowd was the one they were invited to take in the world, back when the invitation cost something and the road was still open.

---

## Verse 37:24

> And detain them, for they must be questioned.”

*Qifūhum* — stop them, hold them in place — interrupts the march of the previous verse: "lead them ˹all˺ to the path of Hell" (37:23). The halt is for questioning, not for delay, and the questioning is certain. The Book swears to it: "So by your Lord! We will certainly question them all" — about what they used to do (15:92–93). The scene also shows that the Day is not a blind sentence: the procession stops at the gate and the case is put. Detention turns a crowd of prisoners into defendants, and the verse reports no answer, because the silence that follows is the answer. The scholars read these interrogations as rebukes rather than inquiries, since God already knows every answer; the questioning exists so that the guilty hear their own record. That reading reconciles this verse with the passages that say no one will be asked about his sin on that Day (55:39): the questions are put, but nothing is being discovered.

---

## Verse 37:25

> ˹Then they will be asked,˺ “What is the matter with you that you can no longer help each other?”

The question mocks the solidarity the prisoners counted on in the world. *Tanāṣur* — mutual aid, allies rushing to each other's defence — was the pillar of tribal security, and a man lived by his clan. Here the pillar is gone, and the verse is built on that contrast: those who helped one another against the prophets cannot help one another now, while the gods, chiefs, and kin they relied on cannot reach them. The taunt also exposes what the idols actually were. A people who walked past the truth for the sake of their gods now discover that the gods are standing next to them in the same line, without a word to offer. That is why the question needs no reply and gets none: the silence of the next verse is the answer. What the prisoners can still do is talk to each other, and the verses that follow show where that talk goes — into mutual blame (37:27) until the leaders admit they had no authority at all (37:30).

---

## Verse 37:26

> In fact, on that Day they will be ˹fully˺ submissive

The answer to the taunt is silence and posture: *bal humu al-yawma mustaslimūn* — no, on that Day they are handed over, fully submissive. *Istislām* is surrender under compulsion, and it is not the voluntary *islām* of faith; the two look alike and are opposites in value. Faith bows before the evidence is complete, choosing to trust what it has not yet seen; surrender bends after the evidence has arrived and the door has closed. Pharaoh is the Book's standing example: he confessed as the water covered him, and the answer was that the confession came too late (10:90–91). Yūnus' people are the mirror image, believing in time and being spared (10:98). The verse also places the submissiveness before the quarrel: the prisoners will not speak a word before the bench, and one verse later they cannot stop talking. What they cannot withhold is the posture; what they cannot avoid is the blame that follows.

---

## Verse 37:27

> They will turn on each other, throwing blame

Blame replaces aid. The same sūrah shows the saved turning to one another as well, but with a different result: in the gardens, the people of Paradise "turn to one another inquisitively" (37:50), delighting in each other's company, while here the turning is a quarrel. The Book sets out the same exchange at length elsewhere: the followers in the Fire plead, "Our Lord! We obeyed our leaders and elite, but they led us astray from the Right Way" — and ask that the leaders be given double punishment (33:67–68). It also records the moment the followers try to disown the leaders who ruined them, and the leaders' refusal to carry any part of the load (2:166–167), which is where the blame of this verse is heading. Community offers no cover in the Fire, only company. In the world the group was a shield; here it becomes the channel through which blame travels, and the argument changes nothing except the temperature.

---

## Verse 37:28

> The misled will say, “It was you who deluded us away from what is right.”

The followers open the case. The Arabic says the leaders came *ʿan al-yamīn* — from the right hand, the side of strength and standing. The phrase carries both senses the interpreters explored: the leaders approached from the side of power and rank, and they came wearing the appearance of right guidance, the side a person trusts. The charge itself is specific: you turned us away from *al-ḥaqq*, the right, the true, the real. In this sūrah the truth is what the Prophet brought (37:37), and the critics never refuted it; they steered people past it. But the verse also names the limit of the charge, since a deceiver can only mislead someone willing to be diverted. The Book gives the same account of the fall in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, where the leaders promise and the followers follow (7:38–39). That is exactly the ground the answer takes in the next verse: no, you disbelieved on your own.

---

## Verse 37:29

> The misleaders will reply, “No! You disbelieved on your own

The reply refuses the excuse without denying the invitation. In the world the leaders called, adorned, and frightened; in the Fire they insist they never compelled. Satan states the same principle at his own sentencing: "I had no authority over you except that I invited you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves!" (14:22). Two truths therefore stand together. Those who teach error carry burdens along with their own (29:13), and those who chose it carry their own choice. The Book is exact about the limit of the tempter's power: he has no authority over God's servants (15:42; 16:99; 17:65), which is why the whole quarrel in the Fire is about responsibility rather than about power. Nobody hands the sentence to someone else, and the argument is settled before either party admits it — as the next verse shows, when both agree that they will taste the punishment (37:31).

---

## Verse 37:30

> We had no authority over you. In fact, you yourselves were a transgressing people

The chiefs' defence rests on one word: *sulṭān* — authority, warrant, coercive proof. They claim they held no power that could force an inward choice; they only invited. The argument is true as far as it goes and useless as an excuse, and the verse marks the turn with *bal*, "in fact": the transgression belonged to the followers themselves. The plea repeats the oldest defendant's words (14:22) and leaves the same accounting standing — whoever starts an evil way carries a share of its weight, and whoever follows it carries his own (29:13). The Book's doctrine of responsibility is stated in both directions and never yields: no one is punished for a deed he did not do (6:164; 53:38–39), and no one escapes the deed he did. The Fire gives each side enough blame to fill its hands, which is why the quarrel in these verses can never produce a verdict: the verdict was already written before the first accusation was spoken.

---

## Verse 37:31

> The decree of our Lord has come to pass against us ˹all˺: we will certainly taste ˹the punishment˺

The quarrel ends in agreement. The words "our Lord" (*rabbinā*) come from mouths that refused to serve Him in life: whichever creature they blamed a moment ago, they now confess a master over the whole affair. The sentence has already been passed — the decree came true against them (*ḥaqqat ʿalaynā kalimatu rabbinā*), and the taste of punishment is fixed. The Book uses the same formula for the sentence that settles on the deniers: the Word of your Lord has come into effect upon those who disbelieved (40:6). Their earlier denial, "we had no authority over you" (37:30), dies at exactly this point, because a decree that runs against them proves a Lord they cannot dismiss. The Book adds elsewhere that the deniers' confession comes when it can no longer help them, and that they will ask for a second chance that will not be given (6:27–28). Confession in the Fire costs nothing and buys nothing.

---

## Verse 37:32

> We caused you to deviate, for we ourselves were deviant.”

The leaders' second admission is worse than the first. Having denied any authority over their followers (37:30), they now confess what their influence actually did: they made deviation look right, and they did it because they were deviant themselves. *Ghāwīn* — astray, ruined — is the opposite of guidance, and it is the state they admit to rather than the one they blame on others. The verse answers an old human question: what is the responsibility of the one who shows others the wrong road? The Book's answer is that he carries his own burden and a share of theirs (29:13), and the Fire's answer is that he cannot transfer any of it back. The same sequence of claims appears in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, where the leaders say the followers had no right to expect anything better of them (7:38–39). There is no leader's exemption in the Fire, and no follower's either, and this verse is where the leaders stop pretending otherwise.

---

## Verse 37:33

> Surely on that Day they will ˹all˺ share in the punishment

Nobody is singled out. Those who led and those who followed share the punishment, and the followers' own words in the previous verse — "we ourselves were deviant" (37:32) — are the proof that the sharing is just. The Arabic is *mushtarikūn*, partners and sharers, and the Book's rule of requital in kind is at work: partnership in the crime becomes partnership in its wage. The same gathering is described in the same terms elsewhere: "Whenever a group enters Hell, it will curse the one before it" (7:38), and Sūrah al-Aʿrāf adds the follower's plea for a double share of the torment for the leaders who misled them (7:38). The verse therefore closes a circle opened at 37:27 with the quarrelling: blame went round the room and came back to each of them. Vain hopes feed hotter fires, and the company is equal because the sentence is shared, down to the portion.

---

## Verse 37:34

> That is certainly how We deal with the wicked

The verdict follows the scene as a settled rule: this is how God deals with those who persist in wrongdoing. *Mujrimīn* — the wicked, the criminals — is the Book's word for those who cut themselves off from the truth by their own act, and the sentence is stated as a canon rather than as an event, so that the reader hears it as a rule rather than a story. The sūrah pairs it with its counter-refrain: after each of the prophets it approves, it says "this is how We reward the good-doers" (37:80, 37:105, 37:121, 37:131), and here it states the negative twin. The same formula closes warnings elsewhere — "This is how We deal with the wicked" (77:18) — which is part of the teaching: a reader finishes one account, hears the verdict, and is meant to recognise the pattern already running past his own door. The verse is short because a rule needs no elaboration; the scenes before it have done the arguing.

---

## Verse 37:35

> For whenever it was said to them ˹in the world˺, “There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah,” they acted arrogantly

The verse names the offence plainly. Whenever the call to worship one God was raised before them, they turned away — not because they had an argument, but because they had pride. The Arabic for this pride is *istikbār*, the assuming of greatness, the refusal to be a servant; the Book says elsewhere that God turns away from His signs those who are arrogant in the earth without right (7:146), which is the same judgement stated from the other side. So the message was not hidden from them: it was said to their faces in plain words, and the word of oneness was exactly what they could not bear. This is the point the verse fixes, and it is why the judgement of 37:34 can be stated as a rule. The refusal described here is not a lack of information but a rejection of the plainest thing they were told, which is why the next verse records them arguing against a messenger rather than against a message.

---

## Verse 37:36

> and argued, “Should we really abandon our gods for a mad poet?”

Their objection shows the shape of the rejection. Putting the charge as a question — should we abandon our gods for a mad poet? — tells the reader that the message itself was never examined. The charges came in a heap and contradicted one another: "a set of confused dreams! No, he has fabricated it! No, he must be a poet!" (21:5) — which is the mark of people rejecting what they cannot answer. The Book names the same two accusations in the mouth of the rejecters elsewhere: a poet, and a madman (52:29–30; 68:2, 68:51), and the sūrah's closing verdict answers both by naming God above what they claim (37:180). Their real objection appears at the end of the verse: the call to oneness they had just heard (37:35) and the inherited worship they would not leave. Insults are cheap; the claim they were aimed at was never looked at.

---

## Verse 37:37

> In fact, he came with the truth, confirming ˹earlier˺ messengers

The answer is one sentence, and the first word turns the charge around: *bal* — no, the opposite. He came with *al-ḥaqq*, truth that is real and cannot be otherwise, and his coming *confirms* the messengers before him rather than replacing them. *Muṣaddiq* is the word the Book uses of itself in relation to the earlier scriptures (2:97; 3:3), and it is the word here: the message does not announce a new God or a new religion, it authenticates what was sent before. That is the Qur'an's standing claim about the prophets: "I am not the first messenger ever sent" (46:9). Later the sūrah names the content of that truth — God is "far above what they claim" (37:159) — and it hands the praise for the whole account back to Him at the close (37:182). So the charge of madness is not answered with a counter-insult. It is answered with a description of what was delivered, and the description is left to stand on its own.

---

## Verse 37:38

> You will certainly taste the painful torment

The argument of the wrongdoers ends in a verdict. *Alīm* describes a pain that reaches the centre of a person rather than a loss that can be counted, and it is the sūrah's standing description of what follows denial (37:31). The same pair of words, *ʿadhāb alīm*, closes warnings across the Book, and it functions less as a threat than as a diagnosis: what the prisoners will taste is exactly what their choice produced. The Book puts the same sentence in the second person to the deniers of every generation — "You will certainly taste the painful torment" — and states the reason in the next verse, so that nothing about the punishment is left unexplained. That formula closes the quarrel of 37:27–32 without repeating any of it. Leaders and followers had agreed that they were partners in the deed; the verdict gives them the partner's wage, and the Book states the same principle elsewhere: a person has only what he has worked for (53:39).

---

## Verse 37:39

> and will only be rewarded for what you used to do

*Kuntum taʿmalūn* — you used to do — puts the sentence in the tense of habit: the reward answers a settled pattern of life, not a single weak moment. This is the sūrah's doctrine of retribution in one clause, and it comes back at the close of each of its stories (37:80, 37:105, 37:121, 37:131). The Book states the same rule in the same words at the end of Sūrah al-Ṭūr: "You will only be repaid for what you used to do" (52:16), and it repeats the principle that no soul bears another's burden (6:164). Nowhere does it accept a verbal claim in place of work, and nowhere does it promise that one good act outweighs a lifetime of denial. The quarrel in the previous verses was about who was responsible for the deeds; this verse answers that the deeds themselves are the only thing being read out, and that the reading does not distinguish between the man who taught the error and the man who chose it.

---

## Verse 37:40

> But not the chosen servants of Allah

**Mercy Carved Out of Judgement**

The exception arrives after the judgement, not before it. *Illā ʿibāda Allāhi al-mukhlaṣīn* marks out those whom God singled out for Himself. In this sūrah sincerity of service is itself God's gift: the same phrase returns later (37:74, 37:160), and at the end of the sūrah the praise for it is handed back to God — "And praise be to Allah—Lord of all worlds" (37:182). The word *mukhlaṣīn* names people God has purified, which is why the same exception appears in the dialogue with Satan, who confesses it in exasperation: all will be misled except Your chosen servants (15:40). Deeds are still weighed and wages are still paid: "rewarded for what you used to do" (37:39) is the rule, and this verse names the people who did it. The order matters, so that no one reads the exception as permission to neglect the deed, or the deed as a claim that forces God's hand.

---

## Verse 37:41

> They will have a known provision

**A Known Provision Answers the Hunger of Uncertainty**

Certainty is the first course of the meal. Rizqun maʿlūm — a provision known — promises not only food but freedom from the oldest anxiety about food: whether it will come, how much will come, and how long it will last. Al-Qurṭubī's reading, often quoted on this verse, turns on exactly that contrast: in the present world no heart is ever fully free of the fear that today's blessing may vanish by tomorrow, while the provision of the Garden is certain and everlasting at once. The adjective therefore does double work. It describes the food, which arrives in kinds the eaters recognize, and it describes the eaters' knowledge, which is free of calculation and dread. The recipients were named in the previous verse — “the chosen servants of Allah” (37:40) — and naming precedes feeding for a reason: belonging is established before bounty begins.

**Known Can Mean Fixed in Time**

A second reading hears schedule in the adjective. On this view maʿlūm means appointed at known times: the provision arrives morning and evening with a punctuality the present world never quite keeps. The cross-reference is Maryam, where the Garden's people receive their provision “morning and evening” (19:62) — a phrase the commentators quote here almost by reflex. The strength of the reading is its concreteness: eternity is not formless duration but a rhythm of renewed arrivals, each one expected and each one fresh. Against it stands the question of whether Paradise keeps hours at all, since its light never fails and its people never tire. The two readings need not compete. A provision can be both certain in substance and appointed in season; indeed that combination is precisely what earthly provision lacks, arriving either irregularly or anxiously or both. What turns on the debate is the texture of bliss: whether the Garden's joy is a steady state or a repeated event. The verse allows both, and the wisest commentaries refuse to choose.

**Provision Here Means Pleasure, Not Survival**

The word rizq itself changes meaning at the Garden's gate. In the Garden nothing needs keeping, because no one hungers, thirsts, tires, or ages. Al-Rāzī drew the consequence sharply in connection with the fruits below: the cuisine of Paradise exists for taste rather than need, for the fulfillment of wish rather than the repair of weakness. Provision so survives its own purpose and becomes pure gift — not fuel but favor. The deniers were told they would taste torment as wages — “rewarded for what you used to do” (37:39) — and the servants are told they will receive provision as wages.

---

## Verse 37:42

> fruits ˹of every type˺. And they will be honoured

**Fruits Names the Whole Menu in One Word**

One word carries the whole menu. Fawākihu — fruits — arrives without verb or preface, as if the provision of the previous verse needed no introduction beyond its own name. The grammarians call this a clarification by apposition: “a known provision” (37:41) is announced, and “fruits” explains what the announcement contains. Brevity here is not poverty but confidence; the Book trusts a single noun to open appetites that paragraphs could not. Short verses in this passage all work the same way, dropping one image at a time — provision, fruits, Gardens, thrones — so that each blessing lands separately and lingers. The Garden is furnished the way treasuries are inventoried: item by item, without hurry. What matters first is the category itself. Before kinds, quantities, and settings, the verse establishes that the provision is food, and food of the most delightful order. Everything after this word is detail; everything before it was promise. The colon of verse 41 pointed forward, and this word is what it pointed to.

**Fruits Without Seasons or Stinginess**

Earthly fruit comes with conditions the Garden abolishes. Here a fruit has a season, a price, a distance, and an owner; it ripens, rots, and runs out, and the best clusters hang highest. There the clusters hang within reach — the image of fruit brought low for its eaters, as another passage describes clusters near at hand (69:23). Nearness removes toil, perpetuity removes anxiety, and together they remove the small economics of scarcity that govern every earthly meal. No one guards these orchards, because no one needs to; no one rations them, because they never thin. The contrast is deliberate across the whole passage: everything the Fire inflicts by force — hunger, thirst, crowding — the Garden relieves by arrangement. Fruit in this setting is not a crop but a climate, permanent as weather and free as air. To eat without season is to live without countdown.

---

## Verse 37:43

> in the Gardens of Bliss

**Bliss Names the Gardens by Their Effect**

The Gardens take their name from what they do. Naʿīm — bliss, delight, ease — comes from a root that means softness and comfort: life without friction, pleasure without price. Most places are named for where they are or who built them; these are named for how they feel. The grammar makes bliss the owner and the Gardens the owned, as if delight itself held the deed. Into these Gardens the previous verse's bounty is now gathered — “fruits ˹of every type˺” (37:42) — so that food and setting authenticate each other: the place is blissful because of what grows in it, and the fruits are delightful because of where they grow. Naming by effect is also reassurance by effect. No one entering needs a map; the name is the map, promising that every step lands in softness. Etymology here is itself preaching: the word comforts before the eye sees.

**Gardens of Bliss Is a Standing Name**

The phrase is not invented for this passage; it is one of the Qur'an's standing names for Paradise, recurring wherever bounty is described — in al-Māʾidah, Yūnus, al-Ḥajj, Luqmān, al-Wāqiʿah, and al-Qalam (5:65; 10:9; 22:56; 31:8; 56:12; 68:34). It keeps company with Firdaws, ʿAdn, Khuld, and Maʾwā, the other revealed names, each stressing a different facet: height, permanence, eternity, shelter. Naʿīm stresses enjoyment. Translators have long felt the word's pull: Yusuf Ali renders the Yūnus occurrence as Gardens of Bliss, preserving the genitive's claim that bliss owns the grounds. The choice of name here is context-driven. A passage itemizing food, drink, and company reaches naturally for the name that means delight, just as passages stressing permanence reach for eternity. Names in the Book are never mere labels; each occurrence argues.

**Bliss Against Torment: The Passage's Antithesis**

Structure turns these Gardens into an answer. The passage opened its otherworldly account with torment tasted as wages — “taste the painful torment” (37:38) — and now balances it with bliss inhabited as wages. Naʿīm stands opposite ʿadhāb alīm in every particular: pain against pleasure, tasting against dwelling, force against gift. The antithesis is moral before it is aesthetic. Two parties heard the same messengers and made opposite choices; two addresses now house the results, and the architecture refuses to blur them. One might ask why the Book lingers over both destinations at such length. The answer is pedagogical: choice needs visualized alternatives, and alternatives need weight. Weight is what these verses supply — torment with its menu of horrors, bliss with its menu of fruits — until choosing feels as serious as it is. Symmetry is theodicy made visible: equal clarity for unequal fates, freely chosen.

---

## Verse 37:44

> facing each other on thrones

**Facing Each Other Abolishes the Back**

The participle describes furniture arrangement as moral theology. Mutaqābilīn — facing one another — means no one in this company ever sees another's back. Backs in human seating are small humiliations: the turned shoulder, the excluded auditor, the lesser guest placed behind. Ibn Kathīr, Qatādah, and Ṭabarī all draw the same inference, which the verse's economy confirms: that no one sits behind anyone is itself the honor, distributed without remainder. Faces turned toward each other declare mutual regard made permanent. The setting was named just before — “in the Gardens of Bliss” (37:43) — and the setting now fills with a geometry of respect. Arrangement preaches: where you sit is who you are, and here everyone sits facing honor. Courtesy is architecture here, built into thrones rather than requested of guests.

**Thrones Declare Rank Without Rivalry**

Surur — thrones, couches, raised seats — gives every servant what earth gives to one. A throne in the present world is a singularity: one sitter, many standers, and rivalry for the cushion. Here the plural reigns, and reigning is shared without being lessened. The Book elsewhere embroiders these seats and arrays them in rows (56:15; 52:20), and seats spouses together in shade upon them (36:56) — details this verse compresses into a single plural, trusting the wider description to furnish the scene. Rank without rivalry is the paradox only omnipotence can stage: everyone elevated, no one diminished, each throne a declaration that its occupant matters absolutely. Earthly honors shrink by sharing; these grow. Sharing here enlarges every share. The mathematics of mercy defies earthly arithmetic. To throne all guests is to abolish guests: all are hosts of honor.

**Envy Evicted Before the Seating**

Faces can meet because hearts have been cleaned. The Book states the prerequisite elsewhere: rancor is lifted from breasts before the seating, so that brothers sit with nothing unspoken between them (15:47). Envy is the back-turn of the soul — the inward refusal to face another's good — and it is evicted before anyone takes a throne. Al-Wāqiʿah repeats this verse's exact arrangement for its own foremost (56:16), confirming that facing is the fixed posture of honored company across sūrahs. Purification precedes placement everywhere in the Garden's logic: mouths before fruits, hearts before faces. Without that order the scene would be torture, for company without love is the definition of a crowd. With it, every face is welcome because every breast is clean. Heaven seats only reconciled people; reconciliation is part of the furniture. Cleansed breasts make facing a comfort rather than a confrontation. Purity of heart precedes posture of honor.

**Conversation Is the Feast's Main Course**

Facing exists for speech, and speech is the feast's true main course. Fruits feed the body; conversation feeds the company, and the verse arranges bodies so that talk never strains. The same sūrah will later show these very sittings in use, when the blessed turn toward each other with the ease of old friends — “turn to one another inquisitively” (37:50). Set that beside the damned, who also turn toward each other, but only for accusation — “throwing blame” (37:27). Same verb of turning, opposite eternities: interrogation here savors gall, inquisitiveness there savors joy. The arrangement of thrones so anticipates the arrangement of talk. Furniture that faces each other produces speech that faces the same way: honest, unguarded, and kind. Eternal small talk would weary; eternal candor delights.

## Verse 37:45

> A drink ˹of pure wine˺ will be passed around to them from a flowing stream

**A Cup Means the Drink Inside It**

The vessel names its contents. Kaʾs in classical usage is not an empty cup but a cup with drink in it — the vessel and its fill named as one, as Jalālayn notes on exactly this verse. English “drink” catches that union better than “cup” alone, since what circulates is refreshment, not crockery. The Arabic leaves the liquid unnamed, and the translator's gloss of pure wine supplies what context and parallel passages confirm. Reserve in the noun is itself eloquent: the Book need not advertise the vintage where purity is total. The company to be served sits already arranged — “facing each other on thrones” (37:44) — so service finds its diners ready. Recall too that all of this is provision made specific: the “known provision” (37:41) now pours. Grammar moves from promise to pouring without changing subject: the same mercy, now liquid. Metonymy is hospitality: the cup stands for the care.

**Passed Around Implies Servants**

A passive verb hides active servants. Yuṭāfu — it is brought round, circulated — never names the bearers, but rounds do not walk themselves, and Ṭabarī's inference stands: attendants move among the thrones. Elsewhere the service wears gold, silver, and crystal, with vessels passed and platters borne (43:71; 76:15) — plate worthy of the guests it honors. The unnamed servers matter theologically. To be served without serving is the definition of the guest, and the Garden keeps its people guests forever: no chore interrupts the feast, no duty recalls them to labor. Circulation also implies number; one cup could be handed once, but rounds suggest many vessels, many bearers, abundance in motion. The verse shows hospitality as an institution, not an incident. Anonymity dignifies the servers and elevates the served: all attention stays on joy.

**Pure Wine Without the World's Evils**

Purity here is not the absence of flavor but the absence of harm. The translator's “pure” renders a whole theology of paradisal wine: every delight retained, every evil removed. Muḥammad's image of the Garden's rivers includes wine expressly among water, milk, and honey, and calls it “delicious for the drinkers” (47:15) — delight asserted before any qualification. Another passage adds the moral seal: the Garden's cups carry neither idle chatter nor sin in their train (52:23), the twin poisons that earthly drinking always smuggles in. What the present verse claims by source — a flowing spring — these passages claim by effect: pleasure that never curdles into shame. The details of that immunity belong to the verses ahead, which itemize them; here the claim stands general and absolute. Wine without wound is the promise; how it holds will be shown.

**Wine Unnamed Keeps the Verse Modest**

The Arabic never says the word wine. Kaʾs stands unqualified — a cup, a drink — and the identification comes only from parallel passages, where the same flowing source fills cups that the Book elsewhere names among rivers of water, milk, honey. Wine (47:15), and where the same word maʿīn describes paradisal cups whose effects the passage then qualifies (56:18–19). Why the reserve? Partly euphemism: the Book often approaches delicate subjects obliquely before speaking plainly, letting context carry what bluntness would cheapen. Partly focus: what matters in this verse is service and source, not vintage — the passing around and the flowing stream. The translator's gloss of pure wine therefore reports a sound inference rather than an explicit term. Restraint is rhetoric here: purity so total it need not name itself.

## Verse 37:46

> crystal-white, delicious to drink

**White Is the First Thing Seen**

Color precedes flavor in the verse's order. Bayḍāʾ — white, bright white — meets the eye before ladhdhah meets the tongue. The sequence teaches that Paradisal drink persuades sight first and palate after. Ibn Jurayj and al-Rāzī are reported to have said it is whiter than milk, whiteness beyond the whitest familiar thing. Zayd ibn Aslam, as quoted by al-Qurṭubī, sharpens the contrast: the wine of the Garden is white and brilliant, while earthly wine comes reddish, blackish, yellowish, or brownish — colors naturally repugnant to sound taste. Our translator's “crystal” intensifies the Arabic, adding transparency to mere color: not only white but shining through. The drink arrives, then, already vindicated by its looks. No one needs persuading to lift what gleams like this. And the source guarantees the shine: it is drawn “from a flowing stream” (37:45), running water that never stagnates or stains. Purity of origin shows as purity of color. What the spring promises, the cup displays.

**Whiteness Will Return in the Maidens' Simile**

The Book rarely spends a color once. White will sound again below, when the companions of these drinkers are likened in sheltered whiteness (37:49). The recurrence builds a chromatic code: purity visible across categories — drink, faces, companions. Faces whiten on the Day of entry, as Āl ʿImrān describes the radiant admitted (3:106–7). Color becomes moral shorthand: white for the kept, dark for the ruined. The code works because nature taught it first — milk, light, and morning against blood, night, and smoke. Revelation does not invent the association; it ratifies and perfects it. To read white here is to read it everywhere: one color, one promise, many bearers. The wine's brightness is so a preview in miniature of the whole Garden's palette.

## Verse 37:47

> It will neither harm ˹them˺, nor will they be intoxicated by it

**Two Denials Guard One Pleasure**

Lā... Wa lā — neither... Nor — builds a double fence around a single cup. The verse denies harm and denies intoxication, two guards at one gate. Double negation in Arabic rhetoric signals completeness: every exit of evil closed. What was praised just before — “delicious to drink” (37:46) — now stands protected. Delight without defense would be fragile; defended delight is doctrine. The structure answers earthly experience precisely, where wine's pleasure always travels with its punishments. Heaven separates what earth always joins. Pleasure stays; punishment goes. The fence has only two posts, but nothing passes between them. Negation here is the most generous grammar. To deny evil of a good is to praise the good twice. Twice-praised wine needs no further witness.

**Earthly Wine's Ledger Stands Opposite**

The verse's denials assume a ledger the Qur'an has already published. Asked about wine and gambling, the Book answers that both carry great sin alongside some benefit for people — but the sin outweighs the benefit (2:219). A later ruling names wine defilement, Satan's handiwork, to be avoided completely (5:90). Earthly wine is so benefit chained to sin, pleasure mortgaged to ruin. Heavenly wine breaks the chain and keeps the pleasure. The present verse performs that surgery with two cuts: no harm, no intoxication. What remains after both cuts is exactly the benefit without the sin. Inversion is complete: the forbidden becomes the furnished, the defiling becomes the delicious. Same name, opposite nature — similarity of word, contrariety of thing. Only the Lawgiver who bound can loose, and only where He promises.

**Purity Explains the Earlier Gloss**

Recall the translator's interpolation two verses back: “˹of pure wine˺” (37:45). Pure stood then as promise; this verse redeems it as fact. Purity here receives its working definition: harm absent, intoxication absent. A gloss is vindicated when the text catches up to it, and here the text does. The Arabic of verse 45 never said pure; the Arabic here proves it. Definition by denial suits subjects whose excellence is freedom from flaw. Some things are praised by what they contain; this wine is praised by what it cannot do. Impotence toward evil is the highest potency. The gloss guessed; the verse guarantees. Translators interpolate at their peril, and this one is repaid. Repayment comes two verses late but exact to the letter. What was supplied in brackets is now sworn in the text.

Al-Wāqiʿah's parallel passage keeps the same pair — neither headache from it nor intoxication by it (56:19).

## Verse 37:48

> And with them will be maidens of modest gaze and gorgeous eyes

**Gorgeous Eyes Translates ʿĪn**

ʿĪn — the verse's final word — is the plural of a beauty Arabic never tired of praising. It means eyes wide and dark-pupiled against clear white: largeness, contrast, brilliance together. Translators converge on the effect if not the anatomy — lovely, beautiful, gorgeous, wide. The canon is desert-born: in a landscape of glare and dust, such eyes signify clarity and life. Eyes matter because persons meet in them; to praise eyes is to praise the soul's windows.

**With Them Seats Companions Beside Thrones**

Wa ʿindahum — and with them, beside them — adds company to comfort. The preposition places companions at the scene already furnished: thrones, faces, cups. Bliss until now was furnished but unpeopled beyond the drinkers themselves. Now the circle widens without thinning: more joy, same honor. The seated company was pictured earlier — “facing each other on thrones” (37:44) — and these attendants join that facing circle. Presence is the verse's first gift, before beauty is even described. To be with is already to be blessed; what follows specifies the blessing. Companionship completes every prior furnishing the way inhabitants complete a house. A feast of food without fellowship would be rations; this is society. The verse peoples Paradise one pronoun at a time.

**Earthly Wives Are Not Excluded**

The verse names maidens; it does not unname wives. Other passages explicitly gather righteous households: spouses rejoining spouses amid gardens and shade (13:23; 36:56; 40:8; 43:70). The Garden's company is therefore inclusive rather than replacement — addition, not substitution. Continuity of affection belongs to the Garden's logic: grace perfects nature rather than discarding it. Discarding beloveds would wound the bliss; perfecting them completes it. Against narrowing the verse to one class stands the Book's own breadth of promise. Households of faith continue; households of discord dissolve. What love began in patience here, presence perfects there. Exclusion would diminish the bliss the verse exists to enlarge. Every beloved finds its place; no place lacks its beloved.

**Sobriety Meets Modesty**

Set this verse beside its predecessor: “nor will they be intoxicated by it” (37:47). Clear minds now meet chaste company — the passage guards sense and society together. Wine without clouding, companions without wandering: every appetite arrives with its innocence intact. Coherence across the passage is exact: thrones face, heads stay clear, eyes stay home. Disorder in any one furnishing would spoil the rest; order in all completes each. Earthly feasts decay because drink dissolves restraint; this feast endures because restraint is built in. Sobriety and modesty are twin disciplines, one of cup and one of glance. Together they keep the Garden what it is: delight without damage. The verses rhyme in morals as in sound.

## Verse 37:49

> as if they were pristine pearls

**Eggs or Pearls: The Translators Divide**

The Arabic says bayḍun maknūnun — and translators part ways on bayḍ. Our translator renders “pristine pearls” (37:49), following Ibn Jurayj, Ibn Kathīr, and Ṭabarī, who read eggs as pearls. Others keep eggs: Pickthall's hidden eggs of the ostrich, the rendering of Sahih International as delicate eggs well-protected. Maududi finds the hidden peel beneath an egg's shell; Usmani pictures eggs sheltered under feathers from all pollution. The eggs party leans on the plain noun: bayḍ first means eggs. The pearls party leans on the parallel in al-Wāqiʿah, which likens wide-eyed maidens to sheltered pearls (56:22–23). Each side's ground is textual, which is why the division persists across centuries. Our translator's choice favors luminosity over fragility: pearls gleam, eggs merely pale. Either vehicle carries whiteness guarded — the difference is brilliance. The dispute gives what neither side alone could: two images for one beauty.

**A Simile Completes the Portrait**

Kaʾannahunnā — as if they, as though — introduces likeness with a hedge. “As if” confesses that beauty exceeds equation: like, but more; comparable, but beyond. The subject stands already portrayed — “maidens of modest gaze and gorgeous eyes” (37:48) — and the simile crowns rather than corrects. Simile is the rhetoric of ineffability: where statement ends, likeness begins. The Book uses comparison for transcendent things precisely because direct terms fail. To liken is not to lessen; the greatest realities arrive by analogy. Light upon light, gardens beneath rivers, faces like moons — the technique recurs wherever glory strains speech. Here the vehicle waits in two nouns at the verse's end. Crowning by comparison honors both the beauty and the reader's imagination. What cannot be stated can still be shown.

**White Is the Point Either Way**

Bayḍ sits beside bayāḍ and abyaḍ — egg beside whiteness beside white. The root's color shows through the noun whatever the vehicle: whiteness is the constant, eggs-or-pearls the variable. White in this Book's palette means purity made visible. Faces whiten on the Day, as Āl ʿImrān promises the admitted (3:106–7). The wine above was “crystal-white” (37:46) — one adjective, same code. Drink, faces, companions: three bearers, one color, one promise. Purity you can see answers doubt you cannot voice. The eye confirms what faith confessed: the kept are bright. No other color could carry this freight; white alone means clean. Whatever bayḍ means, it means it brightly.

## Verse 37:50

> Then they will turn to one another inquisitively

**Then Marks the Turn From Scene to Speech**

Fa — then, and so — pivots the passage from tableau to talk. Five verses furnished the scene; this one peoples it with voices. The pivot is narrative, not temporal: what follows happens within the bliss already described. Last came the likeness — “as if they were pristine pearls” (37:49) — beauty's final brushstroke. First now comes inquiry — curiosity's first word. Order teaches hierarchy of delights: setting, then society; sights, then speech. The Garden's furniture exists for the Garden's fellowship. Thrones without talk would be mere display; talk makes them thrones indeed. Fa is small because the turn is natural: furnished people converse. Scenery complete, the inhabitants speak. Every prior verse was stage direction; this verse raises the curtain.

**Mutual Questioning Is Mutual Delight**

Yatasāʾalūn — they question one another — repeats the verb of verse 27 in a new world. There the turning produced accusation — “throwing blame” (37:27); here it produces curiosity. Same grammar, opposite Sark: the verb keeps its shape and reverses its soul. Ibn Kathīr explains the talk: they ask one another about their situation, how they lived in the world, and what they suffered there. Memory shared becomes sweetness doubled; hardship recalled from safety tastes like victory. Questions here seek stories, not scalps. To be questioned is to be found interesting — attention as affection. The damned interrogate to wound; the blessed inquire to cherish. Curiosity without suspicion is Eden's own conversation. What verse 27 poisoned, this verse purifies. One verb, two eternities: the Book's most economical antithesis.

## Verse 37:51

> One of them will say, “I once had a companion ˹in the world˺

**One Speaker Steps Out of the Chorus**

Qāʾilun minhum — a speaker among them — narrows the plural to a singular. Anonymity universalizes: this could be any blessed soul with a story. The chorus was established just before — “turn to one another inquisitively” (37:50) — and now one voice rises from it. Every listener in Paradise, and every reader of the passage, leans in. The speaker's tale will occupy the verses ahead; its first line is identification.

**A Human Friend or an Assigned Devil**

The earliest readers split on the companion's identity. Most — Ibn Jurayj, Ibn Kathīr, Ṭabarī — hear a human friend: an unbelieving associate who denied resurrection to the believer's face. Ibn ʿAbbās specifies further: an idolater with a believing companion in this world, two men walking together through one life into two eternities. The human reading's ground is the dialogue itself: questions asked, answers implied, a shared mortal life behind both. Others hear the technical qarīn — the devil appointed to each forgetful soul, as another passage describes the appointment (43:36). The devil reading's ground is terminological: qarīn is the Book's own word for that shadowy associate. Each side reads faithfully; the noun genuinely bears both senses. Which fits better will emerge as the tale unfolds; the human reading has the narrative's shape on its side. A devil needs no looking-down to be located, and no past-tense “once had” to be lost. “Once had” suggests a bond death severed — a human grammar of loss. The verse keeps both doors open for one more line. Live questions hold attention better than settled ones.

## Verse 37:52

> who used to ask ˹me˺, ‘Do you actually believe ˹in resurrection˺

**Used to Ask Marks the Habitual Taunt**

Yaqūlu — he used to say, he would keep saying — stretches one sneer across years. The imperfect tense makes mockery a habit, not an incident. Habits of scorn shape relationships: the believer lived inside this question. Lived-in mockery weathers faith the way weather wears stone — slowly, steadily. The companion's identity was given just before — “I once had a companion ˹in the world˺” (37:51) — and now his voice arrives. Voice follows naming as echo follows sound. What the friend was in standing, he now is in speech: a denier. Years of asking imply years of answering, or years of silent enduring. Either way the believer paid a daily tax for his convictions. Taxation by taunt is persecution's quietest form. The verse lets one verb carry the whole biography of pressure.

**The Same Sneer the Sūrah Already Answered**

Nothing in this question is new to the sūrah. The deniers opened the chapter with the same mortality objection, matter to matter and no return (37:16–17). Public deniers in the marketplace, private deniers at the fireside: the believer heard both. Hearing twice tests twice: doctrine assaulted in company and in confidence. The sūrah answered the public version with argument; the tale answers the private version with outcome. Argument convinces minds; outcomes vindicate hearts. Both answers stand, and each needs the other. Repetition across the sūrah is pedagogy, not redundancy. Old objections meet new exhibits until doubt has nowhere left. The companion's question is Exhibit B in a settled case.

## Verse 37:53

> When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be brought to judgment?’”

**Death Described as Reduction**

The Arabic asks: aʾidhā mitnā wa kunnā turāban wa ʿiẓāman — when we die and become dust and bones. “Reduced to” renders the translator's reading of becoming: diminution, not transition. Reduction is the materialist creed in miniature: man is matter, and matter disperses. Dispersal, on this view, is dissolution: scattered parts cannot reassemble. The objection assumed what it needed to prove — that death ends agency. Assumption disguised as observation is the oldest fallacy in the skeptic's book. The sneer it completes was quoted just before — “Do you actually believe ˹in resurrection˺?” (37:52). Frame quoted then, content quoted now: the tale fills in its own blanks. Filling blanks is testimony's method: voice first, wording after. Wording preserved across death itself testifies to memory's Paradisal strength. Even enmity is remembered exactly where truth matters absolutely.

**Dust and Bones Is the Book's Most Repeated Objection**

The formula recurs across the Qur'an like a refrain of refusal. It sounds in al-Raʿd, al-Isrāʾ twice, al-Muʾminūn twice, al-Naml, Yā-Sīn, this sūrah before, Qāf, al-Wāqiʿah twice, and al-Nāziʿāt (13:5; 17:49, 98; 23:35, 82–83; 27:67; 36:78–79; 37:16–17; 50:3; 56:47–48; 79:11). Repetition on this scale diagnoses an universal intuition: what rots cannot rise. Intuition feels like logic until revelation exposes its limits. Yā-Sīn answers directly: He who originated bones the first time reanimates them (36:78–79). First origination silences objections to second: making surpasses remaking. The Book repeats its answer as often as deniers repeat their question. Patience in argument mirrors patience in judgment: both outlast mockery. Familiarity with the formula should not dull its function here. Each occurrence convicts a new mocker with an old answered question. Old answers stand; new askers fall.

**Really Repeats the Sneer**

Aʾinnā — shall we really, can we indeed — doubles the scorn of verse 52. Two interrogatives of denial in two verses: the companion's signature rhythm. Rhythm reveals habit: this man questioned everything unseen the same way. “Really” in English piles doubt upon doubt, daring the believer to affirm twice.

**Brought to Judgment Translates Madīnūn**

Madīnūn comes from the root of dīn — judgment, reckoning, repayment. The form is passive: to be requited, judged, brought to account. Passivity matters: the judged do not judge themselves; Another reckons. Translators circle the participle variously: Pickthall's brought to book, the rendering of Muhsin Khan as reward or punishment according to deeds, Ghali's judged or doomed. Each rendering stresses a facet: record, requital, ruin. Our translator's brought to judgment stresses the tribunal: process before penalty.

**Dust Cannot Argue Against Its Maker**

Every dust-and-bones objection forgets its objector. The mocker was himself once dust unformed, then fluid despised, then a disputant express — nothing to something by Another's doing. Yā-Sīn presses exactly this amnesia: the disputer forgets his own creation while disputing recreation (36:78–79). Forgetting what one was, one denies what one shall be. The fallacy is autobiographical: each skeptic is himself the refutation he demands, a walking resurrection from nonbeing. First making exceeds second by every measure — conjuring from nothing surpasses rejoining from parts. Dust therefore cannot argue against its Maker; the creature's existence concedes the Creator's power. Objections from impotence collapse before exhibits of omnipotence. The companion's question answers itself wherever beginnings are remembered.

## Verse 37:54

> He will ˹then˺ ask, “Would you care to see ˹his fate˺?”

**The Invitation to Look**

The speaker in the garden turns to his companion and asks, "Would you care to see?" — *qāla hal antum muṭṭaliʿūn*. The verse moves the conversation from telling to showing. The argument between the two men has been about the resurrection, and the companion who denied it was described in the verses just before as saying it was nothing but ancient fables (37:53). Now the question of what became of him is not answered by an argument but by a look.

What follows completes the scene: the speaker looks and sees his old companion in the middle of the Fire, and says to him, "By Allah, you nearly ruined me. Had it not been for the favour of my Lord, I would have been among those brought there" (37:55–57). Two things are worth marking in the passage. The first is that the invitation is offered, not imposed: *would you care to see* — the man who was granted paradise is given the sight as a kindness. The second is his own reaction, which is not gloating but gratitude. He names God's favour as the only thing that separated the two of them, and the reader is left with the point the passage has been making all along: the difference between the two men was not intelligence or station but guidance.

The picture of one group looking down on another has a parallel in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, where those who stand on the heights recognize the people of the Fire by their marks and call out to them (7:46). In both places the sight is granted from above, and in both it is given so that the difference between the two destinies can be seen rather than merely described.

## Verse 37:55

> Then he ˹and the others˺ will look and spot him in the midst of the Hellfire

*Fa-ṭṭalaʿa* — he looked, he peered down — describes a view rather than a journey: the speaker stays where he is and sees his old companion in the middle of the Fire. The Book gives the same vantage to the people of the Garden in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, where the companions of Paradise call across the barrier to the people of the Fire and the people of the Fire answer (7:44–47). The difference between the two states is drawn in their ability to see each other and in what they say. Here the sight is granted as the answer to a question — "Would you care to see?" (37:54) — and the Book presents it as part of the saved man's gratitude rather than his triumph. He does not gloat; he explains his own rescue. The verse also completes the story that began with the mocking friend of 37:51–53, whose scepticism about resurrection is answered not by argument but by the sight of him in the blaze he denied.

## Verse 37:56

> He will ˹then˺ say, “By Allah! You nearly ruined me

**Ruined, Destroyed, or Drawn Into Perdition**

Translators divide on turdīnī's shade. Ours and Sahih International choose ruined; Arberry prefers destroyed. Yusuf Ali hears destination: perdition as the endpoint. Sale intensifies motion: drawn me into ruin. Each shade stresses a facet: state, violence, destination, motion. The root ardā means to cause to perish: effect on the man. Effects own the verb; destinations arrive by context. Context here supplies Hell abundantly — the mocker sits centered in it. Supplied setting lets the verb stay spare: ruin sufficed. Translators who add motion or destination interpret, not invent.

**Sight Turns to Speech**

Vision complete, address begins: eyes did their work, tongues take theirs. The sighting was narrated just before — “spot him in the midst of the Hellfire” (37:55) — and now the spotted is spoken to.

Ibn Kathīr confirms the direction: the believer speaks addressing the disbeliever. The circle listens in: tale told to friends now includes testimony to foes.

**Nearly Holds the Verge and the Escape**

The verge frightens precisely because escape happened: closeness measured from safety. Measured nearness magnifies mercy: the nearer the pit, the greater the rescue. Loss here is total: not goods, not health, but the soul's eternity.

Seven verses of tale converge on this “you.” Named in verse 51 as companion — “I once had a companion” (37:51) — he is confronted here.

## Verse 37:57

> Had it not been for the grace of my Lord, I ˹too˺ would have certainly been among those brought ˹to Hell˺.”

In the middle of the bliss of the gardens, one of the saved turns to his companion and describes how close he came to the Fire: *lawlā niʿmatu rabbī* — had it not been for the grace of my Lord. The verse answers the fear raised a few lines earlier, when that companion asked whether people really believed in the reckoning (37:51–53). He does not credit his own cleverness or his own discipline. Ibn Kathīr reads the clause as the man's testimony that God guided him to faith and kept him in it, and the Book makes the same confession for the whole company of the saved, who say: "Praise be to Allah, who has guided us to this" (7:43). The people of Paradise are not proud of their record; they are grateful for a rescue they did not earn, and that is why the conversation there sounds more like relief than triumph. Grace in this verse covers both halves of salvation: the gift of faith at the beginning, and the gift of staying in it to the end.

## Verse 37:58

> ˹Then he will ask his fellow believers,˺ “Can you imagine that we will never die

“Then he will ask his fellow believers” (37:58) — speaker, audience, act, all glossed. The Arabic gives only the question; theater comes from the translator.

**One Voice or Every Voice**

Who asks? The verse as glossed names one speaker. Continuity argues for him: the tale's teller exclaims its moral. Teller-turned-praiser completes his own arc. Others hear all believers: the “we” of never dying belongs to everyone. Belonging to all, the question needs no single mouth. The commentary tradition records both: the believer's continuation, or every believer's joy. Recorded options need weighing, not mere listing. Weighing finds both true in practice: one voice, common jubilation. Common jubilation voiced singly moves hearers doubly.

The Arabic asks expecting glad denial: no death, no dying, ever. “Can you imagine” renders the wonder functionally: marvel with me.

**Never Die Seals Every Delight**

Eternal sweetness needs no savoring strategy: no last sips, no farewells. Ibn Kathīr confirms the setting: believers speak in joy, realizing permanence. Realizing permanence completes crediting grace — “for the grace of my Lord” (37:57) — for grace given implies grace keeping. Realized permanence transforms every pleasure: thrones forever, wine forever, company forever.

The circle was convened long before — “turn to one another inquisitively” (37:50) — and questioning continues as celebration.

## Verse 37:59

> except our first death, nor be punished ˹like the others˺?”

Another passage counts the same two deaths framing two lives: "You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all be returned˺" (2:28). The two are not the same thing. The state before the first birth is not the death the verse is talking about; it is the condition of being created, and the first death is the one that closes the first life. So the exception in the speech of the saved means this: the death we have already tasted is the only death we will taste, and the punishment the others face — the second death, which the Book describes as an unending torment — will not reach us. The pair of verses shows the people of Paradise speaking after the terror, and their sentence is the summary of their safety.

---

## Verse 37:60

> This is truly the ultimate triumph

**The Summary of Everything**

The verse closes the catalogue of the previous twenty verses with one word for all of it: *hādhā*, this, is the great triumph. The demonstrative gathers up the gardens, the provision, the fruits, the honour, the thrones, and the view. Ibn Kathīr notes the report that heaven itself answers the people of Paradise at this point: it will be said that this, truly, is the supreme success. The word *fawz* is the Book's word for winning a contest and coming out of a risk unharmed, and translators render it variously — supreme triumph, great achievement — because no single English noun carries the sense of a wager won. The sūrah uses the same noun in 37:60 and once more at its close (37:182), where the praise is handed back to God. The catalogue of joy is long on purpose, so that the summary at the end is as large as the promise it summarises.

---

## Verse 37:61

> For such ˹honour˺ all should strive

**Like This Points Backward**

Li-mithli hādhā — for the like of this — gathers the preceding twenty verses into two words. “This” means everything since the passage turned Paradisal: provision, fruits, thrones, wine, companions, rescue, deathlessness. Demonstratives do theology here: salvation can be pointed at, not merely described. What was triumphantly named just before — “the ultimate triumph” (37:60) — now becomes a goal to pursue. Naming precedes commanding throughout the Book: show the prize, then summon the race. Prizes displayed motivate more powerfully than duties bare of promise. The verse therefore pivots the whole episode from description to urging. Pivots like this one turn narrative into sermon without breaking stride. Unbroken strides carry hearers from Garden visions to earthly resolve. Resolve formed in dunyā harvests in eternity.

**Workers Are Commanded to Work**

Fal-yaʿmali al-ʿāmilūn — let the workers work, let laborers labor. The imperative repeats its own root: working workers, laboring laborers. Root-repetition intensifies: work worthy of the name working. Unworthy work — drowsy, distracted, discontinuous — fails the verse's standard. Standards set high honor the prize: great aims demand great effort. Tanwīr al-Miqbās hears hastening: let those who hasten to righteous deeds hasten. Hastening workers outrun procrastination, the thief of triumphs. Tanwīr adds competing in spending: let spenders compete in Allah's way.

The sūrah stated the principle early: workers are “rewarded for what you used to do” (37:39). Principles precede exhibits: the rule announced, the tale illustrated, the command now seals.

Exertion belongs to the verse: Paradisal honor costs comfort.

---

## Verse 37:62

> Is this ˹bliss˺ a better accommodation or the tree of Zaqqûm

The question compares the two destinations the sūrah has just described: the gardens with their fruits and honour, and the single tree of *Zaqqūm*, which the Book names elsewhere as the food of the sinful (44:43–46; 56:52–53). The Arabic verb behind "accommodation" is *nuzul* — the guest's welcome, the lodging a host prepares — and the verse puts the two welcomes side by side as though asking a traveller which inn he prefers. The answer is not in doubt, and that is the point of asking: the Book puts the question in order to make the hearer answer it himself. Two tables are set, one for the servants of God and one for the wrongdoers, and there is no third. The verse also answers the mockery of the people of Makkah, who laughed at the descriptions of the Hereafter and asked how the promise compared with what they already had.

---

## Verse 37:63

> We have surely made it a test for the wrongdoers

The tree is itself a trial. The sūrah reports that the idolaters made the description of Zaqqūm a joke, and the reports of the occasion name the man who led the laughter: Abū Jahl, who called for dates and butter and invited those around him to eat, saying that this was the Zaqqūm they had been threatened with. The Qur'an calls that reaction a test of the people (37:63), and it does the same for the night journey in a verse that names the trial outright: "We made the vision which We showed you only as a trial for the people, and likewise the accursed tree in the Qur'an" (17:60). *Fitnah* carries both senses here, testing and exposure. The mockery was not neutral: it revealed what the mockers were made of. A teaching that could be laughed at was still the truth, and the laughter becomes part of the evidence against those who chose it.

---

## Verse 37:64

> Indeed, it is a tree that grows in the depths of Hell

The verse fixes where the tree stands: in the bottom of the Fire (*al-jaḥīm*). The point of the detail is that the punishment has been prepared, not improvised — the Book gives the Fire its own landscape, its own food and its own drink (37:66–68; 56:52–55). It also answers the objection that stood behind the mockery in Makkah, that a fire burns trees and cannot grow them. Ibn Kathīr reports the classical reply that the tree is nourished by the same fire that punishes, having been created for it, which is to say that the ordinary rules the hearer knows are no measure of what God has prepared. The two destinations are set side by side in this sūrah, and each is described with the things a person knows — fruit, shade, drink — so that what lies beyond the description is still recognisable by its use. The Book states the same contrast in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah, where the people of the right hand are given lote trees and bananas piled high (56:28–29), and the people of the left are given the tree of Zaqqūm.

---

## Verse 37:65

> bearing fruit like devils’ heads

*Ruʾūs al-shayāṭīn* — heads of devils — is deliberately repulsive, and the commentators take it as a description of something hideous rather than a claim about the appearance of demons: the Arabic leaves the shape unspecified because the imagination supplies worse than any description. The verse extends the argument of 37:62–64: the tree that the mockers asked about has a name and a purpose, and now it has a face. The Book never makes the Fire attractive, and never makes its food sound like food; where the gardens are described with fruits and shade and honour, the Fire is described with what is monstrous. That asymmetry is part of the warning. The same tree is called cursed in 17:60, and the same fruit appears in 44:43–46 as something that boils in the belly like molten brass, so that the repulsion of this verse is the beginning of a description the Book completes in two other sūrahs.

---

## Verse 37:66

> The evildoers will certainly ˹be left to˺ eat from it, filling up their bellies with it

They eat because they must. *Famāliʾūna minhā al-buṭūn* — filling their bellies with it — gives hunger that nothing else can touch, in a place where nothing else is offered. Zamakhsharī asks the question the wording raises: is this hunger that drives them, or force that compels them? Both readings are left open by the verse, and both describe the same helplessness. The Book names the food in the imperative elsewhere — the wrongdoers "will eat of the trees of Zaqqum" (56:52) — and pairs it there with the drink of boiling water that follows (56:54–55), exactly the sequence of this passage. The verse is set against the same word used of the people of the gardens, who have fruits and whatever they wish (37:42–44). In this Book, hell is not only pain: the ordinary comfort of food is turned into part of the punishment, and the men who once ate what they pleased now eat what they cannot refuse.

---

## Verse 37:67

> Then on top of that they will be given a blend of boiling drink

*Thumma inna lahum ʿalayhā lashawban min ḥamīm* — and on top of it a blend of scalding water. The Arabic *shawb* means a mixture, and the commentators take it as the drink watered down for the drinkers of the Fire: the cup is not even presented as a single thing but as a brew. The Book pairs the same two courses elsewhere, with boiling fluid and oozing pus as the only hospitality (38:57–58), and in Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah it adds the manner of drinking — like thirst-crazed camels (56:54–55). Against all of this stands the drink of the gardens, "from a flowing stream" (37:45), and the rivers of water, milk, wine and honey that the Book describes for the righteous (47:15). The point of naming the drink is the same as the point of naming the food: nothing in either destination is left vague, and the two menus are complete opposites.

---

## Verse 37:68

> Then they will ultimately return to ˹their place in˺ Hell

After the meal comes the return: *thumma inna marjiʿahum la-ilā al-jaḥīm* — then their return is to the Fire. The food and drink of the previous verses are stages on a road, not an escape from it, and the verse uses the language of journey and destination that the sūrah also uses for the believers' entry into the gardens. The sentence has the force of *thumma*, which marks a step after a step: having been given the welcome and the meal, the guests are sent back to where they came from. Sūrah al-Raḥmān describes the same rotation between the blaze and the boiling water (55:44), and Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah closes its account of the tree with the sentence that this is their entertainment on the Day of Judgement (56:56). The Book's hospitality to the deniers is complete, and the last course of it is the place they started from, which is what makes the welcome of the gardens in 37:41–49 the real invitation of the sūrah.

---

## Verse 37:69

> Indeed, they found their forefathers astray

The verse gives the reason the punishment falls on followers as well as leaders. They did not arrive at denial by investigation; they found their fathers on a road and took it as *the* road. The Book gives the same excuse in the mouths of the idolaters who were shown the message: "We follow what we found our fathers doing" (2:170). It reports Ṭabarī's diagnosis of the verse's word *muḥtadūn* — they imagined themselves guided, which is the deadliest form of the error, because a person who thinks he is guided does not look for a road. The sūrah has already shown the same fault in the quarrel of the Fire, where the misled blame those who misled them (37:27–29), and it is why the next verse says they rushed along the footprints. Inherited practice is one of the most durable forms of unbelief, because it is not defended by argument at all — only by the assumption that a tradition cannot be wrong.

---

## Verse 37:70

> so they rushed in their footsteps

*Fahum ʿalā āthārihim yuhraʿūn* — they rush along their traces. The verb describes hurrying with something close to panic, and the picture is sharp: not a calm inheritance but a stampede, with eyes fixed on the prints ahead instead of the evidence. The Book uses the same image for people who reject a prophet out of loyalty to what their fathers did, and it reports their answer when they are called: "We found our fathers following a certain way, and we are guided by their footsteps" (43:22–23). The sequence of verbs in the two verses is deliberate: they found the traces (37:69), then they ran in them (37:70). Nobody running looks at the road, and nobody with his father's footsteps in front of him stops to ask where they lead, which is why the warnings of the next verse did not move them. The Qur'an reports the same defence from the Fire itself, where the followers say they were obeying the leaders who showed them the way (33:67).

---

## Verse 37:71

> And surely most of the earlier generations had strayed before them

The frame widens: this generation is not the first to go astray, and being part of a long tradition proves nothing. The Book uses this argument repeatedly against the claim of ancestry — most of the ancients, it says here, had gone the same way — and the exception is named three verses later, God's chosen servants (37:74). Rāzī reads the sentence as both warning and consolation: a warning to a community that measures truth by numbers, and a comfort to a prophet whose own people refuse him, since the pattern of rejection is as old as the prophets themselves. The Book's own summary of that history is blunt: in every generation the majority followed what they inherited, and the few who held to the truth were the pattern that mattered. It is a hard lesson for a community that equates continuity with correctness, and the sūrah states it as flatly as it can.

---

## Verse 37:72

> although We had certainly sent warners among them

This sentence removes the last excuse. The generations that went astray were not left without a message: warners were sent among them (*mundhirūn*), which means the knowledge reached them and the judgement rests on what they did with it. The sūrah's own stories are the proof — Noah, Abraham, Moses, Elijah, Lot and Jonah were all sent to warn (37:75–148), and each faced a community with inherited practice. The Book's rule is stated in many places: God does not punish a community until a messenger has come and the case has been made, so that no one can say afterwards that he was never told (6:131; 17:15), and the Book adds that every community has had its warner (35:24). The verse therefore also turns the sūrah's own text into a warning to the person reading it: he has now been warned by the same kind of messenger he is reading about, and the question of what he does with it has become his own.

---

## Verse 37:73

> See then what was the end of those who had been warned

*Fa-unẓur* — see, then look — turns the account into an investigation. The command is addressed to whoever hears the revelation, and the evidence is the record of the warned communities, whose stories this sūrah has just told. Rāzī notes the double address: the command is spoken to the Prophet, and through him to every denier who hears it. The same formula appears in Sūrah Yūnus at the end of the story of Noah: "See then what was the end of those who were warned" (10:73), and the Qur'an uses it again at 37:137, where the Meccans are told they pass by the ruins of Lot's people on their travels. It is the Book's habitual method: it does not only assert that denial is punished, it points at the ruined sites, names the communities, and asks the listener to draw his own conclusion from what he can see with his own eyes on the road he travels.

---

## Verse 37:74

> But not the chosen servants of Allah

The exception closes the survey of the generations that went astray: among all those multitudes, the servants God chose for Himself were kept. Rāzī weighs what the exception attaches to: were the chosen saved from the straying itself, or from the ruined end that followed it? The second reading fits the passage, since the previous verse asked about the end of the warned, and the chosen are precisely those who escape that end. The phrase *ʿibād Allāh al-mukhlaṣīn* recurs at 37:40, 37:128 and 37:160, and each time it marks the same boundary between the saved and the lost. The sūrah will spend its second half naming them — Noah, Abraham, Moses and Aaron, Elijah, Lot and Jonah — so the reader can see what the exception looks like in practice. The verse is short because it is a hinge: the verdicts are finished, and the Book now turns to the stories of those who were spared.

---

## Verse 37:75

> Indeed, Noah cried out to Us, and how excellent are We in responding

The stories begin with the prophet whose call lasted longest and whose answer was the most terrible for his people. *Nādā* — he cried out — is the prayer of a man who had preached for centuries without result, and the Book records the prayer in full elsewhere: "I have been overcome, so help me" (54:10). The verse's second half is praise of God for answering — *niʿma al-mujībūn*, how excellent are the responders — and the answer is described in the next verse as the deliverance of Noah and his family from the great distress. Noah is the model of the *ʿibād Allāh al-mukhlaṣīn* named in the previous verse: chosen, and therefore kept. The scholars also note that the Book presents the deliverance as an answer to the call rather than a reward for the centuries of preaching, which is the point of the praise: the credit for the rescue belongs to the Responder, not to the man who called.

---

## Verse 37:76

> We delivered him and his family from the great distress

The great distress (*al-karb al-ʿaẓīm*) is the drowning of the world and the mockery that came before it; the same words describe the deliverance God granted in the calamity of the sea. The Book says Noah called, and the sea itself became the answer (54:11–12); his household was spared, and the rest of his people were not. The verse is one of a series of rescues in this sūrah — Abraham from the fire (37:98), Moses and Aaron from Pharaoh (37:116), Jonah from the fish (37:142–144) — and the series teaches the reader what the service of God promises: rescue rather than comfort. The Book repeats the same account in Sūrah Hūd, where the boat is described as running under God's eyes and the waters are called off when the decree is fulfilled (11:37–44). That the reader of this short verse knows exactly what stands behind it: a household, a ship, and a world under water.

---

## Verse 37:77

> and made his descendants the sole survivors

After the flood, the human line that continued was Noah's. *Dhurriyyatahu* — his descendants — are the peoples who descend from his sons, and the Book mentions their scattering across the earth after the waters subside: "O Noah, descend with peace from Us and blessings on you and on the peoples of your descendants" (11:48). The claim matters to this sūrah's first audience, because the Quraysh traced themselves to Noah's line and honoured him as a forefather, and Sūrah al-Isrāʾ goes further, calling the Israelites the descendants of those carried in the ship with him (17:3). The verse quietly separates a noble ancestor from the practice of those who claim him: lineage is not a shield, and the religion of the idols is not the religion of Noah. The story's order also matters — deliverance, descendants, mention — since the honour that follows in the next verses is the fruit of faithfulness, not of family.

---

## Verse 37:78

> And We blessed him ˹with honourable mention˺ among later generations

Wa-taraknā ʿalayhi — "and We left upon him" — is how the Qur'an describes a blessing that stays after a person dies: he was left among the later generations as a name spoken with honour. Ibn ʿAbbās explained it as being "remembered in a good way." The proof is that the greeting of this verse is still alive: the blessing did not remain written in one book but entered the mouth of every later generation. It entered the prayer itself, where peace is sent upon Abraham in every tashahhud. Reward in the Qur'an is not measured by length of life or size of wealth; here a man's trial on one day became a honour that runs to the end of time. The verse closes the story it opened — the son was ransomed, the father was thanked, and both were left with a good name among the peoples who came after.

---

## Verse 37:79

> “Peace be upon Noah among all peoples.”

"Peace be upon Noah among all peoples" is scripture itself greeting a prophet. The greeting is not placed in the mouth of the angels or of the believers; Allah says it, which is why no greeting can be higher. It is "among all peoples" — not one nation only — because Noah was the messenger of a very long age and the father of the believers who came after the flood, so every later people inherit the duty to say this peace. Maududi notes that such greetings in the Qur'an are a form of continuing prayer: peace is asked for the prophet again and again, generation after generation. The sūrah has just told how his people were drowned and he was delivered; the greeting is the verdict on that whole history. A man opposed by his own household and mocked by his town for centuries is answered at last by his Lord's own salutation.

---

## Verse 37:80

> Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers

"Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers" is the refrain of the sūrah. It comes after Noah, and it will come again after Abraham, Moses and Aaron, Elijah and Lot and Jonah (37:105, 37:110, 37:121, 37:131, 37:148), because the same rule governs them all. The word for the reward here is delivered in the honour of the previous verse: the good man is given a name that later generations greet with peace. The Book states the rule as a question elsewhere — "Is the reward of goodness anything but goodness?" (55:60) — and it defines the good-doers in the same sūrah's own terms: those who submit their faces to God and do good (4:125). What the verse does not say is equally important. The reward is not payment for services rendered but the fitting outcome of a life, which is why the sūrah states it as a rule rather than as a favour.

---

## Verse 37:81

> ˹For˺ he was truly one of Our faithful servants

"He was truly one of Our faithful servants" — the honour of Noah is stated in two words: servant, and believer. Servanthood (ʿubūdiyyah) is the highest rank the Qur'an gives a human being; it was given to the Prophet ﷺ at his greatest moments, in the night journey ("Glory to Him who took His servant by night," 17:1) and at the revelation of the sūrah of the stars. "Faithful" (muʾmin) adds trust: Noah believed Allah's promise while the flood had not yet come and while the whole society disbelieved it. Faith is measured at exactly this point — holding to the word of Allah when nothing around you supports it. The verse therefore tells the reader what to aim for. Not prophethood, which is not ours to choose, but faithful servanthood, which is. Whoever reaches that rank stands with Noah, and the reward of the good-doers is his by right.

---

## Verse 37:82

> Then We drowned the others

"Then We drowned the others" compresses the end of a very long story into a single act. "The others" — *al-ākharīn* — does everything: those who mocked the ship, called the warning madness, and stood watching the animals board are not named, not counted, and not argued with. The Book tells the same story at length in Sūrah Hūd, where the waters are described as boiling over, the ark running under God's eyes, and a son refusing to board until a wave comes between him and his father (11:40–43). Here the reader gets only the outcome, and the brevity is the point: after the cry, the deliverance, and the lineage, the sūrah refuses to make the destruction a spectacle. It is mentioned, and it is over. The same sentence closes the account in 26:120, where the ark, the rescue, and the drowning are given in three verses and left behind.

---

## Verse 37:83

> And indeed, one of those who followed his way was Abraham

"And indeed, one of those who followed his way was Abraham." The sūrah moves from one prophet to the next not by blood only but by succession of truth: Noah had a way (millah), and Abraham walked in it. The Qur'an elsewhere commands the Prophet ﷺ and every believer to follow "the creed of your father Abraham" (42:13), so his way is a standing model: pure monotheism, public argument against idols, trust in Allah under persecution, and worship carried out to the last detail when commanded. To be "one of those who followed his way" is presented here as the highest genealogy — better than descent from a prophet is obedience to a prophet's path. The verse also introduces the long Abraham passage that fills the rest of the sūrah's first half: Noah's story ended in drowning; Abraham's will end in ransom and greeting, because the following verses show how Allah rewards a servant who argues, migrates, and obeys.

---

## Verse 37:84

> ˹Remember˺ when he came to his Lord with a pure heart

"He came to his Lord with a pure heart" — the Arabic is qalb salīm, a heart that is safe and whole. Ibn ʿAbbās explained it as a heart that bears witness that there is no god but Allah and is free of doubt and pollution. The phrase names the only capital Abraham carried into his historic confrontation: no army, no wealth, no tribe standing behind him — one sound heart. Everything he did afterwards, the argument with his father, the mocking of the idols, the walk into the fire, the migration, the wait for a child, the knife on his son — all of it issued from that one inward purity. The verse turns the mirror on the reader: deeds are many in every age, but what enters the presence of the Lord is the heart carried to Him. A heart can be "safe" only when it is emptied of rivals to Allah and settled on Him; that settlement, not the noise of works, is the beginning of every true service.

---

## Verse 37:85

> and said to his father and his people, “What are you worshipping

Abraham begins his mission with a question, not a proclamation: "What are you worshipping?" The same dialogue is recorded at greater length in Sūrah 26, and there his people answer honestly: "We worship idols and will continue to worship them" (26:70–71). The question is deliberately open, because the argument Abraham will build depends on the answer. He does not tell them that their gods are false before they have said what those gods are and what they do; he lets them describe the worship and then shows what it amounts to. The scene also establishes the method of the whole Abraham narrative that follows: the confrontation is with his own father and his own people (37:85, 6:74), and the Book presents him as arguing from within his community rather than denouncing it from outside. The next verses take the argument through their idols and into the sky.

---

The question is the opening of Abraham's case, and the sūrah of the Poets preserves the exchange in full: he asks them what they worship, they answer that they worship what their fathers did, and he tells them that they and their fathers are in clear error (26:70–77). The sūrah of the Cattle records the same reasoning from inheritance in the mouths of the idolaters themselves (6:148), so the question here is chosen to force the admission that the practice has no reason behind it other than custom. Abraham's own path out of that inheritance is narrated a few verses later in this sūrah: he looked at the star, the moon and the sun and gave each up as it set (6:76–78 in the sūrah of the Cattle, and 37:88–89 here).

## Verse 37:86

> Is it false gods that you desire instead of Allah

"Is it false gods that you desire instead of Allah?" — the question exposes the exchange. The idols are called "false" (āliha) precisely because they can claim worship but deliver nothing. Abraham's generation, like every generation, had made its preferences and dressed them as religion: gods nearer to hand, images easier to approach than the unseen Lord. The parallel verse (26:71) shows they had no defence ready — they could only say, "We found our fathers doing this." The verse therefore connects two sins that seem different: the religious error (worshipping what cannot benefit) and the social comfort (following ancestors). Abraham's question cuts both: you *desire* these — so the choice was desire, not knowledge. Every age repeats the exchange, replacing the unseen Lord with what it can see, manage, and carve. The verse asks the reader to check what he turns to first in hope and fear, because that, not the creed recited, is what he desires instead of Allah.

---

## Verse 37:87

> What then do you expect from the Lord of all worlds?”

"What then do you expect from the Lord of all worlds?" — the question completes the argument. If you set up rivals, what is your estimate of the Lord you set them beside? Qatādah read the verse as a question of fear: do you fear these idols and not fear the Lord of the worlds? Either way — hope or fear — the idols have made the people small in their own religion. They expect little from the One who owns all worlds and much from stones that cannot speak. The phrase "Lord of all worlds" re-enters here deliberately: the sūrah used it to honour Allah at the head of a surah of the worshippers, and Abraham uses it in debate to measure the absurdity of the exchange. A person's religion is finally a set of expectations — what he truly thinks will act for him. The verse presses the reader to answer for his own expectations, for no one escapes the question: what do you expect?

---

## Verse 37:88

> He later looked up to the stars ˹in contemplation˺

*Fa-naẓara naẓratan fī al-nujūm* — he looked a look into the stars. The Book gives the gesture without the reasoning, because the reasoning decides what kind of Prophet Abraham was in this scene: some readers take his words in the next verse about being sick as a ruse to leave the festival, and the glance at the stars as the excuse he used. His people were star-worshippers and readers of the sky, and Ṭabarī reports the view that Abraham used their own science against them, telling them by the stars that the night held something for him. What follows settles the point: he does not leave to escape worship, he leaves to reach the idols (37:91–93). The scene is a turning point in the sūrah's Abraham narrative, the moment the argument moves from words to action, and the Book tells it as the prophet's own initiative rather than a divine command.

---

## Verse 37:89

> then said, “I am really sick.”

He turns to his people and announces: "I am really sick." The words are a plan, not a lie in the ordinary sense, and Ḥasan al-Baṣrī explained it as a device to be left behind from their idol-feast so that he could act on their gods. The scholars who discussed this verse drew the careful line: a believer does not lie, but a statement that turns the enemy away from harm — called in law tawriyah, an utterance with a true inner meaning (a sickness of heart over their idols) — is permitted in defence of faith. Abraham, the "intensely truthful" (ṣiddīq), is the one who does this, which itself proves the exception is narrow and need-based, not a licence. The verse also shows the prophet's patience of method: he does not storm the feast; he lets the people walk away from him. The next verses show why — the argument he is about to make needs an empty temple and an absent crowd.

---

## Verse 37:90

> So they turned their backs on him and went away

"So they turned their backs on him and went away." The plan worked to the letter: the town, confident in its gods and dismissive of the young man who claimed sickness, leaves him alone. The verse is quiet, but it records one of the great turnarounds of the whole story: the moment a whole society stops watching the one man who has been threatening its religion. Qatādah and the commentators note the irony — they went to glorify their gods and and so handed their gods over to their opponent. There is a standing lesson in how Allah prepares deliverance: the opening for truth often arrives dressed as an insult ("let him alone, he is sick"). The believer who is mocked out of a gathering may be being positioned by Allah for exactly the work that gathering could not afford to witness. The next verses open the temple door behind them.

---

## Verse 37:91

> Then he ˹stealthily˺ advanced towards their gods, and said ˹mockingly˺, “Will you not eat ˹your offerings˺

Alone in the temple, Abraham turns to the idols with mock courtesy: "Will you not eat?" The question weaponises a known fact — offerings are laid before the gods, and the gods never touch them. The verse says he came "stealthily" (the bracket explains, quietly, by design), because the argument needs no audience of idolaters, only an audience of idols. In Sūrah 21 the fuller scene appears: Abraham asks them, "What is this you worship, you and your fathers?" and swears by Allah, "I will surely plot against your idols after you have gone away" (21:57–58). Here the sūrah keeps only the knife-edge of it: the offering question. It is the argument of common sense — worship is due to what gives, and what does not even take cannot give. The reader is meant to feel the silence of the temple: the gods eat nothing, and the silence is their whole biography.

---

## Verse 37:92

> What is wrong with you that you cannot speak?”

The second question goes to the point: "What is wrong with you that you cannot speak?" An idol that cannot answer cannot guide, cannot warn, cannot thank, cannot curse — cannot do the least thing a worshipper needs a god to do. The Qur'an states the verdict plainly elsewhere: "Do they have legs to walk with, hands to strike with, eyes to see with, ears to hear with?" — "Say: Call upon your 'partners' and plot against me. Give me no respite" — for "those you call upon besides Allah are servants like you" (7:195–194). Abraham's mockery is not cruelty; it is the fastest route to make a whole people see what they have always seen and never looked at. Every idolater has heard his idol's silence. The verse asks the modern reader the same question with different objects: whatever cannot speak to you — wealth, image, faction — is standing in the place of a God who speaks.

---

## Verse 37:93

> Then he swiftly turned on them, striking ˹them˺ with his right hand

"Then he swiftly turned on them, striking with the right hand" — Abraham breaks the idols, beginning the strike from the right, and the verb "turned" gives the movement of a man working through the temple. Sūrah 21 completes the picture: he left the largest idol standing and hung the axe's work on it — "Ask them, if they can speak" (21:63–64). The sūrah's version keeps only the act itself, the physical demolition of the sacred objects. Why the right hand? The commentators note the strength and decisiveness the phrase carries — this is no covert vandalism but an open, deliberate act of argument by deed. Idols had claimed lordship; a hand with an axe disproves the claim in public fact. The scene teaches that when speech is refused ("they turned their backs"), truth may still act, and that no object worshipped by men is too sacred to be broken by the command of the One who made men.

---

## Verse 37:94

> Later, his people came rushing towards him ˹furiously˺

"His people came rushing towards him" — the word is "rushing" (aqsū), the speed of scandal. The news that the temple gods lie broken has emptied the feast, and the whole town arrives in a body. The verse does not hide the danger: a lone man, surrounded by an enraged crowd that has just seen its holiest objects destroyed. Yet within the rush the reader can see Allah's timing: Abraham wanted an audience, and now he has the whole city in one place. The rage that means to kill him will first have to hear him, and the argument he prepared in the empty temple will now be delivered at full volume to the very people who walked away from it. Prophetic history turns often on this hinge — the try to silence a voice becomes the assembly that hears it. The next verse shows Abraham completely unhurried, asking them a question as if nothing had happened.

---

## Verse 37:95

> He argued, “How can you worship what you carve ˹with your own hands˺

The crowd asks who did this to their gods. Abraham answers with the question that has already done its work: "How can you worship what you carve?" — then, with the irony already prepared in the temple, he points to the biggest idol: "Nay, it was this one, the largest of them, that did it — ask them, if they can speak" (21:63). The verse here keeps the core: worship is due to the Maker, not to the made, and carving is the confession of the made. An image is a receipt of human labour — someone designed it, chiselled it, carried it into the shrine. To bow to it is to bow to a fellow creature's workmanship. The argument has never expired: every religion of man-made objects and man-made ideas asks for worship while carrying its maker's fingerprints. Abraham's question remains the shortest test of any claim to divinity: did hands make you?

---

## Verse 37:96

> when it is Allah Who created you and whatever you do?”

The crowning argument: "when it is Allah Who created you and whatever you do." The verse settles in one line the oldest question of destiny: our acts are Allah's creation, and yet they are ours — we do them, and we are judged for them. The commentators balanced the two truths with acquisition (kasb): the ability, the limb, the opportunity, the outcome — all are created by Allah; the willing and the doing are attributed to the servant, who earns their weight. The verse in its context answers the idolaters first: your carving is also a created act — your gods are creatures of your own hands twice over, shaped by you and "created" in you by Allah. But the verse cannot be locked into that context, and the scholars refused to lock it: it is a full statement of the relationship between the Creator and every human deed. Man acts freely and is accountable; Allah wills all and is judged by none.

---

## Verse 37:97

> They said ˹to one another˺, “Build him a furnace and cast him into the blazing fire.”

The people's answer to the argument is a construction project: a furnace built to hold a fire large enough for a man. The same scene appears in Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ with a sharper command — "Burn him up to avenge your gods, if you must act" (21:68) — and the Book tells there what came of it: "O fire! Be cool and safe for Abraham!" (21:69). The verse is deliberately short. It records the moment argument ends, when a community that has lost the discussion turns the disagreement into an instrument of death, and the narration moves at once to God's answer, because the interest of the passage is not the furnace but what came out of it. The same escalation appears elsewhere in the Book — the magicians of Pharaoh threatened with crucifixion, the chiefs of Lot's city threatening expulsion — and in each case force begins where the argument has failed.

---

## Verse 37:98

> And so they sought to harm him, but We made them inferior

*Fa-arādū bihi kaydan fa-jaʿalnāhum al-asfalīn* — they intended a plot against him, and We made them the lowest. The verdict reverses the attempt in the same sentence, and the word for their plan (*kayd*) is the word the Book uses for scheming that comes to nothing against God's purpose. The scene is picked up in Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ, where the fire becomes coolness and safety (21:69), and in Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt, where the answer is stated as a general rule: those who plot against the truth become the losers (29:4). The Book also gives the outcome of this particular plot at length in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where Abraham is shown the ruined sanctuaries of those who were given power before him (2:258). The verse closes the section by naming the reversal rather than the punishment, which is the pattern of the sūrah's accounts: the rescue is reported, the ruin is mentioned, and the reader is left with the difference between the two.

---

## Verse 37:99

> He later said, “I am leaving ˹in obedience˺ to my Lord. He will guide me

Abraham leaves. "I am going to my Lord" — the Arabic is hijrah, migration, and Maududi notes the meaning: he emigrates for his faith, leaving father, homeland, and the valley of idols, going nowhere in particular because he goes to Allah. The statement contains a complete doctrine of trust: not "I am fleeing them" but "I am going to Him — He will guide me." A believer may leave a place without leaving the path, because the destination is not geography. The Qur'an honours this exact move elsewhere — "those who emigrate in the way of Allah... Surely Allah will guide them" — and Abraham's hijrah ends not in exile but in a son, a sanctuary-line, and a name blessed among generations. The verse consoles every believer who has had to leave home, work, or companionship for the sake of religion: the road out, walked for Allah, is itself the road of guidance.

---

## Verse 37:100

> My Lord! Bless me with righteous offspring.”

Settled in his new land, Abraham prays one prayer: "My Lord, grant me a righteous child." The wording matters — not a child, but a righteous one; Abraham asks for quality of faith, not only of lineage. The prayer is recorded again in Sūrah 14, where it continues with protection for his descendants from idolatry, and his fear that they might yet worship idols is stated there openly. Maududi draws the lesson of the order of the story: the man who argued with a nation, out-plotted a priesthood, and walked out of a fire now asks for a child with the humility of a beggar — "grant me" (hab lī). Great servants of Allah keep the smallness of supplication. The prayer also fixes the purpose of family in faith: children are asked for as a trust for righteousness, "of the righteous," so that the way of Noah and Abraham continues. The next verses show how completely the prayer was answered — beyond what he asked.

---

## Verse 37:101

> So We gave him good news of a forbearing son

"So We gave him good news of a forbearing son" — the Arabic bashīr, news of joy, and the child is named by one quality: ḥalīm, forbearing, slow to anger, able to bear the hard command without bitterness. The commentators identify the son as Ismāʿīl, because the scene that follows fits the "patient, forbearing" character and because the early Muslims of the Prophet's ﷺ city, children of Ismāʿīl's line, understood it so. A minority read Isḥāq, and the difference of opinion is old and honest — the Qur'an's own sequence supports Ismāʿīl here and mentions Isaac's good news separately afterwards. What the verse teaches does not depend on the name: the answer to "grant me a righteous child" is a child whose defining trait is the ability to bear the command of Allah. The good news of heaven is not ease for the father but virtue in the son — and the next verse will show how far that virtue went.

---

## Verse 37:102
> Then when the boy reached the age to work with him, Abraham said, “O my dear son! I have seen in a dream that I ˹must˺ sacrifice you. So tell me what you think.” He replied, “O my dear father! Do as you are commanded. Allah willing, you will find me steadfast.”

**A Son Who Has Become a Companion**
The verse opens with the moment the boy reaches the age of working alongside his father. That phrase is the hinge of the story. Abraham is not asked to give up an infant. He is asked to give up a grown son who now shares his labour and his purpose, which is what makes the command so heavy.

The verse also shows how he handles it. He tells the boy about the dream instead of hiding it, and that openness is part of what the Book holds up in this account. The boy answers with the obedience the sūrah presents as a model (37:102 records his words): you will find me, if God wills, steadfast. So the verse contains three things at once: a prophet obeying something he does not fully understand, a father being honest with his son, and a young man agreeing to be part of what God asked.

**The Dream and What It Means**
The Book reports the dream as a vision, and the scholars discuss whether a prophet's vision in this tradition amounts to a command, since the visions of prophets are treated as a form of revelation rather than as ordinary dreams. The sūrah's own account of Abraham's earlier years supplies the background: he was tested with the stars, the moon and the sun, and with the fire, and he passed each test (6:75 through 6:79 record the first test and 21:68 and 21:69 record the fire). This sūrah says plainly that he fulfilled what he was commanded (37:105 states it). Then the sūrah of the Heifer sets his willingness among the tests he fulfilled and names him a leader of the people after them (2:124 gives the promise).

**The Ransom and What Follows**
The scene ends with the ransom that replaced the boy, described in the sūrah as a great sacrifice (37:107 names it). Then comes the announcement of a second son, named Isaac, and the promise of blessing on him and his descendants (37:112 and 37:113 give both). The sūrah places the trial and the promise in one continuous account, so the reader sees the gift, the test and the gift again in sequence.

**What the Passage Teaches About Surrender**
The whole passage works as the Qur'an's fullest statement of what submission looks like when it costs something. Abraham is shown in the act of handing back the very thing he had begged God for over decades. He does not argue, and he does not hide the matter from the person most affected by it. The boy answers in the same register, and the sūrah treats both of them as models. This episode is also where the community's festival of sacrifice finds its root, since the rite repeated every year across the Muslim world commemorates this ransom and the willingness behind it. The outward act of the festival is a reminder of the inward act recorded in this verse: a person placing what he loves most on the table and letting God's command decide what happens to it.

---
## Verse 37:103

> Then when they submitted ˹to Allah’s Will˺, and Abraham laid him on the side of his forehead ˹for sacrifice˺

"Then when they submitted" — the Arabic is aslamā, "both surrendered," father and son together. The grammar of the sentence carries the whole story: the dream commanded Abraham to sacrifice the boy; the boy answered, "My father, do as you are commanded; you will find me, if Allah wills, among the patient" (37:102). Here the surrender is complete on both sides, and Abraham lays the son on the side of his forehead — face down, so as not to look into his eyes, and the blade passes over the neck's edge at once, the quickest mercy the act allowed. The commentators pause on the detail: the forehead, not the throat, tells of a hand shaking with love and obedience at once. Islam means this surrender — not the word only, but the will placed under the command at the exact point where it costs most. The whole religion of tawḥīd is named after what father and son did on that morning.

---

## Verse 37:104

> We called out to him, “O Abraham

"We called out to him: O Abraham!" — a direct call from the Lord of the worlds, by name. In the Qur'an, the personal call marks the great moments: to Mūsā in the sacred valley ("O Mūsā!"), to Adam ("O Adam!"), and here to Abraham at the knife's edge. The commentators note what the call means: the trial is over, and the servant is not left in silence — Heaven speaks his name. Consider the timing: not after the trial began to spare him, but at its completion, when the surrender was total and the blade had reached its mark. The reward of tested obedience is audience. Every believer who says the prayer of Abraham in the tashahhud repeats the honorifics that grew from this moment, but the verse itself is more intimate than any liturgy: a man alone on a hill with his son, and the Lord of the worlds calls him by name to stay his hand.

---

## Verse 37:105

> You have already fulfilled the vision.” Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers

"You have already fulfilled the vision" — the dream of a prophet is revelation, so the command was real, and the "fulfilment" was the whole-hearted going-forth with the act, not the shedding of blood. This is why the jurists say Abraham is the pattern of carrying out Allah's command to its brink: obedience is measured by the intention completed under the limb's obedience, and Allah stopped the blade. The verse then closes with the refrain: "Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers." The reader should feel the scale of "reward": a knife stayed mid-air, a son returned alive, a name blessed to the end of time. No test commanded by Allah ends in pure loss; either the event is turned aside at the last instant, or it is repaid beyond counting on the other side. The trial of the vision asked for everything; the reward answered with everything. That is the standing law this refrain keeps repeating.

---

The words "you have already fulfilled the vision" state the measure of the requirement in the same moment they lift it: the prophet's dream is revelation, so the intention and the surrender were the act, and the Book calls this the reward of those who do good. The sūrah of the Heifer uses the same language about Abraham's fulfilment of his Lord's words (2:124), and the Book's principle here is stated in the sūrah of the Heifer regarding sacrifice: it is not the flesh or the blood that reaches God but the mindfulness of the one who offers (22:37). The verse that follows names the ransom of a great sacrifice, which is the exchange made in place of the act.

## Verse 37:106

> That was truly a revealing test

"That was truly a revealing test" — balāʾ mubīn, a trial made manifest. Jalālayn explains: an evident, plain trial, and the verse invites the reader to see how plain it was — the thing most precious to Abraham, requested by Allah, at the age when the son was his only companion and hope. The word "test" (balāʾ) in the Qur'an never means Allah needs to find out; it means the quality is brought out into the open, for the servant, for the people, for the generations. Abraham's monotheism was already famous; this trial exposed its depth — was the "pure heart" attached to Allah even against his own child? The test was "clear" in a second sense: it made clear to every later believer what Islam asks and what it is worth. The sūrah says of the trial what it says of the reward: both were public, both were real, and neither was a metaphor.

---

## Verse 37:107

> And We ransomed his son with a great sacrifice

"And We ransomed him with a great sacrifice." The word "ransom" (fadaynāhu) means a life was taken as due and another given in its place. The "great sacrifice" (dhibḥ ʿaẓīm) is understood by the commentators as the ram sent from heaven, "great" because it came from Allah Himself, and great in what it would become. The scholars connect it to the sacrifice of the pilgrimage and of ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā, which the Prophet ﷺ called the sunnah of your father Abraham: every year the ummah repeats the ransom, and by it the humanity of the believer joins the obedience of Abraham. The deeper lesson stands behind the ritual: life belongs to Allah; the command proved the heart, and the blood of the animal, as the Prophet ﷺ said, does not reach Allah — "what reaches Him is the fear of you" (22:37). The ransom has never been about blood. It is about the surrendered heart, memorialised in every sacrifice offered since.

---

## Verse 37:108

> and blessed Abraham ˹with honourable mention˺ among later generations

"and blessed Abraham with honourable mention among later generations" — the blessing is concrete and audible. In the prayers of the Jews ("in you all families of the earth shall be blessed" was the promise to Abraham), in the greetings of the Christians. Above all in the five daily prayers of the Muslims — "O Allah, send blessings upon Abraham and upon the family of Abraham" — the man is named with honour by billions, every day, centuries after his time. The Qur'an states the fact without mechanism: Allah "left" him a good name (the sense of 37:78), and the leaving was Allah's own act, not the world's accident. For the believer the verse is a window into how reward works: the currency of the hereafter can be paid out inside history as remembrance, influence, and honour that outlive death. Abraham asked for "a truthful tongue among the later generations" (26:84); the answer still runs in every mosque on earth.

---

## Verse 37:109

> “Peace be upon Abraham.”

"Peace be upon Abraham" — the greeting follows the blessing as its echo: he who is remembered with honour is greeted with peace. Yūsuf ʿAlī's translation doubles the phrase — "Peace and salutation to Abraham" — bringing out the Hebrew-style fullness of the salutation, salām plus ʿalayhi salām, the completeness of a wish that no harm ever touch the honoured servant. The greeting is now the permanent possession of the sūrah's readers: whoever recites al-Ṣāffāt recites it. In the prayer the Muslim says of Abraham, "upon him be peace and mercy of Allah," and in the pilgrimage the pilgrim walks the path of his hijrah and stands where he stood. The verse is short because a greeting is short; it is placed here so that the story of the knife ends not with trauma but with salutation — Allah's own etiquette for His friend: the trial told, and then the peace pronounced.

---

## Verse 37:110

> This is how We reward the good-doers

"This is how We reward the good-doers" — the refrain sounds again, now after the greeting, so that the reader ties the peace and the honour directly to the doing of good. The Arabic muḥsinīn — those who do good — is the broadest rank of virtue: not sinless perfection, but iḥsān, the doing of what is right with excellence and sincerity. The repetition through the sūrah (after Noah in 37:80, after Abraham here, after Moses and Aaron in 37:121) drills one law into the hearer: outcomes differ in this world, but the ledger of Allah never mispays. The reward already listed is staggering — ransom from death, a son, an eternal name, the greeting of Allah — and it was paid for one quality: he "came to his Lord with a pure heart" and obeyed at the point of greatest cost. The refrain is placed so that no reader can miss the application: this is how. The same standing reward waits on the same standing quality.

---

## Verse 37:111

> He was truly one of Our faithful servants

"He was truly one of Our faithful servants" — the pericope of Abraham ends where Noah's ended, with the same two-word rank: servant, believer (ʿabd, muʾmin). The repetition is deliberate: the sūrah's roll of honour does not advance by inventions or titles but by one qualification repeated. Abraham, the intimate friend of Allah (khalīl), the slayer of idols, the builder of the sanctuary, is summarised as a faithful servant — the same summary as Noah. The lesson for the reader is both humbling and bracing: nothing in the Qur'an's estimate of a human being outranks faithfulness. Genius, courage, and eloquence are all in the story, but the verdict line contains none of them; it contains trust obeyed. Whoever aspires to stand in this roll — and the sūrah's recitation is meant to create that aspiration — knows exactly the entry requirement, because it is printed after every name: servant of Allah, faithful.

---

## Verse 37:112

> We ˹later˺ gave him good news of Isaac—a prophet, and one of the righteous

"We ˹later˺ gave him good news of Isaac — a prophet. One of the righteous." The word "later" (bashsharnāhu bi-isḥāq) settles a famous question: the son of the sacrifice passage has just concluded, so the sacrificial son and Isaac are two different sons, and the majority of the commentators — reading with the sequence — identify the ransomed son as Ismāʿīl. Isaac's news is given separately, as a second gift after the first trial: to the man who surrendered one son, Allah gives another. Marks this one with prophethood before his birth — "a prophet, one of the righteous." The verse teaches that Allah's giving is not impoverished by our trials: what was asked of Abraham did not close the door of joy against him. And the order of the titles is instructive — prophethood (the office) is joined to righteousness (the character), as if to say the second qualifies the first: in the Qur'an's estimate, a prophet must first be one of the righteous.

---

## Verse 37:113

> We blessed him and Isaac as well. Some of their descendants did good, while others clearly wronged themselves

The blessing flows to both: "We blessed him and Isaac," and from their households came those who did good and those who "clearly wronged themselves." The verse states a truth the Qur'an insists on everywhere: righteousness is not inherited automatically. The children of prophets included believers and wrongdoers — Isaac's line carried both the Israelite prophets and those who "did wrong," and the Qur'an never hides it. "Wronged themselves" (ẓallama nafsah) is the Qur'an's exact phrase for sin: the sinner's real victim is his own soul, whatever harm reaches others; he traded lasting good for a moment's appetite. For the reader the verse is both comfort and warning in one line. Comfort: a righteous parent's blessing is a real gift sought by prayer. Warning: the gift must be received by each soul in faith and practice. No lineage, including the family of Abraham, carries anyone to paradise without the person's own surrender.

---

The blessing falls on both Abraham and Isaac, and the verse immediately notes that the descendants of the two would divide into the doers of good and the plainly unjust, which the Book states of the descendants of Noah as well (11:48) and of the Israelites repeatedly. The commentators noted the purpose of the observation: the merit of a prophet does not flow to his family as an inheritance, and the sūrah's account of Abraham's father and his people has already made the point. The promise is so a promise about the prophets' line rather than about their households' conduct, and the Book states the same rule for the family of Abraham in the sūrah of the Heifer: the covenant does not reach the wrongdoers (2:124).

## Verse 37:114

> And We certainly showed favour to Moses and Aaron

The sūrah now leaps from Abraham to his great descendants in prophethood: "And We certainly showed favour to Moses and Aaron." Maududi notes the honour of the pairing — two brothers made prophets together, the only such pairing in the Qur'an's history of messengers. That the hardest mission of the Torah era was carried by mutual support. The "favour" (man) here names the whole sequence that follows in three compressed verses: deliverance, scripture, lasting mention. For the reader who knows the long Exodus narratives of Sūrahs 20, 26, and 28, the compression is the point: al-Ṣāffāt is a roll of honour, not a chronicle, and each name receives its medal in a line. The placement is also deliberate — the family of Abraham continues in Israel's prophets, so the chain of "those who followed his way" runs unbroken from Noah to Moses to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ.

---

The mention of Moses and Aaron follows Abraham and Isaac in the sūrah's roll of the honoured, and the pairing of the two brothers in prophethood is given as the reason for the favour: the Book names them together in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā, where Moses asks for his brother's support and is answered (20:29–36), and in the sūrah of the Stories, where Aaron is given as a helper (28:34–35). The sūrah's list of the messengers continues with Elijah and Lot, so the passage functions as the Book's roll-call of the figures the idolaters' ancestors had rejected, and the next verses name their reward: the Garden, and among the chosen (37:40–50).

## Verse 37:115

> and delivered them and their people from the great distress

"and delivered them and their people from the great distress." Maududi explains the distress (karb al-ʿaẓīm) as the whole Egyptian captivity: the enslavement of a people, the killing of their sons, the tyranny of Pharaoh who said "I am your lord, most high" (79:24). Deliverance from it is the defining mercy of Moses' mission — the splitting of the sea, the destruction of the chariots, the passage of a broken nation into freedom. The verse keeps the theology exact: the deliverer was not Moses' staff but Allah — "We delivered them." Prophets are agents of rescue, never its author. For every community crushed under an unbearable order, the verse is a charter of hope: the "great distress" of history is real, and Allah has destroyed one before. The next verse adds the seal — "and We helped them, so they were the ones overpowered" — the rescued become the upper hand.

---

## Verse 37:116

> We helped them so it was they who prevailed

*Naṣarnāhum fa-kānū humu al-ghālibūn* — We helped them, and it was they who prevailed. The verse lands after the account of Moses and Aaron and the signs they were given, and the order of the sentence is deliberate: the help is God's, the victory belongs to the men who were sent. The Book uses the same pattern in its accounts of battle, where the believers' task is to stand firm and the outcome is God's (3:160; 9:40), and it states the principle in Sūrah Ghāfir: certainly We will help Our messengers and the believers in this world and on the Day when the witnesses arise (40:51). The verse also turns the reader's attention away from the drowned army and towards the two messengers, because the mission, not the disaster, is what the passage is about, and the next verse adds the guidance that accompanied the help.

---

## Verse 37:117

> We gave them the clear Scripture

"We gave them the clear Scripture" — the Torah, described as kitāb mubīn, a writing that makes things plain. The Qur'an honours the revealed book of Moses even while correcting what hands did to it later, and the description "clear" carries both praise and a standard: revelation's work is to remove ambiguity about the two great matters — who Allah is, and what He requires. Elsewhere the same gift is called "the Criterion" (al-Furqān, 21:48), because a clear scripture judges between truth and its imitations. The verse sits between the deliverance from Egypt and the lasting blessing that follows it, teaching the order of Allah's gifts to a community: first freedom, then guidance — liberation that arrives without revelation collapses into a new slavery, as the episode of the golden calf showed within weeks. A people saved by Allah needs the book of Allah immediately, and Allah did not delay it to them.

---

## Verse 37:118

> and guided them to the Straight Path

*Wa-hadaynāhumā al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* — and We guided them to the straight path. The guidance here follows the help of the previous verse: the same two prophets who were given the signs were also held on the road. *Al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*, the straight path, names both the way and the walking of it, since a messenger can be given the truth and still need to be kept faithful to it. The phrase is one the sūrah has already used for the Fire's path in 37:23, and the contrast is stark: one road leads to the blaze, the other is the road God keeps His messengers on. The Book applies the same guidance to the prophets it approves elsewhere — Abraham and his descendants among them (6:87) — and it is why the account of Moses and Aaron ends on guidance rather than on the signs, which were shown to an enemy who did not believe them.

---

## Verse 37:119

> And We blessed them ˹with honourable mention˺ among later generations

The formula returns: "And We blessed them with honourable mention among later generations." What was said of Abraham is said of Moses and Aaron — the same words, the same currency of reward. This is the third time the sūrah has used the formula, and the reader should now feel its weight: heaven's gift to His faithful servants is a good name that survives them. For Moses, the promise is astonishingly literal — his story fills more of the Qur'an than any other prophet's; his name is spoken daily in the prayer alongside Abraham's; the Torah and the Qur'an together keep his memory alive among nations who never met him. The blessing cost him decades of confrontation with the strongest empire on earth. The verse quietly teaches the economics of the hereafter: what is spent of self for Allah's message is repaid in remembrance that does not decay, while the empire that resisted is remembered only as the background of his story.

---

## Verse 37:120

> “Peace be upon Moses and Aaron.”

"Peace be upon Moses and Aaron" — the same salutation pronounced over Noah, and soon over Elias and Jonah. The roll call of al-Ṣāffāt is punctuated by these greetings because the sūrah's subject is the company of the honoured. In the etiquette of that company, peace is both protection and position: "peace" is the word of the Hereafter's safe-greeting (the believers are greeted with "peace" in paradise, 10:10), and it is spoken here over those who finished their term safely in faith. The pairing of the two brothers in one greeting mirrors their pairing in one mission — neither is greeted without the other, so close were their labours. For the reciter, the verse trains a habit: the men and women who carried Allah's word through history are not neutral figures in a textbook; they are to be greeted, loved, and asked-for in peace, because love of the prophets is part of the covenant of their Lord.

---

## Verse 37:121

> Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers

"Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers" — after the greeting, the law is restated. The sūrah has now applied the identical formula to Noah, to Abraham, and to the two sons of Israel, and the monotony is the message: heaven does not have private arrangements with its favourites, only a public rule. What did Moses and Aaron "do good" with? They confronted the tyrant of their age without an army; they delivered a nation; they carried a scripture; they bore the ingratitude of their own people in the wilderness without seeking revenge. The reward — deliverance in history, salutation in scripture, honour among generations — is the same shape as Abraham's. The verse invites a simple examination: the rule is published; the entry conditions are published; the refrain reminds the reader after every story that the same door stands open. Between the refrains the sūrah keeps inserting the qualification: servant of Allah, faithful, patient, believing.

---

## Verse 37:122

> They were truly ˹two˺ of Our faithful servants

"They were truly of Our faithful servants" — the verdict formula closes the Moses-and-Aaron passage exactly as it closed Noah's and Abraham's. Maududi points to the singular standard behind the plural honour: different missions, different eras, one evaluation — faithfulness. Moses, who spoke with Allah, and Aaron, who bore the people's mockery beside him, are both filed in the register under the same two words used for the man who argued with idols in Ur. The Qur'an's system of honour is so completely unlike the world's: the world files men by victory, wealth, and fame; the Qur'an files them by ʿubūdiyyah and īmān — servanthood and faith. The reader who has followed the roll this far — Noah, Abraham, Moses, Aaron — has seen kings of nations passed over in silence while servants are crowned. The next verse adds a new name to the roll, Elias, and the verdict lines continue unchanged.

---

## Verse 37:123

> And Elias was indeed one of the messengers

"And Elias was indeed one of the messengers" — Ilyās, the prophet of the northern kingdom of Israel, sent to a people who had traded the worship of Allah for Baal. Qatādah is among those who identified Elias with Idrīs, a view some commentators transmit, though the majority keep them distinct; what the sūrah itself asserts is enough — he was truly a messenger, restored here to the honour his own generation denied him. The Bible remembers him confronting King Ahab and winning the great contest on Mount Carmel; the Qur'an compresses that ministry into the argument of the next verse. The naming is itself the vindication: a prophet hunted by his own king, "wearied" (as the Bible says) into the wilderness, is entered by Allah into the permanent roll of al-Ṣāffāt between Moses and the successors of Abraham. The world's verdict on a prophet and Heaven's verdict are two different documents.

---

## Verse 37:124

> ˹Remember˺ when he said to his people, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺

"When he said to his people: Will you not fear Allah?" — the prophet's opening is a question of the heart, not a lecture of theology. Taqwā — fear that becomes avoidance of wrong — is the root Elijah attacks: a people who had stopped taking Allah seriously enough to fear Him could be sold any idol. The Baal cult he confronted offered rain, fertility, and empire — religion dressed as prosperity — and the prophets of Baal outnumbered him by hundreds (the Bible counts four hundred and fifty). The verse preserves the beginning of every reform: one man, one question, addressed to a whole society — will you not fear Allah? Not "will you not fear the consequences," nor "do you not see the ruins" — fear of Allah is the first and organizing repentance. The sūrah places the question here so that the reader hears it as still open: every generation is Elijah's audience now.

---

The name used here is Ilyās, and the sūrah gives him the same call as the other messengers in the Book: fear God, and leave the idol you serve (37:125). The story of Elijah (Ilyās in the Arabic) is the confrontation with Baal worship, and the verse that follows names the people's abandonment of the best of creators and their worship of Baal, while the false report about God's having a son is answered in the same passage (37:125–128). The sūrah's pattern is consistent: each messenger is shown bringing the same single command, so the call of Ilyās is set beside Abraham's, Moses's and Aaron's as the same message to different generations.

## Verse 37:125

> Do you call upon ˹the idol of˺ Ba’l and abandon the Best of Creators—

Elijah's reproach names the actual object of his people's worship: *Baʿl*, "the lord", the title of the storm deity whose cult dominated the region where he was sent. The verse sets that idol against *aḥsan al-khāliqīn* — the Best of Creators — which is the argument of the whole sūrah in a single line: the worshipper is choosing the work over the Maker. The question form is the Book's way of pressing denial. The next verse completes it by naming the God they had abandoned: "Allah, your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers." The sūrah's refrain of the chosen servants frames the scene on both sides (37:128, 37:132), which means the story of Baʿl is told for the same reason as the rest of them: to show what happens to a community that trades its Maker for something it can see.

---

## Verse 37:126

> Allah, your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers?”

*Rabbukum wa rabbu ābāʾikumu al-awwalīn* — your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers. Elijah's argument closes by taking the ground the idolaters claimed for themselves. They honoured their ancestors, and the God of those ancestors is exactly the One they were refusing. The sūrah has already named the fault of people who follow their fathers' footsteps (37:69–70), and here the same idea is turned round as an argument for the truth: the Book does not reject lineage, it names the right ancestor to follow. The phrase also does for this story what 37:126 does for the sūrah as a whole — it refuses to let the debate be about a new message, since the God Elijah preached was the God Abraham preached before him. The next verse states the verdict.

---

## Verse 37:127

> But they rejected him, so they will certainly be brought ˹for punishment˺

"But they rejected him, so they will certainly be brought." The word "brought" (l-muḥḍarūn) means escorted, driven into presence — the refused prophet's people will be marched to the reckoning whether they believe in it now or not. Maududi reads the verse as the standing consequence of national refusal: the prophets bring the only message by which a people can answer for themselves, and rejection postpones the accounting but never cancels it. Elijah's own history underlined the warning — the kingdom that mocked him sank into the collapse the prophets foretold, and the Assyrian conquest swept Baal's altars away with the dynasty that housed them. For the reader the verse fixes a fact about the future: no majority, no empire, no indifference is large enough to vote away the Day. The rejected messenger loses only the pleasure of their company; the rejecting people lose their final argument. The appointment stands — "they will certainly be brought.

---

## Verse 37:128

> But not the chosen servants of Allah

Once more the exception is stated, and once more it comes after the punishment of a community. The phrase is identical at 37:40, 37:74 and 37:160, which is the sūrah's way of saying that the same line runs through every story it tells: the many perish with their error, and the servants whom God chose are kept. In Elijah's case the sentence also tells the reader where to look after the destruction — not at the ruins of the idolaters, but at the believers who survived them. The Book's report of Elijah elsewhere makes the same contrast: he called a people to the living God while they clung to a name that could not answer (37:125–126), and the sūrah gives him the honours of a prophet in the verses that follow. The reader is meant to notice which group he would have belonged to.

---

## Verse 37:129

> We blessed him ˹with honourable mention˺ among later generations

*Wa-taraknā ʿalayhi fī al-ākhirīn* — We left for him honourable mention among later generations. It is the same blessing granted to Abraham (37:108) and to Moses and Aaron, and it means the praise of him that continues in every community that reads the Book. The Book states the gift in its own words elsewhere: We gave them a true tongue of honour (19:50), which is the same idea — a name that keeps being spoken well. In the Qur'an, honour after death of this kind is the reward of the righteous, and it answers the sūrah's interest in legacy: the unjust leave ruins behind them, while the truthful leave a name that is greeted with peace in every age. The next verse gives that greeting in words.

---

## Verse 37:130

> “Peace be upon Elias.”

*Salām ʿalā Īlyāsīn* — the greeting stands in the sūrah as a liturgical line, the same formula used for Noah (37:79), Abraham (37:109) and Moses and Aaron (37:120), closing each story with the community's blessing on the prophet. The name *Īlyāsīn* is read by the commentators as either a variant of Elijah's name or a reference to his family and followers, and either reading keeps the verse in the same place: a prophet and those with him are greeted by God. The form places Elijah in the company of the messengers the Book approves, and it leaves the reader with a model rather than a tale. The greeting is also the Book's answer to what his people did to him: they refused his call, and the last word over his story is peace.

---

## Verse 37:131

> Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers

The line closes Elijah's story in the same way the sūrah closes those of Noah (37:80–81), Abraham (37:110–111) and Moses and Aaron (37:121–122): honourable mention, peace, and then the statement that this is how the good are rewarded. The repetition is structural — the sūrah uses it as a refrain — and each time the qualifying word is *al-muḥsinīn*, those who do good, not the learned or the powerful or the well-connected. The Book states the same rule in its own summary of the reward of the doers of good (9:120; 29:69), which is why the sūrah repeats it after each prophet instead of once. A reader who follows Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt to its end learns the pattern by heart: a certain kind of life, and the same verdict after every story.

---

## Verse 37:132

> He was truly one of Our faithful servants

"He was truly one of Our faithful servants" — the verdict falls on Lot, closing his passage as it closed the passages before him. The honour is startling on first reading: Lot, whose mission ended with his wife looking back, his city in ruins, and his guests fleeing before dawn — yet Heaven's register enters him as a faithful servant. The Qur'an so separates the outcomes of prophethood from its faithfulness: Lot saved the believers with him (27:58), lost his society, and is not blamed for the loss. The commentators note that the phrase mirrors what was said of Noah — both prophets saw their peoples destroyed, and both are crowned with the same words. The lesson for every servant whose sincere work meets stubborn refusal is in the verdict: success in the Qur'an is faithfulness executed, not crowds converted. Heaven's audit asks what the servant was given and what he did with it — Lot did all his Lord commanded.

---

## Verse 37:133

> And Lot was indeed one of the messengers

*Wa-inna Lūṭan la-min al-mursalīn* — the story opens without genealogy or introduction, because the audience already knew who Lot was: Abraham's nephew, sent to the cities of the plain. The verse confirms his standing among the messengers, as the Book does in its other lists of prophets (6:86; 21:71), and it prepares the reader for an account whose outcome is the destruction of a whole community with only his household delivered (37:134). The pairing with Abraham is deliberate, and the two accounts sit next to each other in the sūrah because the Book usually places Lot within Abraham's story (11:69–83; 29:26). Here he is given his own entry, which means the reader who reaches this verse is expected to hold in mind both the uncle who argued with his people and the nephew who warned a city.

---

## Verse 37:134

> ˹Remember˺ when We delivered him and all of his family

The rescue is stated before the crime is described, which is how this sūrah handles all of these accounts: the reader sees the deliverance first, so the judgement that follows reads as the protection of the innocent rather than the punishment of the many. *Idh najjaynāhu wa-ahlahu ajmaʿīn* — when We delivered him and all his family — uses the word for rescue (*tanjī*) that the sūrah has already used for Noah (37:76) and will use for Jonah (37:142). Lot's family appears in the same role at 26:170 and 27:57, and in each case the Qur'an notes the one member who did not believe and was not saved (11:81; 26:171). The pattern of a household being spared for the sake of one faithful man runs through the sūrah — Noah, Abraham, Moses and Aaron, Lot, and Jonah — and it is the pattern the exception of 37:74 promised: the chosen are kept, and their rescue is the sūrah's proof.

---

## Verse 37:135

> except an old woman, who was one of the doomed

"except an old woman, who was one of the doomed." The angels' rescue of Lot's household had one exception, and the sūrah names it with the mercy of precision: the old woman — identified by the commentators as Lot's wife — "was of those who stayed behind" (the sense of 27:57). Her doom was not ignorance; she had lived under a prophet's teaching and shared his table, yet her heart had stayed in the city she was leaving. The Glorious Qur'an makes her the standing example of the believer's own household who fall away — the Prophet's ﷺ own words about a man whose deeds are lightened by family ties apply to her case ("she will be of the doomed" was said of a wife who betrayed her husband's trust). The verse teaches the painful boundary of faith: salvation is never by proximity to the righteous. A wife of a prophet, a daughter of Noah (11:43) — both are named in the Book as lost. Each soul reaches its own belief.

---

## Verse 37:136

> Then We ˹utterly˺ destroyed the rest

"Then We destroyed the rest" — the final word on the Cities of the Plain. Maududi stresses the completeness: the corruption of Sodom was total — highway robbery, shaming of guests, public lust legislated as habit — and the punishment matched it, the cities overturned and rained with stones "layer upon layer" (11:82). The plain around the Dead Sea still offers the traveller ancient sites along the shore, several bearing clear traces of destruction by fire, and the Qur'an will point to them in the next verses as a sign passed on the road. Theologically the verse answers the question of collective doom: "the rest" — those who were not of the believing household, who did not leave with Lot, who laughed at his restraint — shared the city's guilt by choice and its ruin by justice. No age is entitled to think total corruption a stable condition; the Qur'an records its end once, in fire from the sky, as a lesson signed into geography.

---

## Verse 37:137

> You ˹Meccans˺ certainly pass by their ruins day

The Meccan traders passed the ruined cities on their caravan route to Syria, which is why the verse can address them directly: *wa-innakum latamurrūna ʿalayhim muṣbiḥīn* — you pass by them in the morning. The Book uses this argument more than once — the settlements of the punished are visible on the very roads they travelled (15:76–77; 25:40) — and the purpose is to move the account out of the category of old stories and into the landscape of the hearer's own business. The next verse continues the same address, adding that they pass by night as well, before the passage turns to the resurrection and the rebuke. The verse is a reminder that the evidence for the Book's warnings is not only in the past: for this audience, it was geography, and the ruins were a landmark on a route they used every season.

---

## Verse 37:138

> and night. Will you not then understand

"and night. Will you not then understand?" — the verse completes 37:137's image: "you pass by them in the morning, and by night." The ruins of the Dead Sea cities stood directly beside the caravan route between Madinah and Syria, and the Qur'an's audience passed them literally on business journeys. Allah turns the roadside into a classroom: the traveller who saw the overturned land, dead of vegetation, sunk below the surrounding plain, was looking at the consequence of a moral choice made by people who spoke, traded, and married like himself. "Will you not understand?" — the question is not rhetorical mockery but an invitation: the same eyes that take in the ruins are able to take in the lesson. The Qur'an makes this use of ruins its habit — "travel through the earth and see how was the end of the deniers" — and this particular ruin is the most visited of all, passed morning and night for three thousand years, still asking its question.

---

## Verse 37:139

> And Jonah was indeed one of the messengers

"And Jonah was indeed one of the messengers" — Yūnus, Dhū al-Nūn, "the man of the fish." The declaration matters because of the story that follows: Jonah fled his mission, was swallowed, and despaired of his people — conduct that might seem to disqualify a prophet. The sūrah asserts his rank first, then tells the failure, because the two together are the lesson: a true prophet can stumble, and his stumble does not unmake his prophethood; and a true prophet's stumble is itself repented in a way that becomes guidance. Jonah is also unique in another honour the Qur'an states elsewhere: the Prophet ﷺ said no one should say he is better than Jonah (a report in Bukhārī and Muslim), precisely because of this story's end. The roll of al-Ṣāffāt does not hide the weakness of its heroes — that is what makes the roll believable, and its verdicts ("faithful servant") trustworthy.

---

## Verse 37:140

> ˹Remember˺ when he fled to the overloaded ship

*Idh abaqa ilā al-fulki al-mashḥūn* — when he fled to the laden ship. The verb *abaqa* is used of a slave running from his master, and the Book chooses it deliberately: Jonah left his people before his mission was finished, and the ship he boarded was already full. The story that follows is the Book's most vivid account of a prophet in trouble — the lots, the fish, the darkness, the prayer (37:141–144) — and it is the one account in this sūrah that begins with the prophet's own mistake. The Qur'an fills in the background in Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ, where he departs in anger and the fish swallows him, and notes that he called out in the darkness (21:87–88). That is why the summary of his rescue turns entirely on the mercy that followed, and why he is called the companion of the whale in Sūrah al-Qalam, where the Book says his Lord had favoured him and he was saved from grief (68:48–50).

---

## Verse 37:141

> Then ˹to save it from sinking,˺ he drew straws ˹with other passengers˺. He lost ˹and was thrown overboard˺

*Fa-sāhama fa-kāna mina al-mudḥaḍīn* — he cast lots and was among those thrown out. The word for casting lots (*sāhama*) appears once in the Book, and the word for being thrown out (*mudḥaḍ*) is the same one used when the sea's waters threaten to overwhelm. The sailors' custom of drawing lots to find the man who had brought misfortune appears here in a single line, and the Book treats the result as God's decree rather than as chance: the man who fled is thrown into the sea, swallowed, and answered from the belly. The scene also sets up the exception of the next verses, because Jonah's rescue is not granted for his own sake in the way the other prophets' rescues are. It comes after he calls out in the darkness, and the Book states the condition openly, so that the reader sees the difference between a servant who was kept and a servant who was retrieved (21:87–88; 68:49–50).

---

## Verse 37:142

> Then the whale engulfed him while he was blameworthy

"Then the fish engulfed him while he was blameworthy." The word mulīm — blameworthy — is the Qur'an's own verdict on Jonah's flight: he boarded the ship, drew the lot. Was thrown to the sea as the storm demanded, and the fish received him as a man under blame, not as a martyr of circumstance. The three layers of the darkness he named in his prayer inside the fish — "darknesses" of the deep, of the night, of the belly (21:87) — begin with this verse's fact: the engulfing. Yet even the blame is framed with mercy: the fish was "commissioned" (the Qur'an says of it nūn, a fish that "swallowed him while he was blameworthy" — appointed, not accidental; the creature of Allah does the guarding while it does the punishing). For every servant who has run from a duty of religion, the verse states the anatomy of the consequence: the flight itself becomes the fish. And the next verse will show the single act that opens its mouth.

---

## Verse 37:143

> Had he not ˹constantly˺ glorified ˹Allah˺

"Had he not been of those who glorify Allah" — the conditional that explains the rescue. Inside three darknesses, Jonah "called out in the darknesses: there is no god but You, glory be to You; I was of the wrongdoers" (21:87) — a confession containing tawḥīd, God's transcendence, and his own guilt, with no excuse offered. The Prophet ﷺ taught this verse as the prayer of the distressed: "The prayer of Dhū al-Nūn which he prayed inside the fish — no Muslim prays with it for anything but Allah answers it" (Tirmidhī). The sūrah's conditional teaches the mechanics: the glorification did not earn rescue as wages; it *was* the rescue, because "had he not been of the glorifiers, he would have remained in its belly until the Day they are resurrected" (37:144). A drowning man's tongue, remembering his Lord, weighs more than a life's course in the balance — such is the power of tasbīḥ issued from the bottom of need.

---

## Verse 37:144

> he would have certainly remained in its belly until the Day of Resurrection

The conditional carries the whole weight: *la-labitha fī baṭnihi ilā yawmi yubʿathūn* — he would have remained in its belly until the Day they are raised. The fish was not a grave because its prisoner prayed. The Book records the prayer in Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ — "There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except You. Glory be to You! I have certainly done wrong" — and adds that God answered him and saved him from the distress, then states the general promise: this is how We save the believers (21:87–88). The verse makes the rescue depend on that turning, which is the same condition the sūrah states after every other prophet's story: obedience, and then deliverance. The Book also notes what the prayer confessed — *innī kuntu mina al-ẓālimīn*, I was among the wrongdoers — and the commentators observe that this admission of fault from a prophet is what the mercy answered. The belly of the fish becomes the Book's most literal image of a place from which only God can bring a person out.

**The Prophet in the Fish, and the Report the Prophet Gave**
The Book tells the story in three places. In the sūrah of the Prophets, the man is named and the prayer is given word for word, with the promise that God answers the believers in the same way (21:87-88); in the sūrah of the Pen, the Book says that if the favour of his Lord had not reached him he would have been thrown out on the bare shore, blamed, and then says that his Lord chose him and made him one of the righteous (68:49-50); and Jonah is counted among the prophets whom God favoured over the worlds (6:86). The Prophet related the story to the community in his own words. A report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Muslim records his saying: none of you should say that I am better than Jonah, son of Amittai (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3413), and another report says that Jonah called out in the darkness of the whale with the words the sūrah of the Prophets records, that there is no god but You, glory be to You, I was among the wrongdoers (21:87). The reports make the point the verse is making. The deliverance did not come because the man was a prophet and the whale was obliged to release him. It came when the prisoner in the dark said the words the Book prints for its readers.

**The Belly as the Book's Picture of Repentance**
The verse says the man would have stayed there until the Day of Resurrection, which makes the fish's belly a picture of the grave and of the mercy that interrupts it. The Book puts the same rescue in the mouth of Jonah in the Sūrah of the Pen, where God says that if the favour had not reached him, he would have been thrown out on the bare shore, blamed — and then says his Lord chose him and made him one of the righteous (68:49-50), and the same sūrah of the Prophets ends the account of the prayer with the promise that God saves the believers in this way (21:88). The community has read the verse from the beginning as a comfort for the man who has failed and turned back: the door out of the belly is not closed, and the thing that opens it is the confession the messenger made in the dark.

---

## Verse 37:145

> But We cast him onto the open ˹shore˺, ˹totally˺ worn out

*Fa-nabadhnāhu bi-al-ʿarāʾ wa-huwa saqīm* — We cast him onto the open shore, and he was sick. The Book uses the same verb when the infant Moses is placed in the river (20:39, *fa-alqīhi fī al-yamm*), and trust is the theme there too. *Al-ʿarāʾ* describes bare, unsheltered ground: the man is thrown where there is no cover, no food and no shade, and the word *saqīm* adds that he is worn out after his days in the dark. There is no complaint in the verse, only the picture of mercy arriving to someone who has nothing left, and the Book notes elsewhere that God cast him out while he was blameworthy, then chose him and made him one of the righteous (68:49–50). That is why the plant that grows over him in the next verse is so often remembered as the gentlest detail in the story.

---

## Verse 37:146

> and caused a squash plant to grow over him

*Wa-anbatnā ʿalayhi shajaratan min yaqṭīn* — a plant of the gourd family, growing over him. The Book mentions it because it does nothing else: it shades a sick man in the open, and the next stage of the story, his return to a people who believed (37:147–148), begins from that shade. Ibn Kathīr notes the classical description of the plant as providing both shade and nourishment, and the commentators remark on the choice of a broad-leaved vine rather than a tree: it grows fast, covers quickly, and its fruit is food. The detail is characteristic of the Qur'an's way of telling such accounts — the deliverance is shown to be complete, down to the sort of leaf that covered him. The plant also stands in pointed contrast to the tree earlier in the sūrah: one tree grows in the Fire (37:64), this one grows over a prophet just brought out of the sea, and the difference between them is the difference between the two destinations the sūrah has been comparing.

---

## Verse 37:147

> We ˹later˺ sent him ˹back˺ to ˹his city of˺ at least one hundred thousand people

"We sent him to a hundred thousand or more." Ibn ʿAbbās explained the figure as the population of Nineveh, Jonah's city — "a hundred thousand or more," the Qur'an's wording allowing for the indefinite excess (the same phrase appears in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī as a gloss on this verse, where the Prophet ﷺ was asked about "the people of the whale" and gave this number). The number matters theologically: Sūrah Yūnus laments that "no town has believed and its belief profited it — except the people of Jonah" (10:98). One man, freshly delivered from a fish, walks into an Assyrian capital of over a hundred thousand — the empire that would one day crush Israel — and the whole city believes. The verse stands in the roll of al-Ṣāffāt as the measure of Allah's power to turn hearts: the prophet who could not stand his own people at first became, by Allah's command, the single most successful preacher of warning in the recorded history of the prophets.

---

## Verse 37:148

> who then believed ˹in him˺, so We allowed them enjoyment for a while

"So they believed, then We gave them enjoyment for a while." The belief of Nineveh is the exception the Qur'an underlines: an entire city, warned, believed at once — "and We removed from them the punishment of disgrace in the life of this world" (10:98). Maududi notes the order of events: belief first, reprieve after — the "enjoyment" (mutamattiʿīn) was not the reward of paradise but a postponement of worldly doom, life lived out to its natural term under Allah's lifted sentence. The verse teaches both the speed of mercy and its conditional nature: the reprieve of a warned people lasts while the belief lasts, and generations that forget return to the ordinary law of history. For the reader the verse completes Jonah's arc with its meaning: the prophet who despaired of his people was made the instrument of the largest collective repentance on record — Allah's answer to his premature verdict on hearts he did not own.

---

## Verse 37:149

> Ask them ˹O Prophet˺ if your Lord has daughters, while the pagans ˹prefer to˺ have sons

The sūrah turns to the religious scandal of the Makkans: "Ask them — does your Lord have daughters, while they prefer sons?" The pagan Arabs traced angels as Allah's daughters — "the daughters of Allah" — while they themselves, in their shame culture, resented the birth of daughters. The Qur'an exposes the double standard across half a dozen sūrahs: "When one of them is given news of a daughter, his face darkens and he chokes with grief" (16:58) — yet he assigns to Heaven the very sex he despises. The verse's method is the prophet's method: put the question to them directly and let their own convictions answer it. A man who believes his god has daughters must explain why daughters are dishonour among men but ornaments among gods. The question remains a standing test of every invented theology: does it honour Heaven — or merely relocate the prejudices of earth?

---

## Verse 37:150

> Or ˹ask them˺ if We created the angels as females right before their eyes

"Or were the angels created as females before their eyes?" — the second question strikes at the supposed evidence. No one ever saw an angel at all, let alone a female angel; the "witnesses" of the pagan picture of the heavens are pure invention, and the verse says so: "before their eyes" — they were present at their own fiction's birth. The Qur'an elsewhere states the truth of the angels' nature: "they say, the Most Compassionate has taken a son... They are but honoured servants who do not precede Him in speech and act at His command" (21:26–27) — beings of light, neither male nor female, neither eating nor sleeping. The Arabs made the unseen world a mirror of their own society, complete with marriages of the gods. The verse's lesson reaches every age: religions built on imagination rather than revelation end up describing heaven with the fashions of the street. What is unseen must be learned from the One who sees it, or not claimed at all.

---

## Verse 37:151

> Indeed, it is one of their ˹outrageous˺ fabrications to say

The sūrah turns from the stories of the truthful to the lies of the idolaters, and the word it uses is *ifk* — a slander, a fabrication passed around as though it were true. What they invented comes in the next verse: that God has children while they kept the sons for themselves (37:149–153), a division they would never have accepted in their own households. Against the standard of God's messengers and their reward, which the sūrah has just finished establishing, a claim about God's offspring is not a difference of opinion but an invention. The verses that follow press it with three questions — about their reasoning, their memory, and their evidence — and a demand for a scripture (37:154–157), which they cannot produce.

---

## Verse 37:152

> “Allah has children.” They are simply liars

The verse quotes the claim and answers it in the same breath: Allah has children, and they are liars. The charge is not merely error but falsehood, and the sūrah has already given the motive — the idolaters assigned daughters to God while reserving sons for themselves (37:149–150). The Book objects on two grounds: the claim has no evidence, and it is degrading, since it turns the Creator into a being with a family like a creature's. Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ answers the same claim in four lines, ending with the word that settles it — He has no equal (112:4) — and Sūrah Maryam describes the claim as something the heavens almost split apart over (19:88–92). The next verses demand the proof they do not have.

---

## Verse 37:153

> Has He chosen daughters over sons

"Has He chosen daughters over sons?" — the syllogism stated in its final absurdity. The pagan position, laid out by their own lips, amounted to this: Allah, the Greatest, has chosen for Himself exactly what the Arabs chose to despise — daughters. The verse asks it as a question of "choice" (iṣṭafā), the word the Qur'an uses for Allah's choosing of prophets and pure servants: does the Lord of the worlds "choose" for His own household the very thing His creatures would refuse? The question needs no formal refutation; its absurdity refutes it. The Qur'an comments on this species of blasphemy directly: "When the girl buried alive is asked, for what sin she was killed" (81:8–9) — the same society that invented divine daughters buried its own. The verse holds up the logic of invented religion until it collapses of its own weight: men fashion gods in the shape of their own double standards.

---

## Verse 37:154

> What is the matter with you? How do you judge

"What is the matter with you? How do you judge?" — the divine cross-examination. Jalālayn reads it as: what has corrupted your judgment, that you pass such verdicts? The question is addressed to the intellect before the conscience: the pagan claim about the angels is not only impious, it is irrational — it has no evidence, contradicts the majesty it pretends to honour, and offends the very values its makers held. The Qur'an habitually appeals to precisely this faculty — "affix your intellect; I am not, in the manner of your slanders, an insane poet," the sense of 68:36 — "What is the matter with you? How do you judge?" The verse teaches that faith in the Qur'an is never a surrender of judgment but its highest exercise; blasphemies survive only where judgment is asleep. And the question lingers past its first audience for every reader who has ever inherited a belief he has never once examined.

---

## Verse 37:155

> Will you not then be mindful

"Will you not then be mindful?" — tadhakkarūn, will you not receive reminder? The previous verse questioned their judgment; this one questions their receptivity. Between the two stands the whole purpose of warning: judgment can analyse, but only mindfulness turns analysis into change. The verse closes the daughters-of-Allah passage not with a proof but with an appeal, because the pagans' failure was finally not intellectual but moral — they had the questions answered in their hearing and returned to their idols. The Qur'an names this state in the verse after: "Has the Reminder been made to fall upon them?" (38:8, the sense) — the strange resentment of those who resent being reminded. For the reciter, the sequence is a mirror-check: proof heard, conscience addressed — will you not be mindful? The word is plural, and daily prayer repeats the sūrah; the reminder keeps arriving until the heart answers or refuses outright.

---

## Verse 37:156

> Or do you have ˹any˺ compelling proof

*Am lakum sulṭānun mubīn* — or do you have a clear warrant? The word is the same one the chiefs of the Fire used when they denied having authority over their followers (37:30): proof that can be produced and examined. It is the third question in the series — 37:154 asks about their reasoning and 37:155 about their memory — and it leaves the idolaters with nothing to bring. The Book's method is worth noting: it does not simply overrule the claim about God's children, it asks for the evidence, and it does so in the same terms it uses for its own assertions. The next verse points them to their own scripture, and they have none to point to.

---

## Verse 37:157

> Then bring ˹us˺ your scripture, if what you say is true

The demand is for documentation: *fa-ʾtū bi-kitābikum*, bring your book, if you are truthful. The idolaters had no revelation of their own to cite. The Book they were dismissing was the only scripture in the argument, and it said the opposite of what they claimed (37:158). The verse also states the standard the Qur'an applies to its own claims: a statement about God must rest on something sent down, not on inherited opinion. That is why the sūrah ends the section with the demand rather than with a refutation — the burden is placed where it belongs, on the ones making the claim. By this point the reader has been shown what a supported claim looks like in Noah, Abraham, Moses, Elijah, Lot and Jonah, and the contrast does the arguing.

---

## Verse 37:158

> They have also established a ˹marital˺ relationship between Him and the jinn. Yet the jinn ˹themselves˺ know well that such people will certainly be brought ˹for punishment˺

*Jaʿalū baynahu wa bayna al-jinnati nasaban* — they invented a kinship between God and the jinn. The sūrah reports it as one more version of the same error: turning the Creator into a member of a family, with relations and offspring like a creature. The verse then adds the twist that the jinn themselves, the beings the idolaters were trying to involve, know that those who make this claim are destined for punishment — the lie is denied by its own witnesses. The passage belongs to the sūrah's closing corrections of what was said about God, the angels and the jinn (37:150, 37:158, 37:180), and the Book notes elsewhere that the jinn who heard the Qur'an believed in it and rejected exactly this kind of claim about their Lord (72:2–3).

**The Kinship the Arabs Claimed**
The claim the verse reports comes out of the old Arab belief about the jinn: they were held to be unseen relations of God, and the Makkans built on that the argument that the angels, whom they called God's daughters, were close enough to Him to intercede (37:149-153). The Book corrects the idea in two movements. It first names the claim for what it is: they made the jinn partners with God, though He created them, and God is far above what they describe (6:100; 37:159-160). Then it gives the sentence that shuts the door: the jinn themselves know that they will be brought before Him, so how can any of them be a relative or a partner of the Judge? The Sūrah of Sabaʾ records the same denial from the angels' own mouths on the Day: glory be to You, our patron is You, not these (34:41).

**The Witnesses Who Refuse the Claim**
The sharpest part of the correction is the testimony the Book puts in the mouths of the jinn themselves. When a company of them heard the Qur'an and went back to their people, their report began with the words high is the majesty of our Lord; He has taken neither a companion nor a child (72:3). So the beings the idolaters attached to God reject the relation assigned to them, and the sūrah of their own account closes with the statement that God has counted everything and numbered it exactly (72:28). The verse in front of us does not argue at length; it lets the claim fall under the weight of its own witnesses, and leaves the reader with the Book's standing rule: the Creator has no kin, no household and no partner in His rule.

---

## Verse 37:159

> Glorified is Allah far above what they claim

"Glory be to Allah, high above what they describe" — the verse of transcendence. The pagan claims about Allah — daughters, kinship with jinn, partners — are answered not with a detailed rebuttal but with one word: subḥān, the declaration that Allah is utterly beyond and free of every defect said of Him. The formula is the Qur'an's great guardrail of theology: whenever description risks dragging the Creator down to creaturely measure, "Glory be to Allah" lifts the line again. The Prophet ﷺ taught that when a servant says subḥān Allāh, he is answered from beneath the Throne with "you have spoken truly" (a report of Muslim in the ḥadīth of the ascension). The verse also states the principle of all sound speech about God: descriptions of the Unseen are received, not invented; what the tongue cannot prove from revelation should be returned to silence. High above what they describe — and above what any tongue will ever describe.

---

## Verse 37:160

> But not the chosen servants of Allah

"Except the chosen servants of Allah" — the exception that saves the verse from despair. The previous verse declares the glorification of "the seven heavens, the earth, and whoever is in them"; this one removes from the condemnation a named class: ʿibād Allāh al-mukhlaṣūn, Allah's purified, chosen servants. The Qur'an uses the same exception elsewhere: "his chosen servants" are the ones who "do not speak until He has spoken" and "act at His command" (21:27). The commentators identify them as the angels among creatures and the sincere believers among men — all who worship Allah with exclusive devotion and purified intention. The verse carries a quiet consolation: in an age when religion itself was dressed in lies, Allah keeps an exception-list of His own, known fully to Him, who do not share the corruption. It also carries a question: would the exception include the reader? Purity of devotion — ikhlāṣ — is the entry requirement, and it is tested most where lies are fashionable.

---

## Verse 37:161

> Surely you ˹pagans˺ and whatever ˹idols˺ you worship

"So you and what you worship" — the verse begins the sentence that concludes in 37:163: you and your idols together "cannot tempt anyone away" except the one bound for Hell. The structure is staggering in its contempt: the entire apparatus of Makkan idolatry — the 360 idols of the sanctuary, their priesthood, their processions — and it is gathered into one phrase, "you and what you worship," and assigned to the same court file. The Qur'an elsewhere seals the same verdict in the picture of the Hereafter: "Then they will be hurled into it upon their faces — they and the deviators, and the hosts of Iblīs, all of them" (26:94–97), where the idols disown their worshippers. The verse teaches the futility of the whole enterprise: a religion without revelation cannot save even its own gods, much less its followers. What follows in the next verses is the narrow, precise exception — and the fate of those outside it.

---

## Verse 37:162

> can never lure ˹anyone˺ away from Him

The line completes a sentence that began in the previous verse: the idols, and those who worship them, cannot turn anyone away from God. It is the answer to the tempters described earlier in the sūrah (37:29–30) and to everything the idolaters claimed on behalf of their gods. The Book states the limit plainly: misleaders can invite, but they cannot compel, and God's own servants are out of their reach — the Book gives Satan himself the admission that he has no authority over the sincere (15:42; 16:99). The next verse gives the reason the limit holds: the forces of God will prevail, and no alliance against Him has ever stood.

---

## Verse 37:163

> except those ˹destined˺ to burn in Hell

"Except those destined to burn in Hell" — the precision of the exception completes the verdict on the idolaters and their idols: the alliance "cannot lead astray" Allah's servants (the sense of the passage), except those who were already "of the destined" (l-āmin) — the ones for whom hell was written by their own persistent choice. The commentators note the justice in the wording: none is led astray by force; the idols "tempt" only those whose record had already resolved on refusal. Fate in the Qur'an is not a chain laid on the innocent; it is the seal on a history. The verse also removes an excuse the pagans might raise — "our gods overpowered us" — no: the temptation reached only those for whom the Fire was already the fit destination. Free choice, persistent refusal, and finally the sealed outcome: the three verses together state the Qur'an's whole doctrine of destiny, warning and warning again against reading it as compulsion.

---

## Verse 37:164

> ˹The angels respond,˺ “There is not one of us without an assigned station ˹of worship˺

"There is not one of us without an assigned station." The speaker changes: the angels answer the pagan slander of their nature. The word maqām maʿlūm — a known station — carries two truths at once. Order: heaven is not a chaos of spirits but a ranked service, every angel fixed at his post — the bearers of the Throne, the keepers of Paradise and Hell, the recorders, the rain-bearers — and none steps outside his assignment. Devotion: the "station" is a station of worship, as the bracket renders, for the angels' posts are prayers that never lapse. The pagan picture of angels as Allah's daughters made heaven a harem of favourites; the angels' own testimony corrects it: we are functionaries, bonded to our tasks. The verse dignifies the ordered life of religion: to have a "known station" — a fixed prayer, a fixed duty, a fixed place before Allah — is not limitation but angelic nobility, and the reversal of the pagan fantasy could not be more complete.

---

## Verse 37:165

> We are indeed the ones lined up in ranks ˹for Allah˺

"And we are indeed the ones ranged in ranks" — the angels continue their testimony. Muslim records from Ḥudhayfah ibn al-Yamān that the Prophet ﷺ said, "You see what your Lord has sent down upon you — the angels do not cease, in a night and a day, from ranks" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). A report in Bukhārī describes the angels of the night and the day meeting at the prayers of dawn. The image is of disciplined hosts — ṣaff, the word used for a battle line and for a prayer row — standing shoulder to shoulder in the service of their Lord. The pagan Arabs made heaven a court of rival personalities; revelation shows a perfectly drilled order. The verse also sets the etiquette of congregational prayer on earth: the Prophet ﷺ commanded his followers to "straighten your rows, for the straightening of rows is from the completion of prayer" (the sense of Bukhārī and Muslim) — earth's worship taking its posture from heaven's hosts.

---

## Verse 37:166

> And we are indeed the ones ˹constantly˺ glorifying ˹His praise˺.”

"And we are indeed the ones glorifying Him" — the third article of the angels' confession: fixed station, ranged order, unceasing praise. The Qur'an insists that glorification is not silence but speech: "the seven heavens and the earth and whatever is in them glorify Him, and there is nothing that does not glorify Him with praise, but you do not understand their glorification" (17:44). The angels' tasbīḥ is the perfect form of what human remembrance stammers at — praise that never tires, never wanders, never seeks anything back. And the pagans had slandered these very beings with kinship claims; the angels answer the slander with their office: the beings you call Allah's daughters are consumed, morning and night, in declaring His freedom from everything you say. The verse quietly sets the standard for the human worshipper too: the prayer of the sūrah's title row — al-Ṣāffāt, those ranged in ranks — is perfected when it becomes praise without interruption.

---

## Verse 37:167

> They certainly used to say

"They certainly used to say" — the speaker changes again. The commentators read the verse as the confession of the pagan Arabs, quoted before their conversion, or as the admitted claim of the earlier idolaters among the "peoples of the Reminder." The next verse states what they said: "If only we had a Reminder like the peoples before, we would have been Allah's devoted servants." The verse that introduces this confession is short and deliberate: heaven records what the unbelievers used to say, word for word. Nothing a generation says against its own accountability is forgotten; the excuse they constructed for themselves is preserved and will be read back to them. The Qur'an does this repeatedly — quoting the disbelievers' own words as the evidence of their knowledge: they knew revelation came to earlier peoples, they knew what devotion meant, and they knew what they would have done. The verse teaches that language is recorded, and every "if only" is archived.

---

## Verse 37:168

> “If only we had a Reminder like ˹those of˺ earlier peoples

"If only we had a Reminder like the peoples before" — the hypothetical that damns its speakers. The "peoples of the Reminder" are the Jews and Christians, holders of earlier scriptures; the pagan Arabs knew it, and admitted in their councils that guidance required a book and that a book would be obeyed — by them, they claimed. The Qur'an freezes the wish here so that the next verse can confront it with the fact: the Reminder came, "and now they disbelieve in it." The passage is the Qur'an's standing answer to every generation that blames its circumstance for its unbelief — wrong prophet, wrong language, wrong century, insufficient evidence. The Arabs had the highest evidence a people ever received: an unlettered man among them, reciting a book none could imitate, with their own names and history in it. The verse teaches the worthlessness of unreal piety — devotion promised under conditions never met is not devotion but self-flattery awaiting its excuse.

---

## Verse 37:169

> we would have truly been Allah’s devoted servants.”

"We would surely have been Allah's devoted servants" — the conclusion of the pagans' hypothetical confession. Its verdict is supplied by the next verse: "But they disbelieved in it, so they will come to know." The phrase "devoted servants" (ʿibād mukhliṣīn) is exactly the rank the angels claimed for themselves in 37:165–166 — ranged, praising, singly devoted. The pagans knew the rank and named it; the knowledge makes the refusal inexcusable. The Qur'an exposes here the anatomy of insincere religion: love of a virtue one declines to practise. It is easy to promise obedience to a revelation that never came; the test arrives with the revelation itself, and the promise dissolves. For the believer the verse is a warning against the same disease in subtle form — admiring the Path, describing the Path, praising the Path, while deferring the walking. The confession is preserved precisely so no one can claim the thought was ever the deed.

---

## Verse 37:170

> But ˹now˺ they reject it, so they will soon know

"But they disbelieved in it, so they will soon know" — the hypothetical is answered by fact: the Reminder came in their own tongue, by the tongue of one of their own, and they disbelieved. "So they will soon know" — the phrase carries the Qur'an's grim courtesy: knowledge will arrive, but by then it will be the knowledge of outcome rather than of invitation — they will know the truth of the message when its consequences stand before them. Elsewhere the Qur'an fixes the same formula at the moments of reckoning: "they will know when the iron collars are upon their necks" (40:71). The verse balances 37:168–169 perfectly: they knew enough to describe what they would do with a Reminder; now they refuse the Reminder itself; the knowledge that remains is the terrible kind. The verse teaches the reader to close the gap in his own life while the gap is still mercy — to know now, by believing, rather than then, by suffering.

---

## Verse 37:171

> Our Word has already gone forth to Our servants, the messengers

"Our Word has already gone forth to Our servants, the messengers" — the sentence begun here runs to 37:173: the word is a decree, already issued, binding heaven's policy — "that they will be helped. That Our army — they are the ones who will prevail." The decree is stated as settled history before its events: every messenger of Allah enters his mission with this verdict pre-written. The commentators read "Our Word" as the promise recorded since the first warnings — Noah assured against his people, Abraham rescued from the fire, Moses delivered from Pharaoh — the pattern sealed, in the sūrah's own roll, by verse after verse the reader has just passed. The verse addresses the Prophet ﷺ in his hardest Makkan years: boycotted, mocked, and outnumbered, he is told the outcome is not pending but decreed. For every servant of truth, the verse fixes the end of the story in advance: the help of Allah is not a possibility to be won but a fact to be waited through.

---

## Verse 37:172

> that they would surely be helped

"That they will surely be helped" — the second clause of the decree. The Arabic lan-nunṣiranna is the strongest form of the future: "We will most certainly help." The help promised here is defined by the verse that follows — "and My army, they are the ones who will prevail" — and its meaning in the prophetic record was never victory in a single battle but the unstoppable survival and triumph of the message itself. Noah's message outlived his drowning people; Abraham's outlived the furnace; Moses' outlived Pharaoh; the Prophet's ﷺ outlived the Quraysh who planned his death at every stage of thirteen years. The verse therefore defines help the way heaven defines it: sometimes the servant is rescued from the fire, and sometimes the fire is made cool around him while the message walks out. The believer who serves Allah's cause is told in advance how the story ends — the only variable is the road, never the destination.

---

## Verse 37:173

> and that Our forces will certainly prevail

*Wa-inna jundanā la-hum al-ghālibūn* — Our forces will certainly prevail. The sūrah's stories have shown what those forces can look like: water for Noah, a fire made cool for Abraham, the sea for Moses, the fish for Jonah. The promise is the answer to the idolaters' confidence in their own numbers and standing, which the sūrah has been answering from its opening lines. The Book gives the same assurance to the messengers who were mocked: God will certainly help those who help Him (22:40; 47:7), and the verse states the outcome of the contest rather than its duration. It also explains why the command to the Prophet in the next verses is simply to wait and watch.

---

## Verse 37:174

> So turn away from the deniers for a while ˹O Prophet˺

"So turn away from them for a while" — the command given to the Prophet ﷺ at the height of Makkan hostility. The verse is not a dismissal of the deniers; it is a strategy of patience: "for a while" (ḥattā ḥīn) — a term set by Allah, unknown to the servant. The Qur'an repeats the instruction in several forms — "leave Me with those who deny this speech" (73:11), "so turn away from them, you are not blameworthy" — and each verse teaches the same balance: the messenger delivers. The results belong to Allah; the denier is not pursued into coercion ("there is no compulsion in religion," 2:256). The Prophet ﷺ lived this verse for thirteen years in Makkah — preaching without retaliation — before Madinah. The verse consoles every caller to goodness who meets refusal: turning away is not defeat; it is obedience to a rhythm Allah Himself set — a "while" that belongs to Him, after which the decree of help arrives exactly as written.

---

## Verse 37:175

> You will see ˹what will happen to˺ them, and they too will see

The command to watch is repeated at 37:179, and between the two lines the sūrah states the demand of the mockers, who wanted the punishment brought at once (37:176–177). The command is not impatience; it is an invitation to wait for the outcome, which the reader of the Book already knows and the mockers will discover. *Fa-sawfa yubṣirūn* — they will see — turns the taunt back on the taunters: the one who asks for the punishment to be hurried is told that he will be present when it arrives. The same frame works around the description of the morning that is coming, and it is the Book's habitual answer to mockery: not a rebuttal, but a record of the past and a certainty about the end.

---

## Verse 37:176

> Do they ˹really˺ wish to hasten Our punishment

*Fa-bi-ʿadhābinā yastaʿjilūn* — they want the punishment hurried. It is the same challenge the sūrah's stories have already shown, where people who think a threat is empty ask to see it produced. The Book's answer is that the delay they mistake for weakness is time given for repentance, and the Qur'an records the demand in several places, each time replying that the day comes at its appointed hour and that no one can bring it forward (10:50–51; 22:47; 29:53). The verse states the demand as a question, which is the Book's way of exposing it: the men asking for the blaze to be brought are asking for their own ruin, and they do not hear themselves asking.

---

## Verse 37:177

> Yet when it descends upon them: how evil will that morning be for those who had been warned

*Fa-sāʾa ṣabāḥu al-mundharīn* — the morning of the warned will be terrible. The image is drawn from the form the judgement took in the stories just told: the towns of Lot were destroyed at dawn (15:66; 54:38), and the Book uses daybreak as its picture of a settled deed coming due. So the mockery of the previous verse is answered not with a threat about the distant future but with the language of events the audience already knew about. The morning came for those communities, and their ruins are on the caravan routes the first hearers travelled (37:137). The word *mundharīn*, the warned, also echoes the warners of 37:72: the morning belongs to those who were told and did not turn.

---

## Verse 37:178

> And turn away from them for a while

*Wa-tawalla ʿanhum ḥattā ḥīn* — turn away from them until a time. The command is given to the Prophet, and the same instruction appears at 37:174 and 37:179, so the three lines bracket the description of the punishment with a command that is patience and warning at once. He is not to force the issue or answer mockery with anger, because the outcome has already been promised to God's forces (37:173). The Book records the Prophet's distress under this kind of treatment and the comfort given to him — that he is not the first messenger to be mocked (6:34; 36:30) — so the command models what the sūrah has been teaching: the truth is delivered and left to work, and the delay belongs to God's mercy rather than to His neglect.

---

## Verse 37:179

> You will see, and they too will see

"And see, then they will see" — the verse and its twin (37:175, 179) frame the sūrah's final warning. The first "see" is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ and the believers: watch the decree unfold — the help promised to the messengers, the outcome of the refusers. The second "they will see" is the deniers': what they now dismiss as tales will present itself as fact, and seeing will no longer be optional. The Qur'an uses this double-seeing formula repeatedly — "they see it far, and We see it near" (70:6–7) — because the entire dispute of unbelief is about which things are real. The verse teaches the believer the posture of certainty: not anxiety at the world's refusal, but a watching patience — "see" — anchored in the decrees already quoted. History kept the promise in the Prophet's ﷺ own lifetime; and the final seeing, the one the sūrah ultimately points to, remains on schedule for every soul that deferred it.

---

## Verse 37:180

> Glorified is your Lord—the Lord of Honour and Power—above what they claim

The argument of the sūrah closes with a declaration of God's transcendence. *Rabbu al-ʿizzati* — the Lord of honour and power — is above everything the idolaters said about Him: above the children they assigned Him and the kinship they invented with the jinn (37:152–158). The formula *ʿammā yaṣifūn*, above what they describe, appears at the end of sūrahs where errors about God have been corrected (23:91; 21:22), and here it prepares the two closing lines of peace and praise. The Book's way of ending a dispute is worth noticing: after the questions, the demand for evidence, and the warning about the morning, the last word is not a rebuttal but a glorification, as though the claims had already fallen away on their own.

---

The closing verse answers the accusations the sūrah has been recounting: God is above the children they assign to Him (37:149–153), above the falsehood of their ancestors, and above the slander of the well-known report about the angels. The name used, the Lord of honour and power, is paired in the next verses with the peace sent upon the messengers (37:181) and with the praise belonging to God, the Lord of all worlds (37:182), which is the sūrah's formal conclusion. The Book ends several sūrahs with the same declaration of transcendence over what people attribute, and the sūrah of the Children of Israel gives it in nearly the same words: exalted is your Lord, the Lord of honour, above what they describe (43:82).

## Verse 37:181

> Peace be upon the messengers

The greeting the sūrah has given to each of its prophets in turn — Noah, Abraham, Moses and Aaron, Elijah, Jonah — is now said once over all of them, in the plural: *salāmun ʿalā al-mursalīn*. It is the Book's way of stating that the message is one message and the messengers one line, and it explains why the sūrah told so many stories: the reader is meant to greet them together at the end. The line is liturgical, and Muslim recitation treats it as part of the sūrah's close, followed by the final verse of praise. It is also the answer to those who mocked one messenger: the Book blesses them all, and the peace of God is said over every one of the men their city rejected.

---

## Verse 37:182

> And praise be to Allah—Lord of all worlds

The sūrah ends with *al-ḥamdu lillāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīn*, the sentence that also opens the Book's praise in its first sūrah (1:2). After the debates with the idolaters, the stories of the destroyed communities, and the descriptions of Fire and gardens, the last word is worship. It is also the direct answer to the claims the sūrah has just rejected: God is the Lord of all the worlds — of the jinn and the angels whom the idolaters tried to associate with Him — and not the head of a family. The Book's arguments finish where worship begins, and the reader who has followed the whole sūrah from the oaths of its opening verses to this doxology has been shown the case and then given the sentence to say.
