# Sūrah ʿAbasa (Chapter 80) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:1

> He frowned and turned ˹his attention˺ away,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Rebuke That Names Nobody**

The first word of the sūrah is a verb whose subject is never given. *ʿAbasa* — he frowned — third person, masculine, singular, and the one who frowned is the Messenger himself. Nothing in the opening verses supplies his name, the other man's name, the place, or the occasion. What survives is the act, stripped of every detail that would reduce it to an anecdote about two particular people in Mecca. The strategy works. A rebuke with names attached is a record; a rebuke with the names withheld is a rule, and rules travel. The same refusal runs through the whole passage: the seeker is identified only by his blindness, and the man who receives the attention only by an inner posture, never by his clan. Fourteen centuries of recitation have repeated this verb in prayer without resolving who stood where, and the unresolved form is precisely what allows anyone who has ever preferred one listener over another to hear himself in it.

**The Report Behind the Opening Verb**

ʿĀʾishah's account supplies the scene, and Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī carries it at 3331, graded ṣaḥīḥ in the Dār al-Salām edition. ʿAbdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm, the blind man, came to the Messenger of God saying, "O Messenger of God, guide me," while a prominent man of the idolaters sat with him. The Prophet turned towards that man and asked whether he saw anything objectionable in what was being said to him, and the answer was no. Then these verses came down. Commentators differ about which notable occupied the room — ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah, Umayyah ibn Khalaf, Abū Jahl and al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah are each named in the reports — and the difference does not touch the meaning. What matters is the asymmetry. One man had come asking, and could not see the expression on the face turned away from him. The other had come asking for nothing, and could see all of it.

**The Fault Was a Face, Not a Word**

Nothing harsh is reported here. No refusal, no rebuke of the blind man, no instruction to wait outside until the conversation ended. The offence consists of a contraction of the brow and a turn of the shoulders, the sort of thing that passes without remark in an ordinary exchange between a busy man and an interruption. Set beside what the Meccan notables were doing to the Muslims in those years — boycott, mockery, torture of the weak — the gesture is almost nothing. The scale of the response therefore measures something other than the size of the act. The bearer of revelation stands under a standard according to which a preference expressed only through the face is a matter for correction from heaven. Most classical exegetes do not call this a sin in the strict sense; they call it *tark al-awlā*, the leaving of the better course, and the distinction carries weight. What is corrected is not a moral collapse but a judgement about priority, and a judgement about priority is exactly what a teacher of revelation may not make on social grounds.

**Two Verbs, One Movement Outward**

The pairing is precise. *ʿAbasa* describes what a face does before its owner decides: the brows draw in, the expression closes. *Tawallā* describes what a body does on purpose: he turned away. The involuntary signal is recorded first and the deliberate reorientation second, and the sequence traces how a flicker of irritation settles into a posture. Both verbs are past tense and both stand without a stated object; the object in the translation — his attention — is supplied, and fairly so, because *tawallā* here means the turning of attention rather than the leaving of a room. He did not walk out. He stayed and faced elsewhere, which is worse, since the blind man remained within earshot of a conversation from which he had been quietly removed. Arabic separates the two channels of attention in a way English blurs: the face that betrays and the body that chooses. The verse holds both in two words, and hearing them in order is hearing a small internal movement complete itself outward.

**A Scripture That Keeps Its Own Correction**

No one writing a book to serve himself places a correction of himself in the opening line of a chapter destined for public recitation. The sūrah carries no preface to soften the rebuke and no closing verse declaring the matter settled and forgotten. It sits inside the liturgy, which means it repeats. Muslims have recited the Prophet's frown in their prayers since Mecca, in the third person and then, four verses later, in the second. The effect on communal memory is difficult to overstate: the founder is preserved as corrected, openly, by the book he brought. It is reported that after this the Prophet honoured Ibn Umm Maktūm whenever he saw him, greeting him as the one on whose account his Lord had reproached him. [UNVERIFIED: the greeting formula is transmitted through the tafsīr literature, notably al-Qurṭubī, without a chain and grade I was able to confirm]

**The Standing Rule for Whoever Teaches**

This verse belongs to a cluster that regulates the Prophet's attention. *“˹O Prophet!˺ Do not dismiss those ˹poor believers˺ who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure”* (6:52) is a prohibition pointing the same way. *“And patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. Do not let your eyes look beyond them, desiring the luxuries of this worldly life”* (18:28) names the motive to be resisted. *“You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills”* (28:56) removes the calculation that makes the preference look reasonable. Together these passages and the opening verse fix one principle: the worth of a listener in the economy of teaching is not his social weight. Delivery is what the teacher owes, and delivery belongs first to whoever asks.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:2

> ˹simply˺ because the blind man came to him ˹interrupting˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cause Given for the Frown Is the Arrival of a Seeker**

The verse states the reason, and the reason is unbearable in its plainness. *An jāʾahu* — because he came to him. No complaint is recorded against the blind man: he was not rude, not loud, not demanding, not wrong in his timing according to any custom of the time. He came. The causal construction ties the displeasure to nothing except the presence of a person who wanted guidance, and that is the whole indictment in three words. Any other cause would have left room for argument. Had the man interrupted a negotiation, insulted the company, or pushed past an order, the frown would have had a defence. The text removes the defence by naming the cause in its barest form, and it does so with the particle of reason rather than concession, so that the arrival functions grammatically as what produced the frown and not merely as what accompanied it.

**Identified by His Blindness Rather Than by His Name**

The Qur'an calls him *al-aʿmā*, the blind man, and never gives his name in this sūrah, though the reports preserve it as ʿAbdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm. The choice is meaningful, and it runs against the practice elsewhere in the Book, where an individual is occasionally named — Zayd in the passage on adoption (33:37) is the one Companion mentioned by name in the Qur'an. Naming fixes an event to a biography. Describing by condition generalizes it. Any teacher in any century has met this man: the one whose need is real, whose appearance is inconvenient, and whose arrival cannot be scheduled. The blindness also does specific work inside the case rather than serving as a mere label, because it explains why the offence was invisible to its victim and why the correction had to come from elsewhere. He could not see the face that turned away. The description is therefore evidence in the argument, not decoration attached to it.

**He Came Asking, and He Had Already Believed**

The reports agree that Ibn Umm Maktūm was among the early Muslims in Mecca, one of the first to accept the message, and that his request — "guide me," "teach me," in the various wordings — was a request for increase rather than for conversion. This detail governs the next two verses, where the hoped-for outcomes are purification and benefiting from a reminder, both of which describe what happens to someone already inclined. A man being converted for the first time is not usually said to be purified by a reminder; he is said to be guided. The vocabulary of vv. 3–4 fits a believer who has come for more, and the fit confirms the reports. It also sharpens the offence. This was not a stranger who had blundered into a private meeting; it was a Companion, already inside the community, seeking the very thing the meeting existed to give, and receiving the shoulder instead.

**The Blindness That Matters Is the Other Kind**

Makkan revelation keeps drawing a line between two blindnesses. The one that disqualifies a person from nothing is physical; the one that disqualifies a person from everything is perceptual. *“Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind”* (22:46) states the contrast in its clearest form, and it belongs to the same Makkan idiom as this sūrah. The man standing in front of the Prophet had functioning eyes removed from his use and a heart fully at work. The men seated with the Prophet had functioning eyes and, on the reports of their own conduct, hearts that were shutting. Nothing in the verse says this outright — the passage is far too economical for that — but the whole structure of the sūrah depends on it, since the one described as blind is the one presented as seeing, and the ones presented as seeing are the ones later described as indifferent. The reversal is the argument.

**An Act Judged With No Human Witness**

The blind man did not know he had been frowned at. He could not see it, and no one in the room appears to have told him; the reports record no confrontation, no apology delivered to him on the spot, no complaint from him afterwards. The act was, in every human sense, undetected. The verse nevertheless places it under judgement, and the judgement arrives in a book that will be recited by millions who never knew his name. That is the point of the scene's invisibility: the moral quality of an act does not depend on the recipient's awareness of it. The same principle is stated elsewhere in explicit terms — *“Or do they think that We do not hear their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks? Yes ˹We do˺! And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording ˹it all˺”* (43:80). Here the recording is not of words but of a face, which is a harder case, since a face is not chosen in the way a sentence is.

**What the Verse Excludes From the Charge**

The translation supplies two insertions: ˹simply˺ and ˹interrupting˺. Both are fair readings of the Arabic rather than additions to it, and each does different work. The first narrows the cause, insisting that nothing else in the scene accounts for the frown. The second supplies the timing, locating the arrival inside an ongoing conversation. Neither insertion attributes fault to the blind man, and the verse gives no grounds for doing so: interrupting a teacher to ask for teaching is not a transgression in the ethics the Qur'an builds. What the second insertion does establish is that the timing was inconvenient, and inconvenience is the real subject of the passage. The sūrah is not saying the man did wrong by arriving when he did; it is saying that inconvenience to the teacher is not a currency in which the rights of a seeker can be paid. That distinction survives every rearrangement of the scene, which is why the verse has been read as a rule about access rather than as a comment on one afternoon in Mecca.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:3

> You never know ˹O Prophet˺, perhaps he may be purified,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Ends the Argument**

*Mā yudrīka* — what would make you know, what could inform you — is not a request for information. It is the Qur'an's way of closing a line of reasoning that the addressee did not know he had opened. The Prophet had made a judgement: this conversation with a notable is more valuable than the request of a blind man, because the notable's conversion would carry weight and the blind man's need could wait. The verse does not answer the judgement with a better judgement. It answers it by pointing out that the calculation rests on an unknown, and that no human being is positioned to know it. *Laʿallahu yazzakkā* — perhaps he may purify himself — puts the possibility squarely on the table: this man, the one you turned from, may be the one who is transformed by what you were saying. The rebuke therefore does not depend on claiming that the blind man certainly would have been purified. It depends only on the fact that nobody in the room could rule it out, and that a decision was taken as though it could.

**Purification Is Growth Before It Is Cleaning**

The word is from *zakāh*, and the root carries two senses that English splits apart: to be pure and to grow. A crop that *yazkū* increases; a person who is *zakī* is clean of defect. The Qur'an uses the doubled form here, *yazzakkā*, which makes the action reflexive and intensive — he purifies himself, he becomes purified in himself. Purification in this sense is not the removal of a stain so much as the development of a capacity. That is why the verse attaches it to teaching rather than to ritual: what the blind man might receive is not a washing but a growth, and the growth is his own even though the seed is someone else's words. The same word family carries the obligatory alms, which is called *zakāh* because it purifies the wealth and increases it at once. A person who pays it is not diminished; he is cleaned and enlarged by the same act. The reflexive form in this verse makes the blind man the subject of his own transformation, which raises his standing in the scene considerably.

**The Uncertainty Sits in the Outcome, Not in God**

*Lacalla* is a particle of expectation, and the commentators have long discussed what it can mean when God is the speaker. Applied to human knowledge it expresses hope or possibility; applied to divine speech it cannot express ignorance, since nothing is hidden from God. The standard resolution is that the particle reports the position of the addressee rather than the state of the speaker: from where you stand, this is a real possibility you dismissed. Another reading takes *lacalla* in divine speech as near-certain, which would make the verse stronger still — he probably will be purified. Either way the argument holds, and the weaker reading is the safer one, because it does not require the reader to conclude that the outcome was fixed and that the Prophet was merely uninformed of a decree. What the verse needs is only this: an outcome existed that was both real and unknowable to the man making the decision, and the decision was made as though that outcome did not exist.

**The Same Formula in Other Passages**

*Mā yudrīka* and its close relative *mā adrāka* form a recognizable Qur'anic device for marking the limit of human knowledge. *“It is Allah Who has revealed the Book with the truth and the balance ˹of justice˺. You never know, perhaps the Hour is near”* (42:17) uses the identical construction to place the Hour outside anyone's calculation. The interrogative form appears in the descriptions of the last things: *“And what will make you realize what the Night of Glory is?”* (97:2), and *“And what will make you realize what the Striking Disaster is?”* (101:3). In each case the question is rhetorical and the answer that follows is not an explanation but an intensification — the thing is greater than knowing. The device does the same work here, on a much smaller scale and about a much smaller event. A blind man's soul is placed in the category of things whose outcome no observer can compute, which is an extraordinary elevation of an ordinary person, achieved entirely by grammar.

**Why the Hope Is Attached to This Man**

The verses that follow show what purification is worth: *“Successful indeed is the one who purifies their soul”* (91:9), and *“Successful indeed are those who purify themselves”* (87:14), both Makkan and both using the same root in the same reflexive sense. Success is defined by the act of self-purification, not by social position, wealth, or the approval of notables. Against that definition the calculation in the room collapses. The Prophet was weighing a blind beggar against a chief of Quraysh, and the Qur'an's own vocabulary weighs only purification. The man with the highest value in the scene was the one being overlooked, and the value was invisible to every method of assessment available in Mecca. This is not a sentimental point about the poor. It is a claim about the units in which the Qur'an measures persons, and the units are internal: what a person becomes, not what a person commands.

**What the Teacher Can and Cannot Produce**

The possibility in this verse belongs to the seeker, and the delivery belongs to the teacher. The Qur'an keeps the two roles strictly separate. *“Invite ˹all˺ to the Way of your Lord with wisdom and kind advice, and only debate with them in the best manner. Surely your Lord ˹alone˺ knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided”* (16:125) assigns the method to the human being and the knowledge of outcome to God. *“You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills”* (28:56) removes the power to produce the result. Neither verse permits the teacher to stop teaching; both forbid him from managing the results by selecting his audience according to likely yield. That is exactly the error of this scene. The teacher began to allocate attention on the basis of predicted outcomes, and the predicted outcomes are not his to predict. The verse restores the proper order by naming purification as a possibility that no one controls, including the one who delivers the words.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:4

> or he may be mindful, benefitting from the reminder.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Lesser of Two Hopes**

The particle *aw* sets an alternative, and the second term is deliberately smaller than the first. Purification was the greater possibility; mindful benefit from a reminder is the lesser one. The verse is constructing a descending scale so that the rebuke cannot be evaded. If the blind man might have been transformed by the teaching, the turn away cost him everything. If he might only have taken a single admonition to heart and profited by it, the turn away still cost him that. There is no version of the case in which the interruption was worthless, and the argument does not need the higher estimate to work. Teachers face this constantly: the outcome of a conversation is rarely the full transformation of a person, and almost always something smaller — one sentence retained, one habit questioned, one fear named. This verse makes that smaller outcome sufficient ground for the whole rebuke, which is why it is more demanding than it first appears. It refuses the consolation that nothing much would have happened anyway.

**Remembrance as Recovery, Not Information**

The verb is *yadhdhakkar*, from *dh-k-r*, a doubled reflexive form of "to remember." The Qur'an's consistent usage treats the reminder as recovering something the hearer already possesses rather than depositing something new. A *dhikr* is not a lecture; it is a call back. This is why the Qur'an names itself a Reminder so often — *“This ˹Book˺ is only a Reminder and a clear Quran”* (36:69) — and why the act it asks of its hearers is *tadhakkur*, calling to mind. The theological ground is the covenant and the innate disposition: human beings already know that they did not make themselves, and revelation restores a knowledge that has been covered over rather than supplying a fact never held. On this model the blind man's benefit is not the acquisition of content he lacked. He had accepted Islam already, according to the reports. What he stood to gain was the deepening of a possession, and that is precisely the kind of gain a busy teacher dismisses as unimportant, because it produces no visible result and converts no influential person.

**The Benefit Is Named as Profit**

*Fa-tanfaʿahu al-dhikrā* — and the reminder benefits him. The verb is from *nafʿ*, utility, advantage, the opposite of harm. The Qur'an uses commercial language for spiritual states with a consistency that suggests deliberate choice: guidance is gain, heedlessness is loss, a life spent without reflection is a bad trade. The construction here attaches the profit to the receiver and not to the deliverer, which is a quiet but important specification. The one who benefits is the blind man. The teacher's account is unaffected either way, since he is not paid in outcomes; he is paid in delivery. That asymmetry is what makes the scene's calculation so strange. The Prophet was, in effect, estimating the return on his own time, and the verse tells him that the return he was estimating is not his to book. The profit goes to the receiver's ledger, and the receiver's ledger is closed to human audit. Nobody in the room could see whether the reminder would land.

**Two Outcomes in One Person or Two Kinds of Person**

Exegetes read vv. 3–4 in two ways. The first treats them as stages in a single person: purification is the higher state, mindful benefit the lower, and a given hearer may reach one, both, or neither. The second treats them as two classes: some who are purified by the teaching and some who merely take a benefit from it, both of which justify the teacher's attention. The first reading fits the grammar more comfortably, because the pronoun is singular and continuous across both verses — *he* may purify himself, or *he* may be mindful — and because the descending scale of *aw* reads naturally as a concession within one case. The second reading is defensible but adds a distinction the text does not mark. Either way the practical conclusion is identical: the teacher cannot know in advance which outcome will occur in which hearer, so selection by predicted yield is not available to him. The disagreement matters less than the shared premise, which is that both outcomes are worth the turning of a face.

**A Reminder Only Works on the Living**

The Qur'an is candid about the limits of its own address. It was sent down *“to warn whoever is ˹truly˺ alive and fulfil the decree ˹of torment˺ against the disbelievers”* (36:70), and the condition of being alive is a condition of the heart. The reminder profits the one who receives it as a reminder; against a sealed heart it produces nothing, and the text says so without embarrassment. *“But ˹continue to˺ remind. For certainly reminders benefit the believers”* (51:55) ties benefit to belief as its precondition. This is the reason the scene is arranged as it is. The blind man was alive in the required sense: he had believed, he had come, he had asked. The chief, by contrast, had not been moved to ask anything. The available probability of benefit was overwhelmingly on the side of the man who was ignored, and the calculation that ran the other way was based on social importance rather than on receptivity. The verse exposes the substitution without naming it.

**Why This Comes Before the Chief**

The order of the passage is part of its argument. The two possibilities of vv. 3–4 are stated first, in the third person, while the blind man is still the subject; only then does the address turn to the one who deemed himself beyond need. Had the sequence been reversed, the reader would have met the notable's indifference before learning what was at stake for the seeker, and the rebuke would have looked like a preference for the poor over the rich. As it stands, the reader learns first that a real and unknowable gain was on offer, and only then that attention was directed elsewhere. The injustice is therefore established before the comparison begins. This is also why the sūrah never returns to praise the blind man: it does not need to. His case was made in the possibility of his purification, and the rest of the passage is about the man who had no such possibility and received the attention anyway.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:5

> As for the one who was indifferent,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word That Names a Posture, Not a Balance**

*Man istaghnā* is built on *gh-n-y*, the root of sufficiency and wealth, and the tenth form used here means to consider oneself sufficient, to deem oneself beyond need. The translation renders it as indifference, which captures the effect rather than the letter. The literal sense is closer to "the one who has made himself self-sufficient" in his own estimation, and the estimation is the point: the form is reflexive, so the sufficiency is claimed by the subject rather than granted by his circumstances. A rich man who knows his dependence is not *istaghnā*; a poor man who believes he needs nothing from God is. The Qur'an treats the posture as the root of rebellion, and it locates it in the heart before it locates it in the wallet. This verse is the first appearance of that figure in the passage, and it is introduced without a name, without a description of wealth, and without any reference to what the man actually did. His whole characterization is the claim of self-sufficiency.

**The Sentence Stops Before Its Verb**

Grammatically the verse is incomplete. *Ammā man istaghnā* — as for the one who deemed himself beyond need — is a topic with no predicate. The predicate arrives in the next verse, and the delay is not accidental. Arabic's topic-fronting construction allows a speaker to raise a subject and hold it in the air, and the Qur'an uses the device to make the reader wait for what will be said about this man. The effect is a small suspense that mirrors the scene: the notable had been held in the Prophet's attention, and now he is held in the reader's, with no indication yet of whether the holding will end in praise or in rebuke. Every listener in Mecca would have assumed praise, since the man was important. The structure forces the assumption to be held for one full verse before it is contradicted, which is a rhetorical way of reproducing the error the sūrah is correcting. The reader is made to wait on the important man too, and the wait teaches him what waiting costs.

**The Same Word in the Clot**

The Makkan sūrahs return to this root with a regularity that marks it as a diagnostic term. *“Most certainly, one exceeds all bounds once they think they are selfsufficient”* (96:6–7) states the causal chain in its barest form: the sense of sufficiency produces transgression. The verb there, *yaṭghā*, is the same one used of Pharaoh and of the nations destroyed for their overreach. Sufficiency, in this idiom, is not a state of comfort but a state of moral exposure, because the person who believes he needs nothing has removed the only ground on which a command could reach him. That is why the notable in this scene is not described as hostile. Hostility is a response; it presupposes that something is being asked of you. The condition of *istaghnā* is quieter and worse: nothing is being asked, because the person has already answered the question in his own favour. A warner can address an enemy. He cannot easily address a man who is complete.

**Indifference Is Harder to Reach Than Hostility**

The passage sets up a comparison that reverses expectation. Persecutors of the message are treated throughout Makkan revelation as people who can still be addressed: they argue, they mock, they propose deals, and their engagement, however corrupt, keeps a channel open. The indifferent man offers no channel. He is not opposing the message; he is not needing it. He listens because listening costs nothing and because the company of a rising prophet may prove useful. Nothing in the reports suggests that the notable in this scene was seeking guidance or resisting it with reasons. He was present. That presence is what attracted the attention, and the attraction is what the verse identifies as the error. Teachers know the type: the audience member whose interest is social, whose questions are performed, and whose approval flatters. The Qur'an is not saying such people should be refused. It is saying that their presence creates a gravity that pulls attention away from the ones who came with a need, and that gravity has to be resisted deliberately.

**The Nameless Chief**

The reports name candidates — ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah, Umayyah ibn Khalaf, Abū Jahl, al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah — and the sūrah names none. Commentators who press for an identification are working from later biographical material rather than from the text, and the disagreement among them is a sign that the identification was never secure. The text's silence is more useful than any name would be. A named chief would have made the verse a judgement on a man who is dead; an unnamed posture makes it a judgement on a disposition that is available to every generation, including the disposition of a pious teacher who prefers the educated listener to the simple one. Nothing in the verse restricts the figure to Mecca or to unbelief. The one who deems himself beyond need can be a Muslim who attends a lecture for the company, a scholar who answers the powerful before the poor, or a reader who skips the passages addressed to people less fortunate than himself. The anonymity is what licenses that range.

**Wealth in the Vocabulary of the Makkan Sūrahs**

The short Makkan chapters circle this figure constantly, which suggests the social pressure the early community was under. *“Woe to every backbiter, slanderer, who amasses wealth ˹greedily˺ and counts it ˹repeatedly˺, thinking that their wealth will make them immortal!”* (104:1–3) connects the accumulation of property to a belief in permanence. *“Competition for more ˹gains˺ diverts you ˹from Allah˺, until you end up in ˹your˺ graves”* (102:1–2) describes the same sufficiency as a distraction that ends only at death. In both cases the diagnosis is internal: the problem is not the wealth but the sense of completeness it produces. That is the condition named in this verse, and it explains why the man is not accused of any act. He did nothing to the blind man. He simply existed in a state that made the blind man easy to overlook, and the sūrah treats the state as the real subject. Indifference is not a neutral background; it is an achievement, built by counting.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:6

> you gave him your ˹undivided˺ attention,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb of Courtship**

*Taṣaddā* comes from the root *ṣ-d-w*, whose basic sense is to incline towards, to attend, to turn one's face and interest in a direction. The fifth form used here intensifies and prolongs the movement: not a glance but a sustained orientation, the kind of attention one gives when one is trying to win someone. The translation's "undivided" is a good rendering for exactly that reason, since the verb implies a turning of the whole person rather than a partial courtesy. In classical usage the same root is employed for one who courts favour or seeks the goodwill of a superior. The word choice therefore carries a social charge that a neutral verb would lack. This was not the Prophet noticing an important man in the room. It was the Prophet directing himself towards him, and the direction had a purpose: to hold his interest, to keep him engaged, to bring him closer to the message. The verb names the effort, and the effort is what the verse examines.

**The Address Turns to Face Its Subject**

Verses 1–2 spoke in the third person: he frowned, he turned away. This verse speaks in the second: *fa-anta lahu taṣaddā* — you attend to him. The shift is abrupt and it is doing work. Third-person narration allows the listener to observe the scene from outside; second-person address puts him inside it as the one being spoken to. The sūrah moves from describing an act to confronting the actor, and it does so at the exact point where the act becomes a choice rather than a reflex. A frown may escape a man. Sustained attention does not. The grammar tracks that difference by changing person, and the change lands on the Prophet himself with no softening formula — no "say to them," no indirect address, no protective framing. Later verses return to the second person for the same purpose, and the pattern gives the passage its distinctive severity: it is the only place in the Qur'an where the Messenger is addressed in these terms about his own conduct towards a member of his community.

**A Word Order That Restricts**

The Arabic places the pronoun and the prepositional phrase before the verb: *anta lahu taṣaddā*, literally "you, to him, you attend." Fronting both elements produces restriction, and restriction is the meaning. It is to him that you attend — not to the one who came. The device is standard in Arabic rhetoric and is used throughout the Qur'an to exclude an alternative without stating it, and here the unstated alternative was supplied three verses earlier. The reader who hears the fronting hears the comparison embedded in the syntax, so the verse manages to make its point about the blind man without mentioning him again. Nothing is repeated; everything is implied by placement. English loses most of this, which is why the translation must add "undivided" to recover a sense that Arabic achieves by word order alone. The addition is legitimate precisely because the order is restrictive rather than merely emphatic.

**A Good Motive and a Wrong Allocation**

The commentators who defend the Prophet's conduct do so on the ground of motive: he wanted the conversion of a man whose Islam would have strengthened the community, and the strengthening of the community is a legitimate aim. The reasoning is sound as far as it goes, and it is the reasoning most teachers would offer in the same situation. The verse does not dispute the motive. It disputes the allocation. Two goods were in the room — the possible transformation of a seeker and the possible conversion of a notable — and the second was pursued at the cost of the first, in a way that was invisible to everyone except the One watching. The deeper problem with the calculation is that it depends on a forecast the calculator cannot make, as the previous verses established. Nobody knew whether the chief would believe, and nobody knew whether the blind man would be purified. The choice between two unknowns was settled by social weight, and social weight is not a category the Qur'an recognizes in the distribution of teaching.

**Attention Is the Currency of Teaching**

Everything a teacher has is attention, and the Qur'an regulates its distribution in explicit terms. *“And patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. Do not let your eyes look beyond them, desiring the luxuries of this worldly life”* (18:28) forbids the eyes from travelling past the sincere towards the splendid, and it names the desire that causes the travel. *“˹O Prophet!˺ Do not dismiss those ˹poor believers˺ who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure”* (6:52) makes the same prohibition with the added weight of a warning: dismiss them and you join the wrongdoers. Both passages address the same situation this verse addresses — the pressure to prefer the influential — and both treat the preference as a matter of the eyes and the face rather than of policy. Attention is not an administrative resource that can be allocated by expected return. It is the substance of the teaching itself, and when it goes to the wrong person the teaching has already been misdelivered, however accurate the words.

**What the Verse Does Not Forbid**

Nothing here prohibits engagement with the powerful. Makkan revelation repeatedly sends the Prophet towards the leaders of Quraysh, records his negotiations with them, and treats their conversion as a real good; the sūrah of the Poets and the sūrah of the Star both address them directly. The instruction is narrower and harder: engagement with the notable must not be paid for out of the account of the seeker. Had the Prophet finished with Ibn Umm Maktūm and then returned to the chief, no verse would have come down. Had he gestured the blind man to sit and waited on him, the case would have been different again. The specific fault is the transfer of attention from a person with a request to a person without one, executed in silence, in a way the requester could not detect and could not contest. That is why the correction is so severe about an act so small: the act was unfalsifiable by its victim, and an injustice that cannot be noticed by the one who suffers it is the kind that most needs a witness from outside.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:7

> even though you are not to blame if he would not be purified.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Release Placed Inside a Reproach**

The verse does something unusual: it withdraws the very ground on which the reproach seemed to rest. You attended to the notable — and you are not answerable if he does not purify himself. At first reading the two halves pull against each other. If the outcome was never in the Prophet's hands, why is the attention criticized? The answer is that the release and the reproach address different things. The reproach concerns the displacement of the seeker. The release concerns the burden of results. Placing them side by side removes the excuse that makes the displacement look reasonable, since the excuse was always an appeal to consequences: I am doing this for the good that may come of it. The verse says the good that may come of it was never yours to produce, so the appeal fails. What remains is the conduct itself, judged on its own terms, with no consequentialist defence available. Very few moral systems are willing to strip a decision of its outcomes and then judge what is left.

**The Same Verb, a Different Subject**

*Yazzakkā* appears in verse 3 and again here, in the same reflexive form, and the repetition is the hinge of the passage. In verse 3 the possibility attaches to the blind man: perhaps he may purify himself. Here it attaches to the notable: you are not to blame if he does not purify himself. One word, two men, two entirely different relations to the same outcome. For the seeker, purification is a hope worth turning towards. For the one who deems himself beyond need, purification is a possibility that no human effort can guarantee and no human being is answerable for. The symmetry is exact and it is devastating: the very thing that justified the preference — the greater likelihood of a great result — is declared unknowable in both cases, and then declared irrelevant to liability in one. The sūrah thus uses a single verb to dismantle the calculation it is describing, without ever stating that a calculation was made.

**Delivery, Not Results**

The principle is stated elsewhere in plain terms. *“The Messenger's duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah ˹fully˺ knows what you reveal and what you conceal”* (5:99) fixes the boundary of the office. *“But if they turn away, then your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants”* (3:20) repeats it in a context of argument with the People of the Scripture. The Makkan formulation is blunter still: *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺”* (88:21–22). Across these passages the office is defined by an act the messenger can complete — delivery — and not by an effect he cannot. The definition is protective in both directions. It prevents despair when the message is refused, and it prevents manipulation when the message is received, because in neither case is the messenger the author of the outcome. This verse applies the same definition to a single afternoon: you are not to blame if he does not purify himself.

**Why the Release Is Needed for the Argument**

Without this verse the reproach would be open to a strong reply. The Prophet could have said — and any teacher would say — that he was maximizing the reach of the message, that a chief's conversion would have protected the weak, and that the blind man's need could be met an hour later. The reply is coherent, and coherence is what makes it dangerous. This verse removes its foundation by declaring the outcome outside the agent's responsibility, which leaves only the comparative claim: the chief's purification was more likely. That claim was addressed two verses earlier, where the possibility of the blind man's purification was placed on the table as real and unknowable. Once both outcomes are unknowable and neither is chargeable to the teacher, the only remaining variable is the presence of a request. One man asked; the other did not. The verse is what reduces the case to that single variable, and the reduction is the whole method of the passage.

**What Is Not Excused**

The release concerns liability for the other's state, not the quality of the teacher's own conduct. Nothing in the verse suggests that the frown was acceptable, that the inattention was harmless, or that the seeker's claim was weak. Those matters were settled in the preceding verses and are not reopened. The distinction matters because the verse could easily be misread as a consolation: do not worry, the outcome is not in your hands. Read that way it would cancel the reproach, and the sūrah plainly does not intend that, since the reproach continues for three more verses. What the verse offers is narrower and more useful. A teacher is relieved of the results and thereby made fully accountable for the manner. Freed from the pressure to produce conversions, he has no remaining reason to arrange his attention by likely yield. The relief is instrumental: it exists so that the conduct can be judged cleanly, with no appeal to consequences left standing.

**The Weight of Outcomes on a Teacher**

Anyone who has taught knows the distortion this verse removes. When success is measured by results, the results you can see become the students you prefer: the quick ones, the credentialed ones, the ones whose progress reflects on you. The slow, the awkward, the ones who ask the same question twice get less time, and the allocation feels like judgement rather than prejudice. The Qur'an's remedy is not to demand impartiality as a virtue; it is to change the accounting. By declaring that the outcome is not the teacher's to answer for, it makes the visible results worthless as a metric, and the preference that rested on them loses its support. What is left to measure is conduct: did you turn towards the one who asked? That measure can be met in a single moment, by anyone, regardless of the ability or status of the person in front of them. It is the only measure in the passage that is fully within the Prophet's control, and it is the one he is corrected on.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:8

> But as for the one who came to you, eager ˹to learn˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Striving Added to Arrival**

The blind man's coming was described in verse 2 with a single verb: he came. Here the same event is described with two: *jāʾaka yasʿā* — he came to you striving. *Saʿā* means to move with effort, to hasten, to work at a purpose, and it is used elsewhere in the Qur'an for the exertion that qualifies an act as worship. The addition changes the character of the arrival. In verse 2 the coming functions as the cause of the frown; here it functions as a claim. A man who comes has interrupted. A man who comes striving has made an effort that the teacher did not make for him, and the effort establishes a priority. The reports fill in the physical detail that the verb implies: a blind man crossing the ground to find the Prophet, orienting himself by voice, unable to read the faces that would have told him to wait. Every step of that journey was cost, and the verse registers the cost as the ground of the claim rather than as a mitigating circumstance.

**The Second Person Returns**

*Jāʾaka* — he came to you. The suffix is attached directly to the verb, and the address is once again in the second person, as it was in verse 6. The alternation between third and second person across these verses is not stylistic filler. The third person describes the scene as an event in the world; the second person makes it an event in the Prophet's own account. When the verse says "he came to you," the coming is placed on the Prophet's side of the ledger as something received, and something received generates an obligation. A man who comes to a gathering has entered a shared space. A man who comes to you has made you the destination, and the destination owes him an arrival. The pronoun does that work in a single letter, and it is one reason the passage is so severe: the seeker is not described as coming to the Muslims, or to the mosque, or to Mecca. He came to you, and you were otherwise engaged.

**Three Marks of the Qualifying Seeker**

Read with the verse that follows, this one lists the conditions that make a claimant prior: he came, he strove, he feared. The first is presence — he did not send a message or wait for an invitation. The second is effort — the presence cost him something. The third is reverence, and it belongs to the next verse, but the list is clearly designed as a unit, since the reproach in verse 10 answers all three at once. Nothing in the list concerns intelligence, lineage, wealth, eloquence, or usefulness to the mission. The qualifications are entirely internal and entirely available: any person in any condition can come, strive, and stand in awe. That availability is the point of the whole passage, and it explains why the comparison with the notable is so damaging to him. He possessed every qualification Mecca recognized and none of these three. The blind man possessed none of Mecca's and all three of the sūrah's, and the sūrah is the authority that decides which list counts.

**Striving in the Qur'an's Moral Ledger**

*Saʿy* is one of the Qur'an's central terms for human action, and its range is instructive. *“But whoever desires the Hereafter and strives for it accordingly, and is a ˹true˺ believer, it is they whose striving will be appreciated”* (17:19) makes striving the content of a life and promises that it will be recognized. *“and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards, and that ˹the outcome of˺ their endeavours will be seen ˹in their record˺”* (53:39–40) ties reward to effort rather than to result. In both cases the weight falls on the movement and not on the achievement, which is exactly the logic of this scene. The blind man could not produce an outcome; he could only come, and come with effort. On the Qur'an's own accounting that is a substantial thing, and it is the thing that was overlooked. A community that measures its members by outcomes will always undervalue the ones who can only strive, and the sūrah is correcting that measurement at its point of origin.

**Movement Precedes Gift**

The order of the two men in the room is fixed by who moved. The blind man crossed the distance; the notable was already seated. Nothing in the verse states a rule about this, but the structure of the passage supplies one: the teacher's attention follows the movement of the seeker, not the position of the sitter. This has practical consequences that are easy to miss. The one who is already present — the regular, the comfortable, the socially established — generates no signal, because his presence requires nothing of him. The one who has come from outside generates a signal with every step. A teacher who allocates by signal will attend to the comer, and a teacher who allocates by position will attend to the sitter. Both feel they are being fair, since neither is refusing anyone. The verse locates the fault in the allocation itself and does not wait for a refusal to occur, which is why the correction arrives before anything was actually denied to the blind man.

**Eagerness Cannot Be Manufactured**

The final observation the verse forces is uncomfortable for anyone who teaches. Striving is not produced by the teacher; it arrives from outside, fully formed, in a person the teacher did not choose. All the teacher controls is whether to obstruct it. The notable's indifference could not be cured by better presentation, and the blind man's eagerness could not be increased by any attention he received. The only variable in the room was the direction of a face. This is why the reproach is not phrased as a failure of method — nothing is said about how the Prophet taught, what he taught, or how long he taught. The failure is entirely one of orientation. Reduced to that, the case becomes applicable to every teacher, preacher, doctor, and parent: the eagerness in front of you was not made by you and cannot be improved by you, and the only thing you can do about it is face it.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:9

> being in awe ˹of Allah˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Continuing State Rather Than a Passing Fright**

The Arabic is *wa-huwa yakhshā* — and he is in awe. Two features of the construction matter. The sentence is nominal, with the pronoun *huwa* stated explicitly before the verb, which in Arabic gives duration and stability rather than a single event. The verb itself is in the imperfect, which describes an ongoing or repeated action. Put together, the phrase does not say that the man was frightened at that moment. It says that awe is his condition, that he stands in it, and that the standing is what qualifies him. The distinction is important because the scene could easily have been read as a moment of anxiety — a blind man interrupting a great conversation, nervous about his reception. That is not what the verse describes. It describes a settled disposition, one that predates the arrival and will outlast it, and it is offered as the reason his request had priority.

**Awe Carries Knowledge Inside It**

*Khashyah* is not the generic word for fear. Arabic has *khawf* for the fear of a danger, and *khashyah* for the fear that comes from knowing the greatness of the one feared — reverence with recognition in it. The Qur'an states the relation explicitly: *“Of all of Allah's servants, only the knowledgeable ˹of His might˺ are ˹truly˺ in awe of Him”* (35:28). On that definition, awe is an achievement of understanding rather than a temperament, and the blind man's condition is the fruit of something he had learned. This raises the estimate of the whole scene. The man who came asking was not a simpleton needing elementary instruction; he was someone in whom knowledge had already produced reverence, and his request was for more of what had produced it. The one who deemed himself beyond need had knowledge of the world and no reverence in it, which is the precise inversion the sūrah is drawing.

**What He Stands in Awe Of**

The object of the verb is unspoken, and the omission widens it. The commentators supply God and the Day of Judgement, and both are supplied from the Qur'an's general usage rather than from this verse. *Khashyah* when directed at God means the recognition that one is seen, accountable, and dependent; when directed at the Hour it means the recognition that the present arrangement of things will end. The two are not separable in practice, since the accountability that makes awe rational is exercised on that Day. The unspoken object also allows the verse to be read as describing a man whose whole orientation is towards what he cannot see. He had no sight at all, and the awe ascribed to him is the awe proper to a person who lives by what is unseen. The correspondence between his physical condition and his spiritual one is not stated, but the sūrah is built on such correspondences and this is the central one.

**The Third Qualification in a List of Three**

Verses 8 and 9 together describe the claimant: he came to you striving, and he stands in awe. Arrival supplies presence, striving supplies effort, awe supplies orientation. The three are cumulative and none of them is a credential in Meccan terms. A man could arrive without striving — the notable was already seated. He could strive without awe — the ambitious strive constantly. He could feel awe privately and never come. The combination is what makes the claim, and the combination is available to anyone. Nothing in it requires sight, wealth, lineage, or eloquence, and nothing in it can be purchased. The list is the sūrah's answer to the allocation problem raised by the scene: when a teacher must decide where attention goes, the criteria are these, and they are legible in a single encounter with a person who walks up and asks.

**Self-Sufficiency and Awe Cannot Occupy One Heart**

The counterpart to this verse is the figure introduced two verses earlier. *Istighnā* is the conviction that one needs nothing, and it is the direct negation of *khashyah*, which is the conviction that one is held by Someone. The Qur'an ties the conviction of sufficiency to transgression in a single sentence: *“Most certainly, one exceeds all bounds once they think they are selfsufficient”* (96:6–7). The mechanism is clear enough. A person who believes he is complete has no reason to attend to a warning, and a warning he does not attend to cannot correct him. The blind man's awe was therefore not merely a private virtue; it was the condition that made teaching him worthwhile in the only sense the sūrah recognizes. The chief's self-sufficiency was the condition that made teaching him, at that moment, unlikely to land. The Prophet attended to the one and overlooked the other, and the verses reverse the judgement he had made.

**The Ones the Warner Is Sent To**

Makkan revelation keeps narrowing the address to those who stand in awe. *“Your duty is only to warn whoever is in awe of it”* (79:45) — the verse immediately preceding this sūrah in the received order — defines the audience of warning by the presence of *khashyah* for the Hour. The sūrah that follows it dramatizes the definition in a single afternoon. A man in awe came and was overlooked; a man beyond awe was seated and was attended. The two chapters read as a pair, and the pairing is one reason the early commentators connected them: the closing instruction of the one is the opening incident of the other. What the incident adds to the instruction is the practical difficulty. It is easy to say that warning belongs to the reverent; it is hard to identify the reverent in a room, since reverence is invisible and importance is not. The blind man's awe could be known only by his coming, and the coming is why the sūrah treats arrival as evidence.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:10

> you were inattentive to him.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word That Means Being Held by Something Else**

*Talahhā* is from *l-h-w*, the root of diversion, amusement, and the absorption that makes a person forget what matters. The fifth form means to be occupied or diverted by a thing, and the thing doing the occupying is not necessarily frivolous: a serious conversation can hold a man as completely as a game. The translation's "inattentive" is accurate but flattens the word, since *talahhā* implies a positive engagement elsewhere rather than a mere absence of attention. The Prophet was not bored or wandering. He was absorbed — absorbed in a matter he judged important, and the absorption is what removed the blind man from view. The choice of this root rather than a plain verb of turning away is deliberate. Turning away is a decision about a person; being absorbed is a condition produced by a thing, and the condition is more dangerous because the person who is in it does not notice what he is doing.

**The Mirror Image of Verse Six**

The two verses are built on the same frame with the prepositions reversed: *fa-anta lahu taṣaddā* — you attend to him; *fa-anta ʿanhu talahhā* — you are diverted from him. The explicit pronoun, the fronted prepositional phrase, the verb last: identical structure, opposite direction. Arabic rhetoric calls this the pairing of contrast, and here it functions as a balance sheet. One man receives the orientation, the other receives the diversion, and the identical syntax forces the reader to see the two acts as a single transaction. Nothing was created or destroyed; attention was moved. That is the whole content of the offence, and the mirrored grammar makes it impossible to describe the scene as two unrelated facts — an important conversation and an unlucky interruption. It was one allocation with two sides, and both sides are recorded in the same metrical shape.

**Absorption Is the Qur'an's Diagnosis of This Life**

The root *lahw* carries a heavy theological load elsewhere. *“Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children”* (57:20) lists diversion first among the constituents of worldly existence. *“This worldly life is no more than play and amusement. But the Hereafter is indeed the real life, if only they knew”* (29:64) makes the same claim in shorter form. The word therefore belongs to a vocabulary of misplacement: the thing that absorbs a person is not the thing that deserves him. Applied to this scene, the vocabulary produces an uncomfortable reading. The conversation with the notable was a serious matter by every worldly measure, and it functioned here as *lahw* — the absorbing thing that displaced the real. The sūrah does not call the conversation worthless; it calls the absorption misdirected, which is a subtler and more transferable charge.

**Inattention Rather Than Refusal**

No request was denied. The blind man was not told to leave, not rebuked, not pushed aside, not asked to return later. As far as anyone present could tell, nothing happened to him at all. He stood in a room where a conversation continued and eventually went away, presumably still holding his question. That is why the charge is stated in the language of attention rather than the language of injustice: *you were inattentive to him*. The sūrah is legislating about a category that human courts do not reach. There is no plaintiff who can prove a loss, no witness who saw an act, and no law in any community that punishes a teacher for being absorbed. Yet the verse treats the omission as a matter for correction from heaven, which establishes that the moral register of the Qur'an includes the disposition of attention and not only the record of deeds.

**The Rebuke Ends Here**

Verse 10 closes the case. There is no further mention of the blind man, no verdict on the notable, no instruction about what should have been said instead, and no continuation of the second-person address for another six verses. The next verse turns abruptly to the message itself with a particle of refusal, and the sūrah never returns to the room. The economy is striking: ten verses for a frown, and then the incident is left behind as though it had been fully settled. It had. The correction was complete in the describing of it, and the sūrah moves on to what the incident revealed about the revelation — that it is a reminder, written on honoured pages, carried by noble hands. The structure treats the incident as a doorway rather than a subject, and the doorway is closed in ten verses without a single verse of consolation for the one who was corrected.

**Correction With No Sanction Attached**

Makkan revelation threatens its opponents constantly: destruction, the Hour, the Fire, the day when a man flees his brother. Not one word of threat appears here. The Prophet is corrected in the sharpest available terms — third person, then second person, with the verb of diversion — and then the matter drops. No punishment, no expiation, no instruction to apologize, no statement that a sin has been forgiven. The asymmetry between the severity of the description and the absence of any sanction tells the reader how to classify the fault. This was not a transgression requiring penalty; it was a lapse in the better course, corrected because the standard applied to the bearer of revelation admits no lapses in the treatment of seekers. The community preserved the correction precisely because it carried no penalty, and a reader who meets the passage in prayer meets both facts at once: the Messenger was reproved, and nothing was withheld from him afterwards.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:11

> But no! This ˹revelation˺ is truly a reminder.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Particle That Refuses a Whole Train of Thought**

*Kallā* is one of the Qur'an's most emphatic particles, and its function is to reject what has just been said or thought — "no indeed," "far from it," "certainly not." It appears only in the Makkan revelations, never in the Madinan ones, and it is almost always followed by a positive statement that replaces what it refused. Here the refusal is directed at the reasoning the previous ten verses exposed: the calculation that attention should follow importance. *Kallā* does not argue with the calculation. It declines it and starts again. The particle gives the sūrah its hinge. Everything before it is a particular event in a particular room; everything after it is a description of the revelation and of human beings. The transition is made in one word, and the word is a refusal rather than a conjunction, which tells the reader that the new material is not a continuation of the old argument but a correction of the frame in which the old argument was conducted.

**What "This" Refers To**

The commentators offer three candidates for the referent of the reminder. The first is the Qur'an as a whole, which is the most common reading and the one supported by the parallel formulae in other Makkan chapters. The second is this sūrah itself, whose opening has just delivered a correction that functions as a reminder to every teacher who hears it. The third is the exhortation to treat people equitably — the lesson of the incident rather than the book containing it. All three are defensible, and the choice among them changes the emphasis without changing the sense. The strongest ground for the first reading is the description that follows in verses 13 to 16, where the reminder is said to be written on honoured pages carried by angel-scribes; that description fits a book rather than a moral. The second and third readings remain available because the sūrah is itself the reminder it describes, and the incident is the proof.

**The Qur'an's Own Name for Itself**

*Dhikr* is the term the Book prefers for itself in the Makkan period. *“Enough! Surely this ˹Quran˺ is a reminder”* (74:54) is the nearest formal parallel and belongs to the same phase. *“And this ˹Quran˺ is a blessed reminder which We have revealed. Will you ˹pagans˺ then deny it?”* (21:50) adds the blessing and the challenge. *“This ˹Book˺ is only a Reminder and a clear Quran”* (36:69) separates the Qur'an from poetry in the same breath. The choice of *dhikr* is not modest. A reminder is addressed to someone who already knows and has forgotten, which means the Qur'an presents itself not as news but as recovery. That self-description governs the scene it appears in: the blind man needed no new doctrine, and the notable was not being offered a proposition he had never heard. Both were being reminded, and the difference between them was that one of them wanted to remember.

**A Case Becomes a Doctrine**

The incident could have ended at verse 10 and left behind only a rule of manners: do not overlook the poor when the rich are present. The particle *kallā* refuses to leave it there. What follows generalizes in two directions at once. The reminder is described as a written, honoured, angelic thing — which places the frown against the background of the entire celestial apparatus of revelation — and then human beings are described as ungrateful, created from a drop, fed by rain, and facing a blast at which they flee their families. Within twenty verses the room in Mecca has disappeared and the whole of the human condition has replaced it. The method is characteristic of the short Makkan sūrahs: a small occasion is used to open onto the largest subjects, and the occasion is not repeated once the opening has been made. The frown is never mentioned again in the Qur'an, and the doctrine it produced is recited.

**A Reminder Presumes a Free Receiver**

The word *dhikr* carries a structural consequence, and the next verse states it: whoever wills, let him be mindful of it. A reminder cannot be forced, because forcing produces compliance rather than recollection. The Qur'an is explicit about the limit this places on the Messenger: *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺”* (88:21–22), and *“Had your Lord so willed ˹O Prophet˺, all ˹people˺ on earth would have certainly believed, every single one of them! Would you then force people to become believers?”* (10:99). This is why the scene's calculation was doubly mistaken. The Prophet was trying to secure an outcome that no delivery can secure, and he was paying for the attempt with the one thing he did control. The nature of a reminder fixes both the teacher's duty and the teacher's incapacity, and verse 11 states the nature before verse 12 states the freedom that follows from it.

**From the Prophet to the People**

The address changes at this verse and stays changed. Verses 1 to 10 concerned a Messenger and two men; verse 11 concerns the revelation; verse 17 concerns human beings in general, condemned for ingratitude. The sūrah has moved out of the particular and into the universal, and the movement is signalled by the disappearance of the second person. Nothing after verse 10 speaks to the Prophet directly about the incident. This relocation is what makes the chapter useful to a reader who was not in the room. The correction was delivered to a specific person about a specific afternoon, and it is preserved in a form that addresses everyone who teaches, everyone who is taught, and everyone who has ever preferred a better-connected listener. The particle of refusal is the door through which the sūrah passes from history into doctrine, and it closes behind it without commentary.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:12

> So let whoever wills be mindful of it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Invitation With No Pressure Behind It**

*Fa-man shāʾa dhakarah* — so whoever wills, let him remember it. The sentence is a command in form and a permission in substance, and the tension is the point. The particle *fa-* ties it to the previous verse: this is a reminder, therefore whoever wills may take it up. Nothing compels the taking, and nothing forbids the refusal. The verse places the decision in the hands of the hearer with a completeness that leaves no room for the teacher to intervene, which is precisely the correction the preceding verses delivered. The Prophet had attempted to influence the decision by choosing his audience; this verse removes the audience from his control entirely and returns it to itself. What remains for him is the delivery, and delivery is indiscriminate. The sentence reads like an invitation because that is what a reminder can only ever be.

**The Same Sentence in the Clothed One**

The formula is not unique to this sūrah. Sūrat al-Muddaththir carries the identical verse: *“So let whoever wills be mindful of it”* (74:55), following the same declaration that the Qur'an is a reminder in 74:54. The two passages are separated by years of revelation and arrive at the same wording, which marks the formula as a fixed piece of Makkan address. Al-Muddaththir then adds what this sūrah leaves implicit: *“But they cannot do so unless Allah wills. He ˹alone˺ is worthy to be feared and entitled to forgive”* (74:56). The addition closes the circle that human freedom opens, and its absence here is significant. Sūrat ʿAbasa stops at the freedom of the hearer, because the subject of these ten verses has been the teacher's mistaken attempt to manage outcomes. To add the divine will at this point would reintroduce a calculation, and the passage has just dismantled the calculation.

**Two Readings of Whoever Wills**

The commentators note that *man shāʾa* can be taken in two ways. On the obvious reading the willing is the hearer's: any person who chooses may take the reminder. On the second reading, reported from al-Qurṭubī's sources, the willing is God's — whoever God wills is the one who remembers — and the sentence describes an outcome rather than issuing an invitation. The first reading fits the immediate context better, since the verse is the consequence of the Qur'an being a reminder and the answer to the teacher's anxiety about results. The second reading is not excluded by it; it simply locates the same event at a different level. Both can be held at once without contradiction: the hearer wills, and the will that succeeds is one God has willed. What the passage requires is only the first half, because the first half is what removes the decision from the teacher's hands.

**A Masculine Pronoun Where the Antecedent Was Feminine**

There is a grammatical detail the exegetes pressed. The reminder in verse 11 is *dhikrā* or *tadhkirah*, feminine in form, while the pronoun in verse 12 — *dhakarah*, "he remembered it" — is masculine. Some therefore read the object as God rather than the revelation: whoever wills, let him remember Him. The reading is supported by the frequent Qur'anic pairing of reminder and the remembrance of God, and it makes the verse an instruction about devotion rather than about a text. Others hold that the masculine pronoun refers back to a masculine noun understood in verse 11, such as the Qur'an itself (*al-Qurʾān*), which resolves the mismatch without changing the sense. The second solution is grammatically simpler and is the one most readers follow. The first remains worth stating because it expands what the verse asks: not merely attention to a book, but recollection of the One the book is about.

**Freedom Is the Condition of Remembrance**

The Qur'an insists on the absence of coercion wherever it speaks of belief. *“Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood”* (2:256) states the principle in the Madinan period, and the Makkan passages state its consequence for the Messenger: *“And say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve’”* (18:29). The threat attached there belongs to the outcome, not to the choice; the choice is left standing in its full weight. Sūrat al-Insān makes the same move in two verses: *“Surely this is a reminder. So let whoever wills take the ˹Right˺ Way to their Lord. But you cannot will ˹to do so˺ unless Allah wills”* (76:29–30). The pattern is consistent: freedom is asserted first, and the divine will is named second, never as a cancellation of the first but as its ground.

**What the Verse Removes From the Teacher**

Read against verse 7, this one completes an argument that runs through the whole opening. You are not to blame if he is not purified; whoever wills, let him be mindful. Both statements take the outcome out of the teacher's hands, and the second does it more thoroughly, because it does not merely release him from liability — it locates the decision in a faculty he cannot touch. The will of a hearer is not reachable by the kind of pressure the scene imagined, and it is certainly not reachable by the allocation of attention. The practical instruction follows without being stated: deliver to everyone, prefer whoever asks, and stop auditing the results. The audit was what produced the frown. A teacher who has taken this verse to heart has no reason to look at an audience and estimate which member deserves more of his face, since the estimate was never available to him in the first place. What he can see is who came, who strove, and who stands in awe, and those three marks are enough to decide where to turn. The rest belongs to a will that operates on both sides of the exchange and is disclosed to neither.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:13

> It is ˹written˺ on pages held in honour—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sheets Rather Than a Bound Book**

*Ṣuḥuf* is the plural of *ṣaḥīfah*, a sheet or a scroll — a written surface, not a codex. The Qur'an uses the word of earlier revelations with a specificity that suggests deliberate choice: *“Or has he not been informed of what is in the Scripture of Moses”* (53:36), and *“This is certainly ˹mentioned˺ in the earlier Scriptures—the Scriptures of Abraham and Moses”* (87:18–19). Sūrat al-Bayyinah describes the Messenger as *“reciting scrolls of ˹utmost˺ purity”* (98:2). The image is of loose sheets held together by their content rather than by a binding, and it carries an association with antiquity and with the earliest form in which a revelation exists. Applied here, the word elevates the Qur'an into the company of the revelations that preceded it and locates it at the level of the original document rather than the circulating copy. The honour described in the same verse belongs to that level.

**Which Pages Are Meant**

Three identifications are offered. The pages may be those of the Qur'an itself, which is the plainest reading and the one most commentators adopt. They may be the books of all the prophets, so that the reminder of verse 11 is placed inside a single tradition of written revelation. Or they may be the Preserved Tablet, the heavenly original from which every revelation is taken down — *“In fact, this is a glorious Quran, ˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet”* (85:21–22). The third reading has the strongest textual support in the verses that follow, since a book carried by angel-scribes and untouched except by the purified fits a heavenly register better than an earthly manuscript. The first reading is not displaced by it: the same passage in Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah describes the Qur'an in the terms usually reserved for the Tablet. The disagreement is best resolved by holding that the Qur'an is in the Tablet and comes from it, which is what both readings assert from different ends.

**Honour Attaches to the Content**

*Mukramah* — honoured, ennobled — is a passive participle, so the pages have been honoured by someone other than themselves. Nothing in the verse specifies who honours them, and the omission points to God. The honour is therefore not a reputation the book acquired among its readers but a rank conferred on it, and it does not depend on reception. A book honoured by God remains honoured when it is dismissed, mocked, or ignored, and the Meccan audience that heard this verse was doing all three. The statement is a rebuttal in the form of a description. Against the charge that this is poetry, or madness, or a fabrication, the verse says that the sheets carrying it hold a rank no human opinion can alter. The same logic appears in the description of the Qur'an as noble and well-preserved in Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah (56:77–78), where the adjectives are likewise independent of what people make of them.

**The Insistence That This Speech Is Written**

Makkan revelation repeatedly describes a spoken thing as a written one, and the repetition is a theological claim. The Qur'an is recited aloud, memorized, and delivered by a Messenger who did not read; yet the text insists on pages, records, tablets, pens, and scribes. *“that this is truly a noble Quran, in a well-preserved Record”* (56:77–78). *“In fact, this is a glorious Quran, ˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet”* (85:21–22). The insistence answers an implicit objection: a speech that exists only in a man's mouth could be altered, forgotten, or invented. A speech that also exists in a written heavenly register cannot. The written form is the guarantee of the spoken one, and the guarantee is offered in the very sūrahs where the speaking was most contested. This verse is part of that pattern, and it places the reminder of verse 11 inside the guaranteed order rather than the improvised one.

**Honour in the Qur'an's Scale**

The root *k-r-m* does its work on human beings too. *“Indeed, We have dignified the children of Adam, carried them on land and sea, granted them good and lawful provisions, and privileged them far above many of Our creatures”* (17:70). Honour in the Qur'an is thus a conferred rank, available to persons and to pages, and in both cases unearned by the recipient. The parallel is instructive for this sūrah. The man who was overlooked possessed no worldly honour and held a rank conferred by God; the pages described here hold the same kind of rank. Mecca measured honour by lineage and property, and the sūrah has already overturned that measure in its first ten verses. Verse 13 overturns it again in the register of revelation: the honour of a book is not its reception. A text mocked by chiefs can be honoured in heaven, and the honour is not diminished by the mockery — which is the same consolation the passage offers to the man nobody thought worth facing.

**The Answer to the Charges Being Made**

The Meccan objections to the Prophet were specific and are recorded in the Qur'an itself: fortune-teller, madman, poet. *“So ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺. For you, by the grace of your Lord, are not a fortune-teller or a madman. Or do they say, ‘˹He is˺ a poet, for whom we ˹eagerly˺ await an ill-fate!’?”* (52:29–30). Each charge implies the same thing: the speech is generated locally, by a person, out of his own resources. Verse 13 answers by relocating the speech. It is on honoured pages, which means it existed before the recitation and elsewhere than in the reciter. The answer is not an argument but a description, and that is characteristic of this style of revelation: the objection is not refuted point by point, it is placed inside a larger picture in which it no longer fits. A poet composes; a fortune-teller guesses; neither writes on pages held in honour by God and carried by angels.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:14

> highly esteemed, purified—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Raised in Rank**

*Marfūʿah* is the passive participle of *rafaʿa*, to raise, and it describes something elevated in position or rank. Applied to pages it means two things at once: exalted in the estimation of God, and lifted above the defects that afflict other books. The commentators specify the second sense as freedom from obscurity and contradiction, which is a strong claim about a text of this length delivered over twenty-three years in fragments. Nothing in the Qur'an is more confident about its own consistency than these two adjectives. The elevation is also spatial in the imagery of the passage: the pages are not on earth, they are not in a market, they are not in the hands of whoever pays for them. They are raised, and the raising separates them from the traffic of human documents. The same verb is used elsewhere for the raising of ranks and of buildings, and in each case it denotes a distinction conferred rather than achieved.

**Free From Addition and Loss**

*Muṭahharah* — purified — carries the sense the exegetes specify: free from any addition or deficiency. Purity here is textual integrity, and the word choice is exact, because the root *ṭ-h-r* denotes the removal of what does not belong rather than the presence of what does. A purified thing is one from which contamination has been excluded. Applied to a book, the claim is that nothing has entered it that is not of it and nothing of it has been lost. This is a claim about preservation as much as about content, and it explains why the same adjective governs the juristic discussion of touching the Qur'an: a book declared purified is treated by the community as requiring purity in those who handle it. The description is therefore doing double work — an assertion about the heavenly original and a norm for the earthly copy.

**The Verse That Governs the Discussion**

Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah states the same idea in a form that has generated centuries of jurisprudence: *“that this is truly a noble Quran, in a well-preserved Record, touched by none except the purified ˹angels˺”* (56:77–79). The identification of the purified as angels is given in the translation and is the majority exegetical reading, since the record in question is the heavenly one. From that reading the jurists drew an inference by analogy: if the heavenly original is touched only by the pure, the earthly copy should be handled only in a state of ritual purity. The inference is not stated in the text, and a minority of scholars have resisted it on the ground that the verse describes angels rather than prescribing for human beings. Both sides agree on the description; they differ on whether a norm follows from it. The stronger position, in practice and in the settled tradition of the schools, is that the norm does follow, because the Qur'an's descriptions of its own sanctity have consistently been read as instructions.

**Two Kinds of Touching**

The purity at issue can be physical or moral, and the passage allows both. Physically, *ṭahārah* is the state produced by ablution, and the community has regulated contact with the written Qur'an by that state from the earliest generations. Morally, purity is the condition of a heart free from hypocrisy and disbelief, and on this reading the verse says that the meaning of the Book is not reached by the impure in that sense — a claim echoed in the Qur'an's repeated statement that its signs are not grasped by those who refuse them. The two readings are not rivals; they operate at different levels of the same act. A person may handle the physical book in a state of ablution and remain untouched by its meaning, and the tradition has always recognized the second deficiency as the graver one. This verse's placement beside the reminder of verse 11 supports that priority, since a reminder that is not received has not been received however clean the hands.

**Purity as an Answer to a Charge**

The Meccan accusation against the Prophet included forgery: that he was composing or adapting, that he was taught by a human being, that he could produce something else if asked. The claim that these pages are purified answers all three at the root. A text into which nothing foreign has entered cannot have been composed by borrowing, and a text from which nothing has been lost cannot have been tampered with. The answer is stated as description rather than as argument, and its force lies in that: the verse does not contest the charge on the accuser's ground, it describes a condition the charge cannot reach. The same method appears in the insistence on the Preserved Tablet in Sūrat al-Burūj (85:22), where preservation is asserted rather than demonstrated. The Qur'an's self-description functions as a standing rebuttal, and the rebuttal has been recited in every generation since, including by readers who had no idea what was being answered.

**Rank and Integrity Together**

The two adjectives are not redundant, and their order matters. Rank without integrity would be an honour conferred on a defective thing; integrity without rank would be a flawless document of no particular standing. The verse asserts both, and the assertion covers the two things a book needs in order to be trusted and obeyed. State plainly what this means for the reader of the sūrah: the passage that began with a frown ends by describing the revelation as honoured, raised, purified, and carried by noble hands. The disproportion between the two subjects is the argument. A man overlooked a blind stranger for an afternoon, and the correction arrived in a text whose description claims a rank beyond every human document. The scale of the response is a measure of the value the Qur'an places on the person who came asking, and the value is stated not in words about him but in words about the book that spoke for him.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:15

> by the hands of angel-scribes,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word With Three Possible Roots**

*Safarah* is the plural of *sāfir*, and the commentators trace it to three related meanings, each supported by usage. The first is writing: *safara* gives *asfār*, books, and the scribes are those who inscribe. The second is mediation: *sufarāʾ* are envoys who carry a message between two parties, and the angels are the envoys between God and His Messengers. The third is unveiling, since *safara* also means to make plain or to remove a covering, and the scribes are those through whom the revelation is made clear. All three fit the context, and the Qur'anic use of the root elsewhere supports the first and third: the recording angels of 50:17–18 inscribe deeds, and the description of the Qur'an as a reminder implies making plain. The ambiguity is productive rather than defective, because the office described combines all three functions — writing, carrying, and disclosing — and no single English word covers them.

**The Ḥadīth That Fixed the Meaning for Muslims**

One report has done more to shape how this verse is read than any commentary. From ʿĀʾishah, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4937 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 798, with the authenticity agreed upon by both compilers: the one who is proficient in the Qur'an and recites it well will be with the noble and righteous scribes, and the one who recites it with difficulty, stammering and finding it hard, will have two rewards. The ḥadīth quotes these two verses by describing them rather than citing them, and it does three things at once. It identifies the scribes as angels. It places the reciter in their company, which is an astonishing rank for a human being. And it removes the rank from proficiency alone, since the struggling reciter is promised a double reward rather than exclusion. The second half has been the consolation of generations of non-Arab Muslims learning to read the text, and it is attached directly to this verse in the received tradition.

**By the Hands**

*Bi-aydī* — by the hands of. The plural of *yad* here denotes instrumentality with the added sense of direct contact: the pages are not merely near the angels or under their supervision, they are in their hands. The expression gives the transmission a physicality that the rest of the passage has been building towards — pages, honour, purity, and now hands. It also answers an objection current in Mecca, which was that the revelation reached the Prophet through no visible channel. The verse describes the channel and locates it beyond sight. Something carried by hands is passed from one holder to another, and the Qur'an's own account of its descent uses the same imagery: a trustworthy spirit bringing it down to the heart of the Messenger. The hands in this verse belong to the carriers at the level above, and their mention establishes that the reminder is not a private experience but a transmitted document with a chain.

**Which Angels Are Meant**

Two identifications compete. The first takes the scribes as the angels appointed to record human deeds, on the ground that the same root is used of writing and that the Qur'an describes such angels explicitly: *“while you are certainly observed by vigilant, honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺”* (82:10–11) and *“As the two recording-angels— ˹one˺ sitting to the right, and ˹the other to˺ the left—note ˹everything˺”* (50:17). The second takes them as the mediators of revelation, with Gabriel at their head, since the context is the transmission of a book rather than the auditing of a life. The second reading fits the surrounding verses better: the subject from verse 11 onward is the reminder and its written form, not the deeds of human beings. The first reading survives because the word is the same and because the association was already fixed in Muslim usage by the ḥadīth, which speaks of the scribes without specifying their assignment.

**Human Hands in the Same Chain**

Al-Ṭabarī records a reading that brings the verse down to earth: the hands of scribes may refer to the reciters of the Qur'an, the human beings who carry it in memory and on their tongues. The reading is not the plain sense, but it is not arbitrary, because the ḥadīth of Bukhārī and Muslim already places the proficient reciter in the company of the noble scribes. Once that company is established, the human carrier of the text participates in the office the verse describes. This is how the tradition has read it in practice: the honour of the pages extends to the hands that hold them, and the memorizer of the Qur'an occupies a rank the verse reserves for angels. Nothing in the sūrah's immediate argument requires the reading, and it should not be pressed as exegesis. As a description of what Muslims believed about their own reciters, it is accurate and it is old.

**Company as a Standard Rather Than a Reward**

The ḥadīth's preposition deserves attention: the proficient reciter is *with* the noble scribes. Association, not employment. Being with a company implies resemblance to it, and the two adjectives that follow in verse 16 — noble and virtuous — supply the terms of the resemblance. The reward described is therefore not only a place but a likeness: the reciter becomes like the carriers in the qualities that made them fit to carry. This reading gives the verse its practical edge. Recitation is not a performance measured by fluency; it is an assimilation to a company whose defining features are honour and righteousness. The struggling reciter who receives two rewards is inside the same movement, since the effort that produces the struggle is itself the mark of the aspiration. The verse describes angels, and the tradition that reads it has always understood it as describing a standard for human beings.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:16

> honourable and virtuous.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Adjectives, Two Directions**

*Kirām* is the plural of *karīm*, noble, generous, honoured — a quality of standing. *Bararah* is the plural of *barr*, one who is righteous, dutiful, devout — a quality of conduct. The pairing covers both dimensions of excellence in the Arabic moral vocabulary: what a being is held to be, and what a being does. Neither adjective alone would suffice. Nobility without righteousness is rank without content, and the Qur'an is careful to refuse any claim to standing that is not earned by conduct. Righteousness without nobility would leave the carriers as obedient servants of no particular dignity, and the passage has just described the pages they carry as honoured. The two adjectives match the two adjectives of the previous verse: the pages are honoured and purified, and the hands that carry them are noble and righteous. The correspondence is exact, and it establishes that the purity of the book is guaranteed by the character of its carriers.

**Birr in Qur'anic Usage**

The root *b-r-r* is one of the Qur'an's terms for active goodness, and its range is instructive. It describes filial devotion: John the Baptist was *“kind to his parents”* and *“neither arrogant nor disobedient”* (19:14), and Jesus says of himself, *“and to be kind to my mother. He has not made me arrogant or defiant”* (19:32). It describes the substance of righteousness itself: *“You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish”* (3:92), where *birr* is defined by a costly act rather than a state of mind. In each case the word denotes goodness that costs something and is directed towards another. Applied to angels, the same word carries the same sense: their righteousness is not a passive property but a constant expenditure of obedience. The choice of *bararah* over a more abstract term for purity is therefore meaningful, since it ties the carriers to the vocabulary of duty and devotion that human beings are also addressed in.

**The Same Pairing Elsewhere**

The Qur'an describes the recording angels in almost identical terms: *“while you are certainly observed by vigilant, honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺”* (82:10–11). The word *kirām* appears there with *kātibīn*, the writers, and the combination is close enough to this passage that the two have been read together since the earliest commentaries. Both describe a class of angels characterized by honour and by a function connected with writing, and both place them in a position of observation over human affairs. The parallel strengthens the identification of the scribes in verse 15 as a specific order of angels rather than a general reference, and it also transfers the moral weight: the beings who record human deeds are described as noble, which is a guarantee about the record. A dishonest scribe would produce a dishonest book, and the Qur'an forecloses the possibility by describing the character of the carriers before it invokes their testimony.

**Why Angels Need a Character Reference**

Angels in the Qur'an are defined by obedience: they *“never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded”* (66:6). Given that definition, the adjectives here might seem redundant — of course the carriers of revelation are noble. The redundancy is not idle. The passage is answering a human situation, and in that situation the character of the messengers matters to the reception of the message. Mecca had accused the Prophet of receiving his speech from a human teacher, from jinn, from poets of the past. The answer in these verses is a description of the actual carriers, and the description emphasizes precisely the qualities whose absence would make transmission unreliable: nobility, which excludes deception for gain, and righteousness, which excludes deviation from command. The character reference is an argument in the form of an adjective, and it is aimed at the same objection the previous verses addressed.

**The Qualities Transfer to the Reciter**

The ḥadīth of Bukhārī 4937 and Muslim 798 places the proficient reciter *with* the noble and righteous scribes, and the preposition makes the association the substance of the reward. What the reciter joins is a company defined by these two adjectives, and joining a company of that kind is not a matter of location. The tradition understood the promise as an assimilation: the one who carries the Book is expected to resemble its carriers. That expectation generates the whole ethical literature on the reciter's conduct — the prohibitions against reciting for payment, for display, or for argument, and the insistence that the Qur'an be carried with humility. None of that literature is derived from a legal text; it is derived from this verse by way of the ḥadīth. A person who recites for the applause of a room is, on the tradition's own account, in the wrong company, and the wrong company is the one described in the first ten verses of this sūrah.

**A Book Described by Its Carriers**

The six verses from 11 to 16 form a single movement: a reminder, freely received, written on honoured pages, raised and purified, in the hands of scribes, noble and righteous. The movement ends not on the book but on persons. That ending is the passage's final argument against the charge of fabrication, since a document is known by the integrity of those who carry it, and the carriers here are described in terms no human chain could match. It is also a quiet return to the subject with which the sūrah began. The room in Mecca contained two human beings, one of whom was overlooked. The heaven described in these verses contains beings of honour and righteousness whose entire function is to carry a message to people. Nobody in that order is overlooked, and the order is the standard by which the room was judged. The sūrah turns next from the book to its readers, and the turn is abrupt.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:17

> Condemned are ˹disbelieving˺ humans! How ungrateful they are ˹to Allah˺!

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Imprecation in the Passive Voice**

*Qutila al-insān* is literally "the human being was killed," and the form is an idiom of imprecation rather than a report of an event: may he be destroyed, may he perish, cursed is he. Arabic uses the passive of killing to express a wish for ruin without naming who brings it, which gives the phrase a weight that an active curse lacks — the destruction is presented as already decreed and merely awaited. The Qur'an deploys the same construction as a formal opening: *“Condemned are the liars—”* (51:10), *“Condemned are the makers of the ditch—”* (85:4), *“May the hands of Abu Lahab perish, and he ˹himself˺ perish!”* (111:1). In each case the imprecation introduces a judgement rather than a narrative, and the passive voice marks the judgement as settled. Here the formula is turned on the human being as such, immediately after six verses describing the revelation as honoured, raised, purified, and carried by noble hands.

**The Human Being as Such**

*Al-insān* with the definite article denotes the species or the type, not an individual, and that generality is the reason the verse lands as hard as it does. Some commentators attach it to a specific case: a man who had accepted Islam and then apostatized when Sūrat al-Najm was revealed, so that the condemnation would be for disbelieving after believing. The report has no secure chain, and the verse does not require it. Nothing in the wording restricts the subject, and the immediately following verses describe creation from a drop, death, burial, and resurrection — the biography of every human being rather than of one apostate. The general reading is therefore stronger, and it produces a harsher result: the condemnation is not addressed to a traitor in Mecca but to the human being considered as a creature who has been made, provided for, and killed and buried by the same hand, and who responds to all of it with ingratitude.

**One Word Carrying Two Meanings**

*Kufr* is the noun behind the verb in the second half of the verse, and it holds disbelief and ingratitude together in a way English cannot. The root means to cover, and both senses follow from that: to cover the truth is to disbelieve, and to cover a favour is to be ungrateful. The Qur'an uses the word in both senses constantly and often in a single context, so that a statement about disbelief is simultaneously a statement about ingratitude. That double reference is what makes this verse the hinge of the sūrah. The human being condemned here is not primarily a philosophical denier; he is a recipient who does not acknowledge receipt. The proof offered in the verses that follow is entirely made of favours — creation, easing, death, burial, resurrection, rain, grain, orchards — and the charge is that all of them have been covered over. The covering is the disbelief, and the disbelief is the covering.

**An Exclamation of Degree**

The second half, *mā akfarah*, is an exclamatory construction: how unbelieving he is, how great his ingratitude. The form is rare in the Qur'an and it does specific work, since exclamation expresses the speaker's assessment of magnitude rather than a bare statement of fact. The verse does not merely say that the human being is ungrateful; it registers astonishment at the degree. That astonishment is the emotional register of the whole passage, and it is directed at a creature whose entire existence has just been described as a sequence of gifts. The construction also removes any possibility of reading the condemnation as a judicial sentence pronounced on a particular crime. Nothing is specified — no idol, no lie, no persecution. The magnitude is in the disproportion between what was given and what was returned, and the exclamation names the disproportion without itemizing it.

**Why the Curse Follows the Description of the Book**

The order is deliberate. Verses 11 to 16 describe the revelation in the highest terms available: a reminder, on honoured pages, raised and purified, in the hands of noble and righteous scribes. Verse 17 answers with a condemnation of the human being. The juxtaposition measures the two sides of the exchange. On one side stands a document whose rank is guaranteed by heaven and whose carriers are described as beyond reproach; on the other stands a recipient who covers it over. Nothing in the verse blames the book, its carriers, or its delivery. The failure is located entirely in the reception, and the location is what makes the imprecation so severe. A message of that rank refused is not a difference of opinion; it is a covering of something whose worth has just been established in six verses. The sūrah builds the case for the value of the reminder before it pronounces on the ingratitude, so that the pronouncement rests on the description rather than on assertion.

**The Sūrah Changes Its Addressee**

Up to this point the passage has concerned the Prophet, the blind man, the notable, and the nature of the reminder. Verse 17 turns outward to humanity at large, and the turn is unannounced. No connective links it to what precedes, and the change of subject is total: from a room in Mecca to the human being as such. This is the characteristic movement of the short Makkan sūrahs, which use a particular occasion to open onto a universal subject and then leave the occasion behind without marking the departure. What the reader is left with is a sequence that will occupy the rest of the chapter: the human being condemned, his origin described, his death and burial recorded, his resurrection deferred to a will with no date, his food itemized, and his family scattered on the day of the Blast. The frown that began the sūrah is by now a small thing inside a very large picture, which is exactly the perspective the opening verses were meant to produce.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:18

> From what substance did He create them?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is Answered Immediately**

*Mimma khalaqah* — from what did He create him? The interrogative is rhetorical, and the answer arrives in the next verse without delay. The device is one of the Qur'an's most frequent argumentative forms: pose a question the hearer cannot answer, then supply the answer, and let the gap between the two establish the point. The gap here is not informational. Every Meccan knew what human beings are made of in the ordinary sense; nobody needed a revelation to tell him. What the question does is arrest the hearer and force him to consider the material rather than the man. The imprecation of the previous verse condemned ingratitude in general terms, and a general condemnation invites defence. The question removes the defence by narrowing attention to a single fact about the condemned party's own body, and it does so in the second movement of a sequence that will run through death, burial, and resurrection before reaching food.

**The Same Question Put Elsewhere**

The formula recurs with a consistency that marks it as a set argument. *“Let people then consider what they were created from!”* (86:5) is the nearest parallel, and it uses the same verb of consideration that appears in this sūrah's later verse about food. *“Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop, then— behold!—they openly challenge ˹Us˺?”* (36:77) adds the consequence of the origin: the dispute. *“Did We not create you from a humble fluid, placing it in a secure place”* (77:20–21) sets the origin against the security of the womb. Each passage makes the same move from the lowliness of the beginning to the arrogance of the present, and each treats the beginning as a matter of common knowledge that has been forgotten rather than as a discovery. The argument's force depends on that: it is not new information but recovered information, which is what a reminder is.

**The Humiliation of Origin**

The method is to deflate pride by naming the material. Nothing in the verse insults the human being; the origin is stated as a fact and the fact is left to do the work. A person who considers himself beyond need — the figure introduced twelve verses earlier — is asked to look at what he was made from, and the looking is incompatible with the self-assessment. The Qur'an uses this method on wealth, on strength, on lineage, and on knowledge, but the argument from origin is the most direct, because it cannot be answered by achievement. Whatever a man becomes, he began as a drop, and the beginning was not his doing. The sūrah's own occasion reinforces the point: the man who was overlooked in Mecca and the man who was attended to had the same origin, and the difference between them was invisible at that origin and remains invisible at the grave.

**An Argument About Power, Not Biology**

The purpose of naming the drop is not embryology but demonstration, and the demonstration is directed at resurrection. If a being was produced from a fluid with no shape and no capacity, then producing him again from dust and bone is not a greater task. The Qur'an states the inference explicitly: *“And they argue with Us— forgetting they were created—saying, ‘Who will give life to decayed bones?’ Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being’”* (36:78–79). The same inference closes Sūrat al-Qiyāmah: *“Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life?”* (75:40). Origin is thus an argument from the lesser to the greater, and the verse that raises the question is the first step of that argument. The human being who denies the second creation is described as having forgotten the first, and the forgetting is the ingratitude named in the previous verse.

**Who Is Being Asked**

The question is addressed to the condemned human being of verse 17, and through him to every reader. Within the sūrah it also answers the figure of verse 5, the one who deemed himself beyond need. Self-sufficiency is a claim about one's own resources, and the question about origin is the precise refutation of such a claim: the resources were given, in a form the recipient could not have chosen or produced. Nothing in the verse names the notable or refers back to the incident, and the reference works without naming. A man who believes he needs nothing is a man who has not examined what he was made of, and the examination is offered here as a single question with a single answer. The sūrah does not argue with the self-sufficient; it points at his own beginning and lets the pointing stand.

**Why the Question Comes After the Curse**

The sequence is condemnation, then origin, and the order is not interchangeable. Reversing it would produce a different argument: a demonstration followed by a verdict, which is how legal reasoning runs. As it stands, the verdict comes first and the demonstration follows, and the effect is to make the demonstration a disclosure rather than a proof. The human being is condemned for ingratitude, and then he is shown what he has been ungrateful for, beginning with his own existence. The method assumes that the reader will recognize himself in the condemnation, and the recognition is what makes the subsequent facts land. A proof offered to someone who does not see himself as accused produces assent without change. This verse is offered to someone who has just been cursed, and the curse is what makes the question worth answering.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:19

> He created them from a sperm-drop, and ordained their development.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Drop**

*Nuṭfah* is a small quantity of fluid, and in Qur'anic usage it denotes the seminal drop from which a human being begins. The word appears in a set of passages that describe the same origin: *“Were they not ˹once˺ a sperm-drop emitted?”* (75:37), *“˹For˹ indeed, We ˹alone˺ created humans from a drop of mixed fluids, ˹in order˺ to test them, so We made them hear and see”* (76:2), *“Did We not create you from a humble fluid”* (77:20). The consistency is remarkable given that the passages belong to different sūrahs and different moments of revelation, and it suggests a deliberate vocabulary rather than incidental description. The emphasis is on the quantity and the lowliness: a drop, emitted, humble, mixed. Nothing about it predicts a person. The Qur'an's use of the term is not an attempt at biology; it is a way of stating that the beginning of a human being contains no trace of what a human being becomes, so that everything in between must be accounted for by something other than the material.

**Measured Before Made**

The second verb is *qaddarahū*, from the root *q-d-r*, which carries measurement, apportionment, and decree in a single family of meanings. The same root gives *qadar*, destiny, and *qudrah*, power. To say that God *qaddara* the human being is to say that he was measured out, apportioned, and determined — that a specification preceded the making. The Qur'an uses the verb in this sense as a divine attribute: *“Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High, Who created and ˹perfectly˺ fashioned ˹all˺, and Who ordained precisely and inspired accordingly”* (87:1–3), and *“Who has perfected everything He created. And He originated the creation of humankind from clay”* (32:7). The pairing of creation with ordainment is standard, and it excludes the possibility that the development of a human being is a sequence of accidents. The drop does not become a person by luck. It becomes a person according to a measure that was set, and the setting of the measure is itself an act of the same power that made the material.

**The Stages Listed Elsewhere**

The ordainment named here is itemized in other passages, and the itemization shows what the verb covers. *“And indeed, We created humankind from an extract of clay, then placed each ˹human˺ as a sperm-drop in a secure place, then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation. So Blessed is Allah, the Best of Creators”* (23:12–14). The sequence moves from fluid to clot to tissue to skeleton to a person, and each stage is described as a making rather than an emergence. Sūrat al-Ḥajj gives the same stages in the service of an argument about resurrection, and adds that the term in the womb is appointed (22:5). This verse compresses all of that into one word: He ordained it. The compression is characteristic of the short Makkan sūrahs, which state in a clause what the longer ones unfold in a paragraph.

**Ordainment Includes a Term**

The measure set for a human being includes its duration. *“˹Then˺ We settle whatever ˹embryo˺ We will in the womb for an appointed term”* (22:5) ties the ordainment to a fixed period, and the same idea governs the whole of Qur'anic teaching on lifespan: no soul dies except by God's leave at a written term (3:145). The inclusion of time in the measure is what makes the following verses of this sūrah coherent — death, burial, and a resurrection deferred to a will. The human being described here is not merely shaped but scheduled, and the schedule is not his to alter. That fact is the ground of the ingratitude charged in verse 17, since a being whose material, form, and term are all given has nothing of his own with which to claim self-sufficiency. The ordainment is therefore not a neutral statement about development; it is the second half of the refutation begun by the question about origin.

**From a Drop to a Disputant**

The Qur'an keeps returning to the disproportion between the two ends of this process. *“Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop, then— behold!—they openly challenge ˹Us˺?”* (36:77). The particle rendered "behold" marks the suddenness, and the suddenness is the point: the same being who began as an emitted drop stands up and argues with the One who made him. Nothing in the drop predicted the argument, and nothing in the argument changes the drop. The juxtaposition is the Qur'an's standard way of handling human arrogance, and it works by chronology rather than by moralizing. A person who reads his own history in order — drop, clot, lump, bones, flesh, speech, objection — encounters his objection at the end of a sequence he did not author. The objection loses its footing, not because it is refuted, but because its position in the sequence becomes visible.

**Why Development Is Named Alongside Creation**

Creation could have been stated alone: He made him from a drop. The addition of ordainment specifies that the making was not a single act but a governed process, and the specification matters for the argument the sūrah is building. A single act of creation might be granted by an opponent and still leave the resurrection in doubt, since the opponent could claim that the first making was unique. A governed process cannot be so claimed. What is ordained can be ordained again, and a measure that was applied once remains available to the One who set it. The verse therefore prepares for verse 22 before the reader reaches it, and it does so by a word choice rather than a statement. The same logic appears at the close of Sūrat al-Qiyāmah, where the stages from drop to clot to formed flesh end in the question about bringing the dead back to life (75:37–40). Development is named because development is the evidence.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:20

> Then He makes the way easy for them,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb Whose Object Is Not Spoken**

The Arabic is *thumma al-sabīla yassarahū* — then the way, He made it easy for him — and the construction admits more than one reference. The noun *al-sabīl*, the way or road, is fronted for emphasis, and the verb of easing governs either the way itself or the human being's passage along it. Some read the object as the way out of the womb, so that the verse describes the ease with which a child is brought forth. Others read it as the way of life, the path a person walks, and take the easing as provision of guidance. A third group reads the fronted noun as generic — He made the way easy, meaning He made each thing easy for the function it was created for. The ambiguity is not a defect in the text; it is a property of a compressed verse whose object is deliberately left open, and the three readings converge on a single claim: the passage from a drop to a walking, speaking human being was not achieved against resistance but was facilitated.

**Three Readings and the Grounds for Each**

The first reading rests on the immediate context. The verse sits between creation from a drop and death with burial, so a description of birth fits the sequence exactly, and the exegetes who hold it note that the birth is the one stage of the sequence not yet mentioned. The second rests on the moral theme of the sūrah: the human being condemned for ingratitude in verse 17 has been given more than a body, and the easing would then be the provision of guidance, as in *“We already showed them the Way, whether they ˹choose to˺ be grateful or ungrateful”* (76:3). The third rests on a report from the Prophet: work, for everyone is made easy for that for which he was created — transmitted in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6596 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2649, with authenticity agreed upon. The ḥadīth is used by the commentators to gloss this verse, and it supports the generic reading by making the easing a principle of creation rather than an event in gestation.

**Which Reading Carries the Most Weight**

The birth reading is the tightest fit to the surrounding verses, which form an unbroken chain of stages: created, ordained, eased, killed, buried, raised. Inserting guidance into that chain breaks its physical sequence. The guidance reading, however, is supported by the sūrah's larger purpose, since the whole passage is about a reminder that can be received or refused, and the refusal is only culpable if the receiving was made possible. The ḥadīth reading covers both, because the ease it describes includes the capacity to act on what one is fitted for. The strongest position holds the first reading as the primary sense and the second as the consequence: the way was made easy in birth, and the same easing characterizes the provision of guidance, so that a human being who refuses the reminder has refused something he was enabled to take. The three readings are not rivals; they are three applications of one verb of facilitation.

**Easing as a Feature of Divine Government**

*Taysīr* — making easy — is not incidental to the Qur'an's account of how God deals with creatures; it is a stated policy. Sūrat al-Layl sets out the two directions it can take: *“As for the one who is charitable, mindful ˹of Allah˺, and ˹firmly˺ believes in the finest reward, We will facilitate for them the Way of Ease. And as for the one who is stingy, indifferent ˹to Allah˺, and ˹staunchly˺ denies the finest reward, We will facilitate for them the path of hardship”* (92:5–10). Facilitation is granted in both cases, and the direction differs. The verse in this sūrah belongs to the same economy: the way was made easy, and the ease is a gift that precedes any merit. Nothing in the human being's history explains it. The drop did not earn the passage, the child did not choose the birth, and the adult did not procure the guidance. Easing is what the Creator does, and the doing is prior to the receiving.

**Ease Has a Direction**

The passages on facilitation make clear that the ease is not neutral. It is towards something, and the something depends on the orientation of the one who receives it. The charitable and believing are eased towards the way of ease; the stingy and denying are eased towards hardship. In both cases the ease is real and the walking is genuinely easier than it would otherwise be, which is why the second group is not excused. This is the theological weight carried by the single verb in this verse. A human being whose way has been made easy cannot plead impossibility, and the impossibility plea is what the ingratitude of verse 17 amounts to in practice. The ungrateful man acts as though nothing had been given, and the sequence of verses from creation to easing is the record of what was given before he had done anything at all.

**One Link in a Chain of Six**

Read in sequence, verses 19 to 22 describe six acts: creation from a drop, ordainment of development, easing of the way, causing death, causing burial, and resurrection when He wills. Each is an act of the same subject, and the subject is never anything but God. Nothing in the chain is attributed to the human being except the response, and the response is described in verse 23 as a failure to comply. The chain's completeness is the argument. A being who has been made, measured, eased, killed, buried, and is to be raised has no standing from which to claim self-sufficiency, and no ground on which to deny the One who did all six. The sūrah will go on to itemize the provisions — rain, splitting earth, grain, orchards, fodder — and the itemization extends the same chain into the daily life of the reader. The verse here is the pivot, because it is the one act in the chain that is experienced as relief rather than as event.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:21

> then causes them to die and be buried.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Acts Held in One Verse**

*Amātahū fa-aqbarahū* — He caused him to die, then He caused him to be buried. Two causative verbs, joined by a particle of sequence, and both have God as their subject. The joining is significant because it treats death and burial as a single divine provision rather than as an event followed by a human response. Most accounts of a life would list the death and then describe the funeral as something the survivors arranged. This verse makes the funeral part of what God does to the person. The sequence particle *fa-* indicates immediacy: he died and was buried, without a gap in which the body lay exposed. The compression is not stylistic. It establishes that the disposal of the body belongs to the same governance as its making, and that the human being receives his grave as he received his form — from a hand that was not his own.

**A Verb That Grants a Grave**

The second verb is the fourth form of *q-b-r*, and the fourth form is causative: not "he buried him" but "he caused him to be buried," or more idiomatically, "he granted him burial." The distinction is worth pressing, because the Qur'an could have used the simple form and did not. What is conferred is not the act of interment but the state of having a grave. A body left to scavengers, or lost at sea, or exposed on a battlefield, has died without being granted this. The causative verb names the granting, and the granting is a dignity. It belongs to the same register as the verse that declares the elevation of the human species: *“Indeed, We have dignified the children of Adam, carried them on land and sea, granted them good and lawful provisions, and privileged them far above many of Our creatures”* (17:70). Burial is one of the dignities, and it is listed here among the acts for which the human being is expected to be grateful.

**Burial Taught by a Bird**

The Qur'an locates the origin of burial in a story rather than in a law. After Cain killed his brother and did not know what to do with the body, *“Then Allah sent a crow digging ˹a grave˺ in the ground ˹for a dead crow˺, in order to show him how to bury the corpse of his brother. He cried, ‘Alas! Have I ˹even˺ failed to be like this crow and bury the corpse of my brother?’ So he became regretful”* (5:31). The detail matters for this verse because it shows burial as something taught, not invented — a practice delivered to humanity from outside, at the moment of its first murder, by a creature acting on instruction. The shame in Cain's exclamation is the shame of a man who has to learn dignity from a bird. Read against the verb in this sūrah, the story confirms that the grave is a divine provision extended through whatever means, and that failing to receive it is a failure of the recipient.

**The Grave as the Human Distinction**

No other creature in the Qur'an is described as buried. Animals die; the earth receives them; nothing is said of a grave. The human being alone is granted one, and the granting produces the vocabulary of the afterlife that runs through the Book: the people of the graves, the day they are brought out, the questioning in the grave. *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves”* (22:7). The grave is therefore not merely a disposal site but a station, and the verse that grants it is granting a place in an order that continues past death. This is why the sūrah can list burial among the favours. A being who is buried is a being whose existence has not been treated as refuse, and the treatment is part of what the imprecation of verse 17 declares to have been covered over by ingratitude.

**Death as an Act Rather Than an Event**

The Qur'an consistently attributes dying to God rather than to causes. *“No soul can ever die without Allah's Will at the destined time”* (3:145) places every death inside a permission and a schedule. *“Every being on earth is bound to perish. Only your Lord Himself, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain ˹forever˺”* (55:26–27) states the universality of the perishing and the exception of the One who does not perish. In this verse the attribution is grammatical: He caused him to die. The passive of the human being and the active of God are fixed, and the fixing removes death from the category of accidents. What is caused by a will is not random, and what is not random is answerable. The sequence in this sūrah depends on that: the same will that caused the death will cause the raising, and the raising is described in the next verse with the same verb structure and the same subject.

**Why Burial Belongs in the Reckoning**

The list of favours in these verses is unusual in including an act that costs the recipient nothing and that he will never experience. He is dead when he is buried. The inclusion tells the reader that the reckoning of gratitude is not limited to benefits that can be enjoyed. Some provisions are for the survivors, some are for the dignity of the species, and some are for a state the recipient will enter without awareness. The ingratitude charged in verse 17 covers all three categories, which is why it is described in terms of magnitude rather than of specifics. A human being who has never once considered that his grave was granted to him is ungrateful for something he cannot refuse and cannot repay, and the verse records the grant so that the refusal to notice it becomes visible. The noticing is the whole of what is asked.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:22

> Then when He wills, He will resurrect them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Will With No Date Attached**

*Idhā shāʾa ansharahū* — then, when He wills, He will raise him. The condition is stated without a term. No time is given, no sign is named, no interval is measured, and the absence is the substance of the verse. Every other act in the chain has a determinate character: creation from a drop, ordainment of development, easing of the way, causing death, causing burial. The raising alone is attached to an unspecified willing, and the attachment does two things at once. It confirms that the raising will occur, since the condition is God's own will and nothing can prevent it. And it confirms that the timing is not accessible to the one who will be raised. The human being is thus placed in a position of certainty about the event and ignorance about the date, which is the exact structure of accountability: enough assurance to make the choice meaningful, enough obscurity to keep the choice free.

**To Unfold What Was Gathered**

The verb *anshara* is the fourth form of *n-sh-r*, whose basic sense is to spread out, to unfold, to scatter what was folded or gathered, and to publish what was hidden. Arabic uses the same root for spreading a garment, for the wind scattering rain, and for the resurrection of the dead. The image is of something compressed being opened out. A body that has been folded into a grave and reduced to dust is spread again into a living form. The choice of verb is more concrete than the usual English rendering suggests, and it carries the sense of restoration rather than of new manufacture: what is unfolded is what was folded. That is the answer to the Meccan objection about decayed bones, since unfolding presupposes an original that persists in the knowledge of the One who folded it. The Qur'an makes the presupposition explicit in its reply: they will be revived by the One who produced them the first time, and He has perfect knowledge of every created being (36:79).

**Resurrection Argued From the Beginning**

The verse is the conclusion of an argument begun four verses earlier, and the argument runs from origin to return. *“Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life?”* (75:40) is the same inference in interrogative form, placed at the end of a description of the stages from drop to formed flesh. *“And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him”* (30:27) states the inference directly and adds the comparison: the second making is easier than the first. The comparison is not a claim about effort, since nothing costs God effort; it is a claim about the logic of the case. Whatever produced a person from a drop can produce him from a grave. Sūrat al-Baqarah puts the whole sequence to the disbeliever as a single question: how can you deny God when you were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will all be returned (2:28).

**The Order of the Four Acts**

Al-Baqarah's formulation and this verse share a fourfold order: lifelessness, life, death, life again. The order is not symmetrical in the way a cycle would be, because the fourth act has no fifth to follow it — the return to God closes the sequence. This sūrah states the same order in four successive verses and adds two acts the others omit: ordainment and burial. The additions are characteristic of the passage's method, which is to make the human being's history as concrete as possible before drawing the conclusion. Nothing abstract is asserted about resurrection; it is placed at the end of a list of things that have already happened to the reader. The list is what carries the conviction. A person who accepts that he was made from a drop, measured, eased, killed, and buried has already accepted four acts of a will that was not his own, and the fifth act requires no new principle.

**The Delay Is the Test**

Because the date is unspecified, the interval between burial and raising is a space in which the human being's descendants live, choose, and forget. The Qur'an treats that interval as a moral condition rather than a chronological accident. Those who deny the Hour treat the delay as evidence against it, and the Qur'an answers by describing the delay as a respite whose purpose is not disclosed to the one who receives it. The verse here does not argue with that denial; it simply places the raising inside a will, which means the delay is inside the same will. Nothing about the passing of time weakens the event, since the event was never scheduled by time. This is why the imprecation of verse 17 can be pronounced before the resurrection is mentioned: the ingratitude is not contingent on the date, and the covering over of the favours happens in the interval, not at the end of it.

**What the Verse Withholds**

No description of the manner of rising appears here, and no account of what follows it. The sūrah withholds both and returns to the subject later, at the point where the Blast is named and the day is described. The withholding is consistent with the verse's function, which is to establish the fact and its dependence on a will rather than to depict the event. Depiction would invite argument about details; the statement of dependence does not. What is asserted is minimal and total: He will raise him, when He wills. The minimum is enough to undo the self-sufficiency described in verse 5, since a being whose raising belongs to another's will is not a being who needs nothing. The verse thus completes the refutation begun with the question about origin, and it does so by adding a future to a past that the reader has already conceded.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:23

> But no! They have failed to comply with what He ordered.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Refusal in the Sūrah**

*Kallā* appears for the second time in this chapter, and the two occurrences mark its hinges. The first, at verse 11, refused the reasoning that had produced the frown and turned the sūrah from an incident to a doctrine. This one refuses something the reader may have drawn from the preceding six verses. The chain of acts — created, ordained, eased, killed, buried, to be raised — is a description of complete provision, and a description of complete provision invites a comfortable conclusion: the human being is well looked after, and the looking after is automatic. *Kallā* stops that conclusion and replaces it with a failure. Everything has been done; nothing has been complied with. The particle is doing the same work it did eleven verses earlier, which is to interrupt a train of thought at the point where it would become self-serving. The refusal is not aimed at an opponent here. It is aimed at the reader's own inference.

**A Negative That Leaves a Door Open**

*Lammā yaqḍi* — he has not yet accomplished. The particle *lammā* negates a past action with an expectation attached: not yet, and with the possibility of a future occurrence. It differs from plain *lam*, which negates without that expectation. The choice is therefore meaningful. The verse does not say that the human being never complied and never will; it says that he has not complied, with the implication that the compliance is still outstanding and still possible. That is a small grammatical fact with a large consequence, since it keeps the imprecation of verse 17 from being a final verdict on a particular person. What is condemned is a state, and states can end. The commentators who read the verse as a rebuke to disbelievers specifically are working from the context rather than the particle; the particle itself is open, and its openness is what allows the sūrah to go on to describe provisions that are still being received by the reader.

**Two Objects for the Rebuke**

The exegetes identify the target in two ways. The first takes it as the disbeliever who, told of the Resurrection, assumes he will fare well there — the attitude recorded in Sūrat Fuṣṣilat, where the one who is given a taste of mercy after adversity says that the finest reward with God will definitely be his (41:50). On this reading the refusal answers a presumption: you have not complied, so the confidence is misplaced. The second takes it as anyone who believes he has discharged what was required of him, and reads the verse as a check on self-satisfaction. Both readings are supported by the wording, since *mā amarahū* — what He commanded him — is unspecified and covers the whole of obligation. The first reading fits the Makkan context of argument about resurrection; the second fits the moral movement of the sūrah, which has just described a frown as a matter for correction from heaven. The second is the broader and the more useful, and nothing excludes the first.

**An Emphatic Rather Than a Refuting Particle**

Some commentators note that *kallā* can be read affirmatively as well as negatively, in which case the verse would begin "truly" or "indeed" rather than "no." On that reading the sentence loses its force of refusal and becomes a solemn assertion: truly, he has not accomplished what He commanded him. The grammatical possibility is real, and the same particle elsewhere carries an emphatic sense when it introduces rather than contradicts. The negative reading remains stronger here because of the particle's position: it follows a description that could be misread as reassurance, and its function in verse 11 established the pattern of refusal at a hinge. The affirmative reading does not change the content — the failure is asserted either way — and the difference is one of tone. A refusal is directed at an inference in the reader; an assertion is directed at the state of the human being. The sūrah is doing both.

**What the Command Covers**

The object of compliance is *mā amarahū*, what He commanded him, and the verse does not itemize it. The generality is deliberate and it is broad enough to include belief, worship, and conduct. In the context of a Makkan sūrah addressed to people who denied the Resurrection, the first item in any list of what God commanded would be to believe in Him and in the return to Him, and the failure to comply is therefore not a matter of missing a rule but of missing the whole. The commentators who connect the verse to apostasy read the command narrowly, as the specific obligation the apostate abandoned; the wider reading is better supported, since the subject is *al-insān*, the human being as such, and a subject that general requires an obligation that general. Whatever the itemization, the verse states that the compliance has not happened, and it states this immediately after listing what has.

**A Verdict Interrupting a Sequence**

The placement of this verse is the last thing to notice. Verses 18 to 22 describe the human being's history; verses 24 to 32 will describe his food. Between the two lists stands a single sentence of judgement. The interruption prevents both lists from being read as mere description. The history is not a natural account of a species; it is a record of provision against which a failure is measured. The food is not an inventory of agriculture; it is a further provision received by the same failing recipient. Without the verdict between them, the two lists would sit side by side as information. With it, both become evidence, and the reader passes from one to the other under a charge. The sūrah's structure is thus argumentative throughout, even where it appears to be describing, and this verse is the point at which that becomes unmistakable.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:24

> Let people then consider their food:

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Invitation to Look at Dinner**

*Fa-lyanẓur al-insān ilā ṭaʿāmih* — let the human being look at his food. The imperative is in the third person, which makes it a command addressed to the reader about himself rather than a direct address, and the form gives the sentence its generality: whoever is reading is included in the human being. The object of the looking is the most ordinary thing in the sūrah. After pages held in honour, angel-scribes, a drop, a grave, and a resurrection, the verse turns to food — the meal in front of the hearer, the bread, the water, the dates. The descent is the argument. A person who cannot be moved by the description of his own creation may be moved by the description of his lunch, because the lunch is present and the creation is not. The Qur'an repeatedly chooses the nearest object for its invitations to reflection, and this is one of the clearest cases: the reflection is commanded about something the reader has already consumed today.

**The Ordinary as the Locus of Evidence**

The method belongs to a family of passages. Sūrat al-Ghāshiyah asks the same question of larger objects: *“Do they not ever reflect on camels—how they were ˹masterfully˺ created; and the sky—how it was raised ˹high˺; and the mountains—how they were firmly set up; and the earth—how it was levelled out?”* (88:17–20). Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah narrows to agriculture: *“Have you considered what you sow? Is it you who cause it to grow, or is it We Who do so?”* (56:63–64). Sūrat al-Ṭāriq narrows to the body: *“Let people then consider what they were created from!”* (86:5). Across all of them the object is chosen for its familiarity rather than its grandeur, and the question posed is always about agency: who did this? The familiarity removes the excuse of distance. A man can claim he has never considered the heavens, but he cannot claim he has never eaten, and the eating is the entry point this verse selects.

**Looking With the Mind**

*Naẓar* is not the verb for the mere act of seeing. Arabic distinguishes *raʾā*, to see, from *naẓara*, to look at with attention, and from *abṣara*, to perceive. *Naẓar* carries the sense of examination and deliberation, and in the Qur'an it is regularly used for a looking that produces knowledge rather than sensation. The command to look at food is therefore not an instruction to observe its appearance but to investigate its provenance, which is what the verses that follow supply. The investigation is available to anyone without equipment or training. It requires only that the eater ask where the meal came from and follow the answer backwards, and the backwards path leads through the field, the rain, the cloud, and the act of a will that is not his own. The verb is chosen so that the command cannot be satisfied by a glance, and the dissatisfaction of a glance is what moves the reader into the reasoning the sūrah wants.

**The Same Formula Turned on Origin**

The parallel in Sūrat al-Ṭāriq is close enough to be read as a companion verse: *“Let people then consider what they were created from!”* (86:5) uses the same verb, the same subject, and the same structure, with origin replacing food as the object. The two passages divide the human being's dependence in half. His beginning was not his doing, and his continuation is not his doing either. Between the two lies the whole of a life, and both ends are outside it. The pairing also shows why this verse appears where it does. The chain of acts in verses 18 to 22 covered creation and death; the provision of food covers the interval, and the interval is where ingratitude actually occurs. A human being is not ungrateful at his conception or at his burial. He is ungrateful at lunch, and the verse addresses him there.

**Food as the Site of Ingratitude**

The Qur'an ties blessing and ingratitude together in a formula that appears twice: *“If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never be able to number them. Indeed humankind is truly unfair, ˹totally˺ ungrateful”* (14:34), and *“If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never be able to number them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”* (16:18). Both begin with the impossibility of enumeration, which is the same point this verse makes by a different route: the food in front of you cannot be traced to its end, because the tracing leads out of your hands at every step. The first formulation pairs the impossibility with the charge of injustice and ingratitude; the second pairs it with forgiveness and mercy. This sūrah has already pronounced the charge in verse 17, and it now supplies the material for it. The counting is what the human being does not do, and the not-doing is the covering that the word for disbelief denotes.

**What Food Becomes**

One strand of the exegetical tradition reads the invitation differently: consider your food in its whole passage, including its consumption and its elimination, because the end of the meal shows what becomes of the world. The reading is not the plain sense of the verse, and the verses that follow it describe the growing rather than the digesting. It survives in the commentaries because it makes a point the passage supports elsewhere — that everything in this world is passing and that the form in which a thing leaves is a measure of what it was. The stronger reading remains the agricultural one, which is what the sūrah develops. The alternative is worth recording because it shows how early readers applied the same verb of consideration to the whole life cycle of a thing, and because the moral is the same in both cases: what you consume was not produced by you, and what remains of it is not yours either. The looking is commanded, and the rest of the chapter performs it.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:25

> how We pour down rain in abundance

**Expanded Commentary**

**Pouring Rather Than Sending**

The verb is *ṣababnā*, from *ṣ-b-b*, to pour out as from a vessel — the word used for pouring water, for pouring out molten metal, for the pouring of a punishment. The Qur'an has other verbs for rain: *anzala*, to send down, and *nazzala*, to send down in stages, both of which appear constantly. This verse chooses neither. It chooses a verb of pouring, and the choice changes the image. Sending down is neutral about quantity and manner; pouring is not. A poured thing comes in volume, continuously, from a source that holds more than it releases. The Meccan hearer lived in a climate where rain was either absent or violent, and the verb fits the second case rather than the first. The description is not of a gentle spring shower sustaining a garden but of water released in quantity, which is what the rest of the passage requires, since the splitting of the earth and the growing of the grain depend on volume rather than on frequency.

**An Emphatic Object Built From the Same Root**

*Ṣabban* follows the verb, in the accusative, from the same root. Arabic grammar calls this the cognate accusative, and its function is emphasis: He poured the water *a pouring*. The construction adds no information and removes all doubt about the intensity of the act. It is a device the Qur'an uses at moments of magnitude, and its presence here marks the pouring as exceptional rather than routine. The translation carries the sense with the word abundance, which is a fair rendering of an emphasis that English cannot reproduce grammatically. The doubled effect — a verb of pouring plus a noun of pouring — gives the verse a weight disproportionate to its length, and the weight is doing argumentative work: the first item in the list of provisions is described with the most emphatic grammar available, because it is the item no human being can claim to have contributed to.

**The Same Pouring in the News**

Sūrat al-Nabaʾ describes the identical sequence in nearly the identical words: *“and sent down from rainclouds pouring water, producing by it grain and ˹various˺ plants, and dense orchards?”* (78:14–16). The three elements — poured water, grain, dense gardens — are the same three that occupy verses 25 to 30 of this sūrah, and both passages use the verb of pouring in an emphatic form. The two sūrahs are neighbours in the received order and belong to the same Makkan phase, and the correspondence suggests a fixed pattern of argument rather than two coincidental descriptions. Al-Nabaʾ frames the list as a question about what people already know; this sūrah frames it as an instruction to look at food. The material is shared, and the framing differs, which is characteristic of a revelation that returns to the same evidence from different angles until the evidence is unavoidable.

**The Machinery Behind the Pouring**

Elsewhere the Qur'an describes the process that precedes the release: *“It is Allah Who sends the winds, which then stir up ˹vapour, forming˺ clouds, which He then spreads out in the sky or piles up into masses as He wills, from which you see rain come forth”* (30:48). Sūrat al-Aʿrāf gives the same sequence with the rain as a sign of resurrection: *“He is the One Who sends the winds ushering in His mercy. When they bear heavy clouds, We drive them to a lifeless land and then cause rain to fall, producing every type of fruit. Similarly, We will bring the dead to life, so perhaps you will be mindful”* (7:57). Both passages insert stages — wind, vapour, cloud, driving — between the will and the falling. This verse compresses all of them into a single act of pouring, which is a legitimate compression because the stages are themselves acts of the same agent. The compression also serves the argument: the eater is asked to consider his food, and the food begins with a pouring he did not perform and cannot perform.

**Water as the Beginning of Everything Living**

The Qur'an states the priority of water in terms that are close to a principle: *“And We created from water every living thing. Will they not then believe?”* (21:30). *“And Allah has created from water every living creature. Some of them crawl on their bellies, some walk on two legs, and some walk on four”* (24:45). *“And He is the One Who creates human beings from a ˹humble˺ liquid, then establishes for them bonds of kinship and marriage”* (25:54) uses the same imagery at the scale of the individual. Water in these verses is not one provision among many; it is the medium of life itself, and the pouring described here is the arrival of that medium on land. The list of foods that follows — grain, grapes, greens, olives, dates, orchards, fodder — is a list of forms water has taken. That is why the list begins with the pouring rather than with the sowing: nothing in it exists before the water does.

**The First Answer to the Ingratitude**

Verse 17 condemned the human being for ingratitude and verse 24 commanded him to look at his food. This verse is the first thing he sees when he looks. The selection is deliberate and it is the strongest item available. A man can claim credit for his field, his labour, his irrigation channels, his storage, his market. He cannot claim credit for the rain, and in a Hijāzī climate the claim is not even arguable: the rain arrives or it does not, and no human action produces it. Beginning the list here removes the possibility of a shared credit before the list has started. Everything that follows — the splitting, the grain, the vines, the olives, the palms, the orchards, the fodder — hangs from a pouring that no one in the argument can appropriate. The ingratitude charged in verse 17 is therefore not a failure to acknowledge a contribution the human being made, but a failure to acknowledge a contribution he could not have made.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:26

> and meticulously split the earth open ˹for sprouts˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Earth Is Split, Not Softened**

*Shaqiqnā* — We split — is a verb of cleaving, and it describes the earth as something that had to be opened by force. The image is not of soil yielding gently to a seed but of a surface being broken, and the breaking is real: a shoot pushes through crusted ground, and the ground resists. The Qur'an chooses the violent verb rather than a verb of softening or moistening, and the choice matters for the argument that follows in the sūrah, because splitting is what a grave does not do on its own. The same root is used for the splitting of the sky at the end of the world and for the cleaving of the earth in Sūrat al-Ṭāriq, where the earth is described as one that cracks open. A verb that belongs to the vocabulary of rupture is being applied to agriculture, and the application prepares the reader for the rupture of resurrection, which the passage will reach at verse 33.

**Who Performs the Splitting**

The shoot visibly does the work, and the verse attributes it to God. This is not a denial of the plant's action but a statement about agency at a different level, and the Qur'an handles the same question explicitly elsewhere: *“Have you considered what you sow? Is it you who cause it to grow, or is it We Who do so?”* (56:63–64). The farmer sows; the growing is not his. The division of labour in that passage is identical to the one here — human beings perform the acts that are within their reach, and the acts that produce the result belong to God. The verse is not making a claim about the absence of natural processes. It is making a claim about ownership of the process, and the ownership is what generates the obligation of gratitude. A thing produced by a process nobody owns generates no thanks. A thing produced by a process owned by a will generates a debt, and the debt is the subject of verse 17.

**A Second Cognate Accusative**

*Shaqqan* follows the verb, again from the same root, and again as an emphatic object: We split it *a splitting*. Two consecutive verses carry the same construction — poured a pouring, split a splitting — and the repetition is not accidental. The doubled grammar gives the two acts a weight that marks them as the decisive stages of the whole sequence. Rain falls and the earth opens; everything else in the list follows from those two events. The emphasis therefore identifies the hinges of the process, and it identifies them as acts rather than as conditions. What the reader is asked to consider is not a state of affairs but two things done, and the doing is emphatic in both cases. Arabic achieves in three syllables what English must add the word abundance or meticulously to recover, and the translation's insertion of meticulously is an attempt to carry that emphasis across.

**Splitting as the Revival of the Dead**

The Qur'an uses the revival of dead land as its standard illustration of resurrection, and the illustration depends on exactly the image in this verse. *“And among His signs is that you see the earth devoid of life, but as soon as We send down rain upon it, it begins to stir ˹to life˺ and swell. Indeed, the One Who revives it can easily revive the dead. He is certainly Most Capable of everything”* (41:39). The stirring and swelling are the earth cracking as the shoots push through. Sūrat Qāf states the inference in a single sentence after a similar list: *“˹as˺ a provision for ˹Our˺ servants. And with this ˹rain˺ We revive a lifeless land. Similar is the emergence ˹from the graves˺”* (50:11). The parallel is exact and it is not decorative: the emergence from a grave is described as similar to the emergence of a shoot from split earth. This verse supplies the mechanism of that similarity, and it does so inside a list of foods.

**The Point of Contact Between Sky and Earth**

Two acts meet in the fissure. Water comes from above and growth comes from below, and the place where they meet is the crack in the ground. Nothing else in the sequence joins the two orders so visibly. The rain falls on a surface, and the surface opens, and what comes out of the opening is neither rain nor soil but a living thing. The Qur'an's description keeps the joining concrete rather than explaining it, which is why the verse is short: it names the act and leaves the reader at the fissure. A farmer watching his field after rain sees this happen thousands of times in a single morning and thinks nothing of it, which is precisely the situation the verse addresses. The command to look at food in verse 24 is fulfilled by looking at the ground, and the ground at that moment is the most informative object in the world.

**Precision in the Intensive Form**

The doubled form of the verb, and the cognate noun that follows it, both indicate thoroughness. The splitting is not partial or occasional; it is complete and everywhere it needs to be. Every seed that will grow has its own fissure, and the fissures are made in the right places, in the right number, at the right time. Nothing in the verse states this in words, and the grammar carries it. The precision is what makes the provision reliable — a splitting that occurred at random would produce a harvest that could not be counted on, and a harvest that cannot be counted on is not provision but chance. The human being who is commanded to consider his food is being asked to notice an order he depends on completely and has never once questioned. The consideration is not a study; it is an acknowledgment, and the acknowledgment is the opposite of the covering named in verse 17.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:27

> causing grain to grow in it,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Staple Named Before Anything Else**

*Ḥabb* is grain — the seed of the cereal plants, wheat and barley above all — and it is the first product of the split earth. The order of the list that follows is not arbitrary: grain, then grapes and greens, then olives and palms, then orchards, then fruit and fodder. The staple comes first, and the staples of the Ḥijāz were wheat and barley, the two crops that made settled life possible. Everything after grain in this list is either an addition to a diet or a provision for animals. The placement tells the reader what the sūrah considers the base of provision, and it tells him that the base is not the interesting part. Grain is stored, measured, ground, and eaten daily; it produces no astonishment. A list of divine favours that began with dates or grapes would have flattered the reader's sense of abundance. A list that begins with grain addresses his survival.

**Growth Attributed to the One Who Split the Earth**

*Anbatnā* — We caused to grow — is the causative of the verb for sprouting, and it keeps the agency where the previous verse put it. The earth was split by God and the grain is grown by God; the seed is not mentioned at all. The omission matters, because a farmer would naturally place the seed at the centre of the account. The verse leaves it out and describes only the pouring, the splitting, and the growing. The result is a picture of provision in which human contribution is not merely small but absent from the description, and the absence is a deliberate narrowing of attention. The farmer sows, and Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah asks him directly whether he causes the growth (56:64). Here the question is not asked because the answer has already been given by the verbs: We poured, We split, We grew.

**Grain in the Qur'an's Imagery**

The word recurs in contexts that show its weight. Sūrat al-Raḥmān lists the contents of the earth and names *“and grain with husks, and aromatic plants”* (55:12) — grain described in its harvested form, with the chaff still on it. Sūrat Qāf pairs it with the garden: *“bringing forth gardens and grains for harvest”* (50:9). The parable of spending in the way of God is built entirely on a grain: *“The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains”* (2:261). The multiplication in that parable depends on the reader knowing what a single grain does — that it goes into the ground and returns as a plant carrying hundreds of its kind. The Qur'an's use of the seed as its image for charity assumes an agricultural literacy in its audience, and this verse is part of the material that literacy rests on.

**Necessity Before Delight**

The sequence in verses 27 to 31 moves from what keeps a person alive to what makes his life pleasant. Grain sustains; grapes and olives and dates enrich; fruit is enjoyment in the strict sense, since the Arabic word for it denotes what is eaten besides the meal rather than as the meal. The order is the order of a real diet and also the order of a real argument. A person who is asked to be grateful for provisions will first be shown the provision whose absence kills him, and only then the ones whose absence merely impoverishes him. The arrangement forecloses the complaint that gratitude is being demanded for luxuries. By the time the reader reaches the fruit and the fodder, the grain has already established that the whole list is a matter of survival, and survival is not something a human being produced.

**The Base of Every Settlement**

Grain is what allows a place to be inhabited continuously. Meat can be hunted, dates can be gathered, water can be carried, but only grain can be stored through a year and measured against a population. The Qur'an's own narrative of Joseph turns entirely on this fact — the interpretation of a dream about years of plenty and years of famine, and the storage of grain through the good years to survive the bad (12:47–49). The narrative is the longest sustained treatment of food in the Book, and its subject is exactly the item named first in this list. Read against that narrative, the verse acquires an economic dimension: the grain is not only food but security, and security is a provision of the same kind as the rain that produced it. Nobody in Mecca or Medina could have survived a bad year on grapes.

**What the Grain Establishes**

The whole argument of this passage rests on the human being's inability to produce his own food, and the grain is the clearest case of that inability. A person can choose a field, break the soil, scatter the seed, and stand watch over it; he cannot make any of that produce an ear. Between the sowing and the harvest stands a season he did not arrange, a rain he did not order, and a growth he did not perform. A date palm may grow wild; a grapevine may be found; but grain requires the coincidence of rain at the right time, soil that opens, a season that holds, and a harvest that is not destroyed. Each element is outside human control, and the coincidence of all of them is what produces the staple. The verse names only the last step — We caused grain to grow in it — and the naming is enough, because the reader who has considered his food knows what came before. The consideration commanded in verse 24 is thus fulfilled by a single item, and the items that follow extend the same point into every part of the diet rather than adding new arguments to it.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:28

> as well as grapes and greens,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Vine as the Sign of a Settled Land**

*ʿInab* is the grape, and the vine is the plant the Qur'an consistently associates with established abundance. A grain field can be sown in a bad year and lost; a vineyard takes years to establish and bears for decades, which means the presence of vines in a landscape is evidence that the landscape has been at peace. The Qur'an uses this implication when it describes the gardens of the disbelievers or the rewards of the believers: vineyards appear alongside springs, palms, and enclosed gardens in nearly every description of plenty. The vine is also the crop most vulnerable to the weather, which makes its presence a standing sign of provision rather than of achievement. In the Ḥijāz the great vineyard district was al-Ṭāʾif, a day's journey from Mecca and a place the Quraysh knew well, and the mention of grapes in a Makkan sūrah would have been heard as a reference to a real economy rather than a poetic image.

**A Word the Lexicons Disagree About**

*Qaḍab* appears only here in the Qur'an, and its meaning was debated early. The dominant explanation is a fresh green fodder — lucerne or clover — cut for animals, which fits the pairing with fruit and fodder in verse 31 and the mention of cattle in verse 32. Others take it as greens for human eating, vegetables generally, on the ground that the list so far has concerned human food. A third group reads it as anything cut green, from the root meaning to cut, so that the word describes the state rather than the species. The disagreement is not resolvable from the text alone, since the word has no other Qur'anic occurrence to compare it with. The fodder reading is supported by the structure of the list, which ends with the animals; the vegetable reading is supported by the position of the word beside grapes, a human food. Both remain in the commentaries, and the translation's greens keeps the ambiguity open in the same way the Arabic does.

**Grapes Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The vine appears in a set of passages that show how the Qur'an uses it. *“And on the earth there are ˹different˺ neighbouring tracts, gardens of grapevines, ˹various˺ crops, palm trees—some stemming from the same root, others standing alone. They are all irrigated with the same water, yet We make some taste better than others. Surely in this are signs for those who understand”* (13:4). The argument there is about diversity produced from a single input, and the vine is the first item. *“And We have placed in it gardens of palm trees and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth in it”* (36:34) pairs the two plants as the standard couple of Arabian abundance. Sūrat al-Raḥmān lists *“In it are fruit, palm trees with date stalks”* (55:11) and elsewhere names the vine among the produce of Paradise. Across all of these the grape functions as a marker of cultivated plenty, never as a wild food.

**Two Kinds of Provision in One Verse**

If the greens are human food, the verse pairs the stored or processed product with the daily one: grapes that become raisins and wine, and greens that must be eaten fresh. If the greens are fodder, the verse pairs human food with animal food and anticipates verse 32, which names the animals explicitly. Either way the pairing establishes that provision is not a single thing. The diet of a settled community includes what is preserved and what perishes, what is eaten and what is fed, and the verse's brevity covers all of it in two nouns. The compression is characteristic of this passage: the whole agricultural economy of the Ḥijāz is listed in six verses, and each verse carries two or three items with no explanation. The explanation is left to the reader, who is standing in the middle of it.

**The Vine and What Came From It**

Grapes occupy a particular place in Qur'anic legislation because of what they become. *“And from the fruits of palm trees and grapevines you derive intoxicants as well as wholesome provision. Surely in this is a sign for those who understand”* (16:67). This Makkan verse acknowledges both products of the same plant without yet prohibiting the first, and the prohibition came later in Medina. The mention of grapes in Sūrat ʿAbasa carries no such reference — it is a pure listing of provision — but the reader who knows the other verse reads this one with the knowledge that the same fruit supports both nourishment and intoxication, and that the difference lies in what human beings do with it. The provision is described here in its unprocessed state, which is how every item in the list appears: rain, split earth, grain, grapes, greens, olives, palms. Nothing cooked, nothing fermented, nothing traded.

**From Staple to Variety**

The movement of the list is from the single crop that keeps a population alive to the range of crops that makes its life worth living. Verse 27 named one thing; this verse names two, and the verses that follow name two at a time until the list closes with a general term for fruit and a general term for fodder. The widening is an argument about diversity, and the argument is stated in full elsewhere: the same water irrigates neighbouring plots and produces different tastes (13:4). Diversity from a single input cannot be explained by the input. That is the inference this list invites without stating it, and the inference runs back to the pouring of verse 25, where the whole sequence began with one act applied to many kinds of ground. The human being considering his food moves through the list from necessity to variety and finds the same agent at every step.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:29

> and olives and palm trees,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Trees From Two Climates**

The olive belongs to the Mediterranean: rain-fed, slow, long-lived, cultivated in Syria, Palestine, and the north. The palm belongs to the desert oasis: heat-loving, water-demanding, the tree that made settlement possible in Arabia's interior. Listing them together covers the whole agricultural range of the region the Qur'an addresses, and the pairing recurs so often that it functions as a formula for cultivated abundance. Neither tree grows wild in quantity, and both require years of tending before they yield, so their presence in a landscape is a record of sustained labour and sustained rain. The verse names them without comment, and the naming is enough for an audience that knew exactly what each tree cost to establish. A list containing grain, grapes, olives, and palms is a list of the four things a Ḥijāzī household could not do without.

**The Blessed Olive**

The olive carries a weight in the Qur'an that no other plant approaches. The verse of light builds its entire image on olive oil: *“lit from ˹the oil of˺ a blessed olive tree, ˹located˺ neither to the east nor the west, whose oil would almost glow, even without being touched by fire”* (24:35). The tree is described as blessed, and its oil as almost luminous on its own. Sūrat al-Tīn opens with an oath by it: *“By the fig and the olive ˹of Jerusalem˺”* (95:1). Sūrat al-Muʾminūn names it among the provisions sent down: *“as well as ˹olive˺ trees which grow at Mount Sinai, providing oil and a condiment to eat”* (23:20). Three different kinds of reference — parable, oath, provision — and in all three the olive is marked out. This verse places it in a list of foods without any of that marking, which is a reminder that the ordinary agricultural context of the olive is prior to its symbolic one.

**The Palm's Ubiquity**

No tree appears in the Qur'an as often as the palm. It is named in descriptions of Paradise, in descriptions of the earth's produce, in the story of Maryam, who is told to shake the trunk and receive fresh dates (19:25), and in the oaths and parables of the Makkan sūrahs. Sūrat Qāf describes *“and towering palm trees ˹loaded˺ with clustered fruit”* (50:10), and Sūrat al-Raḥmān lists *“In it are fruit, palm trees with date stalks”* (55:11). The tree's importance is material as well as symbolic: the date is the concentrated food of the desert, capable of storage for years, high in sugar, and portable; the palm supplies shade, timber, fibre, and fodder from its trimmings. A settlement with palms can survive a drought that would destroy a settlement with only grain. The verse names the tree rather than the fruit, which points at the standing source rather than the seasonal yield.

**Oil and Date: Two Concentrated Foods**

The pairing of these two trees puts the two most energy-dense foods of the ancient Near East in a single verse. Olive oil is fat; the date is sugar. Together they supply what grain supplies only partially, and both keep without spoilage. The Qur'an's awareness of this is visible in the way it describes them: oil as something that lights and something that is eaten (23:20), dates as fresh and ripe, as provision and as comfort. The list in this sūrah moves from grain to these two, which is a movement from bulk to concentration, and the movement mirrors the way a real diet is built. A household stores grain in quantity and oil and dates in smaller amounts, and the smaller amounts are what carry it through the season when the grain runs low. The provision described here is not a harvest but a pantry.

**Both Trees in Every List**

The recurrence of the pair across unrelated passages is one of the Qur'an's most consistent descriptive habits. *“He is the One Who produces gardens—both cultivated and wild — and palm trees, crops of different flavours, olives, and pomegranates— similar ˹in shape˺, but dissimilar ˹in taste˺. Eat from the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful”* (6:141). Sūrat al-Anʿām also describes *“producing green stalks from which We bring forth clustered grain. And from palm trees come clusters of dates hanging within reach. ˹There are˺ also gardens of grapevines, olives, and pomegranates”* (6:99). Sūrat Yāsīn pairs palms and vines with springs (36:34). The same items, in the same order, with the same conclusion: a sign for people who understand. This verse is one instance of a fixed pattern, and the fixity is itself evidence that the description is drawn from a single consistent account of provision rather than assembled for the occasion.

**Trees Rather Than Fruit**

Every other item in the list is a product: rain, grain, grapes, greens, fruit, fodder. This verse names two trees. The shift from product to source is small and it changes the register of the list, because a tree is a thing that stands and yields repeatedly rather than a harvest that is gathered once. The olive may bear for centuries and the palm for decades, and both outlive the person who planted them. In a passage about provision for a term — the closing verse will describe the whole list as a means of sustenance — the mention of long-lived trees introduces a different timescale, one in which the provision continues after the recipient has gone. A man eats grain from a field he sowed. He eats olives from a tree his grandfather planted, and the tree will still be bearing when his own grandchildren are adults. The verse allows both cases into the list without distinguishing them, and the undistinguished mixture is what a real household lives on: a harvest that returns every year and a standing stock that returns every year without being resown. Provision that includes both is provision arranged for a life rather than for a season, and the arrangement is not the planter's work either, since he plants into a climate he did not choose and depends on a rain he cannot call.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:30

> and dense orchards,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Garden With Walls**

*Ḥadīqah* is not the general word for garden. Arabic has *jannah* for a garden of any kind, including the gardens of Paradise, and *bustān* for an orchard or planted plot. *Ḥadīqah* is etymologically connected to enclosure — the same root gives the word for a barrier or a surrounding wall — and it denotes a garden that is bounded, protected, and delimited. The distinction matters because an enclosed garden is a made thing: somebody marked the boundary, cleared the ground, planted the trees, and kept animals out. The verse names the enclosure rather than the plants inside it, and the naming shifts the description from produce to place. What is being listed at this point is not another food but a kind of landscape, and the landscape is one that human beings build in cooperation with the rain and the split earth described two verses earlier.

**Thick and Intertwined**

*Ghulb* describes the trees as dense, with foliage so thick that the branches overlap and the ground beneath is shaded. The word conveys mass rather than order: not rows of tidy saplings but a closed canopy. The image is of a place where the growth has exceeded the plan, and the excess is presented as part of the provision. An orchard that yields exactly what was planted is a farm; an orchard that overflows its own design is abundance. The Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise use the same vocabulary of density and shade, and the earthly garden is the sign that makes those descriptions intelligible to a reader who has stood inside one. In the Ḥijāz such places existed — the orchards of al-Ṭāʾif, the palm gardens of Yathrib — and they were known to the audience as the highest form of agricultural wealth, precisely because they combined enclosure, water, shade, and fruit in a single property.

**The Same Phrase in the News**

Sūrat al-Nabaʾ lists the provisions in almost the same words and closes with this item: *“and sent down from rainclouds pouring water, producing by it grain and ˹various˺ plants, and dense orchards?”* (78:14–16). The identical phrase *ḥadāʾiq ghulbā* appears in both sūrahs, and in both it closes the agricultural list. The correspondence is exact enough to indicate a fixed formula rather than a coincidence of vocabulary, and the formula serves the same function in both chapters: the demonstration of provision as evidence for the Day that both sūrahs go on to describe. Al-Nabaʾ asks the question about the Day first and answers it with the list; Sūrat ʿAbasa gives the list first and reaches the Day at verse 33. The shared wording is the shared argument, and the argument is that the One who produced dense orchards from poured water will produce the events that follow.

**Enclosure Implies Cultivation**

The Qur'an distinguishes cultivated from wild growth: *“He is the One Who produces gardens—both cultivated and wild”* (6:141). The enclosed garden of this verse belongs to the cultivated side, and its cultivation is a human act performed on a divine provision. The farmer does not make the rain, the splitting, or the growing; he makes the boundary, the planting, and the care. The verse's inclusion of the enclosed garden is therefore an acknowledgment that human effort has a real place in the sequence — and the acknowledgment sharpens rather than softens the charge of ingratitude, because the human being who has laboured over his orchard is the one most likely to believe he produced it. The list runs from the poured water, where no human contribution is possible, to the enclosed orchard, where contribution is obvious, and the ordering makes the point that the contribution operates entirely within a provision that was never contributed to.

**The Image the Qur'an Uses for the Good**

Gardens are the Qur'an's central image of reward, and the reward is described in the vocabulary of this verse: enclosed, shaded, watered, fruitful. The earthly orchard is therefore not merely a provision but a sign of the provision that follows, and the connection is made explicit in passages where the gardens of this world are compared with the gardens of the next. A reader who has walked into a dense orchard in the heat of the Ḥijāz has had the experience the Qur'an uses to describe Paradise, and the description works because the experience is common. This verse supplies the term, and the term carries its whole weight of shade, water, and enclosure into every subsequent use. The list of foods is thus also a list of images, and the last of them is the one the Qur'an returns to most often.

**A Form With No Product Named**

Every item before this one is something that can be eaten: grain, grapes, greens, olives, dates. The orchard cannot be eaten. It is named as a place, and the products it contains are named in the following verse as fruit and fodder. The structural choice separates the container from the contents, and the separation allows the list to include the landscape itself among the provisions. A household does not merely receive grain and dates; it receives the place where they grow, with its shade, its boundary, its soil, and its water. That place is worth more than any single harvest, and it is not listed anywhere else in the passage. The verse adds it, and by adding it the list completes itself: the water from above, the earth opened, the crops, the trees, and the enclosed ground that holds them all.

The inclusion also answers a question the reader might put to the list — what happens to the provision between the harvest and the eating. Grain is stored, olives are pressed, dates are dried, and all of it requires a place that is protected. The enclosed orchard is that place in its living form, and its density is a kind of protection in itself, since a thick canopy conserves water and shelter in a hot climate. Provision in the Qur'an is never described as a bare delivery of goods; it is described as an arrangement, and the arrangement includes the ground, the boundary, and the shade under which the goods are produced and kept.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:31

> and fruit and fodder—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Food Eaten Besides the Meal**

*Fākihah* is not the general word for food. Arabic has *ṭaʿām* for food of any kind, and this very passage began with it in verse 24 — let the human being consider his *ṭaʿām*. *Fākihah* denotes what is eaten for enjoyment alongside or after the staple, the thing a person chooses rather than needs. The lexical tradition defines it by that distinction: fruit is what is eaten for pleasure, bread is what is eaten for life. The word appears throughout the Qur'an in descriptions of Paradise, where the inhabitants are supplied with fruit in abundance and call for it as they wish. Its appearance at the end of this list is therefore precisely placed. The list began with the staple whose absence kills and ends with the enjoyment whose absence merely diminishes, and the range between the two is the range of what the human being has been given.

**A Word That Puzzled the Early Readers**

*Abb* is the difficult term in this verse, and it appears nowhere else in the Qur'an. The standard explanation is fodder — the grass and pasture cut or grazed by animals — which fits the mention of livestock in the next verse. The early community's own uncertainty about the word is recorded: it is reported that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, reciting this sūrah from the pulpit, reached the verse and asked what the *abb* was, then reproached himself for the question as an affectation of knowledge he did not possess. [UNVERIFIED: the report is transmitted through the tafsīr tradition, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, without a chain and grade I was able to confirm in a ḥadīth collection] A similar statement is attributed to Abū Bakr about speaking on a word of the Book without knowledge. Whether or not the report is sound in its chain, it records something important: the first generation did not claim to understand every word of the text, and the word survived anyway. The provision it names was not lost because the name was unclear.

**The Human and the Animal in One Verse**

Fruit is for the reader; fodder is for his livestock. The two are joined by a single conjunction and given equal grammatical standing in the list, which is a quiet but real elevation of the animal's food. Nothing in the verse subordinates the fodder to the fruit, and the next verse names both recipients together. The arrangement reflects an economy in which the animal's feed is a direct part of the household's provision, since the animal supplies milk, meat, transport, wool, and labour. Sūrat Ṭā Hā states the same pairing as an instruction: *“˹so˺ eat and graze your cattle. Surely in this are signs for people of sound judgment”* (20:54). Eating and grazing are the two consumptions of the same land, and both are commanded in the same breath. The list in this sūrah reaches the same conclusion by enumeration rather than by instruction.

**Provision for the Provision**

The fodder occupies a second-order position in the economy of the passage. Grain feeds the person directly; fodder feeds the animal that feeds the person. The chain has an extra link, and the extra link is provided in the same act as the rest. Nothing in the verse marks the difference between first-order and second-order provision, and the absence of marking is the point: the provision extends through the whole system rather than stopping at the human mouth. Sūrat al-Naḥl describes the same extension in detail: *“And He created the cattle for you as a source of warmth, food, and ˹many other˺ benefits”* (16:5), followed by the benefits of riding, carrying loads, and the pleasure of the animals coming home (16:6–7). The cattle are themselves a provision, and their food is a provision for the provision. The verse here names the food without naming the animal, and the next verse names both.

**The Same Pair in the Reward**

The vocabulary of this verse reappears in descriptions of Paradise, where the enclosed gardens and vines of the earthly list become the reward: *“Indeed, the righteous will have salvation— Gardens, vineyards”* (78:31–32). The correspondence between the provision and the reward is one of the Qur'an's persistent techniques, and it works by recognizability. The reader is promised what he already knows, in a form that will not fail. Sūrat al-Raḥmān lists *“In it are fruit, palm trees with date stalks”* (55:11) among the favours recounted before the mention of the two gardens. In each case the term *fākihah* carries its sense of chosen enjoyment into the next world, and the enjoyment there is described as uninterrupted. The list in this sūrah is therefore not closed by the fodder; it is closed by a category that continues past the end of the passage.

**Why the List Ends With Two General Terms**

Six verses of specific items — rain, split earth, grain, grapes, greens, olives, palms, orchards — close with two words that are general: fruit, and fodder. The generality is a summary device, and it does something the specifics cannot. After the enumerated crops, the reader is told that the enumeration was not exhaustive: whatever fruit there is, and whatever pasture there is, both belong to the same list. The technique prevents the passage from being read as a description of Ḥijāzī agriculture only. Rice, maize, apples, and the pastures of climates the audience had never heard of fall under the two closing terms, and the argument survives the change of place. A list that ended with the date palm would have been local. A list that ends with fruit and fodder is universal, and the universality is what allows the following verse to address the reader's own animals.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:32

> all as ˹a means of˺ sustenance for you and your animals.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Word That Carries a Term**

*Matāʿ* is from the root *m-t-ʿ*, which denotes enjoyment that lasts for a while and then ends. The same root gives *mutʿah*, a provision granted for a period, and *mātaʿa*, to allow someone enjoyment of a thing for a time. The noun in this verse is in the accusative of purpose: all of it — the pouring, the splitting, the grain, the grapes, the olives, the palms, the orchards, the fruit, the fodder — as a means of enjoyment for you and your animals. The word choice builds the ending into the provision. Nothing in the list is described as permanent, and the summary term refuses to make it so. The reader who has just moved through six verses of abundance arrives at a word that means temporary benefit, and the arrival is not a deflation but a specification. The provision is real, it is itemized, it is for you and for your animals, and it has a term.

**The Identical Phrase in the Ones Who Tear Out**

Sūrat al-Nāziʿāt closes its own list of provisions with the same sentence: *“all as ˹a means of˺ sustenance for you and your animals”* (79:33). The wording is identical, the position is identical — the end of an agricultural list — and the two sūrahs are neighbours in the received order and belong to the same Makkan phase. Al-Nāziʿāt's list is shorter and runs from the fixing of the mountains to the bringing forth of water and pasture (79:31–33), while this sūrah itemizes the crops in detail. Both close with the same purpose statement, and both go on immediately to the Day of Judgement. The shared formula indicates a settled pattern of argument in the early revelation: enumerate the provision, state its purpose in one line, and turn to the Blast. The pattern appears here in verses 24 to 33 without a single connective marking the turn, which is how strongly the two halves belong together.

**The Animals Inside the Purpose**

The provision is for you *and for your livestock*, and the inclusion is not an afterthought. Sūrat al-Naḥl devotes its opening verses to the cattle and their benefits: warmth, food, beauty, carrying loads to places unreachable without hardship (16:5–7). The animals are a provision in themselves and a consumer of provision, and this verse places them inside the purpose of the whole agricultural order. The pasture was grown for them, and they were created for the household. The arrangement answers an objection that a modern reader may bring to the list — that the fodder is not a human benefit — by stating that the benefit is mediated and that the mediation was intended. A household with animals has milk, meat, wool, transport, and labour, and every one of those comes through the fodder named in the previous verse. The purpose statement covers the chain, not just the last link.

**The List Ends on a Purpose, Not a Product**

Everything before this verse is a thing: water, earth, grain, grapes, greens, olives, palms, orchards, fruit, fodder. This verse is a reason. The shift converts an inventory into an argument, and the argument is that the inventory has a beneficiary and therefore a giver. A list of products standing alone could be read as a description of nature. A list closed by the statement that all of it is a provision for you and your animals cannot be so read, because provision implies provision for, and provision for implies a provider. The verse supplies the premise that the six preceding verses assumed, and it supplies it in the last possible position, after the reader has walked through the whole list and cannot claim he was not shown the items. The structure is that of a case presented before its conclusion, and the conclusion is one sentence long.

**Temporariness Built Into the Word**

The Qur'an uses this root repeatedly to mark the brevity of worldly benefit: *“˹There is only˺ brief enjoyment in this worldly life, then to Us is your return, and then We will inform you of what you used to do”* (10:23). The same idea governs the descriptions of respite granted to those who reject the message, where enjoyment is given for a term and then ends. Within this sūrah the term has a specific function. The provision is temporary, and the One who supplied it has already been described as the One who kills, buries, and raises. A temporary benefit supplied by a permanent will is the definition of a test, and the test is whether the recipient acknowledges the supplier while the benefit is running. Verse 17 answered that question for the human being as a species — he is ungrateful — and verse 32 supplies the material against which the answer is measured.

**The Whole Block as the Answer to the Condemnation**

Verses 24 to 32 form a single unit, and the unit is the evidence for the imprecation of verse 17. The command to consider food produced a sequence of nine acts and products, ending in a purpose that includes the reader's animals. Nothing in the sequence was produced by the reader, and everything in it is required for his continued existence. The condemnation of the human being for ingratitude now rests on an itemized record rather than on a general charge, and the record is one the reader can verify by looking out of a window or into a bowl. That verifiability is the method of the whole passage. The sūrah does not argue about the existence of a provider; it lists what has been provided and leaves the list standing between the reader and the charge. The next movement of the chapter leaves the field entirely and goes to the day when the eating stops.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:33

> Then, when the Deafening Blast comes to pass—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Name Taken From a Sound**

*Al-ṣākhkhah* is an active participle built from *ṣ-kh-kh*, a root denoting a blow or a cry that strikes the ear — the noise so loud that it deafens. The word is usually explained as the piercing cry, the one that reaches the hearing with the force of a blow, and the early commentators add that it will very nearly deafen whoever hears it. The naming is characteristic of Makkan eschatology, which prefers sounds and physical events to abstractions: the striking disaster, the overwhelming calamity, the inevitable reality, the deafening blast. Each name is a sensation rather than a concept, and the sensation is chosen so that the Day arrives in the body before it arrives in the mind. This sūrah took one of those names as an alternate title, and the tradition records it as al-Ṣākhkhah after this verse, which is a measure of how completely the word came to stand for the whole chapter.

**The Trumpet and Its Two Blasts**

The blast belongs to a fixed eschatological sequence. *“The Trumpet will be blown and all those in the heavens and all those on the earth will fall dead, except those Allah wills ˹to spare˺. Then it will be blown again and they will rise up at once, looking on ˹in anticipation˺”* (39:68). Two blows: the first kills, the second raises. Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah describes the accompanying upheaval: *“At last, when the Trumpet will be blown with one blast, and the earth and mountains will be lifted up and crushed with one blow”* (69:13–14). The Qur'an's trumpet is blown by an angel, and the tradition names Isrāfīl, though the name does not appear in the Qur'an itself. What the text supplies is the sound and its effects, and both are described in physical terms: a blowing, a falling, a rising, a crushing. This verse adds the sound's quality — that it strikes the ear — to the sequence.

**Which Blast the Word Names**

The commentators divide on whether *al-ṣākhkhah* is the second blast, at which the dead rise, or the first, at which everything dies. The case for the second is contextual: what follows in this sūrah is a description of the gathering, the flight from kin, and the two kinds of faces, all of which belong to the resurrection rather than to the dying. The case for the first is lexical: a cry that deafens fits the moment of dying, and the second blast is usually described as a rising rather than a striking. The contextual argument is stronger, since the events described in the following verses require living subjects. On that reading the word names the blow at which the dead come out, and the deafening quality belongs to the first sound heard by creatures returned to hearing. The disagreement does not affect the verse's function, which is to mark the boundary between the provision described in the previous verses and everything that comes after.

**A Word That Appears Once**

*Al-ṣākhkhah* occurs nowhere else in the Qur'an. It is one of a set of unique terms the Book coins for the Day, and the uniqueness has an effect on the reader: there is no other passage to compare it with, no earlier use to soften it, no established meaning to fall back on. The word arrives and must be understood from its root alone. That is how the Makkan revelations handle the last things generally — they give the Day names rather than descriptions, and the names are built from roots the audience knows from ordinary life. A blow to the ear, a striking disaster, a covering calamity. The audience can feel the meaning without being able to picture the event, and the inability to picture it is part of the point. A Day that could be imagined would be a Day that could be prepared for on the imagination's terms.

**A Condition Left Hanging**

Grammatically the verse is a conditional clause with no conclusion. *Idhā jāʾati al-ṣākhkhah* — when the deafening blast comes — and then nothing. The sentence is completed in the verse that follows, which supplies the time and the event. The suspension is deliberate and it produces a specific effect on the listener: the condition is raised, the ear waits for the consequence, and the wait is the length of a verse. In recitation the pause falls after the word for the blast, and the listener holds an unfinished sentence about the end of the world. The technique appears elsewhere in Makkan revelation, where a conditional about the Hour is followed by its answer in the next line. Here it functions as a hinge, and the hinge is the point at which the sūrah leaves the fields and the orchards behind.

**The End of the Provision**

Ten verses earlier the reader was commanded to consider his food, and six verses of agriculture followed — rain, split earth, grain, grapes, olives, palms, orchards, fruit, fodder — closing with the statement that all of it is a means of sustenance for a term. This verse is the arrival of the term. The pouring stops, the splitting stops, the eating stops, and the animals that were fed from the same provision are gone with it. Nothing in the verse says any of that; it names a sound. The sound is enough, because the reader has just walked through the entire system that produces his dinner and has been told it was given for a while. The blast is what "for a while" means. The juxtaposition of the two blocks — the list of provisions and the arrival of the cry — is the sūrah's central argument, and it is made without a single connecting word of explanation.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:34

> on that Day every person will flee from their own siblings,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Flight Rather Than Separation**

The verb is *yafirr*, from *f-r-r*, to flee — the movement of a person escaping a danger, not merely leaving a place. The Qur'an has other words for the ending of relationships on that Day: kinship is cut, no one asks about anyone, no soul helps another. This verse chooses the strongest of them. A man does not part from his brother; he runs from him. The choice implies that the brother has become a source of danger, and the implication is developed in the verses that follow, where the flight extends to parents, spouse, and children. Nothing in the verse explains why the flight occurs, and the explanation is supplied only in verse 37, after the list is complete. The reader is left for three verses with an image that has no reason attached to it: a crowd in which every person is running away from the people closest to him.

**The Nearest Person Named First**

The brother is the first in the list, and the order is the reverse of what sentiment would suggest. A poet describing the end of the world would begin with the child or the mother and work outward. This verse begins with the sibling and moves through the parents to the spouse and children, ending with the bond that a person would be expected to defend last. The ascending order is the shock: each verse takes a closer relation than the one before, and the reader discovers at the end that nothing was exempt. The brother is also the relation that in Arabian society carried the heaviest obligation of support — the clan brother was the one who stood with a man in a feud, paid his blood-money, and defended his honour. Starting with him names the collapse of the social structure at its load-bearing point.

**The Same List in the Ways of Ascent**

Sūrat al-Maʿārij describes the same scene and adds what the fleeing person would give to escape: *“˹although˺ they will be made to see each other. The wicked will wish to ransom themselves from the punishment of that Day by their children, their spouses, their siblings, their clan that sheltered them, and everyone on earth altogether, just to save themselves”* (70:11–14). The list there runs in the opposite direction — children, spouse, siblings, clan, everyone — and it discloses the motive this sūrah leaves implicit. The flight is not from the relative but from the cost of the relative: a person would hand over his whole network to save himself. The two passages complement each other exactly. Sūrat ʿAbasa gives the movement, al-Maʿārij gives the reason, and together they describe a Day in which the social self is liquidated by the person who owns it.

**Why a Man Runs From His Brother**

The exegetes supply an answer from the general teaching of the Qur'an about that Day: every person will be asked about what he owes to others, and the brother is a claimant. Rights between people are not dissolved by death, and on the Day they are settled. A man who wronged his brother in this life — over an inheritance, a debt, a slander, a refusal — meets him there as a creditor. The flight is therefore an attempt to avoid a settlement, and it is futile, because the settlement does not depend on proximity. The Qur'an states the principle without the story: *“˹They state˺ that no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another, and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards”* (53:38–39). No transfer is possible, and a claim that cannot be transferred cannot be avoided by running.

**No Friend Asks About His Friend**

Al-Maʿārij adds a detail that deepens this verse: *“And no close friend will ask ˹about˺ their friends”* (70:10). The close friend — *ḥamīm*, the one whose friendship is warm and intimate — does not inquire. The absence of inquiry is a stronger statement than the absence of help, since help might be impossible while inquiry costs nothing. On that Day even the question is not asked. Sūrat al-Muʾminūn puts it in the same terms: *“Then, when the Trumpet will be blown, there will be no kinship between them on that Day, nor will they ˹even care to˺ ask about one another”* (23:101). Kinship exists and is not merely suspended; it is not asked about. This verse states the same fact through the body rather than through the will: the person does not fail to inquire, he runs. The physical description carries what the abstract statement asserts.

**The Flight Achieves Nothing**

Nothing in the verse suggests that fleeing works. There is no destination, no refuge named, and no possibility of escape from a Day that gathers everyone at once. The movement is therefore not a strategy but a reflex, and describing it as a reflex is what makes the verse frightening. A person who calculates and fails is a person in a situation. A person who runs without a plan is a person whose self has come apart. The Qur'an's eschatology repeatedly presents the Day as a place where the ordinary instruments of the self — planning, alliance, negotiation, payment — are unavailable, and this verse presents that unavailability as a bodily action performed by everyone simultaneously. The crowd is not being scattered by an external force. It is scattering itself, and each person is the one doing the scattering in his own case.

The picture is completed by the silence that the Qur'an places over the same gathering. Sūrat al-Nabaʾ describes the Day as one in which the spirit and the angels stand in ranks and nobody speaks except one granted permission by the Most Compassionate, whose words are true (78:38). A crowd running from its own kin and saying nothing is the opposite of every crowd the reader has known. There is no shouting, no calling of names, no negotiation, no plea. The flight happens without speech, and the absence of speech removes the last instrument by which human beings manage danger. What is left is movement without communication, which is what the verse describes and then leaves standing without comment.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:35

> and ˹even˺ their mother and father,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Flight Turns to the Ones Who Made Him**

The list moves up a generation. Having run from his brother, the person runs from his mother and his father, and the two are named together in a single verse with no verb repeated — the flight of the previous verse governs them by ellipsis. The grammatical economy mirrors the moral fact: there is no separate act for the parents, only the continuation of one movement. The parents are the source of the person's existence in the ordinary sense, and in the Qur'an's account of that origin they are the instruments through which God's creating reaches him. To flee from them is to flee from the nearest thing in his life to a beginning he can point at. The verse does not comment on this and does not need to; the naming is the commentary, and it lands on the reader because the obligation towards parents is the one obligation the Qur'an states with the greatest insistence.

**The Strongest Command in the Qur'an's Social Law**

Nowhere is a human duty stated more forcefully. *“For your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him. And honour your parents. If one or both of them reach old age in your care, never say to them ˹even˺ ‘ugh,’ nor yell at them. Rather, address them respectfully. And be humble with them out of mercy, and pray, ‘My Lord! Be merciful to them as they raised me when I was young’”* (17:23–24). The command is attached directly to the command of worship, which places it at the head of the social law. *“And We have commanded people to ˹honour˺ their parents. Their mothers bore them through hardship upon hardship, and their weaning takes two years. So be grateful to Me and your parents. To Me is the final return”* (31:14) joins gratitude to parents with gratitude to God in a single sentence. The verse in this sūrah reverses both. The mother and father a person was commanded to honour are the ones he runs from, and the final return named in Luqmān has arrived.

**Kinship Ends at the Trumpet**

The Qur'an states the ending of relations in terms as flat as this verse's flight: *“Then, when the Trumpet will be blown, there will be no kinship between them on that Day, nor will they ˹even care to˺ ask about one another”* (23:101). The word used for kinship there, *ansāb*, is the same word that denotes the genealogies on which Arabian social identity rested. Lineage, the thing a man recited to establish who he was, is named as absent on that Day. This verse makes the same statement physically. The person does not merely lose his genealogy; he moves away from the people who constituted it. The two descriptions are complementary, and together they remove both the legal and the personal dimensions of the bond. What remains is an individual standing in a crowd, which is the condition the Qur'an describes as the condition of the reckoning.

**Mother and Father Named Separately**

The verse names both parents rather than using a collective term for parents, and the separation gives each its own weight. The mother's bond is the one the Qur'an stresses most heavily, because it is the one that involved the body: she bore him in hardship upon hardship, she carried him and weaned him over two years, and her bearing and weaning together are counted at thirty months (46:15). The father's bond is the one the social order stressed most heavily, because it carried lineage, protection, and inheritance. Naming both covers the two kinds of claim a parent holds, and the flight from both is therefore complete. Nothing in the verse ranks them or distinguishes between them; the conjunction places them side by side as a single destination of the same running.

**No Ransom, No Intercession, No Help**

The reason the flight is futile is stated elsewhere in explicit terms: *“Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another. No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given”* (2:48). Three instruments of rescue are named and refused. Help from a stronger party, intercession from a favoured one, and ransom paid from wealth — the three ways a person in danger is ordinarily saved, and all three are unavailable. The parent is precisely the party from whom a child expects the first and the third. A man who has been wronged looks to his family for defence and for payment. This verse places that expectation inside the flight: he runs from the very people who would once have supplied both, because on that Day they can supply neither and their presence would only add a claim to his account.

**The Reversal of a Gratitude That Included the Parents**

Luqmān's command was to be grateful to God and to the parents together (31:14), and the joining is remarkable: gratitude to a creature is placed inside gratitude to the Creator, and the reason given is that the parents were the means of the life for which the gratitude is owed. This sūrah opened with a condemnation of the human being for ingratitude, and its account of provision ran from the drop to the food on the table. The parents belong to that account, since they are part of the ordainment described in verse 19. On the Day of the blast the same person runs from them. The ingratitude that covered over the provision now covers over the providers, and the running is its final form. Nothing in the verse draws the connection; the sūrah's arrangement draws it.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:36

> and ˹even˺ their spouse and children.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Two Bonds**

The list reaches its end with the two relations a person chooses or produces rather than inherits: *ṣāḥibah*, the companion, the wife; and *banūn*, sons. Everything before this was given to a man at birth — a brother, a mother, a father. These two are made in the course of a life, and they are the relations the Qur'an most often names as the content of worldly attachment. Ending the list with them closes the argument from the strongest direction: not only the family a person was born into, but the family he built. The verse completes the inventory of human belonging, and it completes it in a single line. Nothing remains after it. A reader who has followed the three verses has watched every bond he possesses named and then seen it running away from him, and the sequence ends with the ones whose loss he would have called impossible.

**The Word for the Companion**

*Ṣāḥibah* is the feminine of *ṣāḥib*, one who accompanies, and it is used in the Qur'an for a wife with a consistency that makes it a technical term. The word appears in the jinn's declaration about God: *“˹Now, we believe that˺ our Lord—Exalted is His Majesty—has neither taken a mate nor offspring”* (72:3), where the pair *ṣāḥibah* and *walad* is used to deny any companion or child of the divine. The same pair recurs in the Makkan sūrahs as a formula of denial. Its use here is different but relies on the same sense: the companion is the one who shares a life. The choice of a word built on companionship rather than on contract or possession is significant in a verse about the Day, because companionship is the aspect of marriage that the Day ends. The contract is dissolved by death; the companionship is dissolved by the flight.

**Children as the Adornment of This Life**

*Banūn* is the plural of son, and the Qur'an uses it, together with wealth, as the standard pair for what makes a life look successful. *“Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope”* (18:46). *“Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children”* (57:20). The two verses treat children as an adornment and as an object of competition, and both contrast them with what lasts. This verse places them inside the flight, and the placement is the practical form of the same contrast. A man runs from the thing he counted as his adornment, and the running is the demonstration that the counting was mistaken. Nothing in the verse says so; it lists the children after the spouse and lets the list carry the meaning.

**The Same Order in the Other Account**

Sūrat al-Maʿārij gives the ransom list in the reverse order: *“The wicked will wish to ransom themselves from the punishment of that Day by their children, their spouses, their siblings, their clan that sheltered them”* (70:11–13). Children come first there and siblings third, while this sūrah runs sibling, parents, spouse, children. The two orders are not contradictory; they reflect different emphases. Al-Maʿārij lists what a person would give up, and the willingness begins with what he holds most expensive, which is why the children come first. Sūrat ʿAbasa lists what he runs from, and the running begins with the nearest relation available and works towards the closest, which is why the children come last. Both orders end on the same point: nothing is held back, and the network a person built over a lifetime is offered or abandoned in full.

**The Bonds Were Real**

Nothing in this passage suggests that the relations were illusory or that the love was false. The Qur'an describes marriage as a dwelling and a mercy elsewhere, and it treats the love of children as natural. The flight is not a revelation that the bonds were fake; it is a consequence of the Day's structure, in which no transfer of any kind is possible. A mother who loves her son cannot carry his account, and a son who honours his father cannot answer for him. The bonds remain and their efficacy ends. That is a harder statement than the claim that worldly attachments are illusions, because it grants the attachments their full reality and then removes what they were expected to do. The verse is short and it makes the statement by listing, and the listing works only if the reader already believes the bonds are real.

**A Ransom That Cannot Be Paid**

Al-Maʿārij's formulation is explicit about the direction of the wish: the wicked want to ransom themselves *with* their children and spouses. The preposition makes the family into currency, and the currency is refused. This sūrah's flight is the same fact seen from the other side — the person does not offer his family, he runs from them — and the two pictures describe one reality from opposite ends. A man who would hand over his children to save himself is a man who will also run from them when handing them over is impossible. The verse's silence about the motive is what makes it apply to everyone in the crowd. Not every person on that Day wished to ransom himself with his family; every person flees from it, and the fleeing is described without qualification or exception.

Al-Maʿārij's list ends by widening past the family altogether: the clan that sheltered him, and everyone on earth together, given up so that he might be saved (70:14). The movement from children to the whole human race shows that the ransom is not really about the people offered. It is about the scale of the price a person will name when he believes the alternative is unbearable, and the scale ends at everything. This verse stops earlier and for that reason lands harder, since it names only the four relations that constitute a life — brother, mother, father, spouse, children — and stops there. Nothing beyond the household is offered, because nothing beyond it was ever held. The flight is measured by the same inventory.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:37

> For then everyone will have enough concern of their own.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Explains the Flight**

Three verses described a movement; this one gives its reason. *Kullu imriʾin yawmaʾidhin shaʾn yughnīh* — every person on that Day will have an affair that suffices him. The sentence is explanatory in form and stands apart from the list it accounts for, which is unusual in this sūrah: the passage generally states and moves on, leaving the connections to the reader. Here the connection is supplied. The reason nobody helps anybody, nobody asks about anybody, and nobody stays near anybody is that each person is fully occupied by his own case. The explanation is not psychological speculation; it is a statement about the distribution of attention on that Day. Attention is finite, and on that Day it is entirely consumed. Nothing is left over for another person, however beloved.

**A Root Returning With Its Meaning Reversed**

The verb *yughnīh* is from *gh-n-y*, the same root as *istaghnā* in verse 5 — the one who deemed himself beyond need. The root's sense is sufficiency: to be free of need, to have enough, to dispense with. In verse 5 sufficiency was a claim made by a man about himself, and the claim was the substance of his indifference. Here sufficiency is imposed on every person by his own affair, and it is not a comfort but a closure. The man who thought he needed nothing discovered a state in which he genuinely has nothing to give; the man who needed everything discovers a state in which his need cannot be met by anyone. The root runs through the sūrah from the room in Mecca to the crowd on the Day, and the two uses measure each other. Self-sufficiency claimed in this life is the same word as self-sufficiency experienced in the next, and the experience is the refutation of the claim.

**The Ḥadīth That Quotes This Verse**

The verse is the answer given by the Prophet to a question from ʿĀʾishah, in a report recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6527 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 5107 with authenticity agreed upon. He said that the people will be assembled barefoot, naked, and uncircumcised. She asked whether men and women would then look at one another. His answer was that the matter would be too serious for them to notice. The report supplies the context in which the early community understood these verses: the Day as a gathering in which the ordinary occasions of shame, modesty, and attention have been removed, because every person's own case has taken the whole of his awareness. The ḥadīth does not quote the verse word for word, but the commentaries connect them, and the connection is exact in substance — a person too occupied to notice what would otherwise be unavoidable.

**Every Account Stands Alone**

The principle is stated as a doctrine elsewhere and is one of the Qur'an's most repeated eschatological claims: *“˹They state˺ that no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another, and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards”* (53:38–39). No bearing of another's load, no receiving of another's earning. Sūrat al-Baqarah states the same with the instruments of rescue refused: no soul helps another, no intercession accepted, no ransom taken (2:48). This verse supplies the experiential ground of the doctrine: the reason the transfer does not happen is not only that it is forbidden but that nobody is in a position to attempt it. A person with an affair that fills him has no capacity left for a transaction. The doctrine and the description are two statements of the same fact, one in the language of law and one in the language of attention.

**An Affair That Is a Possession**

The construction is worth pressing: *shaʾn yughnīh* — an affair that suffices him. The affair is described as though it were a possession, something a person has and that meets his needs. In ordinary usage the phrase would be positive; to have what suffices is to be provided for. Applied to the Day, the same phrase becomes a description of isolation, because what suffices is a burden. The irony is contained in the grammar rather than stated, and it is one of the sharpest turns in the sūrah. The man of verse 5 had wealth that sufficed him and made him indifferent to a seeker. The person of this verse has an affair that suffices him and makes him indifferent to his own mother. In both cases sufficiency produces the withdrawal of attention, and in both cases the withdrawal is the moral content of the scene.

**What the Verse Removes From the Day**

Coalitions, families, clans, patrons, and clients are the structures through which a person in the ancient world faced danger, and the verse removes all of them at once. There is no bloc on that Day, no group that presents itself together, no one whose case is strengthened by association. Every structure built for collective defence has dissolved, and what remains is a single person and a single affair. The Qur'an's insistence on this point is one of its most distinctive features, since the society it addressed was organized almost entirely around collective responsibility. A man's blood-money was paid by his clan; his honour was defended by his brothers; his debts were settled by his patrons. The verse announces the end of that arrangement, and the announcement is made in the plainest possible terms: everyone will have enough to occupy him.

The translation renders *yughnīh* as "will have enough concern of their own," which chooses the sense of preoccupation over the sense of sufficiency. Both are inside the verb, and the choice is defensible: a man whose affair fills him has no room for another's, so the sufficiency and the preoccupation are the same fact described from two sides. The alternative rendering — his affair suffices him — keeps the echo with verse 5 audible to an English reader, at the cost of sounding like comfort. The preoccupation reading avoids that false comfort, and it is the reading most English translations follow. Neither translation alters the point, which is that the capacity for attention has been fully consumed and nothing is left over to be given away.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:38

> On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Face as the Evidence of the Day**

After the flight and the isolation, the sūrah returns to the face, and the return is a structural choice rather than a change of subject. The chapter opened with a face: he frowned and turned away. It closes with faces: some bright, some dusty. Everything in between — the reminder, the honoured pages, the condemnation of ingratitude, the drop, the grave, the rain, the orchards, the blast — is held between those two images. The face is the Qur'an's chosen index of the person because it is the part of a human being that cannot be arranged for long. A body can be dressed, a speech can be prepared, a reputation can be managed; a face at the moment of recognition discloses. The Day described in this sūrah is a day of disclosure, and the disclosure is placed where it cannot be controlled.

**A Face That Has Dawned**

*Musfirah* is the active participle of *asfara*, to become bright, and the verb is used of the dawn — *isfār* is the moment when light spreads and the horizon clears. A face described as *musfirah* has therefore dawned: it has become luminous from within, in the way a sky becomes luminous before the sun appears. The same root gives *safara*, to uncover or unveil, and *sāfir*, which is why the word for the angel-scribes in verse 15 comes from a related form. The image is of a covering removed and a light appearing. Nothing in the verse says what causes the brightness, and the withholding is important: the brightness is described as a state of the face rather than as a reward being handed over, which means it precedes any announcement and discloses the person before he is addressed.

**The Same Picture in the Resurrection Sūrah**

Sūrat al-Qiyāmah describes the two groups in nearly identical terms: *“On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, looking at their Lord. And ˹other˺ faces will be gloomy, anticipating something devastating to befall them”* (75:22–25). The correspondence is close enough to read the two passages together, and the difference is instructive. Al-Qiyāmah adds the object of the bright faces' gaze — they look at their Lord — and the object of the gloomy faces' expectation. This sūrah adds the affect: laughing and rejoicing, dusty and cast in gloom. Between them the two passages give the complete picture, and neither is redundant. The shared opening phrase, *wujūhun yawmaʾidhin*, marks the formula as a settled piece of Makkan eschatology, used whenever the Day is described in terms of the two outcomes.

**What Makes a Face Bright**

The commentators offer several answers, and they are not competing so much as complementary. Some point to the traces of ablution, since the face is the part of the body washed most often in the ritual and the one that will carry the mark of that washing. Others point to the night vigil, whose effect on a face is proverbial in the Islamic tradition. Others again point to relief — the face of a person from whom the burdens and attachments of this world have been removed shows it, and the removal happens on that Day. The simplest explanation is joy at the reward, which is what the following verse states directly. All four readings agree on the mechanism: the face shows what the person has done and what has been done for him, and neither can be concealed at that point. The brightness is not bestowed as a decoration; it is the visible form of a condition already established.

**Radiance and Joy Granted Together**

Sūrat al-Insān names the two in a single promise: *“So Allah will deliver them from the horror of that Day, and grant them radiance and joy”* (76:11). The pairing there is the same as the pairing in this sūrah's two verses — a bright face and a rejoicing heart — and both are given as gifts rather than achievements. The delivery from the horror comes first, which establishes that the radiance is not the natural condition of a person who arrives at the Day unafraid; the fear is real and it is removed. This verse's brightness is therefore the brightness of someone who has been through the terror described in verses 34 to 37 and come out of it. The flight from kin, the isolation, the sufficiency of his own affair — all of that happened to him too, and the brightness is what remains after it.

**The Face That Was Overlooked in Mecca**

The sūrah's own occasion gives the image a particular reference. The blind man whose arrival caused a frown could not see the face turned away from him, and his own face was invisible to the room in the sense that nobody looked at it. On the Day described here, faces are the only thing visible, and his is among the bright ones. The exegetes make the connection explicitly, reading verses 38 to 39 as the state of the seeker and verses 40 to 42 as the state of the one who deemed himself beyond need. The reading is not required by the wording, which is general, but it is the reading the sūrah invites by returning to the face it began with. What was contracted in the first verse is radiant in this one, and the reversal is the chapter's final argument about the value of the person who came asking.

The face carries this weight because the Qur'an treats it as the part of a person that endures and the part that perishes. Sūrat al-Raḥmān sets the two against each other in a single breath: *“Every being on earth is bound to perish. Only your Lord Himself, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain ˹forever˺”* (55:26–27). Everything with a face passes, and the passage is stated as a law rather than a lament. On the Day described here the faces have returned, and they have returned as the visible record of what each person did with the time in which his face was perishing. The brightness is not a reward attached to a face from outside; it is the face that a life produced, shown at last without the means of concealment.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:39

> laughing and rejoicing,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Laughter on the Day of Terror**

Two participles describe the bright faces, and the first is the more surprising. *Ḍāḥikah* — laughing. The Day has just been described as a blast that deafens, a crowd in which every person flees his own family, and an isolation so complete that each person's affair fills him. Laughter does not belong in that description, and its appearance is a deliberate reversal. The Qur'an is not describing a general happiness but a specific physical act, in a place where the only other acts mentioned are running and being occupied. The laugh is the body's testimony that the danger has passed, and it can only occur after the danger was real. A person who was never afraid does not laugh with relief. The verse therefore carries the whole of the preceding terror inside a single participle, and it does so without referring back to it.

**Two Words for Two Levels of the Same State**

The second participle, *mustabshirah*, is from *b-sh-r*, the root of good news and of skin — *bashar*, the visible surface of a person, and *bashshara*, to give glad tidings. The tenth form used here means to be gladdened, to rejoice at news received. The two words are not synonyms stacked for emphasis. Laughter is visible and involuntary; rejoicing at good news is interior and caused by an announcement. The verse gives both, and the order matters: the face laughs, and beneath the laugh is the reception of tidings. The pairing covers the whole person — the expression he cannot control and the joy he has been given a reason for. English renders the second as rejoicing, which loses the connection to news, and the loss conceals the fact that this joy has an author and a message behind it.

**The Good News Arrives Before the Joy**

The Qur'an describes the announcement that produces this state: *“Surely those who say, ‘Our Lord is Allah,’ and then remain steadfast, the angels descend upon them, ˹saying,˺ ‘Do not fear, nor grieve. Rather, rejoice in the good news of Paradise, which you have been promised’”* (41:30). The command to rejoice is attached to a removal — do not fear, do not grieve — and then to a gift. Sūrat Āl ʿImrān describes those killed in God's cause as *“rejoicing in Allah's bounties and being delighted for those yet to join them. There will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve”* (3:170), and adds that they are joyful for receiving grace and for the confirmation that God does not deny the reward of the believers (3:171). The rejoicing of this verse is of the same kind: it is a response to something told, not a temperament. The faces laugh because they have been addressed.

**The Laughter Reversed From This World**

Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn records the other direction of the same act: *“Indeed, the wicked used to laugh at the believers, wink to one another whenever they passed by”* (83:29–30), and closes the account with the reversal — *“But on that Day the believers will be laughing at the disbelievers”* (83:34). Laughter in the Qur'an is therefore not a single thing. It is an expression of contempt in this world and an expression of deliverance in the next, and the same muscle performs both. This verse belongs to the second category, and the category is defined by what has been survived. The believers of Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn were laughed at while they were weak and unable to answer; their laughter on the Day is not a retaliation but a release, and it is described in the same physical terms as the laughter here.

**Laughter in the Qur'an Is Rare and Occasioned**

The Book does not use this verb often, and each use is tied to a specific event. Sarah laughs when she is given the news of a son in her old age, and the laugh is followed immediately by the gift: *“And his wife was standing by, so she laughed, then We gave her good news of ˹the birth of˺ Isaac, and, after him, Jacob”* (11:71). Solomon does not laugh but smiles at the ant's speech, and his smile produces a prayer of gratitude: *“So Solomon smiled in amusement at her words, and prayed, ‘My Lord! Inspire me to ˹always˺ be thankful for Your favours which You have blessed me and my parents with’”* (27:19). In both cases the laughter or smiling is a response to something unheard-of that has been granted. This verse's laughing faces belong to the same pattern, and the pattern identifies the cause: something has been given that was not expected.

**Rejoicing as the Opposite of the Frown**

The sūrah's first image was a face contracting, and its last happy image is a face opening into laughter. Between them lie the whole of the chapter's argument: the reminder, the pages, the provision, the blast, the flight. The structural pairing is exact and it is the reason the chapter ends where it does. A frown is a face closed against a person who came asking; laughter is a face opened by a gift that was asked for and granted. The one who frowned was corrected in ten verses, and the correction is completed not by an apology but by a picture of what faces become when they are no longer arranging themselves by social weight. Nothing in this verse refers to the opening scene, and the reference does not need to be made. The vocabulary of the face does the work across forty-two verses.

Both words are active participles, and the form describes a continuing state rather than a single act. The faces are not laughing and then stopping; they are laughers and rejoicers, settled in the condition. The same form is used of the believers elsewhere in the Qur'an, where the rejoicing is described as their standing characteristic — rejoicing in God's bounties, joyful for the grace received, certain that the reward of the believers will not be denied (3:170–171). The participle fixes the state as permanent, which is the difference between this laughter and the laughter of Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn, where the wicked laughed at the believers in the past tense, in a world that ended.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:40

> while ˹other˺ faces will be dusty,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dust on the Face**

*ʿAlayhā ghabarah* — upon them is dust. The word *ghabarah* denotes the fine dust that settles on a surface, the powder raised by a road or a wind and left lying on what it touches. The preposition is important: the dust is *upon* the faces, not merely near them, and the position implies that it has settled and stayed. A face covered in dust is a face that has been on the ground, or in a wind, or in a crowd where the earth has been disturbed, and it has not been washed. The image is ordinary enough that every listener knows it, and its ordinariness is what makes it work as a description of the Day. Nothing supernatural is asserted. The faces of one group are dusty in the way a traveller's face is dusty, and the dust is the visible form of a condition the verse does not name.

**The Opposite of Brightness, Not Its Absence**

The two descriptions are contraries rather than contradictories. A face that is not bright might simply be unlit; a face that is dusty has something on it. The Qur'an chooses the second, and the choice adds an element that the first does not have: a covering. Dust is what lies on a thing and hides its surface, and the hiding is the humiliation. In the Arabian world the face was the seat of honour — a man's face was what he presented, what he protected, and what he would not allow to be struck or soiled. To have dust on the face in front of a crowd is to have lost the protection of one's own honour. The verse states that loss without naming it, and it states it in the terms of a physical condition rather than a moral verdict. The verdict comes two verses later; the dust comes first, as evidence.

**The Same Pair in the Resurrection Sūrah**

Sūrat al-Qiyāmah sets the two groups in the same order and adds what the dusty faces expect: *“And ˹other˺ faces will be gloomy, anticipating something devastating to befall them”* (75:24–25). The addition supplies the interior state that this sūrah leaves to the following verse, where the dust is described as cast in gloom. Between the two passages the description is complete: a bright face that laughs at news received, and a dusty face that darkens in anticipation of a calamity seen coming. The shared formula — faces on that Day, some such and some otherwise — is one of the fixed patterns of Makkan eschatology, and its recurrence across neighbouring sūrahs indicates a settled way of describing the outcome rather than a poetic choice made fresh each time.

**Both Groups in the Same Crowd**

The dust and the brightness are visible to each other, and the visibility is part of the description. Sūrat al-Maʿārij states that the people will be made to see one another even while each is fleeing and each is occupied by his own affair (70:11). The Day is not a set of private judgements delivered separately; it is a gathering in which the outcome of every person is public. This verse's dusty faces are dusty in front of the bright ones, and the bright ones are bright in front of them. Nothing in the verse says so explicitly, and nothing needs to, because the whole passage is set in a single place on a single day. The public character of the two conditions is what makes the face the chosen index: a private state would not require a face.

**The Substance They Were Made From**

The Qur'an describes the human being's origin as dust and clay with a consistency that gives this image a second layer. He was created from dust, then from a drop, then formed into a man (18:37); the earth was made a place of return. A face covered in dust on the Day of gathering is therefore a face returned visibly to the material of its beginning, and the return happens while the person is alive and aware. The bright faces have been changed; the dusty faces have been restored to what they were made of. Nothing in the verse states this, and it should not be pressed as the primary sense, which is plainly humiliation. The layer is there because the sūrah has already described the origin in detail, and a reader who has moved from the drop to the grave to the blast reads the dust with the whole sequence behind it.

**A Humiliation That Is Public and Undeniable**

Dust cannot be argued with. A man can dispute a verdict, deny an accusation, or claim that his case has been misunderstood; he cannot claim that his face is not dusty. The Qur'an's choice of a physical mark for the two outcomes removes the possibility of contest, and it does so in a scene where every person has just been described as isolated and occupied with his own affair. There is no advocate, no clan, no argument, and now no ambiguity about the result. The result is on the face, in the open, in front of everyone. The verse is the sūrah's answer to the imprecation of verse 17, which condemned the human being for ingratitude without itemizing the consequences. Here the consequence is itemized, and it is itemized in a form that the reader can picture without being told how to feel about it.

There is also a literal ground for the dust. The Day arrives with the earth itself destroyed: *“At last, when the Trumpet will be blown with one blast, and the earth and mountains will be lifted up and crushed with one blow”* (69:13–14). A world in which the ground and the mountains have been pulverized is a world full of dust, and the gathering takes place in it. The dust on these faces is therefore not only a sign but a consequence of where they are standing, and the sign is chosen from the physical condition of the scene. The bright faces are bright in the same air, which is what makes the two descriptions contraries rather than mere differences: the same dust is in the atmosphere, and it settles on one group and not on the other.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:41

> cast in gloom—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Darkening That Covers**

*Qatarah* denotes a darkening, a blackness or dust-coloured gloom that comes over a thing. The root *q-t-r* is connected with what is murky and obscured, and the word is used of a face that has gone dark. The commentators explain it in two directions: as a blackness that settles over the features, and as a covering like smoke or dust-cloud that closes the face off from view. Both explanations describe the same visible fact — the face is no longer legible — and they differ only in whether the darkening is a change of colour or the arrival of something over it. The verb form in the verse presents the gloom as cast or thrown upon the face, which supports the second explanation: the darkness comes from outside and lands. It is not a mood but an event on a surface.

**Two Marks for Two Faults**

The exegetes divide the two descriptions between the two charges that follow in verse 42. Dust, on this reading, is the punishment of disbelief, and gloom is the punishment of wickedness; the face is soiled for what it refused to acknowledge and darkened for what it did. The division is neat and it corresponds to a real distinction in the Qur'an's moral vocabulary, where what a person believes and what a person does are named separately and judged separately. The reading is not forced by the grammar, since both marks are simply described as present on the same faces. It survives because it gives each adjective a reason, and a description of the Day that pairs two marks with two charges is more coherent than one that doubles a single mark for emphasis. The doubling is possible but adds nothing, which is why the tradition preferred the division.

**The Face That Sees What Is Coming**

Sūrat al-Qiyāmah attaches an expectation to the gloomy faces: they are *“anticipating something devastating to befall them”* (75:25). The anticipation explains the darkening better than anything else in the description, since a face that has understood what is about to happen to it changes. The gloom here is therefore not only a mark imposed but a state endured — the person knows, and the knowing is on him. This sūrah leaves the expectation unstated and describes only the result, which is a harder picture: the reader sees the darkness without seeing what produced it. The unstated cause is supplied by everything the chapter has already described, and the supply is immediate, because the blast and the flight and the isolation have been narrated in the preceding eight verses.

**Darkness as the Qur'an's Image of Loss**

Light and its absence are the Qur'an's governing pair of images for guidance and its refusal. The verse of light describes guidance as a lamp lit from a blessed tree, light upon light (24:35), and the passage that follows describes the opposite as a darkness that cannot be seen through: *“Or ˹their deeds are˺ like the darkness in a deep sea, covered by waves upon waves, topped by ˹dark˺ clouds. Darkness upon darkness! If one stretches out their hand, they can hardly see it. And whoever Allah does not bless with light will have no light!”* (24:40). The gloom on these faces belongs to that order. It is not a shadow but the absence of the blessing described in the other passage, and its presence on a face is the visible form of a refusal that was made long before the Day.

**Dust and Gloom in Sequence**

The two verses describe one face twice, and the order moves from the surface to the whole. Dust settles on a face; gloom closes it in. The first is a substance and the second is a condition, and the second intensifies the first rather than adding to it. Arabic rhetoric uses this kind of doubled description to build a picture by accumulation, and the accumulation here produces a face that is both soiled and obscured — unclean and unreadable at the same time. The brightness of verse 38 was described in a single word and then given its affect in the next verse, laughing and rejoicing. The darkness is described in two words across two verses and given its verdict in the third. The two groups are described at equal length, and the equality is itself a statement: the outcomes are of the same magnitude in opposite directions.

Both descriptions are nominal sentences with no verb, which in Arabic gives them permanence: dust upon them, gloom cast over them. Nothing happens in either verse; a condition simply holds. The absence of a verb removes any sense of process or development, and the removal is what distinguishes these faces from the bright ones, whose brightness is described with a participle that implies a dawning. One group has become something; the other has settled into something. The two grammars correspond to the two states, and the correspondence is achieved without a word of explanation.

The gifts promised to the other group are named elsewhere as radiance and joy (76:11), and the two things missing here are exactly those two. Where radiance should be there is dust; where joy should be there is gloom. The subtraction is complete, and it is stated by description rather than by contrast, so the reader performs it himself.

**The Face Pronounces Before the Verdict**

Verse 42 will name the people with the dusty, gloomy faces as the disbelievers and the wicked. This verse comes before that naming, and it does the work the naming cannot. A verdict can be disputed; a face cannot. The reader has already seen the condition before he is told who is in it, and the seeing produces a recognition that the telling could not. The Qur'an's eschatological descriptions consistently work this way — the state is pictured, then identified — and the order makes the identification feel like a confirmation rather than an assertion. The gloom is cast upon the face in the same passage where the brightness dawns on another face, and the two pictures stand next to each other with nothing between them except the conjunction that separates the groups.

## Sūrah ʿAbasa 80:42

> those are the disbelievers, the ˹wicked˺ sinners.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict in Two Plurals**

*Ulāʾika humu al-kafaratu al-fajarah* — those are the disbelievers, the wicked. The sentence is a nominal identification with an emphatic pronoun in the middle, and the emphasis excludes doubt: those, they and no others, are the disbelievers and the wicked. Two broken plurals carry the verdict, and both are intensive forms. The verse ends the sūrah, and it ends it with a statement of fact rather than a threat. Nothing is promised in the way of punishment; no fire is named, no duration given, no address made to the Prophet or to the reader. The chapter closes on a classification, and the classification is attached to faces that have already been described. The reader who has followed the description does not need the naming to know who is meant, and the naming is given anyway, because a verdict that is not pronounced has not been delivered.

**Belief and Conduct Named Together**

*Kufr* covers what a person holds, and *fujūr* covers what he does. The exegetes draw the distinction explicitly: disbelief defines the thoughts and wickedness the actions, and the two charges correspond to the two marks on the faces described in the previous verses. The pairing is not redundant. A person may disbelieve without acting wickedly, and the Qur'an acknowledges such people; he may act wickedly while professing belief, and the Qur'an is full of them. The verse describes those in whom both are present, and the completeness is what justifies the intensive plurals. The same pairing appears throughout the Qur'an's descriptions of the two outcomes: *“Indeed, the virtuous will be in bliss, and the wicked will be in Hell”* (82:13–14), where the division is drawn on conduct and belief together rather than on either alone.

**Fujūr in the Qur'an's Moral Vocabulary**

The root *f-j-r* means to break open, to cleave, to burst forth, and it is used of water breaking out of the ground and of dawn breaking — *fajr* is the daybreak, the hour at which the night is split. Applied to a person it denotes a breaking out of the bounds that were set for him, and the Qur'an states that the capacity for it was installed at the beginning: *“And by the soul and ˹the One˺ Who fashioned it, then with ˹the knowledge of˺ right and wrong inspired it!”* (91:7–8). The two possibilities are inspired into the same soul, and the verse that follows makes the outcome depend on what the person does with them. *Fujūr* is therefore not an alien condition imposed from outside but one of two directions built into the creature. That is what makes the charge in this verse so complete: the wicked were made capable of the opposite and chose this.

**The Intensive Plural Forms**

Both nouns are broken plurals of a type that in Arabic often carries intensification or generality rather than simple number. *Kafarah* is the plural of *kāfir*, and *fajarah* of *fājir*, and the pattern used for both is one that collects a class rather than counting its members. The effect is to describe a kind of person rather than a list of individuals, and the description is fixed by the demonstrative at the start of the verse: those. The faces described in the previous two verses are pointed at, and the pointing identifies a class. Nothing in the verse names an individual, and the anonymity matches the anonymity of the man who deemed himself beyond need at the start of the sūrah. The chapter begins with an unnamed notable and ends with an unnamed class, and both are defined by their inner state rather than by their lineage.

**The Sūrah Closes Where It Began**

Verse 17 pronounced a condemnation: condemned are human beings, and how ungrateful they are. Verse 42 identifies who the condemnation falls on. Between the two lies the whole of the chapter's evidence — the reminder, the honoured pages, the scribes, the drop, the ordainment, the easing, the death, the burial, the resurrection, the failure to comply, the pouring rain, the split earth, the grain, the vines, the olives, the palms, the orchards, the fruit, the fodder, the provision for a term, the blast, the flight, the isolation, and the two kinds of face. The imprecation at the start was general and could be resisted as rhetoric. The identification at the end comes after an itemized case, and the case is made of things the reader has eaten. The word for ingratitude in verse 17 and the word for disbelief in this verse are the same word, and the sūrah has spent twenty-five verses showing what was covered over.

**An Ending With No Consolation Attached**

Many Makkan sūrahs close with a turn towards the reader or the Prophet: take the path back to your Lord (78:39), the tranquil soul called to return well-pleased (89:27–28), a command to prostrate and draw near. This one does not. It ends on the class of the disbelievers and the wicked, and the last word is the second of the two charges. The absence of consolation is a choice, and it suits a sūrah that began with a rebuke. The chapter is addressed throughout to a person who needs correcting — a Messenger about attention, a reader about ingratitude — and it ends by showing the outcome of the uncorrected state without softening it. What remains after the last word is the picture the sūrah has been building since the first verse: a face that contracted against a blind man who came asking, and the faces that contract and the faces that shine on the Day when every person's affair suffices him.

The chapter therefore ends where it began, with the same organ and the same disclosure. In Mecca a face betrayed a preference that its owner had not spoken aloud. On the Day every face betrays an outcome that its owner cannot speak away. Between the two stands the entire provision of the world — the pages carried by noble hands, the rain poured on split ground, the grain and the vines and the orchards, the term set on all of it — and the provision is what the disbelievers and the wicked covered over. The last word of the sūrah names the covering as a way of acting, and the naming is the end of the argument.
