# Sūrah al-Takwīr (Chapter 81) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah of the Enveloping**

Al-Takwīr, "the Enveloping," is an early Meccan sūrah of twenty-nine verses, named for its first word — *takwīr*, the wrapping up of the sun — and also carried by the title "When the Sun Is Enfolded." The sūrah stands in the company of the short Meccan sūrahs that open with the signs of the Hour, and the Book's own report gives it a standing office in that company: a narration from Ibn ʿUmar, recorded in the Musnad of Aḥmad and also in al-Tirmidhī, has the Prophet ﷺ directing whoever wishes to look at the Day of Judgment as if seeing it with his own eyes to read this sūrah, then its neighbour that opens with the sky splitting, then the one that opens with the sky split asunder. Three sūrahs, one mirror, and this sūrah is the first of the frames.

**Two Movements, One Argument**

The sūrah moves in two clear sections. The first (verses 1–14) is the list of the Day: a series of "when" clauses describing the unmaking of the world — the sun wrapped, the stars fallen, the mountains moved, the tenth-month camels abandoned, the wild beasts gathered, the seas set on fire, the souls paired, the buried girl asked, the records spread, the sky stripped, the fire kindled, the garden brought near — and a single principal clause at the end: each soul will know what it has brought. The second (verses 15–29) is the defence of the Book: an oath on the receding stars, the falling night, and the breaking day, sworn to the claim that the Qur'an is the word of a noble messenger — full of power, held in honour at the Throne, obeyed, trustworthy — whose receiver was seen by the prophet on the clear horizon, who does not withhold the unseen, and whose word no outcast devil can claim. The sūrah closes with its offer: the word is a reminder to the worlds, for whoever wills to take the straight path, and no one wills except by the will of the Lord of the worlds.

**The Frame**

The sūrah begins with the world's order and ends with the world's Lord. Its first verse is the sun's enshrouding, the first of the cosmos's reversals, and its last verse is the formula of the Book's opening sūrah — the Lord of the worlds — whose will grounds the creature's. Between the two, the sūrah carries its argument from the end of the world to the origin of the word, and from the origin of the word to the will the word addresses: the account, the defence, and the offer, in that order, each standing on the one before it.
## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:1

> When the sun is put out,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sun Wrapped Like Cloth**

The first word of the sūrah is also its name: *takwīr*, the wrapping up. The Arabic verb behind it, *kuwwirat*, carries the image of fabric being folded — a garment gathered and rolled until it no longer gives light. On that Day the sun does not simply dim. It is taken up, gathered, and set aside, the way a carpet is rolled off a floor. The early commentators gave the verb several faces, and all of them point the same direction: the light is wiped out, the disc is absorbed, one part is folded into another and the whole is cast away. A narration preserved in the tafsīr literature states the same in its plainest form, that the sun and the moon will be rolled up on the Day of Resurrection. The verb refuses any gentle reading. What the translation renders *put out* is closer to *enfolded away* than to *switched off*.

**The "When" That Must Come**

The clause opens with *idhā*, the Arabic particle for a time whose arrival is certain and near — the "when" of things that will surely happen, as opposed to the "when" used for settled past events. Every clause in the opening stanza uses this same particle, and the choice is not decorative. The sūrah is built out of certainties. Each "when" is a verdict, not a hypothesis, and the listener is not invited to imagine a possibility but handed a schedule. This is the sūrah's first rhetorical act, and it precedes its images: before describing what will happen, it fixes the grammar of what will happen. The twelve "when" phrases that follow are neither questions nor doubts. They are the table of contents of an event the Book treats as already settled, and the first of them names the sun.

**Light Before Structure**

The list opens with the sun and, in the second verse, the stars — the two lights of the Arabian sky — before anything else is named. Only then do the mountains come, then living things. The order descends: the highest and most distant is named first, and the sūrah works downward from the firmament to the livestock of the desert. This descent is the hidden architecture of the stanza. The cosmic comes before the domestic, the astronomical before the animal, and the human last. By the time the list reaches the buried girl in verse eight, the listener has been carried down from the sun to the sand. The end is not announced as a single explosion of chaos. It is staged as a collapse from the top, layer after layer, until nothing above the human being is left standing.

**An Inversion of the Standing Order**

Every image in the list reverses something that stands. The sun stands fixed in its course, the stars in their spheres, the mountains in their places, and the sūrah's catalogue is a list of reversals: the fixed is moved, the light is wrapped, the barrier is removed. A report transmitted from Ibn ʿUmar, recorded in the Musnad of Aḥmad and also in al-Tirmidhī, gives the sūrah its function in a single sentence: whoever wishes to look at the Day of Judgment as if seeing it with his own eyes should read this sūrah, then the one that opens with the sky splitting, then the one that opens with the sky split asunder. Three sūrahs, one mirror. The report treats the mirror as a standing prescription. The Day cannot be pictured, so the Book pictures it in three voices, and this verse is the first frame of the first of them.

**The Most Reliable Measure, Expired**

For the people being addressed, the sun was not scenery. It was the measure of the day, of the season, of the caravan's route, of the milk and the pasture. A society that keeps no clocks keeps the sun. To announce that the sun is being put out is to announce that the measure of their security is itself being cancelled. The sūrah does not begin with a threat to their walls or their wells; it begins with the expiry of the instrument by which all their security is measured. Whatever they trust is inside the sun's jurisdiction, and the jurisdiction is closing. The first verse therefore aims at trust rather than at comfort. It removes the guarantee before it removes anything else, and it does so in a single clause, without explanation, because the clause is the announcement.

**A Name Borrowed From the First Word**

The chapter is known by its opening word. Al-Takwīr, "the Enveloping," is an early Meccan sūrah, and it also carries the title "When the Sun Is Enfolded." A sūrah named for its first word is named for the act it will perform: wrapping up. The name announces the sūrah's first motion and borrows it for the whole. In the Book's practice a chapter's title usually marks its centre of gravity, and here the centre of gravity is a movement — something being folded, gathered, closed. The remainder of the sūrah, the stars, the oath, the messenger, the reminder, is carried along on that first motion, the way everything downstream is carried on the first fold of a garment being put away.

**The Neighbouring Mirrors**

The mirror is not a single frame. The sūrah's nearest neighbour opens with its own "when": *"When the sky splits open"* (82:1), and the next breath in that sūrah repeats the list — *"and when the stars fall away"* (82:2), *"and when the seas burst forth"* (82:3). The Book runs the same scene a second time with a different verb for each object, and a third time in the sūrah that begins with the sky split asunder. Three voices, one event. Each voice is a complete sūrah, and together they form a single witness, the Book's standing answer to every question about how the end will look.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:2

> and when the stars fall down,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Stars That Fall, Not Blink**

The verb here is *inkadarat*, and the Arabic root behind it, *k-d-r*, is the root of darkness: to darken, to grow dim. The commentators drew three pictures from the one verb, and they fit together rather than compete. The stars lose their light and go dark; they fall to the ground; they spill out the way the contents of a broken vessel scatter. One image is the dimming, one is the descent, one is the disorder, and the sūrah packs all three into a single word. The stars are not extinguished the way a lamp is pinched out, and they are not scattered the way seeds are. They fade, they drop, and they lose their arrangement at the same moment. The verse describes an event with a body, and the body is the sky itself.

**The Day's Light and the Night's Light**

The first verse took the sun, the day's lamp, and wrapped it. This verse takes the stars, the night's lamps, and lets them go. The list works on the two sources of light in the Arabian sky as a pair: first the one that rules the daytime, then the ones that rule the darkness. After these two verses there is no light left in the sūrah's sky that is not already cancelled. The pairing is deliberate. A people who read their nights by the stars are being told that both watches of the sky — the bright one and the dark one — lose their keepers at the same moment. The end does not come during the day or during the night. It comes when the division between the two ceases to exist.

**The Timekeepers Fail**

The stars were the instruments of the desert. By them the caravan chose its course, the tribe marked its seasons, the traveler found his bearing across trackless sand. The Book itself states the office of these bodies: *"And He is the One Who created the day and the night, the sun and the moon—each travelling in an orbit"* (21:33). Orbit is the word, and orbit is order. When the stars fall, the order they served is finished, and with it the whole calendar of navigation, of planting, of pilgrimage by night. The verse therefore does not merely remove lights from the sky. It removes the means by which a mobile society knew where it was. The stars are the second object in the list, and their fall is the list's first act of de-location.

**A Broken Vessel's Image**

Several early commentators took the verb in the sense of spilling: a vessel breaks and its contents come out in disorder, and so the stars leave their spheres. The image does the work the sūrah needs. A sphere is a holding place, and the falling is the failure of the holding. Nothing is thrown; nothing is flung. The containers give up, and what was contained disperses. The same scene returns in the Book with a different verb each time: *"and when the stars fall away"* (82:2), and elsewhere *"So when the stars are put out"* (77:8). The repetition with variation is the Book's way of insisting that the event is real and not a figure of speech, each sūrah re-witnessing the same fall from its own angle.

**Fading and Falling Together**

The dimming and the descent are not alternatives in the verse; they are one event with two faces. The stars do not fall first and then go dark, or go dark first and then fall. *Inkadarat* holds the two together, as the root of darkness holds both the light's loss and the night's thickening. A slow fading, a drop, a scattering — the sequence is compressed into a single present participle that the "when" clause sets in motion. The compression is the point. The sūrah is not writing a chronology of the end; it is writing its simultaneity. Everything in the list happens at once, and the verb forms are chosen to let each object do all its dying in a single motion.

**The Second Object, the Same Direction**

After the sun comes the stars, and after the stars, in the next verse, the mountains. The list keeps descending, and it keeps doing the same thing to each object: it removes the object from its place. The sun is wrapped, the stars are dropped, the mountains will be moved, and whatever comes after — camels, beasts, seas — receives the same sentence. The sūrah's list is a set of cancellations, and the second cancellation establishes the pattern the rest will follow. Each object is named in the place it occupies, and each is shown leaving that place. The stars were the highest objects still standing after the first verse; their fall confirms that height is no protection, and the list proceeds to lower ground. The pattern, once it is established at this second step, never varies through the rest of the stanza, and the verse is the pattern's founding move, the one that turns a single image into a method.

**The Same Chord in the Neighbouring Sūrah**

The neighbouring sūrah strikes the same chord a moment after its opening. *"When the sky splits open, and when the stars fall away, and when the seas burst forth"* (82:1–3) — three objects, three verbs, the same descent from the sky to the water. The two sūrahs are set against each other in the Book, and their second verses name the same falling stars with two different verbs: one *fall down*, the other *fall away*. The agreement of the two witnesses is the point. The event is reported twice, from two directions, and the repetition is the Book's way of making the picture load-bearing.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:3

> and when the mountains are blown away,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pins of the Earth Come Out**

The third object is the mountain, and the verse gives the earth its most concrete loss. The Book states the mountain's office plainly: *"He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you, as well as rivers, and pathways so you may find your way"* (16:15). The mountain is a pin, and the earth is the thing held by the pin. To blow the mountains away is to pull the pins and let the sheet go loose. The sūrah does not need to describe the shaking; it is the direct consequence of the removal, and it is already contained in the first verse's logic. When the fixings fail, the fixed thing moves, and the movement of the earth is the standing of nothing left in place.

**Moved, Shaved, or Scattered**

The verb *siyyarat* means set in motion, and the commentators opened the range wide. Some took it in the motion it names: the mountains are moved from their places, lifted and carried. Others read the displacement as total: the mountains are scattered, as the Book says elsewhere of the same Day, *"and the mountains are blown away"* (77:10). A third reading turned the motion into a transformation: the mountains become light, soft, and shifting — *"and the mountains like (tufts of) wool"* (70:9), *"and the mountains will be like carded wool"* (101:5), reduced to *"dunes of shifting sand"* (73:14). The readings agree on the direction. The solid gives way, and what gave way is gone.

**The Third in the Descent**

The list has now passed the sun, the stars, and the mountains: the light of the day, the lights of the night, and the stability of the ground. The sūrah works from the sky down to the earth, and with the third verse it reaches the surface itself. What followed the sun and the stars was not another light but a support — the heavy, the still, the unmovable. The descent is complete at the third step, in the sense that every layer above the human world has now been named and cancelled. The sun is in the sky, the stars in the sky, the mountains on the earth. After them the sūrah turns to what lives on the earth, and the fourth verse names the camels.

**What the Stability Was Doing**

The mountains were not scenery in the Arabia this sūrah addresses. They were the fixed points of a mobile life: the landmarks between wells, the ridgelines that divided territories, the high ground that caught the little rain. The Book's own image makes their function structural rather than decorative — placed so the earth does not shake. The sūrah takes that image and removes the placement. The verse's force is in the passive: the mountains are blown away, not by a hand the sūrah names. The doing is left to the Day itself, and the Day is the agent the whole stanza belongs to. The stability that held the world together is not attacked. It is simply not there, and the world is left to show what it was when the holding stopped. No further description of the shaking is needed in the sūrah's account, because the shaking is contained in the removal, and the removal is the whole of the news.

**The Horizon of a Stationary World**

The contrast the sūrah draws is between a world that stands and a world that is in motion, and it is the standing that is under sentence. The sun stood in its course, the stars in their spheres, the mountains in their places. Three objects, three stations, all of them fixed, all of them now moving. The list of the first three verses is a list of fixed points, and the verb applied to each is a verb of unfixedness: wrapped, fallen, set in motion. The sūrah is not describing chaos arriving from outside. It is describing the fixed points failing in order, from the highest to the lowest, until the only things left in motion are the creatures who had stood still beside them. The earth shakes because its pins are out; the creatures are undone because their landmarks are gone.

**The Third Object, the Same Verdict**

The pattern of the stanza is now clear, and the third verse locks it in. Each object is named in the place it holds, and each is shown leaving that place. The sun was the measure of the day; it is wrapped. The stars were the keepers of the night; they fall. The mountains were the anchors of the ground; they are blown away. The sūrah's list is a set of cancellations carried out in strict order, and the third cancellation is the last to concern the inanimate world above the surface. What remains in the list — camels, wild beasts, seas, souls — is what lives, or moves, or feels. The turning point of the stanza has been reached, and the sūrah crosses from the architecture of the world to its inhabitants, beginning with the animal that was nearest to the human hand.

**Wool and Sand**

The Book gives the same mountain its end in three textures, and the three textures are the verse's own imagery carried into other sūrahs. *"And the mountains like (tufts of) wool"* (70:9) — the solid made soft. *"And the mountains will be like carded wool"* (101:5) — the soft made light, pulled apart to its fibres. *"And mountains will be (reduced to) dunes of shifting sand"* (73:14) — the light made formless, moving with the wind. The sequence from fixed to tuft to sand is a single process told in three stages, and each stage is a stage of the release this verse announces.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:4

> and when pregnant camels are left untended,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Tenth Month, Chosen**

The Arabic word is *al-ʿishār*, and it is not a general word for camels. Exegetes agree that it names she-camels that have reached the tenth month of pregnancy — the plural of *ʿashrā*, from the root of the number ten, a camel whose gestation, which runs close to a year, is nearly complete. The sūrah could have named any camel. It named the one carrying the most. The choice of the tenth month is the verse's first act of precision: the abandonment described is not the neglect of an animal but the abandonment of an animal at the exact point where its value, and its need, are at their greatest. A camel about to calve is two possessions in one body — the mother and the calf already formed inside her — and the sūrah measures the end of the world by what is done to exactly that.

**The Most Valuable, Left First**

In the economy of the desert this sūrah addresses, the camel was wealth, milk, transport, lineage, and honour in a single animal, and the pregnant she-camel stood at the top of that scale. To leave an ordinary camel untended was carelessness; to leave one in her tenth month was a catastrophe every owner could have felt in his chest. The sūrah therefore selects the strongest available image of neglect and makes it the fourth sign of the Hour. Nothing is said about the reason. The list of the opening verses does not give reasons; it gives scenes, and the scene here is the one every Meccan owner would recognize as total abandonment. The verse works by recognition rather than by explanation, and the recognition is complete.

**An Image Only the Desert Feels**

The sūrah speaks to its audience in the currency of its own life. The sun and the stars were universal objects, but the camel is local: its image only has full force on a people whose security, their pride, their measures of loss and gain, are tied to it. The apocalyptic list descends from the cosmic to the domestic, and with the fourth verse it reaches the domestic floor. The end of the world is described here not as the collapse of the heavens alone but as the moment when even the herd is left — the moment when the herdsman's job stops mattering because the herdsman's own standing has changed. The image is local in its vocabulary and universal in its claim: when the Day arrives, what was most kept is what is first dropped.

**What the Abandonment Measures**

The commentators read the verse as a measure of preoccupation. On that Day, they say, people will be so absorbed in their own condition — their coming account, their approaching reckoning — that they will have no concern left for wealth, not even the most precious property, not even the things of this world at all. The untended camel is the index of the soul's fullness: when the self is entirely taken up with itself, the most valuable thing in the world becomes as far away as the sun. The verse does not describe what the people do; it describes what they stop doing. The cessation of tending is the sign, and the cessation proves that attention has moved somewhere else entirely, to the only matter that remains.

**The Sign in the Prophetic Reports**

The prophetic tradition keeps the camel's place in the portents of the Hour. A narration in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah, counts among those portents the day when the shepherds of black camels — the humblest keepers of the lowest camels — will compete in raising tall buildings, and the same report recalls that the Hour itself is among the five things no one knows except God. The reversal is the same one the sūrah stages, read from the other side. In the sūrah the tenth-month camel is left because it no longer counts; in the report the black camel's keeper rises to the top because the old measures of worth have been overthrown. Both texts agree that the Day arrives when the standing value of the camel is inverted, and the verse here is the inversion stated as scene.

**The Measure of Loss**

The sūrah's logic in choosing its objects is now visible. The sun was the most reliable measure, the stars the most constant, the mountains the most solid, and the tenth-month camel the most valuable. The list does not proceed from small to great. It proceeds from most to most, each object the strongest of its kind, and each is shown failing. The pattern is a ladder of maxima, and the descent down the ladder is the descent into the end. By the time the verse names the camel, the sūrah has established its method: the end of the world is not measured by what is trivial but by what stands first, and the first things are the first to go.

**The Weight That Now Counts**

On the same Day the camel's value leaves the market, another weight enters the scale. The Book states the exchange in two short lines: *"So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it. And whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it"* (99:7–8). The verse of the abandoned camel and the verse of the atom's weight belong to the same arithmetic, told from opposite sides. What no longer has price is the tenth-month camel, and what has acquired the only price is the deed. The sūrah's fourth sign is therefore a transition in the Book's own economy: the measure of worth changes hands at the same moment the herd is left, and the change is total.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:5

> and when wild beasts are gathered together,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ungatherable, Gathered**

The word *al-wuḥūsh* names the wild beasts, the creatures that do not form herds, that do not follow a keeper, that scatter across the desert rather than collect in one place. To gather a wild beast is to do something its nature resists. The verse's image is an inversion of the creature's standing order: the ungatherable is made one, and it is made one by a gathering that no human hand performs. The sūrah has already shown the fixed objects leaving their places; here it shows the scattered objects finding one place, and the reversal is as complete as the earlier ones. What the world could not bring together, the Day brings together, and the bringing is the sign.

**All of Them, Not Some**

The commentators took the verse in its widest reading: it is not a few beasts but every created animal — other than human beings and jinn — that is gathered, the large and the small, down to the least of creatures. The Book states the scope elsewhere in a single sentence: *"All living beings roaming the earth and winged birds soaring in the sky are communities like yourselves. We have left nothing out of the Record. Then to their Lord they will be gathered all together"* (6:38). The verse of the wild beasts is the scene, and this is the warrant for the scene's completeness. Nothing is left out. The gathering is total in the way the record is total, and the two verses say the same thing about the same Day, one as event and one as principle.

**Gathered So That Justice Is Settled**

The reports explain why the gathering happens. God resurrects the animals, they are allowed to settle their scores — the wronged taking their due from the one who wronged them — and when nothing is left to be settled, they all become dust. The prophetic tradition carries the same scene in a single report: a narration in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, from Abū Hurayrah, has the Prophet ﷺ saying that all rights will be restored on the Day of Judgment, to the extent that even the hornless animal will be allowed to avenge itself on the one with horns. The gathering is therefore not a reunion. It is a court session for the creatures who have no lawyers in this world, and its end is the word *be dust*, which closes the session and returns the matter to the ground.

**The Hunter Reversed**

In this world the wild beast is gathered only by the hunter: by the net, the pit, the drive of the circling riders, the slow closing of the circle. The beast's gathering in the worldly sense is the beast's end, and every Arab knew it. The sūrah takes that association and turns it. On the Day the beasts are gathered without a hunter, without fear, without a circle closing — gathered by command rather than by pursuit, and gathered to live long enough to be treated justly rather than to be taken. The relationship between the gathering and the gathered is reversed, and the reversal is the verse's quiet logic. What was once the figure of the hunt becomes the figure of the court.

**After the Camels, the Wild**

The list has now covered both halves of the animal world. The fourth verse named the domesticated camel, the animal bound to the human hand, kept and valued and tended; this verse names the wild beast, the animal unbound, unkept, and of no use to the household. Between the two verses the sūrah has drawn the whole animal kingdom into the scene: what is kept and what is not, both are on the Day's list, both are subject to the same reversal. The kept animal is abandoned, and the unkept animal is collected. The two verses read together are the sūrah's statement that no creature's standing — its usefulness to humans or its freedom from them — survives the turning. The list then returns to the human, and returns through a door the Meccan audience would have wanted to keep shut.

**A Scene, Not a State**

The gathering is described in the moment-tense of the "when" clauses, and it is a scene with a beginning and an end, not a new permanent order. The beasts are gathered, their scores are settled, and they are dust. The sūrah is careful to keep the event within the Day's own sequence: the gathering serves the justice, and the justice ends the gathering. This is why the verse sits where it does in the list — after the abandonment of the camel and before the setting on fire of the seas. The animal world is a chapter in the sūrah's account, and the chapter is short. It opens with the wild gathered, and it closes with the wronged made whole, and then the sūrah moves on to the water, where the list's next reversal begins.

**The Dust After the Score**

The report that closes the scene is the verse's own ending, carried into the prophetic tradition. After the hornless has taken its due from the horned, the reports say, nothing is left to be settled, and the word given is *be dust*. The creatures do not return to their lives; they return to the earth, the same earth whose mountains were blown away in the third verse of this sūrah. The circle is completed at the scale of the animal world: what was raised up for the settlement is put down to its first matter, and the settlement stands. The sūrah's fifth sign is thus a complete episode in miniature — the gathering, the justice, the dust — and its completeness is what allows the list to proceed, without remainder, to the seas.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:6

> and when the seas are set on fire,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Barrier Taken Out**

The sixth object is the sea, and the verse begins by assuming what the Book has already established: that the seas are separated. *"And He is the One Who merges the two bodies of water: one fresh and palatable and the other salty and bitter, placing between them a barrier they cannot cross"* (25:53). Two bodies of water, one barrier, and the barrier is law. The sea in this sūrah is therefore not free water. It is contained water, water holding its place, and the sign of the Day is the failure of the holding. Whatever the verb in the verse means at the point, it means first the end of the separation: the barrier goes, and what was kept apart comes together. The sūrah reaches the sea and finds, as it found the mountains, an object whose standing order is containment, and the containment fails.

**Set on Fire, or Set Loose**

The verb *succirot* is the one the early commentators divided over, and the division is worth stating with its grounds. Several took it in the meaning the translation carries: the seas are set on fire, water turned to flame, the element of life made into an instrument of burning. Others read the same root in its other sense, to boil over and break out: the barrier gives way, the fresh and the salt surge into one another, and the overflow covers the earth when the earth quakes. The two readings are not opposed so much as successive. The waters first break their boundary, and the breaking is described in the language of fire. The sūrah allows both because the event, as the sūrah wants it, is both: the separation fails, and the element itself is transformed.

**Water That Becomes Fire**

The image is an inversion of the element itself, and it is the strongest inversion in the list. Fire and water stand opposite in everything — in cooling and burning, in drying and drowning, in what each undoes in the other. To set the sea on fire is not to add fire to water but to remove the distinction between the two. The sūrah has already wrapped the sun, dropped the stars, and moved the mountains; with the sea it attacks the last standing opposition in the material world. After this verse there is no element left in the sūrah's catalogue that is still what it was. The water is fire, the fire was kept in the water's place, and the world's basic pairs — light and dark, fixed and moving, fresh and salt — have all been cancelled in turn.

**The Third Sea of the Triad**

The neighbouring sūrah gives the same scene its own verb: *"and when the seas burst forth"* (82:3). Bursting is the water's motion — the pressure released, the banks failed, the contents coming out. The sea set on fire is the water's transformation — the substance changed, not merely moved. The two sūrahs witness the same event with two different verbs, and the difference between the verbs is the difference between the sign as motion and the sign as change. The Book repeats the sea's fate the way it repeats the stars': once in this sūrah, once in the neighbour, each time a complete witness to the same happening, so that no reader can treat the image as the ornament of a single poem. The sea is on the list twice, and the doubling is part of the claim.

**The Sea Beyond the Desert's Horizon**

The audience of this sūrah lives in a land that has no sea. The Meccan horizon ends in sand and ridge; the sea is a country their caravans do not cross and their poetry rarely needs. The sūrah's inclusion of the sea is therefore a deliberate widening. The apocalypse it announces is not the end of Arabia; it is the end of the world, and the world extends past the edge of the audience's experience. By naming what the audience has never had to fear, the verse removes the last comfort of locality: the end is not confined to the familiar, and the familiar is not the whole. The desert and the ocean both stand on the same list, and the list does not care which half of the world the listener calls home.

**The Sixth Object, the Same Verdict**

The pattern of the stanza holds through the sixth verse without a break. Each object is named in its place and shown leaving it: the sun wrapped, the stars fallen, the mountains moved, the camel abandoned, the beast gathered, and now the sea unheld. The sea is the last of the objects that do not live. With its removal the inanimate catalogue is complete — the sky, the ground, the water — and the list stands at the threshold of the living. The seventh verse will name the souls, and the movement from the sea to the soul is the sūrah's crossing from the world's matter into the world's persons. The sixth verse is the hinge: it finishes the account of things and opens the account of selves, and it does so by setting the last of the things on fire.

**The Two Bodies of Water**

The Book repeats the barrier's image with the sea on both sides of its argument. Alongside the verse of the two merged waters stands the challenge put to the disbelievers: *"Or (ask them), 'Who made the earth a place of settlement, caused rivers to flow through it, placed firm mountains upon it, and set a barrier between (fresh and salt) bodies of water? Was it another god besides Allah?' Absolutely not! But most of them do not know"* (27:61). The same barrier that this sūrah's sixth verse undoes is the barrier the question in al-Naml holds up as a proof. The barrier is proof while it stands; it is sign when it falls. One object, two offices, and the sūrah's list gives the object its second office.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:7

> and when the souls ˹and their bodies˺ are paired ˹once more˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Soul and Body, Reunited**

The seventh object is the soul, and the verb is *zūjirat* — paired, from the root of the pair, the two made one. The reading the commentators put first is the joining of the soul to its body: on the Day of Judgment the soul is coupled with the flesh it left at death, and the separation of the grave is closed. The sūrah states the reunion as a sign of the Hour in the same flat tense it has used for the sun and the sea, without argument and without qualification. The Meccan objection to bodily resurrection — the claim that rotted bones cannot be put back together — is met here by a clause, and the clause is the meeting of the objection. What the objectors treat as the hard part of the doctrine is treated in this sūrah as the seventh item on a list, named and settled in a single line.

**The Other Pairings**

The commentators did not stop at the first reading, and the alternatives are worth stating with their reasons. Some took the pairing in the direction of reward: the souls of the believers are coupled with the maidens of Paradise, as the Book says elsewhere of the Garden's people that they are joined as spouses, while the souls of the disbelievers are joined to satans who become their companions. Others took it in the direction of division: souls are paired with like souls, the righteous with the righteous and the wicked with the wicked, because the Day is a Day of Division, a sorting in which the mixed world is untangled. The last reading is the one the early tafsīr weighed most heavily, and it stands on a report that every man will be joined to the person who did as he did. The company of the Day, on this reading, is chosen by the deed, and the pairing is the deed made visible.

**The Brackets in the Translation**

The translation's brackets — *and their bodies*, *once more* — mark two things the sūrah insists on, and the insistence is the verse's real content. The first bracket names the body. The verse is not about the souls alone drifting into a spiritual company; it is about the souls and their bodies, the joined pair, the reunited person. The doctrine of bodily resurrection is stated in the middle of an apocalyptic list, where the list's grammar gives it the force of a fact rather than the form of a proof. The second bracket, *once more*, names the repetition: the pairing has happened before. The soul was made with the body, the two were one in this life, and the verse announces the making-over of what was broken. The brackets are small, and they carry the verse's entire theology.

**Pairing as Sorting**

The verb *zawj* does two things at once, and the sūrah uses both. It joins, and it divides: a pair is a two that is set against the rest. The world of this life is mixed — the believer in the marketplace with the denier, the righteous in the tribe with the wicked, the soul with its body held together by habit and blood. The Day undoes the mixture. The pairing of the seventh verse is the beginning of the sorting that the Book calls the Day of Division, the Day on which the categories of this world are separated and no category holds the other. The souls are paired, and in the pairing they are assigned: each soul to its body, to its kind, to its destination. The single verb holds the reunion and the assignment together, and the verse does not let the reader have one without the other.

**The Soul Is Not Alone**

The sūrah's picture of the soul is a picture of a soul in company. The soul in the list is never a single item. It is paired — with the body that was its house, with the kind that was its match, with the deed that was its mark. The verse refuses the lonely soul, the soul that stands before its reckoning with nothing attached to it. What the soul brings, and what the soul is joined to, is stated in the same clause. This is the verse's quiet preparation for verse fourteen, which will say that each soul will know what it has brought: the soul that is paired in verse seven is the soul that arrives in verse fourteen with its load already attached. The sūrah introduces the soul as a bearer before it makes the soul an accountant, and the order of the two verses is the order of the argument.

**The Seventh Object, the Human at Last**

The list has now reached the human being, and it has reached it through the animal world. The camels were abandoned, the wild beasts were gathered, and now the souls are paired — the creatures of the earth giving place to the persons of the earth. The seventh verse is the last of the objects that are not the human on the sūrah's list, in the sense that the soul, though it is the human's, is named here as the object of a cosmic event rather than as the agent of a choice. The soul is paired the way the sea is set on fire: it is done to, not done by. The agency belongs to the Day, and the human reappears in the sūrah as an agent only when the list is finished and the main clause arrives. Until then the soul is one more object on the schedule, and the sūrah's seventh verse places it there with the same care it gave the sun.

**The Bones Question**

The objection the verse answers is stated in the Book as a question, and the question is blunt. *"And I do swear by the self-reproaching soul! Do people think We cannot reassemble their bones?"* (75:2–3). The second sūrah of the short Meccan set takes the same disbelief and presses it to the point: the doubt is not about the doctrine but about the power, and the sūrah answers with an oath and a question. The seventh verse of al-Takwīr answers the same doubt with a sign: on the Day the souls are paired with their bodies, and the pairing is the reassembly stated as an event. The two answers run in parallel — the oath in al-Qiyāma, the list in al-Takwīr — and together they make the reunion as settled, in the Book's treatment, as the falling of the stars.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:8

> and when baby girls, buried alive, are asked

**Expanded Commentary**

**The List Turns to a Child**

The eighth object on the sūrah's list is a baby girl, and the turn to her is the turn of the whole stanza. The sun, the stars, the mountains, the camels, the beasts, the seas, the souls — the list has moved through the architecture of the world and the creatures of it, and now it reaches a human being. The sūrah could have reached the human through the strong: the king, the warrior, the elder whose name was known. It reaches the human through the weakest, the one with no name, no voice, no standing, the one buried before the tribe even knew her. The choice of the first human item is the verse's first act of judgment. The audit of the world begins not with the powerful but with the powerless, and the opening of the human section of the list is the opening of a case.

**The Word That Carries the Charge**

The Arabic noun is *al-mawḍūʿat*, the one put down, with *maqṭūra*, the one cut off, attached to it, and the translation's bracketed gloss — *buried alive* — is the sūrah's own reading of the two words together. Put down: the infant laid into the ground. Cut off: the life severed, the line broken, the child removed from the future. The noun is not a neutral description. It is a charge. The name of the one asked carries the description of what was done to her, and the sūrah names her by the act. In the list of the Hour, where each object is named in the state that marks it, the girl is named in the state of the crime. The two words do the work a prosecution would do: they identify the victim, the act, and the manner, in a single grammatical unit, before the question begins.

**The Custom Behind the Image**

The verse alludes to the pre-Islamic practice of burying a newborn girl alive, and the allusion is local rather than exotic. The custom was driven by the two fears the Book names elsewhere in the same breath: the fear of poverty and the fear of shame. When the family was given the news of a girl, the Book reports, the father's face grew dark and he was angry in himself, and he hid himself from the people, faced with the choice the sūrah's verse presupposes: *"Should he keep her in disgrace, or bury her (alive) in the ground? Evil indeed is their judgment!"* (16:59). The verse here does not need to describe the custom. Its audience knows it. The image is a mirror held up to a recent practice, and the mirror is the verse's instrument: the sūrah does not explain the crime, because the crime is already standing in the room.

**Why the Girl Is in the List**

The list of the opening verses is a list of the Day's signs, and the girl's being asked is on it as a sign, not as a complaint. The sūrah's point is not primarily to condemn the custom — the condemnation is already done elsewhere in the Book, in the verse that forbids the killing of children for fear of poverty. The point here is eschatological: on the Day the buried girl is given a seat in the proceeding. She is asked. The victim, who in this world was silent and unrecorded, is on the Day a party to the case, and her question is part of the official record. The sūrah takes the most unrepresented person in the Meccan order — a girl the tribe buried to erase her — and places her, on the Day, in the position of the one who asks. The list does that, and the list is the sūrah.

**The Question Left Open**

The verse ends mid-sentence. *Are asked* — asked what? The sūrah withholds the completion, and the next verse supplies it: *for what crime they were put to death*. The break is deliberate, and it does real work. The reader is made to stand in the gap between the question and its object, to hear the asking before hearing the asking-about. The sūrah's grammar here imitates the scene: the girl is asked, and the pause is the pause of the courtroom, the moment between the summons and the charge. The verse refuses to let the reader skip the asking itself; the asking is the event, and the question's content follows, as the charge follows the summons, in the next line. The eighth verse is the summons, and its incompleteness is its force.

**The Weakest, Chosen First**

The sūrah's method in choosing its human object is the same method it used in choosing its cosmic ones: it takes the object that carries the most weight in the order it is about to reverse. The sun carried the security of the light; the camel carried the wealth of the desert; the girl carries the honour of the family that buried her. The tribe counted her as a loss, a burden, a disgrace to be removed. The sūrah counts her as a sign of the Day, and the counting is the reversal. What the order of this world subtracted, the order of the Day adds: she is asked, she is named, she is a party. The verse opens the human section of the list with the person the list's audience most wanted left out, and her presence is the first statement of the Day's new arithmetic.

**Do Not Kill Your Children**

The command that stands behind the allusion is stated in the Book without the allusion's restraint. *"Do not kill your children for fear of poverty. We provide for them and for you. Surely killing them is a heinous sin"* (17:31). The verse names the motive the custom ran on — the fear of poverty — and answers the motive with a guarantee: provision for the child and the parent both. The sūrah's eighth verse and this command belong to the same case, told from two positions. The command is spoken to the living, before the burial; the verse of the list is spoken for the Day, after it. Between the two, the sūrah's audience is closed in: the practice is forbidden here, and the buried girl is asked there.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:9

> for what crime they were put to death,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Crime That Was Not a Crime**

The completion of the question is *bimā dhunm*, for what crime, for what sin. The grammar of the question is the verse's logic: it asks for the crime, and the asking of the crime is the indictment. The sūrah does not say *for what reason* or *for what cause*; it says *for what crime*, and the word *dhunm*, sin, is the word of the court. The question therefore contains its own answer in advance. If the girl was buried for poverty, for shame, for the balance of the family's honour, those are reasons, and reasons are not crimes. The question demands a crime, and there is none. The sūrah puts the demand into the mouth of the Day, and the demand's failure — the absence of the crime it requires — is the finding. The question is a trap, and the trap is set for the one who buried, not the one who was buried.

**The Accused Becomes the Accuser**

The scene inverts the courtroom of this world. In the world the girl was buried without a court, without a charge, without a record; the act was performed by the family and covered by the family's honour. On the Day the positions turn. The one asked is the buried girl, and the asking is her asking. The question put to her — for what crime — is the question the one who buried could never be asked in this life, and by directing it at the victim the sūrah directs it, in effect, at the killer. Her answer will be the charge: she was put to death for no crime, and the no crime is the crime of the one who acted as if there were one. The sūrah's ninth verse is the verse of the inversion, and the inversion is complete: the voiceless become the asking, and the asking is the verdict.

**What the Audience Would Have Heard**

The verse is not a general moral. It is an address to a specific audience with a specific recent past. The Meccan listeners of this sūrah are the audience of a society in which the practice was known, recent, and defended by the pressures of shame and poverty. The sūrah's choice of the buried girl as its first human sign is therefore a mirror, and the mirror is aimed at the room. No explanation is offered, because explanation would be a courtesy the verse withholds. The image stands in the sūrah the way the act stood in the society: without excuse, without record, and now without the possibility of silence. The sūrah takes the act the audience would have wanted unmentioned and makes it the eighth and ninth items on the list of the end of the world, where it will be spoken of for as long as the world is spoken of.

**The Honor Economy Reversed**

The custom ran on an economy of honour, and the sūrah reverses that economy point by point. The girl was a burden on the family's standing — the disgrace the father hid from the people, the shame that darkened his face. Poverty was the stated fear, and honour the unstated one. The sūrah's Day revalues both. What the family counted as a loss is counted, on the Day, as a sin — the heinous sin the Book names in its command against the killing of children. What the family counted as silence — the burial, the unspoken, the unrecorded — is counted, on the Day, as a question put in public. The honor economy is not reformed by the sūrah; it is reversed. The same object that was a minus in the old arithmetic is a plus in the new one: the girl asked is the girl who now carries weight, and the weight is the weight of the case.

**The Second Human Sign**

The girl's question is the first of the two human signs in the list's final section, and it prepares the second. The second is the laying open of the records in verse ten, and the movement between the two is the movement from the particular to the general. The girl's case is one case, one burial, one family's crime. The records are every case, every deed, every family's account. The sūrah places the single case before the general record, and the order is the order of a trial: first the specific charge is heard, then the files are opened. The ninth verse is the charge, and it stands in the sūrah as the human item that converts the list from a catalogue of events into a proceeding. The events were the sun and the stars; the proceeding begins with the girl, and the proceeding's files are opened in the next verse.

**The Question the Audience Cannot Answer**

The question is directed, in the sūrah's scene, at the girl, and it is heard, in the sūrah's address, by the audience. The sūrah's grammar gives the question one addressee, but its force lands elsewhere. The Meccan listener who knows the custom cannot answer the question for the girl, because the answer — the absence of the crime — is the finding against the one who buried. The sūrah therefore puts its audience in the position of the one asked and unable to respond, and the inability is the verse's point. The question is unanswerable in the room, and its unanswerability is the silence the custom wanted and the Day will not give. The ninth verse ends the list's descent from the sun to the sand and leaves the audience standing in the sand with the question still open in the air, waiting for the records that the next verse will spread.

**Gloomy Faces**

The verse the sūrah's allusion carries is the portrait of the moment before the act. *"Whenever one of them is given the good news of a baby girl, his face grows gloomy, as he suppresses his rage"* (16:58). The gloom is the face of the honor economy at work — the news received as a blow, the child counted before she is named. The sūrah's ninth verse is the same economy's ending: the gloom is over, the rage is past, and the buried girl is standing, asked, in the record. The portrait and the ending are two frames of one case, and the sūrah places the ending where the portrait can be read in reverse. The face that darkened at the news is the face that will face the question, and the question is the verse this sūrah gives to the case the other sūrah described.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:10

> and when the records ˹of deeds˺ are laid open,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pages Spread**

The tenth object is the record, and the verb is *nuridat* — spread, opened, laid out. The noun *al-ṣuḥuf* is the plural of the page, the sheet, the scroll: not a single book but pages, many pages, the accumulated leaves of the account. The Book states the record's standing order in a verse that belongs to the same scene: *"We have bound every human's destiny to their neck. And on the Day of Judgment We will bring forth to each (person) a record which they will find laid open"* (17:13). Bound to the neck in this life, brought forth on the Day, laid open before the owner. The sūrah's tenth verse is the moment of the bringing forth, and the laying open is the sūrah's word for it: the pages spread, and the account is ready to be read.

**Opened, Not Written**

The verb in the verse is the verb of opening, and the distinction is the verse's quiet doctrine. The records are not written on the Day; they are opened on the Day. The writing was done before, and it is done continuously, by the observers the Book names: *"while you are certainly observed by vigilant, honourable angels, recording (everything)"* (82:10–11). This verse is therefore the unrolling of what was already rolled, the disclosure of what was already written. The distinction matters because it places the human being before a record that is not being made to fit him but is being shown to be true. The account exists, the account is complete, and the Day's act is the act of revelation, not the act of entry. The record is opened, and the opening is the end of the possibility of the unrecorded.

**The Courtroom Is Ready**

The list of the sūrah's opening has been, so far, a list of scenery: the sun, the stars, the mountains, the animals, the seas, the souls, the buried girl. With the tenth verse the list becomes procedure. The record is the instrument of the court, and its laying open is the court's readiness. The sūrah has spent nine verses building the setting of the Day, and the tenth verse places the instrument on the table. The setting was the cosmic reversal; the instrument is the account. The two together are the full picture: a world unmade and a record opened, the scene cleared and the files spread. The sūrah's list is thus not a list of destructions but a list of preparations, and the preparation of the record is the preparation of the judgment, which the sūrah will state in the main clause at the end of the stanza.

**Nothing Small Left Out**

The completeness of the record is the Book's own claim about the record, and the claim is stated in the voice of the one who finds it. On the Day, the wicked will cry: *"Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?"* (18:49). The question is the record's testimony from the other side: even the one it condemns confirms its completeness. The verse stands on the same ground. The pages that are laid open leave nothing out — no small deed unlisted, no large one unreckoned — and the standard of the record is the standard the Book gives elsewhere for the least of deeds: *"So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it. And whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it"* (99:7–8). The atom's weight and the laid-open page are the same measure, and the sūrah's tenth verse is the measure made into an event.

**The Record Already Exists**

The sūrah's tense is the present of certainty, and the record it opens is the record that already exists. The Book's own description of the present is the warrant: *"Not (even) an atom's weight is hidden from your Lord on earth or in heaven; nor anything smaller or larger than that, but is (written) in a perfect Record"* (10:61). The record is perfect now, in the sense that it is complete now, and the Day's opening is the making visible of what was already the case. The sūrah's tenth verse is therefore not an addition to the account but a disclosure of it, and the disclosure is the verse's meaning: the pages spread, and what they show is what was already written. The human being on the Day is not confronted with a judgment being formed. He is confronted with a judgment that was formed, being shown.

**The Tenth Object, the Instrument**

The pattern of the list holds, and the tenth verse adds the instrument to the scene. The first objects were the world's fixtures; the middle objects were its creatures; the ninth was the first human case. The tenth is the instrument by which every case — the first and all the rest — will be handled. The sūrah has moved from the cosmic to the personal, and now to the judicial: the girl was the particular charge, the record is the general instrument. The movement is the sūrah's preparation of its main clause. When the list is finished, the clause will say that each soul will know what it has brought, and the record is the means of the knowing. The tenth verse places the means in the scene, and the placing is the last of the preparations. After it, the sūrah's list turns to the two outcomes, and the outcomes close the setting.

**Read Your Record**

The command that follows the laying open is stated in the Book in two words, and the two words carry the whole scene. *"And it will be said, 'Read your record. You (alone) are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself'"* (17:14). The record is read, and the reading is the soul's own act — not the scribe's, not the angel's, the soul's. The sūrah's tenth verse is the spread of the pages, and the verse of the command is the taking up of them. Between the two stands the soul, alone with the account, and the aloneness is the verse's final word: the pages are laid open, and the one who reads them is the one they were written for.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:11

> and when the sky is stripped away,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Roof Removed**

The eleventh object is the sky itself, and the verb is *nūḥitat* — peeled, stripped, scraped off. The image is the removal of a covering: a skin lifted from the thing it covered, a layer taken off the surface of the world. The sky in the Book is the cover, and the Book states the office of the cover: *"And We have made the sky a well-protected canopy, still they turn away from its signs"* (21:32). Protected is the word, and the canopy is the protection. The sūrah's eleventh verse removes the canopy, and with the removal the world is left without its covering — the roof of the creation taken down, the thing that was above everything now taken away from above everything. The verse is the list's act on the sky as such, not on the sky's contents: the stars have already fallen, and now the sky that held them is stripped.

**Stripped, Split, or Rolled**

The commentators divided the verb, and the division is the verse's range. Several took it in the meaning the translation carries: the sky is stripped off, peeled away from the world, the covering lifted. Others took the same root in the sense of splitting: the sky is cleft, divided, opened along a line. And a further reading, stated in the tafsīr of the verse, took it as rolling up, the sky rolled the way a scroll is rolled, gathered from its spread state into a bundle. The three images — peeled, split, rolled — are three ways of describing one act: the end of the sky's extension. It no longer stretches over the world. It is removed, divided, or gathered, and in each case the covering is finished. The sūrah allows the range because the event, as the sūrah wants it, is the covering's end in whatever form the end takes.

**What the Covering Hid**

The removal of the sky is not, in the sūrah's account, destruction for its own sake. It is disclosure. A covering hides what is under it, and the lifting of the covering is the exposure of what was hidden. The sūrah has just opened the records in the tenth verse, and it strips the sky in the eleventh: the files are spread, and the roof over the court comes off. The sequence is the sūrah's logic. The record is the evidence, and the sky is the cover under which the world has stood unexamined. The Day opens the evidence and removes the cover, and the two verses together are the full act of disclosure. What was under the sky — the world, the deed, the account — is now under nothing, and the being under nothing is the state of the Day.

**The Sequence Is a Courtroom**

Verses ten through thirteen, read together, are a proceeding in four steps. The records are laid open: the evidence is placed. The sky is stripped away: the cover is removed, the scene exposed. The Hellfire is kindled: the sentence of the condemned is pronounced. Paradise is brought near: the sentence of the blessed is pronounced. The sūrah's final four signs are not a mixture of images. They are the sequence of a trial, from the evidence to the two outcomes, and the eleventh verse is the second step, the step that makes the trial visible. The sūrah has built its list as a courtroom all along, and the last four items are the courtroom in operation: the files, the exposure, the two verdicts. The eleventh verse is where the setting becomes the procedure.

**The Canopy That Protected**

The Book's own image of the sky is the image the verse reverses, and the reversal is the verse's meaning. The sky was made a well-protected canopy — protection is its name, and the protection is for the world below. The sūrah takes the protected canopy and strips it, and the stripping is not an attack on the protection but the end of the need for it. On the Day the world is no longer a world that needs a roof; the creatures of the world are no longer creatures that stand under a sky. The canopy is removed because its function — the covering of the provisional order — is finished. The sūrah's eleventh verse is therefore the verse of the end of the covering, and the end of the covering is the end of the world as a place that stands under something. The creatures stand, and above them is nothing.

**The Eleventh Object, the Last Covering**

The list has now removed every covering the world had. The sun was wrapped, the stars were dropped, the mountains were moved, the sea was unheld, and the sky is stripped. The world's objects are all undone, and the undoing is complete in the sense that nothing that covered anything is left. The eleventh verse is the last of the signs that concern the world's architecture, and with it the sūrah's catalogue of the unmade world is finished. What follows — the two outcomes, the fire and the garden — concerns not the world but the persons, and the persons' destinations. The eleventh verse is the hinge of the second kind: the world is now entirely cleared, and the sūrah can turn from the clearing to the judgment, which the clearing was preparing.

**Rolled Up in the Right Hand**

The Book gives the sky's gathering its final form in a verse that carries the same act to its completion. *"They have not shown Allah His proper reverence—when on the Day of Judgment the (whole) earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand. Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate (with Him)!"* (39:67). The sūrah's sky is stripped; this sky is rolled up in the Hand, and the two verbs — stripped, rolled — are the two faces of the same removal. The stripping is the covering's end; the rolling is the covering's gathering into the One whose it was. The eleventh verse is the event, and the verse of the Hand is the event's end, the end in which the removed thing has its owner, and the owner's hand is the only place left for the rolled-up sky.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:12

> and when the Hellfire is fiercely flared up,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fire Kindled**

The twelfth object is the Hellfire, and the verb is *zuḥḍitat* — kindled, flared up, driven to its full blaze. The translation's adverb, *fiercely*, carries the root's force: this is not a fire kept low or a fire kept waiting. It is a fire brought to the height of its nature, the brazier fed until it roars. The Book describes the state of the fire on the Day in a single image: *"When they are tossed into it, they will hear its roaring as it boils over"* (67:7). Boiling over is the fire's motion, and the sūrah's verb is the fire's state: kindled to the point of boiling, flared to the point of roaring. The verse is the list's first sign that concerns a destination rather than an event, and it states the destination in the intensity the destination will have.

**Kindled By What**

The commentators asked what kindles the fire, and their answer names the two sources. The fire is kindled by the anger of God and by the sins of the human beings, the anger fed by the sin, the sin the fuel and the anger the feeding. The two are not separate causes in the commentators' account but one cause with two faces: the sinner's deed and the Lord's response to it, the two together making the blaze. The sūrah's verse does not state the cause; it states the state. The fire is flared, and the flaring is what the list records. But the commentators' answer supplies the sūrah's silence, and the silence is the sūrah's way of keeping the verse a sign rather than a cause: the sign is the fire's state, and the state is enough for the list, because the list is a list of what the Day will show.

**Kindled, or Brought Near**

A second reading of the verb stands beside the first, and it is the reading the early voices put on record. Qatādah and others, among the companions' generation, took the same word in the sense of being brought near: the Hellfire is brought near to its inhabitants, not merely kindled in the distance but advanced to the point where the condemned stand at its edge. The two readings agree on the event and differ on the emphasis. The first emphasizes the fire's state — the blaze; the second emphasizes the fire's relation to the person — the proximity. The sūrah permits both, because the Day, as the sūrah presents it, is both: the fire is at its height, and the fire is at the person's edge. The condemned are not punished from a distance. They are kindled toward, and the two readings are the two sides of the one approach.

**The First Outcome**

The twelfth verse is the first of the list's two outcomes, and its position is the sūrah's statement of the order. The world has been cleared — the sun, the stars, the mountains, the sea, the sky — the record has been opened, the girl has been asked, and now the two destinations are named, the fire first and the garden second. The order is not arbitrary. The sūrah has been a sūrah of the deniers' unmaking, and it places the unmaking's destination before the making's, the fire before the garden, as the list's gravity asks. The twelfth verse is therefore the turning of the list from the account to the judgment, and the judgment's first face is the fire. The fire is kindled, and the kindling is the first pronouncement of the two that close the sūrah's opening stanza.

**A Blaze, Not a Flicker**

The sūrah's choice of intensity is its choice of warning. The fire is not described as a quiet fire or a waiting fire. It is flared, and the flaring is the verse's word for the Day's seriousness. The Book's own cry over the same fire matches the sūrah's verb: *"Woe on that Day to the deniers!"* (77:19). The woe is the fire's address, and the fire's address is to the deniers specifically — not to the negligent, not to the doubtful, the deniers, the ones who saw the signs and closed the hand. The sūrah's twelfth verse is the woe made into a sign: the fire that the woe announces is here, on the list, kindled before the main clause is spoken. The intensity of the sign matches the intensity of the refusal, and the match is the sūrah's arithmetic of warning.

**The Fire in the List's Order**

The fire sits where the sūrah's logic places it: after the records and the disclosure, before the garden and the knowing. The order is the order of the trial. The evidence is spread, the cover is removed, and then the first verdict — the fire kindled — is pronounced, followed by the second verdict, the garden brought near. The sūrah does not mix the verdicts with the evidence; it sequences them, and the sequence is the verse's meaning. The fire is kindled because the record is open, and the record is open because the sky is stripped, and the sky is stripped because the world is cleared. Each sign stands on the one before it, and the twelfth verse stands on the eleventh: the disclosure is done, and the sentence of the condemned begins.

**The Wicked in Hell**

The neighbouring sūrah states the fire's occupants in a single clause, and the clause is the verse's own completion. *"And the wicked will be in Hell"* (82:14) — the wicked, in Hell, no further verb needed, because the being-in is the state. The sūrah's twelfth verse kindles the fire; the verse of the neighbour places the people in it. The two together are the full sign: the fire at its height, and the condemned in it, and the kindling and the placing are the two faces of the same event. The sūrah gives the fire its state, and the Book elsewhere gives the fire its inhabitants, and the sūrah's list, read with the Book, is the complete account of the first outcome.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:13

> and when Paradise is brought near—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Garden Comes to Them**

The thirteenth object is Paradise, and the verb is *zuyyinat* — brought near, in the reading the commentators put first, and adorned, in the reading that stands beside it. The translation takes the first: the garden is brought near, and the bringing is the verse's act. The Book states the same bringing in a verse that names the ones to whom it is done: *"Paradise will be brought near to the God-fearing,"* (26:90). The garden does not wait; it approaches. The blessed are not carried to it across a distance; it comes to them, and the coming is the sign. The sūrah's thirteenth verse is the second outcome of the list, and it is stated in the motion of approach, the garden moving toward the one who has stood in the fire's opposite.

**Brought Near, or Adorned**

The second reading of the verb is the reading of adornment: the garden is made beautiful, decorated, set out in its beauty for the one who enters. The two readings — brought near, adorned — are not opposed in the commentators' account so much as successive, the way the fire's two readings were successive. The garden is approached, and in the approach it is presented, the beauty set out as the nearness arrives. The sūrah permits both because the event, as the sūrah wants it, is the presentation of the garden in its fullness: the distance gone, and the beauty shown. The nearness is the garden's motion, and the adornment is the garden's state in the motion, and the verse holds the two together the way it held the fire's kindling and its approach together in the verse before it.

**The Last Image Before the Result**

The thirteenth verse is the last sign on the list, and the sign is the garden. The sūrah's opening stanza is a list of thirteen signs, and the thirteenth is the one the soul meets last in the list's order: the fire kindled, and then the garden brought near. The sequence is the sūrah's statement of what the soul will face, and the face is two-fold, the fire and the garden, the condemned's destination and the blessed's, named in that order and closed by the garden. The sūrah ends its catalogue on the garden, and the ending is deliberate: the list that opened with the sun wrapped up closes with the garden coming, and the whole of the thirteen signs stands between the two — the unmaking of the world in the middle, the two destinations at the ends. The thirteenth verse is the list's last word before the list gives way to its result.

**Two Destinations, One Grammar**

The twelfth and thirteenth verses are built on the same grammar, and the grammar is the sūrah's point. The fire is flared up; the garden is brought near. Both are passive: the destinations are not chosen in the verses but acted upon, the fire kindled, the garden approached, and the acting belongs to the Day. Both are of proximity: the fire is at its height and at the edge, the garden is coming and is near. And both are the two faces of one judgment, pronounced in the same tense, with the same flat certainty the sūrah has used since the first verse. The sūrah's two outcomes are not a pair of pictures. They are a pair of clauses, and the clauses' agreement is the judgment's form: the same Day, the same motion, the two destinations meeting the soul from opposite sides.

**Proximity as the Day's Logic**

The Book's doctrine of the Day works by nearness, and the sūrah's two outcome-verses are the doctrine in miniature. The garden is brought near, and the Book states the nearness as a standing fact: *"And Paradise will be brought near to the righteous, not far off"* (50:31). The fire, in the same logic, is at the edge of the condemned, kindled toward them rather than far from them. The Day is not a day of distance. It is a day on which the destinations come to the persons, the two outcomes advancing on the soul from both sides, and the soul's position — which side it has stood on in this life — is the position the nearness finds it in. The sūrah's thirteenth verse is the nearness stated for the blessed, and the nearness is the verse's theology: the garden comes, and it comes close.

**The Thirteenth Object, the Last of the Setting**

With the garden the sūrah's list is finished, and the finishing is the verse's function. Every object on the list has now been named and undone or advanced: the sun, the stars, the mountains, the camels, the beasts, the sea, the souls, the girl, the record, the sky, the fire, the garden. The setting is complete, and the sūrah can now say what the setting was for. The next verse is the main clause, the result of the thirteen "whens," and the result is a knowing: each soul will know what it has brought. The thirteenth verse is the last of the preparations, and the preparation is finished in the garden's approach. The world is cleared, the record is open, the two destinations are named, and the sūrah's list stands complete, waiting to say what all of it produced.

**Not Far Off**

The Book's own phrase for the garden's approach is the phrase the sūrah's verb carries. *"And Paradise will be brought near to the righteous, not far off"* (50:31). The nearness is stated twice in the verse — brought near, and not far off — and the doubling is the Book's insistence that the approach is real and not a figure. The sūrah's thirteenth verse is the same insistence inside the list: the garden is brought near, and the bringing is the sign, not a description of a state. The garden's nearness is the last sign the sūrah gives before it turns from the signs to the knowing, and the nearness stands as the list's closing word: the fire kindled, the garden coming, and both at the edge of the soul that is about to learn, in the next verse, what it has brought to the meeting.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:14

> ˹on that Day˺ each soul will know what ˹deeds˺ it has brought along.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Main Clause, At Last**

Thirteen verses are subordinate clauses, and this is the first principal one. The sūrah has spent its opening in a series of "whens" — the sun, the stars, the mountains, the camels, the beasts, the sea, the souls, the girl, the record, the sky, the fire, the garden — and not one of the thirteen has yet said what the "whens" produce. This verse says it. The structure of the stanza is a long suspense, and the suspense is released in a single clause: each soul will know. The sūrah's list was not a catalogue for its own sake. It was the lead-in to this sentence, and the sentence is the point of the list. Every sign on the list was a condition, and the condition's result is the knowing. The sūrah withholds the result for fourteen verses, and the withholding is the sūrah's first act of emphasis: the knowing is what all the rest was for.

**What the Soul Has Brought**

The phrase is *mā aḍḥarat* — what it has brought, what it has prepared, what it has carried along. The soul is the bearer, and the bearing is the deed: the good and the evil, the done and the left undone, the accumulated weight of the life. The Book states the same carrying in a parallel verse of the neighbouring sūrah, and the parallel is close enough to be the same sentence in another voice: *"each soul will know what it has sent forth or left behind"* (82:5). Sent forth and left behind are the two halves of the carrying — the deed done and the deed omitted, the act and the refusal — and the sūrah's phrase, *what it has brought*, holds the two together in the one load. The soul arrives with its load, and the load is the soul's own making. No one brings it for the soul, and no one brings it to the soul from outside. The carrying is the life, and the life is what the soul has brought.

**Knowledge, Not Excuse**

The verb in the verse is knowing, and the knowing is not accompanied by any clause of excuse, of appeal, of revision. The soul knows, and the knowing is the verse's entire content. The Book's account of the same moment states what the knowing means: the body itself is the witness, and the witness does not spare. *"They will ask their skin (furiously), 'Why have you testified against us?' It will say, 'We have been made to speak by Allah, who causes all things to speak'"* (41:21). The skin testifies, the skin speaks, and the speaking is the soul's knowing made external. The sūrah's verse gives the soul the knowledge, and the Book elsewhere gives the knowledge its witnesses: the ear, the eye, the skin, the record. The knowing is total because the testimony is total, and the total testimony is why the knowing is not an excuse but a finding.

**Each Soul, Alone**

The subject of the verb is *kullu nafsin* — each soul, every soul, the soul one by one. The sūrah's knowing is individual, and the individuality is the verse's quiet reversal of the world. In the world the soul is mixed: in the tribe, in the family, in the crowd, the deed shared and the responsibility diffused. On the Day the sūrah separates the soul from the mixture, and the separation is in the grammar: each soul, each one, will know. The crowd is gone, the tribe is gone, the cover of the many is gone, and the one stands with the load. The sūrah's list gathered the world's objects into a scene, and the scene's last item is the one: the soul, alone, with what it has brought. The knowing is the soul's private act, and the privacy is the verse's final word on the community of this life.

**The Knowing Without the Hiding**

The commentators drew from the verse a further consequence, and the consequence is the end of the obfuscation. The soul will know the good and the evil it has done, and the knowing leaves no room for the lie, because the record is open and the body testifies and the soul itself is the witness against itself. The hiding that is possible in this life — the hidden deed, the unspoken word, the sin covered by the silence of the world — is impossible in the knowing, because the knowing is the removal of the cover. The sūrah's verse is the verse of the end of the hidden, and the end of the hidden is the end of the world's last protection for the sinner. The soul knows, and in the knowing the soul finds that there is nothing left to hide, and the finding is the verse's weight.

**The Stanza Closes, the Argument Opens**

The fourteenth verse closes the sūrah's first section, and the closing is a hinge. The thirteen signs and their result are complete: the world is unmade, the record is open, the two destinations are named, and each soul knows its load. The sūrah now turns. The next verse is an oath — I swear by the receding stars — and the turn is the sūrah's second movement, from the account of the Day to the defence of the Book. The knowing of the fourteenth verse is the ground the oath stands on: the Day is certain, the knowing is certain, and now the sūrah swears by the stars to what is being recited. The first section ends with the soul's knowledge, and the second begins with God's oath, and the hinge between them is the verse that completes the list and opens the argument.

**The Left Hand**

The Book gives the knowing its two faces in the scene of the record, and the face of the condemned is the face the sūrah's knowing implies. *"And as for those given their record in their left hand, they will cry (bitterly), 'I wish I had not been given my record'"* (69:25). The wish is the knowing's sound: the soul that knows what it has brought wishes it had not been given the knowledge, and the wishing is the verse of the left hand. The sūrah's fourteenth verse is the knowing stated without the sound; the verse of the record gives the knowing its cry. Between the two stands the soul, and the soul's position — the right hand or the left — is the position the load has fixed. The sūrah closes its first section on the knowing, and the Book opens the knowing's two doors, one after the other, in the scene it tells elsewhere.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:15

> I do swear by the receding stars

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath After the Apocalypse**

The fifteenth verse is the sūrah's turn, and the turn is an oath. The first fourteen verses were the account of the Day, stated in the "when" clauses, and the fifteenth opens the second section with a sworn statement: I swear. The particle *fa-lā uqsimu* is the turning-point of the sūrah, and the *fa* — the *so* — carries the whole of the first section into the second. The sūrah has shown that the Day is certain, and now it swears, on the ground of that certainty, to what it is about to state. The oath is not a new subject. It is the first section's result turned into a guarantee: the Day is settled, and what the sūrah is now going to say about the Book is sworn on the same ground. The sūrah's structure is two movements, and the fifteenth verse is the door between them.

**The Receding Stars**

The oath is by *al-khunnas*, the receding ones, and the commentators took the word in the sense of the stars that go down and come back — the bodies that recede in the day's darkness and reappear in the night's. The reading the early voices put first was the reading of the planets visible to the naked eye, the ones that go and return in their courses, the receding and the returning the two halves of their motion. A further reading took the motion in the sense of the retrograde: the planet that advances and then appears to turn back, the going-forward and the coming-back in the same body. The sūrah's word, *receding*, holds the motion without fixing it, and the commentators' range — the setting, the returning, the retrograde — is the range of the receding itself. The star recedes, and the receding is what the oath takes.

**Swearing by What Will Fall**

The oath's object is the same object the sūrah opened with. The second verse named the stars falling, and the fifteenth swears by the stars receding: one object, two verses, the fall and the receding. The sūrah binds its two sections to the same body. The stars that will fall are the stars that recede now, in their daily going and returning, and the oath takes the daily motion as the ground of the statement that follows. The connection is the sūrah's own: the certainty of the fall is the certainty of the motion, because the motion is the order and the fall is the order's end. The sūrah swears by the order to the Book, and the order is the one whose end it has just described. The two sections share a cosmos, and the shared cosmos is the stars, receding now and falling then, witnessed by the sūrah in both its movements.

**The Shape of the Oath**

The Qur'an swears by creation, and the swearing is a form the Book uses with regularity: the night when it covers, the day when it breaks, the sun and its shining, the sky and its courses. The sūrah's fifteenth verse places the oath in a position the form rarely takes: not at the head of the sūrah but at its middle, after the apocalypse and before the defence of the Book. The oaths that open the short Meccan sūrahs stand at the beginning, and this oath stands at the turning. The placement is the sūrah's choice, and the choice is its meaning: the oath here is not an opening but a pivot, the ground laid by the first section turned into the guarantee of the second. The sūrah swears in the middle of itself, and the middle is where the argument shifts from what will happen to what is being said.

**Why the Stars**

The object of the oath is the object the audience knows. The Meccan listener's sky is the sky of the stars: the navigation, the season, the poetry, the omen, the reading of the night. The sūrah calls to witness the very bodies on which the audience's own astronomy rests, and the calling is the sūrah's address. The stars are not a foreign guarantee. They are the audience's own witnesses, the ones the audience has watched and named and measured by, and the sūrah puts them on the stand. The oath is thus the sūrah's use of the audience's own knowledge against the audience's own denial: by the stars you know, I swear, and what I swear to is the word that is being recited to you. The stars are the ground, and the ground is the audience's.

**The Oath's Object Still Unnamed**

The oath in the fifteenth verse is not yet complete. The *I swear by the receding stars* looks forward: the stars are the first of the oath's objects, and the objects continue in the verses that follow — the night, the day — until the oath's main clause arrives and the swearing is joined to what is sworn to. The sūrah withholds the main clause from the oath, and the withholding is the same withholding it used in the first section, where the main clause waited for the fourteenth verse. The form is the sūrah's standing manner: the conditions are stated, the result is delayed, and the delay is the emphasis. The oath's objects will be named in the next verses, and the oath's statement will come after them, and the fifteenth verse is the first of the oath's clauses, the receding stars taking their place as the first witness called.

**Hide, Advance, Conceal**

The classical reading of the oath's three words, transmitted in the reports on the verse, parses them as a sequence: those that hide, those that advance, those that conceal — *al-khunnas*, *al-jawār*, *al-kunnas*, the three names given to the stars in the same breath. The reading makes the oath a triad of motions, the hiding and the advancing and the concealing, the star's going and its returning told in three words. The sūrah's own translation carries the first of the three — the receding — and the two words that follow in the verse complete the triad in the next line. The three names are the star's motion in full, and the oath takes the motion in its completeness: the star that hides, the star that advances, the star that conceals, all three called to witness, all three receding and returning in the course the sūrah has sworn by.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:16

> which travel and hide,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath's Continuation**

The sixteenth verse is not a new clause. It is the completion of the one the previous verse opened. The *I swear by the receding stars* looks forward to its object's full description, and the description arrives here: the stars that travel and hide. The relative clause joins to the noun across the verse boundary, and the sūrah's grammar makes the two verses one oath. The sūrah is careful with the oath's form: the object is not finished until the motion is named, and the motion — the travelling and the hiding — is what the oath actually takes. The swearer does not swear by the stars as stones. He swears by the stars as they move, and the moving is stated in the second verse of the pair. The oath's object is a motion, and the motion is the travelling and the hiding.

**Go and Hide**

The two verbs in the clause are *yadhhabbāna* and *yaʾlūna* — they go, and they hide. The going is the star's motion in its course, the advancing across the sky, the daily passing from place to place. The hiding is the star's withdrawal, the sinking below the rim of the world, the disappearance into the darkness where the eye no longer finds it. The two verbs together are the star's whole business: it goes, and it hides, and in the going and the hiding it keeps its course. The sūrah's oath is by exactly this: the body that moves and withdraws, advances and disappears, the star in its complete daily act. The motion is the object, and the object is the one the sky performs every day, without failure, in the sight of everyone who looks up.

**The Cycle as Argument**

The regularity of the motion is the argument the oath carries. A motion that repeats exactly — the star goes, hides, and returns, and the going and the hiding are the same each day, in the same order, at the same measure — is a motion that does not happen by itself. The cycle is the cosmos keeping its word, and the keeping is the evidence. The sūrah's oath is thus a proof in the form of an appeal: by the cycle, which stands as the standing order, I swear to what I am about to state. The appeal presumes what the cycle shows — that there is a keeper of the order, and the keeper is the one who swears. The sūrah does not argue the point here; it takes it as the ground, and the ground is the cycle the audience has watched their whole lives without being stopped by it.

**The Singular for the Plural**

The Arabic of the clause uses the singular relative form over the plural noun: the stars, and the one that goes and hides, the relative standing in the singular over the plural body. The small grammar carries a large claim. The stars do not go and hide as a scattered crowd, each on its own. They go and hide as one, the sky acting as a single body, the many stars the many parts of one motion. The sūrah's word choice makes the cosmos an agent: the sky travels, the sky hides, and the travelling and the hiding are the sky's own acts. The unity of the motion is the unity of the order, and the order's unity is what the oath takes as its object. The singular over the plural is the sūrah's way of saying that the stars' motion is one motion, and the one motion is the witness.

**Hiding Is Not Perishing**

The star's hiding is not its death, and the distinction is the verse's quiet theology. The star sinks, withdraws, disappears from the eye, and it does not end: it returns. The cycle of the going and the hiding is a cycle of presence and absence, not of being and nothing. The sūrah's oath is by the bodies that disappear and come back, and the coming back is the point of the oath. The cosmos the sūrah swears by is a cosmos of return, not of perishing, and the return is what makes the final end — the falling of the stars in the second verse — the exception that proves the order. The stars hide and return, day after day, and the one day on which they do not return is the day the sūrah has just described. The hiding is the rule, and the falling is the end of the rule.

**The Oath on Constancy**

The sūrah's oath, taken in its two verses, is an oath on constancy. The receding stars, which travel and hide, are the bodies whose constancy is the sky's own character: they do not fail, they do not vary, they do not arrive late or withdraw early. The constancy is the object, and the constancy is what the sūrah calls to witness the statement that follows. The appeal is the sūrah's standing move in this section: the created order is summoned to bear testimony to the word being recited, and the order's testimony is its constancy. The stars that travel and hide are the witnesses because they keep their course, and the keeping of the course is the ground on which the sūrah swears. The oath is complete in its object, and the object is the constancy of the receding stars.

**The Same Oath in Another Sūrah**

The Book swears by the same bodies in the sūrah that opens the defence of the prophet in the third section of the short Meccan set. *"By the stars when they fade away!"* (53:1) — the stars taken in their fading, the going-dark the oath's object there as the going-and-hiding is the oath's object here. The two oaths stand across the Book, and they take the same bodies in two of their states: the receding and the hiding in this sūrah, the fading in the other. The agreement of the two appeals is the Book's way of making the stars' constancy a standing witness: the same sky is called to testify in two places, and the testimony is the constancy that both oaths take. The sūrah's sixteenth verse is the first of the two appeals, and the appeal's object is the motion that does not fail.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:17

> and the night as it falls

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath Completes the Circle**

The seventeenth verse adds the second of the oath's great objects: after the stars, the night. The oath has taken the receding stars in their motion, and it now takes the night in its coming. The sūrah's objects are the sky's own furniture — the bodies that move, and the dark that covers — and with the night the oath has covered the whole of the sky's daily state. The stars belong to the night, and the night belongs to the stars, and the oath takes the two together: the movers and the covering, the lights and the dark. The circle of the sky is now the oath's object in full, and the sūrah has sworn by the sky as the sky stands, the stars in their courses and the night in its falling. The object is complete in its two halves, and the two halves are the night with its lights.

**The Ambiguous Verb**

The verb in the clause is *ʿasʿasa*, and the verb is the one the grammarians marked as the double-meaning word: it means to come near, and it means to withdraw, the approaching and the leaving in the same root. The commentators took the night accordingly in its two moments: the night as it approaches, the dark coming over the rim of the world, and the night as it departs, the dark lifting and the morning preparing. The ambiguity is not a defect in the sūrah's word; it is the sūrah's range. The night in the oath is the whole night — the coming and the going, the covering and the uncovering — and the one verb that holds the two is the oath's way of taking the night in its completeness. The sūrah swears by the night as it does both: it falls, and it lifts, and the two are the night's own motion.

**The Night as Cover**

The Book's own image of the night is the image of the cover, and the image is the sūrah's verb in its natural sense. The night covers: it comes over the world the way a covering comes over the thing it covers, and the covering is the night's office. The sūrah's *as it falls* is the covering's motion — the dark descending, the world taken under it, the day's light withdrawn and the night's taken in its place. The cover is the night's name in the Book's standing image, and the sūrah's oath takes the cover in its motion, the falling of the covering on the world. The night is not an absence in the sūrah's account. It is a presence with a motion, the cover that falls, and the falling is what the oath swears by.

**The Two Ends, One Oath**

The commentators' two readings — the night's beginning and the night's end — are the two ends of the same object, and the oath takes both. If the verb is taken in the sense of the approach, the night in the sūrah is the night that is coming, the dark at its first falling. If it is taken in the sense of the withdrawal, the night is the night that is going, the dark at its last lifting. The sūrah permits the range, and the range is the oath's completeness: the night in its whole course, from the first falling to the last lifting, is the object sworn by. The two readings are not a choice the sūrah leaves to the reader. They are the two ends the sūrah includes, and the including is the oath's manner. The night is taken in its two moments, and the two moments are the night as the sūrah's sky performs it.

**The Falling, Not the Darkening**

The translation's word, *falls*, is the sūrah's verb in its motion, and the motion is the clause's content. The night does not simply grow dark in the sūrah's image. It falls: it comes down on the world, the cover descending, the dark arriving as a body arrives. The falling is the cover's act, and the act is the oath's object. The sūrah's choice of the motion over the state is its standing choice in this section: it swears by the objects in their acts, not in their conditions. The stars travel and hide, and the night falls, and the falling is the night's act as the travelling is the stars'. The sūrah's oath is an oath on the sky's doing, and the doing of the night is the falling, the cover's descent on the world it covers.

**The Night Between the Two**

The oath's structure is now visible in its three objects: the stars, the night, and — in the verse that follows — the day. The night stands between the two lights, and its position is its office. The stars are the lights of the night, and the day is the light that the night withdraws to make room for, and the night is the interval between the two, the cover that falls when the day's light goes and lifts when the day's light returns. The sūrah's oath takes the interval in its motion, and the interval's motion is the falling. The night between the two lights is the sūrah's second object, and the object's place in the cycle is the object's meaning: the cover that the sky puts on the world, and takes off, and puts on again, in the order the oath has sworn by.

**By the Night When It Covers**

The Book swears by the same night in the sūrah that opens with the night's cover. *"By the night when it covers"* (92:1) — the cover the oath's object, the covering the night's office, and the two oaths standing across the Book on the same object. The agreement of the two appeals is the Book's way of making the night's cover a standing witness: the same falling is sworn by in two places, and the witness is the cover's motion. The sūrah's seventeenth verse is the appeal in the middle of its sūrah, the appeal that stands between the apocalypse and the defence of the Book, and the appeal's object is the night as it does what it does: it covers, it falls, and the falling is the oath.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:18

> and the day as it breaks!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Oath, and the Point**

The eighteenth verse is the oath's last object, and the object is the day. The sūrah has sworn by the stars in their motion, by the night in its falling, and now by the day in its breaking, and with the day the oath's objects are complete. The whole of the sky's daily cycle is now the oath's ground: the lights that move, the cover that falls, and the light that breaks. The sūrah has sworn by the cycle in its three parts, and the swearing is finished in the day's breaking. The exclamation at the end of the verse is the oath's close: the objects are named, the cycle is complete, and the sūrah is ready to state what the oath was for. The breaking of the day is the last of the witnesses called, and the call is done.

**Breath as Light**

The verb in the clause is *najlā* — it appears, it is clear, it breaks — and the commentators gave the day's breaking its image in the act of breathing. The morning, they say, appears the way a sleeper awakens, and its light spreads the way breath spreads: the day breathes out, the light expanding from the first line at the rim of the world until the world is full of it. The image is the sūrah's word in its natural figure, and the figure is the day's act made visible. The day does not arrive in the sūrah's account as a state. It arrives as an act — the breaking, the appearing, the breathing-out — and the act is the oath's object. The light that breaks is the light that breathes, and the breathing is the day's motion as the falling was the night's.

**The Exclamation's Force**

The verse ends with a mark of force, and the force is the oath's closing. The three objects of the oath — the stars, the night, the day — are now named, and the naming ends with emphasis, the way an appeal ends when the last witness has been sworn in. The exclamation is the sūrah's way of marking the completeness: the objects are all here, the cycle is all here, and the appeal stands on its full ground. The sūrah does not state the point of the oath in this verse. The point comes in the verse that follows, and the exclamation is the pause before the point, the held breath before the statement. The force of the exclamation is the force of the completeness, and the completeness is the three objects standing together.

**The Witness of the Turning**

The oath's three objects, taken together, are the turning of the sky: the stars that travel and hide, the night that falls, the day that breaks. The turning is the sky's own act, the daily rotation that brings the lights and the dark in their order, and the sūrah's oath is by the turning in its three states. The witness called is not a body but a motion, and the motion is the one the audience's whole life is set in: they are born in the turning, they keep their accounts by it, they travel by its lights, and they sleep in its cover. The sūrah calls the turning to witness, and the turning is the audience's own world, the world they have never questioned, called now to stand and testify. The witness is the world, and the world is the oath.

**What the Oath Guarantees**

The oath's function is to guarantee the statement that follows, and the guarantee is the sūrah's argument in its form. The sūrah has sworn by the cycle — the stars, the night, the day — and what it swears to is the word that is being recited, the Book itself, and the character of the Book. The appeal is the sūrah's way of placing the Book on the same ground as the cycle: by the order that stands, I swear to the word that is spoken. The order is the created constancy, and the word is the revealed constancy, and the two are set together in the oath. The guarantee is thus the sūrah's claim in its strongest form: the Book stands as the cycle stands, and the cycle's standing is what the oath takes as its ground. The breaking of the day is the last of the ground's parts, and the ground is complete.

**The Oath's Three Objects, One Cycle**

The structure of the oath is now whole, and the wholeness is the verse's meaning. Three objects, one cycle: the stars in their motion, the night in its falling, the day in its breaking. The three are not three separate appeals. They are the one appeal in its three parts, the sky's daily act told in its three moments. The sūrah's form is the form of the cycle: the appeal moves through the cycle the way the cycle moves, from the lights to the cover to the light, and the movement of the appeal is the movement of the sky. The oath is complete when the day breaks, because the cycle is complete when the day breaks, and the sūrah's three verses of the oath are the sky's day in three clauses. The last clause is the breaking, and the breaking is the cycle's completion and the oath's.

**The Same Breath in the Neighbouring Sūrah**

The Book gives the day's breaking its own oath in the sūrah that opens with the wrapped man. *"And the day as it breaks!"* (74:34) — the same verb, the same motion, the breaking the oath's object in both places. And the sky's cycles carry their own appeal in the sūrah of the heaven and the star: *"By the sky with its recurring cycles"* (86:11). The three appeals stand across the Book on the same ground — the breaking, the cycles, the turning — and the ground is the sky's daily act. The sūrah's eighteenth verse is the appeal in its middle position, the appeal that stands between the apocalypse and the statement, and the appeal's object is the day as it does the one thing the day does: it breaks, and the breaking is the oath's last word.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:19

> Indeed, this ˹Quran˺ is the Word of ˹Allah delivered by Gabriel,˺ a noble messengerangel,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Main Clause of the Oath**

The nineteenth verse is the oath's statement, and the statement is the sūrah's second movement in a single sentence. The oath has named its objects — the stars, the night, the day — and now it states what it swears to: this is the word of a noble messenger. The *inna* — the *indeed* — is the particle of the settled claim, and the claim is the sūrah's claim about the Book it is reciting. The sūrah has spent eighteen verses establishing the ground — the certainty of the Day, the constancy of the sky — and the ground now carries its load: the Book is what it claims to be, and the claim is sworn on the cycle. The verse is the hinge of the sūrah's second section, and the hinge turns on a single identification: the word of a noble messenger.

**The Noble Messenger**

The noun is *rūḥun karīm* — a noble spirit, a noble messenger, and the translation's brackets give the identification the early voices put first: Gabriel, the angel through whom the word is delivered. The commentators' majority took the verse in that sense, the messenger being the angel, the spirit the one who brings the word down. A reading that stands beside it took the messenger in the sense of the human: the Prophet ﷺ, the one to whom the word is brought and through whom it is spoken. The two readings agree on the structure — the word has a messenger, and the messenger is noble — and they differ on the messenger's station. The sūrah's own word, *noble*, carries the station in both cases: the angel's nobility is his rank at the Throne, and the man's is his trust, and the verse states the nobility without fixing the station, because the nobility is the claim.

**Word, Not Composition**

The sūrah's word for the Book is *qawl* — the word, the saying, the spoken message — and the word is the sūrah's answer to the charges. The Meccan account of the Qur'an was the account of a composition: the poetry, the sorcery, the divination, the words of a man. The sūrah's answer is the account of a delivery: the word of a messenger, the message with a sender and a bearer, the saying that comes from one and is carried to another. The composition is made by the maker, and the delivery is brought by the bearer, and the sūrah places the Book in the second category. The Book is not the man's word in the sense of the man's making. It is the word of the messenger in the sense of the messenger's bringing, and the bringing is the sūrah's claim, sworn on the cycle of the sky.

**The Chain of Custody**

The verse opens a chain, and the chain is the sūrah's argument for the Book's origin. The word is the word of a noble messenger, and the messenger's nobility will be stated in the verses that follow — the power, the honour at the Throne, the obedience, the trust — and the chain runs from the source to the ear: the Lord of the Throne, the noble spirit, the human receiver, and the word delivered. The sūrah does not argue the Book's content in this section. It argues the Book's custody, and the custody is the argument: a message is as trustworthy as the chain that carries it, and the chain the sūrah states is the chain from the Throne to the man. The verse is the first link, and the first link is the messenger, and the messenger's character is what the following verses will establish, link by link.

**The Repeated Formula**

The Book's own definition of itself is the formula the sūrah uses, and the formula returns in the Book with the same structure: the word, the spirit, the bringing down. *"This is certainly a revelation from the Lord of all worlds, which the trustworthy spirit (Gabriel) brought down into your heart (O Prophet) — so that you may be one of the warners — in a clear Arabic tongue"* (26:192–194). The revelation from the Lord, the trustworthy spirit, the bringing down into the heart: the three parts of the formula, and the formula is the Book's standing self-description. The sūrah's nineteenth verse is the formula in its sworn form, the same identification placed under the oath of the cycle. The Book defines itself the way the sūrah defines it, and the definition is the word of the noble messenger, brought down, delivered, and spoken.

**The Noble, and the Power to Follow**

The nobility in the verse is not a single quality. It is the first of the qualities the sūrah will state, and the statement is the sūrah's method in this section. The messenger is noble, and the nobility will be completed by the power, the honour, the obedience, and the trust, each in its own verse, each a link in the chain. The sūrah's form is the form of the accumulation: one quality per verse, the qualities building to the whole, and the whole the messenger's complete character. The verse stands first in the accumulation, and the first quality is the nobility, and the nobility is the station: the messenger is not a low bearer, not a chance carrier, but the one whose rank is high. The following verses will say what the rank is, and this verse says that the rank is there, and the saying is sworn.

**Whoever Is an Enemy of Gabriel**

The Book puts the identification to its test in a verse that names the angel directly and sets the choice before the listener. *"Say, (O Prophet,) 'Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know that he revealed this (Quran) to your heart by Allah's Will, confirming what came before it — a guide and good news for the believers'"* (2:97). The verse names the messenger by his name, states his office — the revealing to the heart by God's will — and places the listener on one side or the other. The sūrah's nineteenth verse is the same identification in its sworn form, and the verse of the challenge is the identification made into a choice: the word of the noble messenger, and the one who opposes the messenger opposes the word. The sūrah swears to the messenger, and the Book names the messenger, and the two together are the claim and its test.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:20

> full of power, held in honour by the Lord of the Throne,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Attributes Accumulate**

The twentieth verse adds two qualities to the messenger's character, and the accumulation is the sūrah's method in this section. The messenger is noble, and now he is full of power, and held in honour by the Lord of the Throne. The sūrah states the qualities one at a time, verse by verse, and each verse adds a link to the chain: the nobility in the previous verse, the power and the honour in this one, and the obedience and the trust in the one that follows. The form is the accumulation, and the accumulation is the argument: the messenger's character is built up, quality by quality, until the character is complete and the claim stands on its full ground. The verse is the second of the accumulation, and the two qualities it adds are the power and the honour.

**Power and Its Use**

The phrase is *dhū quwwah* — one of power, possessed of strength, and the power is the messenger's capacity, the strength that carries the word from the source to the ear without loss. The Book describes the same power in the sūrah of the stars, where the angel is named in his strength: *"He has been taught by one (angel) of mighty power and great perfection"* (53:5). Mighty power is the phrase there, and the power here is the same power stated in the sūrah's own word. The power is not the messenger's own; it is the power given to the bearing, the strength the word needs to be carried from the Throne to the heart. The sūrah's verse states the power as a fact of the messenger's station, and the fact is the link in the chain: the word is carried by one who has the strength to carry it.

**The Throne and the Messenger**

The second quality is the honour, and the honour is located: held in honour by the Lord of the Throne. The proximity is the highest proximity the Book names — the Lord of the Throne is the Lord in his attribute of dominion, the Throne the attribute's seat, and the messenger held in honour in that presence. The Book gives the Throne its own name in a verse that pairs the attribute with the glory: *"Lord of the Throne, the All-Glorious"* (85:15). The sūrah's messenger stands in the presence the Book reserves for the highest of the angels, and the standing is the verse's claim: the bearer of the word is not a bearer of low rank. He is honoured where the honour is highest, and the highest honour is the honour at the Throne. The proximity is the guarantee, and the guarantee is the messenger's position.

**The Guarantee Is Structural**

The sūrah's argument in this verse is the argument of the structure. A message is trustworthy in proportion to the rank and the power of its bearer, and the sūrah states the bearer's rank and power as the message's guarantee. The claim is not argued in the content here; it is argued in the custody. The word is the word of one who is full of power, and the power is the capacity; the word is the word of one held in honour at the Throne, and the honour is the position. The two together are the structure of the guarantee: the bearer can carry the word, and the bearer stands where the word comes from. The sūrah's twentieth verse is the guarantee in its two parts, and the two parts are the power and the honour, and the honour is the power's place.

**Why the Meccans Would Object**

The charge the sūrah answers is the charge of the weak claim. The Meccan account of the Book's origin was the account of a man: a man of their own kind, a man who could be met, questioned, and tested, a man whose word was his word. The sūrah's answer is the account of the celestial chain: the word is not the man's making but the messenger's bringing, and the messenger is the one of power, honoured at the Throne. The objection the sūrah retires is the objection that rests on the man's smallness — the man is one of you, his word is his word, his claim is a man's claim. The verse retires it by removing the word from the man's making and placing it in the angel's bearing, and the angel's bearing is guaranteed by the angel's rank. The smallness of the human claim is answered by the height of the celestial chain.

**The Second Link of the Chain**

The chain of custody is now two links long, and the second link is the power and the honour. The first link was the nobility — the messenger's rank stated in its first quality. The second link is the power and the honour — the rank stated in its capacity and its position. The chain runs: the word, the noble messenger, the messenger's power, the messenger's honour at the Throne. The sūrah is building the chain link by link, and the verse is the second link, and the link's function is to state what the nobility means in the messenger's actual standing. The nobility was the quality; the power and the honour are the quality's content. The sūrah's form is the form of the unfolding: the quality named, and the quality opened, and the opening is the verse's two phrases.

**Exalted in Rank**

The Book gives the Throne's Lord its own statement of the rank, and the statement is the verse's warrant. *"(He is) Highly Exalted in rank, Lord of the Throne. He sends down the revelation by His command to whoever He wills of His servants to warn (all) of the Day of Meeting"* (40:15). The rank is the exaltation, the Lord is the Lord of the Throne, and the sending down is the act the messenger performs: the revelation sent by the command, to the one the Lord wills. The sūrah's messenger is the one who performs the act the verse of the Throne describes, and the act's performer stands in the rank the verse states. The sūrah's twentieth verse is the messenger in the rank, and the rank is the one the Book of the Throne gives to the Lord and to the one He sends: highly exalted, at the Throne, sending down.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:21

> obeyed there ˹in heaven˺, and trustworthy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Two Attributes**

The twenty-first verse closes the accumulation of the messenger's character with two qualities: obeyed, and trustworthy. The sūrah has stated the nobility, the power, the honour, and now it states the obedience and the trust, and with the two the character is complete. The form of the section is the form of the closing: the last qualities are the ones that concern the delivery itself. The nobility, the power, and the honour concerned the messenger's station; the obedience and the trust concern the messenger's function, and the function is the carrying of the word. The sūrah ends the accumulation on the function, because the function is what the claim needs: the word is carried by one who obeys and one who is trusted, and the two are the delivery's guarantee.

**Obeyed in Heaven**

The first of the two qualities is the obedience, and the translation's bracket gives the place: there, in heaven. The commentators took the obedience in the messenger's station among the angels: he is the one obeyed in the angelic assembly, the one whose bearing is the bearing the angels follow. The obedience is not an effort in the commentators' account. It is a fact of the station, the position that makes the obedience the natural act. The messenger who carries the word is the one the heaven obeys in the carrying, and the obedience is the verse's first quality: the will of the bearer conforms to the word he bears, and the conformance is the fact, not the struggle. The sūrah states the obedience without qualification, and the qualification is the sūrah's silence: the obedience is complete, and the completeness is the claim.

**The Trustworthy**

The second quality is the trust, and the word is *amīn* — the trustworthy one, the one in whom the thing entrusted is safe. The trust in the messenger is the trust of the delivery: the word is not lost in the carrying, not altered, not diminished, not added to. The bearer is the one in whom the word is safe from source to ear, and the safety is the verse's quality. The sūrah's word for the quality is the same word the Book gives to the one who keeps what is committed to him, and the keeping is the delivery's condition. The word is committed to the messenger, and the messenger is the one in whom the commitment is kept, and the keeping is the trust. The sūrah's twenty-first verse is the trust stated as a fact of the messenger, and the fact is the last of the character's qualities.

**The Name Before the Office**

The human at the end of the chain carries the same name in his own history, and the history is the verse's warrant. Before the revelation, the Quraysh knew the man as al-Amīn — the Trustworthy — and the knowledge was the people's own, not the claim of the one it named. The reports state it plainly: the polytheists called him al-Amīn, and they knew that he would not lie, and the knowing was the ground of the name. The name was his before the office was his, and the name is the quality the sūrah states for the messenger. The sūrah's word for the angel's trust is the people's word for the man's character, and the agreement of the two is the verse's quiet completion: the chain is sealed by the same name, the name the people gave the man before they gave him any other.

**The Chain Is Complete**

The accumulation is finished, and the chain stands in its full length. The word is the word of a noble messenger, and the messenger is full of power, held in honour by the Lord of the Throne, obeyed in heaven, and trustworthy. The five qualities — the nobility, the power, the honour, the obedience, the trust — are the messenger's complete character, and the character is the sūrah's guarantee of the word. The sūrah's method in the section is now whole: the qualities stated one at a time, the chain built link by link, and the chain complete in the trust. The word's custody is stated in full, and the custody is the argument: the message is as safe as the chain that carries it, and the chain the sūrah has stated is the chain from the Throne to the ear, unbroken, in every link.

**What Remains After the Attributes**

The attributes are complete, and the sūrah moves to the one who receives them. The messenger's character has been stated, and the next verse turns to the human: the companion is not insane. The movement is the sūrah's logic. The chain of delivery has been guaranteed, and the guarantee of the delivery requires the guarantee of the receiver: the word is carried by the trustworthy one, and the word is received by the sane one. The sūrah's next verse will state the receiver's character, and the character will be stated in the denial of the charge, the charge of madness that the Meccans brought against the man. The twenty-first verse closes the messenger's account, and the closing is the opening of the man's: the chain is complete, and the last link — the human receiver — is the link the sūrah will now defend.

**The Trustee**

The name the people gave the man before the revelation is the name the sūrah's verse gives the angel, and the agreement is the verse's completion. The reports from the early generations state that the Quraysh knew him as al-Amīn, the Trustee, and that the knowing was theirs and not his claim: they entrusted him with what they kept, and the keeping was the proof. The name was earned in the dealing, and the dealing was the man's character before the character became the office. The sūrah's word for the angel's trust and the people's word for the man's trust are one word, and the one word is the chain's seal: the word is carried by the amīn, and it is received by the amīn, and the delivery is safe in the two, from the source to the ear, in the name the people gave before the message came.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:22

> And your fellow man is not insane.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Accusation on the Record**

The twenty-second verse is a denial, and the denial is of a charge the sūrah does not state but assumes: the charge that the companion — the man, the Prophet ﷺ — is insane, *majnūn*, the possessed, the mad. The sūrah's form is the form of the court: the charge is on the record, and the verse is the answer. The answer is not an argument. It is a statement, flat and unqualified: he is not insane. The sūrah does not explain why the charge is false, and the not-explaining is the verse's manner. The denial stands on the ground the previous verses have laid: the word is the word of the trustworthy messenger, and the man who receives the word is not the madman the charge says he is. The verse is the sūrah's turn from the chain to the receiver, and the turn is the denial.

**Why "Mad" Was the Last Resort**

The charge of madness was the Meccan argument's last move, and the last move is the move that comes when the earlier moves have failed. The earlier moves were the moves against the word: the poetry, the sorcery, the divination, the tale of the old times, each a claim about what the recitation was. The charge of madness is the move against the man, and it comes when the claims about the word have not stopped the word. The sūrah's sequence places the denial where the denial belongs: after the word's custody has been guaranteed, and before the defence of the receiver is complete. The charge is the last resort, and the sūrah meets it with the first form of the answer, the flat denial, because the charge is the kind of claim that an answer in the form of an argument would only feed.

**The Denial's Form**

The verse does not say the man is sane. It says he is not insane, and the difference is the sūrah's method. The denial of the disqualifier is not the assertion of the quality: the sūrah does not claim the man's sanity as a positive fact to be weighed. It removes the charge as a ground, and with the ground removed, the word stands on its own. The form is the Qur'an's standing form of refutation: the disqualifier denied, and the content left to itself. The sūrah's logic is that a message is not refuted by the maker's character when the maker's character is a charge without proof, and the charge is removed by the denial, and the removal is the verse. The man is not insane, and the not-being-insane is enough for the sūrah, because the sūrah's claim was never the man's sanity. It was the word's delivery.

**The Possessed, According to Them**

The reports give the charge its occasion, and the occasion is the sight. The Prophet ﷺ saw the angel in his true form, the form the reports describe in their detail — the wings, the measure that filled the horizon — and the sight was the occasion the idolaters took for the charge: the man who saw what he saw was possessed, they said, seized by a jinn, taken by the spirit. The charge was built on the same fact the sūrah will take as the proof, and the building is the Meccan reading of the sight. The sūrah's verse answers the reading with the denial, and the denial is the sūrah's first word on the fact: the sight did not make the man mad. The man who saw was not the possessed, and the not-being-possessed is the sūrah's claim, stated before the proof is stated in the verse that follows.

**The Same Stone at the Other Prophets**

The charge of madness was not the Meccans' invention. It was the standing charge against the messengers, and the Book records it in the mouths of the earlier deniers. When the messenger came to the people of Shuʿayb, the Book reports, they turned away from him, saying: *"A madman, taught by others!"* (44:14). The same two words — the madman, and the one taught by others — stand in the mouths of the Meccan deniers of this sūrah's audience, and the standing of the charge across the Book is the sūrah's warrant for the denial. The sūrah's answer is the answer the Book gives to the charge wherever it is thrown: the denial, flat and unqualified, and the denial stated here for the companion, as it was stated for the messenger before him. The stone is the same stone, and the answer is the same answer.

**The Denial Without Qualification**

The sūrah's denial carries no qualification, and the not-carrying is the verse's force. There is no *if*, no *as you say*, no *in your account*. The man is not insane, and the statement is the sūrah's own, not a reply to a formulation. The sūrah takes the charge as it stands in the air — the charge the audience has heard, the charge that is doing its work in the room — and it states the denial as a fact of the case. The fact is the sūrah's claim, and the claim is sworn on the ground the previous verses have laid: the word is the word of the trustworthy messenger, and the receiver of the word is not the madman. The denial is the sūrah's word on the man, and the word is the flat one, because the charge is the flat one, and the flatness is the answer's match for the charge.

**A Madman, Taught by Others**

The Book's record of the charge gives the denial its full form, and the form is the two words the deniers used. *"Then they turned away from him, saying, 'A madman, taught by others!'"* (44:14). The two words carry the charge in its two halves: the man is mad, and the man is taught — the word is not his own making, and the making is not his own mind. The sūrah's denial answers the first half, and the verse that follows will answer the second: the man is not insane, and he saw the angel who taught, and the seeing is the answer to the teaching. The charge's two words are met by the sūrah's two verses, the denial and the proof, and the two together are the sūrah's full answer to the last resort. The stone the deniers threw is met, in the sūrah, word for word, and the meeting is the section's completion.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:23

> And he did see that ˹angel˺ on the clear horizon,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Seeing Is the Evidence**

The twenty-third verse is the proof that follows the denial, and the proof is a report of perception: he saw. The particle *la-qad* is the particle of the emphasis, the *indeed* that marks the fact as settled, and the fact is the seeing. The sūrah's defence of the receiver does not argue the receiver's sanity. It reports the receiver's sight, and the sight is the evidence: the man who is charged with madness is the man who saw, and the seeing is what the charge misread. The sūrah's logic is the logic of the witness: the fact is stated, and the fact's two readings — the possession, and the proof — are left to the ground the sūrah has laid. The seeing is the sūrah's second word on the man, and the word is the report, flat and emphasized, because the fact is the settled one.

## Sūrah al-Takwīr 81:29

> But you cannot will ˹to do so˺, except by the Will of Allah, the Lord of all worlds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Close of the Oath and the Sūrah**

The twenty-ninth verse is the sūrah's last word, and the last word is the ground of the will. The previous verse granted the will to the creature, and this verse states the will's ground: the creature does not will except by the Lord's will. The sūrah closes the way it opened: with the worlds. **The Two Wills**

The sūrah's doctrine in the verse is the doctrine of the two wills, and the doctrine is the verse's content. The sūrah states the relation in the verse, and the relation is the sūrah's theology in its form: the will granted, and the will grounded, and the two are the one doctrine.

**The Qur'an's Own Formula**

The Book states the relation in its standing formula, and the formula is the sūrah's verse in its parallel. *"But you cannot will (to do so) unless Allah wills. The sūrah's verse is the formula in its closing position, the formula that stands at the end of the sūrah as it stands at the end of the reminder-sūrahs, and the formula's standing is the Book's way of making the relation a standing doctrine: the creature wills, and the willing is by the Lord, and the by-the-Lord is the formula's excepting. **Guidance Is Granted**

The commentators drew from the verse the doctrine of the guidance, and the doctrine is the verse's completion. The last phrase of the sūrah is the phrase that opens the Book's first sūrah: the Lord of the worlds, *Rabb al-ʿālamīn*.
