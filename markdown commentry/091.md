# Sūrah al-Shams (Chapter 91) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:1

> By the sun and its brightness

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath Begins With the Largest Visible Thing**

A chapter that ends with a tribe destroyed under a blast begins by swearing on the sun. The choice is not decorative. The sun is the most powerful object in human experience: it governs the day, ripens the crop, dries the ground and cannot be approached. An oath sworn on it invokes the greatest thing a hearer can see, and the sūrah's seven oaths are arranged so that this one stands first. The effect is to establish scale before the argument begins. Whatever is about to be said will be measured against the visible order, and the visible order is headed by the sun. The hearer is placed under a sky he did not make and asked to attend to a statement about something he has never seen — his own soul. Starting with the largest visible thing sets the scale of everything that follows. The reader is asked to attend to an object he cannot ignore, cannot alter and sees every day, and the oath depends on that combination. Familiarity is not a weakness in the choice; it is the reason the choice works.

**Its Brightness, Not Only Its Body**

The oath names two things: the sun and its *ḍuḥā*. The second term is the light of the forenoon, the sun's brightness at its most diffused and useful. Swearing on the object and its quality is a deliberate doubling. The body of the sun is a fact of the sky; its brightness is what reaches the earth, what makes work possible and what the eye cannot hold. The doubling therefore moves the oath from the distant to the effective. A hearer may not think about the sun as a body, but he lives inside its brightness every hour of his working day. The verse captures both, and the second term is the one that touches him. The same word gave its name to the chapter of the Morning Light, where it was sworn on as the guarantee of a promise to a man accused of abandonment.

**Seven Oaths in Descending Order**

The structure of this chapter's oath is unusual even within the Qur'an. Seven objects are sworn by, and they descend: the sun, the moon following it, the day that unveils, the night that covers, the heaven and its Builder, the earth and its Spreader, and finally the soul and its Maker. The movement is from the furthest and greatest to the nearest and least visible. Nothing in the sequence is arbitrary, and the descent is the argument. A hearer who grants the weight of the first six objects cannot refuse the seventh, and the seventh is where the chapter's real subject lies. The oath list is therefore a staircase, and the reader is led down it before he is told what is at the bottom. The descent is not merely decorative. Each step brings the reader closer to the last item in the series, which is his own soul, and the ordering is what makes the arrival there feel earned rather than abrupt. A list arranged at random would deliver the same objects without delivering the movement.

**The Sun in the Qur'an's Account of the World**

The Qur'an describes the sun with a consistency that suggests observation rather than poetry. "The sun and the moon ˹travel˺ with precision" (55:5). "The sun travels for its fixed term. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing" (36:38). Its function in timekeeping is stated explicitly: God "made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases, so that you may know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺" (10:5). The verse in this chapter swears on a body that the rest of the revelation describes as ordered, measured and purposive. The oath therefore carries an implicit claim: the greatest visible thing is not a god but a mechanism with a term, and swearing by it is a way of pointing past it to the One who set its term. The sun is described elsewhere in terms of a fixed course, a measured journey and a light that serves a purpose, and this oath belongs to the same method. It does not treat the object as divine and it does not treat it as insignificant. It treats it as evidence.

**Swearing by Created Things, Not Worshipping Them**

The distinction between honouring a creature in an oath and worshipping it is one the Qur'an draws sharply. The same revelation that swears by the sun forbids prostration to it: "Among His signs are the day and the night, the sun and the moon. Do not prostrate to the sun or the moon, but prostrate to Allah, Who created them ˹all˺, if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺" (41:37). The verse places the sun inside the category of signs, which is the highest status a created thing holds in the Qur'an and the furthest from divinity. An oath on a sign magnifies the sign and, through it, the One who made it. The audience of this chapter included people who did worship the sun, and the oath is a correction addressed to them in the form of a solemnity. The distinction is what protects the oath from being misunderstood. Swearing by a thing asserts that the thing is real and reliable enough to stand behind a claim; worshipping it asserts something entirely different. The verse performs the first and the reader who confuses the two has misread the chapter at its opening.

**Light as the Frame for a Moral Argument**

The chapter that begins with light ends with a soul that can be purified or buried, and the connection between the two is the work of the whole sūrah. Light is the image the Qur'an consistently uses for guidance, and darkness for its absence. The oath on the sun and its brightness therefore sets up the vocabulary in which the argument will run. A soul that grows is a soul in which something is visible; a soul that is buried is a soul that has been covered. The chapter will use the night's covering as one of its sworn objects, and the covering will turn out to be the image for what a person can do to himself. The first verse supplies the positive term, and the reader is meant to hold it while the negative one arrives.

**The Sun as the Frame of a Religious Day**

The oath on the sun is also an oath on the schedule of the believer's life. The five prayers are fixed by the sun's position, the fast begins and ends with its rising and setting, and the month is reckoned by a body that follows it. Nothing in the Muslim's religious calendar is independent of this object, and the chapter's first verse therefore invokes something the hearer consults several times a day. The swearing is not addressed to an astronomer but to a person who has just used the sun to determine whether he has time to pray. That proximity is what makes the oath effective. The greatest visible thing in the sky is also the most intimately practical, and the verse binds the two facts together before the argument begins. A reader who organises his obedience around this body is being asked to accept a statement about a soul he has never seen, and the request is made on the authority of the thing he already obeys.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:2

> and the moon as it follows it

**Expanded Commentary**

**Following Without Overtaking**

The verb in this verse describes a relation rather than a motion. *Talāhā* — it follows it — places the moon behind the sun in a sequence that never closes. The Qur'an states the rule elsewhere with precision: "It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day. Each is travelling in an orbit of their own" (36:40). The following is therefore not a pursuit that might succeed. It is a permanent arrangement, and the permanence is what makes it worth swearing by. A relationship that could not be otherwise carries no moral information; a relationship that is maintained without ever being completed is a different thing, and it is the kind of order the chapter is invoking. The relationship is one of sequence rather than competition. The second light appears when the first withdraws and does not dispute its place, and the reader who watches the sequence is watching an order he did not arrange. That absence of arrangement on his part is the basis of the oath.

**A Light That Is Received**

One exegetical reading takes the verse as an allusion to the moon's borrowed light. The moon shines by reflection, and its phases are the visible record of its position relative to the sun. The Qur'an makes the distinction explicit in the verse that assigns the two bodies their functions: God "made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light" (10:5). The reading gives the oath a second layer. The second greatest light in the sky is not a second source but a recipient, and its beauty depends entirely on its relation to the first. An audience that worshipped the sun and the moon together is being shown, in the grammar of an oath, that one of them has nothing of its own to give. Receiving rather than generating is the fact the verse fixes. The reader can verify it directly: the object in question is bright, and its brightness arrives from somewhere else. The oath therefore rests on something observable rather than on a claim requiring specialised knowledge.

**The Crescent and the Reckoning of Time**

A second reading connects the verse to the new crescent, which appears at the start of each lunar month low in the west just after sunset and sets soon after the sun. The observation is exact, and it was part of the ordinary knowledge of the first audience, whose calendar depended on it. The Qur'an assigns the same function in the verse on timekeeping: the phases exist "so that you may know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺" (10:5). The moon's following of the sun is thus not only an astronomical fact but a practical one. It is how a community knows when to fast, when to make pilgrimage and when a debt falls due. An oath sworn on that relation invokes something the hearer uses every month of his life.

**Order as the Subject of the Oath**

The four objects sworn by in these opening verses form a system: a source of light, a body that follows it, a day that discloses and a night that covers. The system is the point. Nothing in it competes, nothing in it fails, and nothing in it needs to be managed. The Qur'an presents this as evidence in its own right: "And He has subjected for your benefit the day and the night, the sun and the moon. And the stars have been subjected by His command. Surely in this are signs for those who understand" (16:12). The verse in this chapter is one item in that evidence, and the swearing is the emphasis. A reader who watches the moon follow the sun for a month is watching an arrangement that has never once broken, and the chapter asks him to weigh what he has watched.

**Second Without Being Lesser in Its Own Order**

The moon's position in the oath is second, and the placement is a judgement about hierarchy rather than about worth. The moon is not diminished by following; it is defined by it, and its function in the created order could not be performed from any other position. The Qur'an's cosmology consistently assigns different ranks without treating rank as a measure of value. The sun gives light, the moon measures time, and neither can perform the other's work. The verse therefore offers a model of order that the chapter will apply elsewhere: a hierarchy in which each term does what it was made to do, and in which the following is not a diminishment but a role. The soul addressed later in the chapter has the same structure of assignment. Second place is not a lower rank in the order the verse describes. Each light has its hour, and the second is not present at the wrong time but at its own. The reader who measures the two against each other is applying a comparison the verse does not make.

**Why the Moon Enters the Argument**

The inclusion of the moon is not required by the chapter's theme, which is the purification of the soul. Its presence is explained by the method. The Qur'an's oaths are built from what the hearer cannot dispute, and the moon is among the least disputable of facts. It rises, it changes, it follows, and everyone has watched it. By placing the moon second in a list of seven, the chapter accumulates undeniable premises before it reaches its conclusion. The conclusion will concern an invisible thing — the state of a soul — and it will be delivered with the accumulated weight of six visible objects behind it. The moon is one of those objects, and its borrowed light is part of what the oath says. The moon enters because it completes the picture of a governed sky. Two bodies, each with a fixed appearance and neither able to displace the other, tell the reader that what he is looking at is arranged. The oath needs that conclusion, and the second body is what supplies it.

**Following as an Image of Submission**

The moon's obedience to its position is one instance of a claim the Qur'an makes about the whole created order: "To Allah ˹alone˺ bow down ˹in submission˺ all those in the heavens and the earth—willingly or unwillingly—as do their shadows, morning and evening" (13:15). The shadows are included, and the inclusion is instructive — even the incidental effects of bodies are described as submitting. The verse about the sun and the moon states their precision as a fact of design: "The sun and the moon ˹travel˺ with precision" (55:5). Neither body has ever deviated, and neither has been asked to. The following described in this verse is therefore a form of worship performed without intention, and it stands as a contrast to the creature that appears at the end of the chapter's oath list. A soul can refuse. The moon cannot. The comparison is not stated in the text, but the oath list arranges it, and the arrangement is what gives the final oath its force.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:3

> and the day as it unveils it

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jallāhā: An Act of Disclosure**

The verb in this verse is *jallā*, from a root meaning to unveil, to polish, to bring something into full view. It is stronger than "to show". A thing that is *jallā* has had its covering removed and is displayed in its clarity. Applied to the day, the verb describes what morning does: it takes a world that was hidden and makes it plain. The day is therefore not merely a period of time in this verse but an agent that performs an action. The grammar makes the day the subject of a verb of disclosure, and the object is what was previously concealed. The oath is sworn on that act, which means the chapter is invoking revelation as a feature of the created order before it speaks of revelation as a book.

**What the Day Unveils: Three Readings**

The object of the verb is a pronoun, and the antecedent has been read three ways. It can be the sun, which the day brings fully into view as it climbs. It can be the earth or the world, which the daylight exposes. It can be the darkness, in which case the sense is that the day disperses it. The three readings are not far apart, and each is supported by ordinary observation. The preference for the earth has the advantage of matching the chapter's structure, since the earth is sworn by separately in the verse that follows and the pairing of disclosure and concealment works best when the object is the same in both. The reading of darkness is the simplest and is favoured by those who take the pair of verses as a single statement about the alternation of light and dark.

**Daylight as the Condition of Work and Witness**

The disclosure performed by the day has a moral consequence. Daylight is when human beings act in public: work is done, contracts are made, injuries are committed and seen. The night conceals what the day reveals, and the alternation means that a person's conduct is periodically exposed. The Qur'an builds its ethics of observation on this fact, and it treats visibility as a condition of accountability. A deed performed in daylight has witnesses; a deed performed in the dark has One. The verse's swearing on the day's disclosure is therefore not only cosmological. It invokes the hour in which conduct becomes public, and the chapter that contains it is about conduct that nobody may see. Daylight is the condition of ordinary life, and the verse treats it as such rather than as a symbol first. Work is done in it, dealings are witnessed in it and travel is possible in it. The reader who has never considered the dependence of his week on this fact has been given something to consider.

**The Spiritual Reading: Faith Uncovering Ignorance**

Some of the earliest commentators read the verse symbolically, taking the day's disclosure as an image of the light of faith removing the darkness of ignorance. The reading is consistent with the Qur'an's own vocabulary, in which guidance is repeatedly described as light and its absence as darkness. It also fits the chapter's argument, which moves from physical light to the state of a soul. On this reading the oath is sworn on the moment when something hidden becomes visible, and the moment is then applied to the inner life. The reading does not displace the literal one. It extends it, and the extension is licensed by the chapter's own movement from the sky to the soul. The spiritual reading does not replace the physical one; it builds on it. Ignorance is described as something that is removed rather than something that is argued away, and the imagery works only because the reader already knows what uncovering looks like in the physical case.

**The Day in the Qur'an's Pairing With the Night**

The day never appears alone in the Qur'an's oaths. The chapter that follows this one opens with the night when it covers and the day when it shines (92:1–2), and the alternation is described elsewhere in the language of wrapping: "He wraps the night around the day, and wraps the day around the night" (39:5). The pairing is a sign in its own right, and the Qur'an says so: "Among His signs are the day and the night, the sun and the moon" (41:37). The verse in this chapter is one half of that pair, and its verb of disclosure gains its meaning from the verse that follows, whose verb is concealment. Neither verb can be understood alone, which is why the two oaths are joined. The pairing is consistent across the revelation and it is not accidental. The two are described as signs, as servants of a purpose and as successors of one another, and the reader who gathers those descriptions sees a single account rather than scattered remarks. The oath here is one item in that account.

**Disclosure as a Divine Act**

The day does not disclose itself. The Qur'an attributes the breaking of the dawn directly to God: "He causes the dawn to break, and has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing" (6:96). The verse in this chapter swears by the day performing the act, and the following verses name the One who performs it through the day. The arrangement is characteristic: the created agent is honoured in the oath, and the Creator enters the oath immediately afterwards. A reader who stops at the day has missed the direction of the swearing, which is why the list continues for four more items before it reaches its object. Attributing the uncovering to God keeps the reader from treating it as an achievement. Light appears because it is made to appear, and the same holds for what the light stands for. The verse assigns the act and the assignment is the theological content of the line.

**The Day as the Hour of Earning**

Disclosure has an economic consequence as well as a moral one. The day is when work is possible: fields are tended, goods are carried, trades are practised, and the provision of the household is earned. The night's rest consumes what the day's labour produced, and the alternation is a provision for the body as much as a sign for the mind. The Qur'an states the arrangement plainly: God "has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing" (6:96). The verse in this chapter swears on the first half of that arrangement — the disclosure that makes earning possible — and the swearing carries a claim. The same daylight that enables a person to feed his family enables him to be seen doing what he ought not to do, and the day performs both functions at once. A reader who treats the day only as an opportunity has accepted half of what the oath invokes. The other half is that his conduct inside the daylight is disclosed along with everything else, and the disclosure is not limited to other people.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:4

> and the night as it conceals it

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ghashāhā: The Covering**

The verb that closes the first group of oaths is *ghashshā*, to cover, to veil, to draw something over a thing until it is hidden. It is the exact opposite of the verb in the previous verse, and the pairing is deliberate. The day unveils; the night covers. The same object is acted on in both cases, and the two acts are performed in turn without failure. The strength of the verb matters. A covering of this kind is complete: what is veiled is not dimmed but removed from sight. The night's action on the world is total, and the oath is sworn on that totality. The chapter is invoking the most complete concealment available in the visible order, and it will later use the image of covering for something a person does to himself.

**Concealment as a Provision, Not a Threat**

The night's covering is described elsewhere in the Qur'an as a mercy rather than as a menace. God "has made the night for rest" (6:96), and the alternation is presented as a design for the benefit of the creatures living inside it. A world without covering would give no sleep, no privacy and no end to exposure. The verse therefore swears on a kindness. That fact shapes the reading of the whole oath list. Nothing in these four oaths is frightening; the sun gives light, the moon follows, the day discloses, the night covers and rests the world. The chapter's argument about the soul is built on a foundation of provisions, and the provisions are what make the demand intelligible. A person addressed by a God who arranged his sleep can be asked to arrange his soul.

**The Pair Completed**

Four objects, two pairs, and a closed system. The sun and the moon are bodies; the day and the night are states. The first pair is spatial and the second is temporal, and together they describe the whole frame in which human life is lived. The verse completes the frame and closes the first movement of the oath. What follows shifts register entirely, moving from created things to the One who made them. The completion is therefore structural as well as descriptive. The reader has been given the visible order in four strokes, and the chapter now turns from the frame to what stands behind it. Nothing further will be said about the sky until the argument is finished. The pair is completed by the second term, and the completion is what allows the chapter to move on. A single term would leave the reader with one description; two terms give him a system in which each item has a place and a function. The verse supplies the second half.

**The Night as the Hour of What Is Hidden**

Concealment has a moral dimension that the chapter exploits. The night is when acts are performed without witnesses, and the Qur'an's ethics distinguish sharply between the public and the private deed. It states the ranking plainly: "To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you of your sins" (2:271). The hidden act is ranked above the visible one, and the ranking depends on the existence of a night in which hiding is possible. The verse's oath on concealment therefore touches the same ground. A world that covers its inhabitants makes sincerity possible, because it makes display optional. The chapter will argue that a soul can be buried under acts, and the image is drawn from this verb. Concealment has an hour, and the verse names it. What is hidden, deferred or done without witnesses belongs to this period, and the reader who recognises the pattern in his own week recognises why the night is worth swearing by. It is not a gap in the day but a distinct condition.

**The Spiritual Reading: Sins Covering the Light**

The early commentators read the verse inwardly, taking the night's covering as an image of what persistent sin does to the light of faith. The reading is supported by the Qur'an's own description of a heart that becomes sealed through repeated refusal, and it fits the chapter's movement from the sky to the soul. On this reading the four oaths describe two movements in the inner life as well as two in the sky: the disclosure of guidance and the covering of it. The reading is not allegory imposed on the text; it follows the chapter's own logic, which places the soul at the end of a list of things that reveal and conceal. What the day and the night do to the world, a person can do to himself. The spiritual reading reverses the earlier movement, and the reversal is deliberate. Where daylight uncovers, sins cover, and the reader is shown that covering is possible in the same life in which uncovering occurs. The imagery gives him a way of describing a condition he can recognise in himself.

**Why Concealment Is Sworn By**

An oath honours its object, and honouring the night is a striking choice in a chapter about purity. The reason is that the night is part of the design and is not the enemy of the good. A morality that feared darkness would be a morality of exposure, in which every act must be seen to count. The Qur'an's morality is the opposite: the best act is the one nobody sees. By swearing on the covering night alongside the disclosing day, the chapter establishes that both are provisions and neither is a verdict. The reader is placed inside a world that alternates between showing and hiding, and he is then told that his soul can be grown or buried inside it. The oath has prepared exactly the vocabulary that statement requires. Swearing by concealment treats it as a real feature of the order rather than a defect in it. The night is not an error to be corrected but a period with its own function, and the reader who resents the covering has misunderstood what the oath is affirming.

**The Oath List as the Picture of a Human Day**

Read in sequence, the first four oaths trace a single day. The sun rises with its brightness; the moon sets behind it; the day discloses the world; the night covers it. Nothing in the sequence is exotic, and every hearer has lived it thousands of times. The choice to build the oath from the ordinary is the chapter's first rhetorical move. A reader cannot distance himself from these objects, because they are the frame of his own existence — he wakes inside them, works inside them and sleeps inside them. The swearing therefore begins with what cannot be argued with, and it accumulates four such items before it reaches anything controversial. By the time the oath list arrives at the soul, the reader has already assented four times without noticing. The night's covering is the last of those assents, and it is the one that closes the frame. What follows steps outside the visible order entirely, and it does so with the weight of an entire ordinary day behind it.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:5

> And by heaven and ˹the One˺ Who built it

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Creator Enters the Oath**

The first four oaths named created things. The fifth names a created thing and its Maker in the same breath, and the shift changes the character of the swearing. An oath on the heaven honours the heaven; an oath on the heaven and the One who built it directs the honour past the object to its source. The chapter's argument has been accumulating visible premises, and it now names the term that the premises were pointing toward. The escalation is not rhetorical excess. It is required by the conclusion the chapter intends to reach, which concerns a soul that has been fashioned and inspired. A creator is needed for that claim, and the creator is introduced here, two verses before the soul appears. The maker enters after the made things, and the order matters. The reader has been shown a series of objects and is now shown the one who produced them, so the shift is from observation to attribution. The verse completes what the preceding lines prepared.

**The Grammatical Question: Who or What**

The Arabic word in these three verses is *mā*, which can function either as a relative meaning "the One who" or as a noun meaning "that which". Both readings are attested in the tradition. On the first, the verse reads as it is translated here: by the heaven and the One who built it. On the second, it reads "by the heaven and its building", making the oath entirely about created things and avoiding the question of whether God can swear by Himself. Some exegetes preferred the second for exactly that reason, reasoning that a creature swearing by its Creator is fitting while a Creator swearing by Himself requires explanation. The first reading is the more natural in Arabic and is supported by the parallel constructions elsewhere in the Qur'an. The theological difficulty is not serious: God swears by what He wills, and swearing by His own action is a way of magnifying it.

**Banāhā: Building With Strength**

The verb is *banā*, to build, and the Qur'an uses the same verb of the heaven with an explicit statement of the manner: "We built the universe with ˹great˺ might, and We are certainly expanding ˹it˺" (51:47). The sūrah of the Draggers puts the act as a question that answers itself: "Which is harder to create: you or the sky? He built it, raising it high and forming it flawlessly" (79:27–28). The comparison in that verse is the point — the sky is offered as the greater of two creations, and the reader is expected to concede it. The oath in this chapter invokes that conceded greatness. A hearer who agrees that the sky is harder to make than he is has already granted the premise the chapter needs, and the premise is that the Maker of the sky is competent in matters he cannot see.

**A Roof Raised Without Supports**

The Qur'an's description of the heaven emphasises a feature that the naked eye confirms: it stands without visible support. "It is Allah Who has raised the heavens without pillars—as you can see— then established Himself on the Throne" (13:2). The clause "as you can see" is important, since it makes the claim an appeal to observation rather than to speculation. The verse in this chapter swears on a structure whose most striking feature is the absence of anything holding it up. The oath therefore invokes not only the size of the heaven but the nature of its maintenance. Something is being held in place by an arrangement the observer cannot see, and the chapter will shortly describe another such arrangement operating inside a human being. A roof raised without visible supports is a fact the reader accepts without thinking about it, and the verse uses the acceptance. Nothing is being claimed that requires investigation; the sky is there and it is not falling. The oath rests on the ordinary character of the evidence.

**Why the Maker Is Named Three Times**

Verses 5, 6 and 7 all follow the same pattern: a created thing and the One who made it. The repetition is a deliberate escalation through three realms — the sky, the earth, the soul — and each realm is smaller than the one before it and closer to the hearer. The pattern establishes a principle: everything in this sequence has a Maker, including the last and least visible item. A reader who grants the Maker of the heaven and the earth has no ground for denying the Maker of his own soul, and the granting is what the threefold repetition is designed to produce. The argument is not stated as an argument. It is performed in the grammar of an oath, three times in a row. Naming the maker three times fixes the attribution beyond dispute. The reader is not left to infer that one agent is behind the three objects; he is told three times. The repetition is the argument, and it leaves no room for a division of labour among them.

**Creation as Evidence in the Qur'an's Method**

The Qur'an's characteristic appeal is to what has been made rather than to what has been argued. It asks its hearers to look at the sky, the earth, the alternation of night and day, and the construction of their own bodies, and it treats the looking as sufficient. The verse in this chapter belongs to that method. It does not demonstrate the existence of a Maker; it swears by one, and the swearing presumes that the hearer already knows what the sky implies. The presumption is not naive. It reflects a conviction that the evidence is public and that the denial of it is a choice rather than a conclusion. The chapter's later argument about the soul depends on that conviction, since the soul is not visible and can only be established through the One who made it. Creation functions as evidence in this method, and this chapter is a compact example. The reader is directed to things he already sees and asked to read them as signs, and the reading is available to anyone without training. The accessibility is the point of the method.

**A Roof Raised, and a Balance Set**

The Qur'an joins the raising of the sky to the establishment of a balance: "As for the sky, He raised it ˹high˺, and set the balance ˹of justice˺" (55:7). The connection is not arbitrary. The same act that lifted a structure without supports established a measure by which things are weighed, and the measure applies to human conduct as much as to the movement of bodies. The verse in this chapter swears on the built heaven, and the swearing invokes both halves of that description — the structure and the standard. A reader who grants the structure has already been introduced to the standard, and the standard is what the chapter's second half applies. The soul will be weighed in the terms of what it grew or buried, and the weighing is possible because the One who built the sky also set the balance. The oath is therefore not only about size. It is about the fact that the largest thing in view was made by a Maker who measures.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:6

> and the earth and ˹the One˺ Who spread it

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ṭaḥāhā: Spreading a Habitable Surface**

The verb chosen for the earth is *ṭaḥā*, which describes the spreading out of a surface so that it can be used. The image is of a bed laid out or a hide stretched flat, and the Qur'an uses comparable language elsewhere: "As for the earth, He spread it out as well" (79:30). The sense is not that the earth is flat in a geometrical claim but that it has been made available — level enough to walk, stable enough to build on, extensive enough to sustain a population. The verse swears on that availability. A hearer standing on the ground is standing inside the object of the oath, and the swearing asks him to notice what he has never had to think about. Spreading describes a surface made usable, and the verse chooses the word because usability is the fact that matters. A reader standing on the ground is not contemplating an abstract object; he is occupying a place prepared for occupation. The oath points at that preparation.

**The Same Grammatical Question as the Verse Before**

The word *mā* appears here again, and the two readings carry over: "the One who spread it" or "its spreading". The consistency across the three verses is evidence that a single construction is intended, and the parallelism of heaven and earth in the Qur'an supports the personal reading. The same pairing appears in the sūrah of the Winnowing Winds: "We built the universe with ˹great˺ might, and We are certainly expanding ˹it˺" (51:47), where the making of both realms is attributed to God in the first person. A reader who adopts the impersonal reading in one verse must adopt it in all three, which weakens the escalation the chapter is building. The personal reading preserves the movement from creation to Creator and is the stronger. The same grammatical question arises here as in the preceding verse, and the answer is the same. The maker is the one being sworn by, and the made thing is the occasion of the oath. The reader who settles the question once settles it for the whole series.

**Spreading as Provision**

The Qur'an does not describe the earth's preparation in neutral terms. It is described as provisioning. "He laid out the earth for all beings" (55:10), and the sūrah of the Draggers completes the sequence: spreading the earth, "bringing forth its water and pastures, and setting the mountains firmly ˹upon it˺— all as ˹a means of˺ sustenance for you and your animals" (79:30–33). The sūrah of al-Ḥijr says the same: "As for the earth, We spread it out and placed upon it firm mountains, and caused everything to grow there in perfect balance. And We made in it means of sustenance for you and others, who you do not provide for" (15:19–20). The last clause is the sharpest: some of the provision in the earth is for creatures the reader does not feed and does not know. The oath in this verse invokes a provision system wider than its beneficiary.

**A Sign Under the Reader's Feet**

The Qur'an repeatedly directs attention downward. "and the earth—how it was levelled out?" (88:20). "Have We not smoothed out the earth ˹like a bed˺, and ˹made˺ the mountains as ˹its˺ pegs" (78:6–7). The questions are rhetorical and they assume the answer is visible. The verse in this chapter belongs to that method, and its position in the oath list gives it weight. The heaven was named first because it is the greater; the earth is named second because it is the nearer. Between them the whole of the reader's physical world has been sworn by, and both have been attached to a Maker. The argument is now complete at the level of the visible, and the next verse leaves the visible entirely. The sign is under the reader's feet, which is what makes it unavoidable. He cannot decline to notice it by looking away, and he cannot postpone it by claiming ignorance. The verse is built on the impossibility of evading this particular piece of evidence.

**The Two Realms and Their Maker**

Heaven and earth form the standard Qur'anic pair for the whole of creation, and the pairing appears in oaths, in descriptions of power and in statements of ownership. Swearing by both is a way of swearing by everything that exists outside God. The verse therefore completes the cosmic half of the oath list, and it does so with the same construction as the verse before it: a thing and its Maker. The repetition is not padding. It establishes that the relation between a creature and its Creator is uniform — the sky is made, the ground is made, and the making is of one kind. That uniformity is the premise the next verse needs, since it will apply the same relation to something that cannot be seen. Two realms are named and one maker is asserted, and the pairing excludes the arrangement the reader might otherwise suppose. Nothing in the sky is governed by one power and the ground by another. The verse states the unity and the statement is the reason the oaths can proceed to the soul.

**From the Cosmos to the Person**

The verse's function in the chapter is transitional, and the transition is the argument. Six oaths have been taken on the visible order: light, a following body, a disclosing day, a covering night, a built heaven, a spread earth. The seventh will be taken on the soul. A reader who has granted the first six has already accepted that everything around him has been made by a single Maker with a purpose. The verse in front of us is the last step on that ground, and it is placed immediately before the step off it. Nothing about the soul will be observable in the way the earth is, and the oath prepares for that by establishing, in the plainest possible terms, that the same Maker is responsible for both.

**The Ground That Provides and the Ground That Receives**

The earth sworn by in this verse performs two functions in the Qur'an's account, and the chapter uses both. It is the place of provision: "And We made in it means of sustenance for you and others, who you do not provide for" (15:20). It is also the place of return, where the body is laid after death and where the tribe described at the end of this chapter was levelled. The same ground that feeds a person receives him, and the two functions are held together in the Qur'an's imagery without strain. The verse's oath therefore invokes the whole of a human being's relation to the earth — what he takes from it and what he is returned to. The reader standing on that ground is standing inside both facts at once. The chapter that swears on it goes on to describe a soul that can be buried, and the verb it uses for the burial is the verb used for putting a body into this earth.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:7

> And by the soul and ˹the One˺ Who fashioned it

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Object of the Oath Cannot Be Seen**

Six objects have been sworn by, and every one of them is visible: light, a following body, a disclosing day, a covering night, a built heaven, a spread earth. The seventh is none of these. The soul has no horizon, no orbit and no season, and the reader cannot step outside to look at it. The placement is the argument. The chapter has spent six oaths establishing that the visible order is made, ordered and purposive, and it now applies the same claim to the one thing the reader knows better than anything in the sky and cannot observe at all. The descent from the sun to the soul is not a diminishment. It is a movement from what is public to what is private, and the private term is the one the chapter is actually about.

**Sawwāhā: Fashioned in Proportion**

The verb is *sawwā*, which means to make level, to proportion, to bring a thing into its proper shape. The Qur'an uses the same verb of the human being directly: God is the One "Who created you, fashioned you, and perfected your design" (82:7). The sūrah of the Fig states the result: "Indeed, We created humans in the best form" (95:4). The claim is that the soul is not a residue or an accident but a construction, and a construction implies a constructor with an intention. The verse in this chapter names both in a single phrase — the soul and the One who fashioned it — and the pairing is the same one used for the heaven and the earth. What is true of the cosmos is true of the person: it was made, and it was made by design.

**The Three Souls the Qur'an Names**

The Qur'an describes the soul in three states, and the three together define the range in which this chapter's argument operates. The soul at peace is addressed at the end of the sūrah of the Dawn: "O tranquil soul! Return to your Lord, well pleased ˹with Him˺ and well pleasing ˹to Him˺" (89:27). The self-reproaching soul is sworn by in the sūrah of the Resurrection: "And I do swear by the self-reproaching soul!" (75:2). The third is described by Yūsuf's wife in a confession: "indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil, except those shown mercy by my Lord" (12:53). The three are not three souls but three conditions of one soul, and the movement between them is what the next two verses describe. A soul can be cultivated toward peace, left in self-reproach, or allowed to follow its inclination.

**Fiṭrah: An Orientation Given at Creation**

The fashioning described in this verse is not neutral. The Qur'an describes human beings as created with an orientation already in place: "the natural Way of Allah which He has instilled in ˹all˺ people. Let there be no change in this creation of Allah" (30:30). The soul is therefore fashioned toward something, and the fashioning is the ground on which the command to purify it can be issued. A soul with no orientation could not be purified, since purification implies a standard; a soul oriented toward evil alone could not be blamed for following it. The verse establishes the condition in which the chapter's central statement is fair. The soul was made proportioned and directed, and what happens to it afterwards is the person's contribution. An orientation given at creation is not a set of instructions and not a verdict. It is a direction the soul already faces, and the reader who feels the pull toward something better than his current conduct is feeling that orientation. The verse names what he experiences without his vocabulary for it.

**Why the Soul Is Honoured With an Oath**

Oaths magnify their objects, and swearing by the soul places it above the six visible things that precede it in the list. The ranking is a judgement. The sun governs the day and the earth sustains the body, but neither is the site of moral choice, and only a site of moral choice can bear the weight of an oath in a chapter about success and ruin. The verse therefore makes a claim about value as well as about origin. What matters most in the created order is not the largest thing in it but the thing that can choose. The reader who has followed the oath list has been led, without being told, to a ranking that puts his own soul above the sun. Honouring the soul with an oath places it alongside the sun, the moon and the sky, and the placement is significant. The reader is being told that what is inside him belongs to the same order of evidence as the objects he sees. The scale of the series makes the point.

**The Term the Whole Oath Was Built Toward**

The structure of the chapter makes this verse its destination. Everything sworn by before it exists to establish the reliability and the authority of the Maker, and the Maker is introduced three times in succession — the Builder of the heaven, the Spreader of the earth, the Fashioner of the soul. The threefold repetition reaches its term here. A reader who accepts the first two has no consistent ground for refusing the third, and the third is where the argument lands. The verse is therefore both the last item in a list and the first statement of the chapter's real subject. The two verses that follow will state what is to be done with the thing just named, and they can do so because this verse has established that it was made. Everything before this verse was leading here, and the reader can feel the shift when he arrives. Six objects have been sworn by and the seventh is his own soul, so the chapter moves from the world outside him to the thing he carries. The arrival is the purpose of the series.

**A Verb the Chapter Will Use Again**

*Sawwā* appears once more in this chapter, and its second appearance is grim. The same verb used for fashioning the soul is used for what happened to the tribe that destroyed itself: their Lord crushed them and levelled them (91:14). The wordplay is not accidental. The One who proportioned a soul is the One who can flatten a civilisation, and the two acts are performed by the same hand with the same ease. The chapter places the two uses eleven verses apart and leaves the reader to make the connection. A soul fashioned with such care can be buried by its owner, and a people who bury theirs can be levelled by their Maker. The verb holds both statements together. The verb introduced here returns later in the chapter, and the return is where the argument closes. A reader who notes the introduction will recognise the reuse and will understand that the two occurrences describe one process from two ends. The chapter depends on that recognition.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:8

> then with ˹the knowledge of˺ right and wrong inspired it

**Expanded Commentary**

**Alhamahā: Instilled, Not Taught**

The verb in this verse is *alhama*, from a root meaning to cause something to be present in a mind without instruction. It is the verb of inspiration in the strict sense: knowledge that arrives without a teacher, without a text and without an argument. The object is twofold — its *fujūr* and its *taqwā*, its wrongdoing and its reverence — and both are given together. The verse therefore claims that every human being arrives equipped with a recognition of the difference between the two, before any revelation reaches him and before any argument is put to him. The claim is bold, and its boldness is what the chapter needs, since the argument that follows holds every person responsible. Instilling is not teaching, and the difference is what the verse turns on. A capacity that is installed does not require a teacher, a curriculum or an occasion of learning, and the reader who looks for where he was instructed in the distinction between the two will not find such a moment.

**Two Readings: Teaching or Placing**

The construction allows two senses, and both are recorded in the tradition. The first takes the verse as stating that God taught the soul what its wrongdoing and its reverence are, so that it can distinguish them. The second takes it as stating that He placed both capacities within it, making the soul the site of a conflict rather than the recipient of information. The difference is real but narrower than it appears. On either reading the soul possesses both terms, and on either reading the choice between them remains the person's. The first reading emphasises knowledge; the second emphasises disposition. The chapter's next two verses favour the second, since they speak of growing and burying rather than of knowing and not knowing. A soul that must be cultivated is a soul with a disposition to work on, not merely a set of facts to recall.

**Knowledge That Precedes Revelation**

The claim of this verse is that moral recognition is prior to scripture. The Qur'an states the same priority elsewhere, in the account of a covenant taken before birth: "And ˹remember˺ when your Lord brought forth from the loins of the children of Adam their descendants and had them testify regarding themselves. ˹Allah asked,˺ ‘Am I not your Lord?’ They replied, ‘Yes, You are!’" (7:172). The purpose of that testimony is stated in the same verse: so that nobody can plead ignorance on the Day of Judgment. The two verses belong together. A covenant taken in advance and a knowledge instilled at creation produce the same result — no person can claim that the distinction was never shown to him. The chapter's own list of endowments in the sūrah of the City makes the same point from the other direction, where humanity is described as shown "the two ways ˹of right and wrong˺" (90:10).

**The Report on Decree and This Verse**

The verse has been at the centre of a discussion about destiny since the earliest generations. A report preserved in both principal collections records a man asking the Prophet ﷺ whether the people of Paradise and the people of the Fire are already distinguished, and — on being told that they are — asking what use deeds then have. The answer was: "Everyone will be facilitated to that for which he was created" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6596; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2649). The exegetes connect that answer to this verse, since the facilitation described is the working out of the two capacities instilled here. The connection does not resolve the tension between decree and choice, and the report does not claim to. What it establishes is that the two terms are not treated as alternatives in the Prophet's own teaching. A person is facilitated toward what he was made for, and he is still commanded to act.

**Two Capacities, Not Two Verdicts**

The verse names wrongdoing and reverence as things instilled, and the naming is symmetrical. Neither term is presented as the soul's default and the other as an intrusion. A human being is equipped for both, and the equipment is what makes him responsible. A creature capable only of good would not be virtuous; a creature incapable of wrong could not be blamed. The verse describes the condition in which moral language makes sense at all. That is why the chapter can move directly from this verse to the two outcomes. Success and failure are attached to what a person does with an endowment he did not choose, and the endowment includes the capacity to fail. Two capacities are given and no verdict is pronounced in advance, and the balance is what makes the following verses meaningful. A reader who has been given the ability to recognise wrongdoing has not been condemned by receiving it, nor has he been licensed by it. The capacities are equipment.

**The Hinge of the Chapter**

Structurally, this verse is the hinge on which the whole sūrah turns. Seven oaths have been taken, ending on the soul and its Maker; this verse states what was done to the soul; and the next two state the consequence. Nothing in the argument works without it. If the soul were not equipped with both capacities, the command to purify it would be meaningless, and the warning about burying it would be unjust. The verse supplies the premise that makes the chapter's central statement — success for the one who purifies, ruin for the one who corrupts — a fair statement rather than an arbitrary one. The reader who reaches the next two verses has already been told that he had what he needed. This verse is the hinge because everything the chapter asserts depends on it. If the soul had not been equipped, the commands that follow would be unjust, and if it had been equipped with only one capacity, the choice would not be real. The verse establishes both.

**A Verse the Prophet Paused On**

The tradition records that the Prophet would stop at this verse when reciting the chapter and pray for his own soul's reverence, addressing God as its protector and the best one to purify it. Whether or not the report is relied on for practice, it records the effect the verse has on a reader who takes it seriously. A statement about capacities instilled at creation is not a comfortable text. It says that the capacity for wrongdoing is inside the reader alongside the capacity for reverence, and that both were placed there by the same hand. The natural response is not analysis but a request, and the report preserves exactly that response from the person to whom the chapter was revealed. The pause is significant because the verse is short and self-contained. A reader who stops here holds the whole of the chapter's premise in one line: the soul was made and then furnished with the knowledge it needs. What follows is the consequence of that furnishing.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:9

> Successful indeed is the one who purifies their soul

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer the Oaths Were Taken For**

Seven oaths, and then a single sentence. The verse delivers what the swearing guaranteed, and the delivery is short enough to memorise: successful indeed is the one who purifies it. The emphatic particle at the head of the sentence marks the statement as settled, and the indefinite subject — whoever, the one who — makes it apply to anyone. Nothing in the verse restricts the promise to a community, a time or a class. The seven objects sworn by, descending from the sun to the soul, exist to give this sentence its weight, and the sentence turns out to be about conduct rather than about cosmology. The reader who followed the oath list arrives at a rule he can apply within the hour. The answer is short because the oaths were long. Seven lines of preparation resolve into a single statement, and the brevity tells the reader that what was being established was not in doubt but in need of emphasis. The verse gives him the conclusion without qualification.

**Zakkā: To Purify and to Grow**

The verb is *zakkā*, and its root carries both purification and growth. A thing that is *zakā* has been cleansed and has increased, and the two senses are not separable in Arabic usage. The same root gives the word for the obligatory alms, which is understood as a means by which wealth is both purified and made to grow. Applied to the soul, the verb therefore describes a process with two movements: removal of what spoils it and increase of what belongs to it. The verse is not a command to be clean in a negative sense, as though virtue consisted only in the absence of vice. It describes cultivation. A soul that is purified is a soul that has been made to grow, and the growth is the evidence of the purification. Purifying and growing are one act described from two sides. What is removed and what is added happen together, and the reader who treats them as separate stages will look for a point at which the first ends. The verse joins them so that neither is attempted alone.

**Purification as the Revelation's Own Programme**

The Qur'an describes its own purpose in these terms. The mission of the messenger is summarised as "reciting to them His revelations, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom" (3:164), and the same threefold description appears in the prayer of Abraham for the city's future (2:129) and in the account of what was done for a people who had previously been astray (62:2). The reminder to the believers repeats it: "Since We have sent you a messenger from among yourselves—reciting to you Our revelations, purifying you, teaching you the Book and wisdom" (2:151). The verb in this verse is the same verb that appears in all of those passages. The chapter's central statement is therefore not an isolated aphorism but a compressed statement of the revelation's purpose. The programme is stated elsewhere in the same terms, and the consistency is what makes it a programme rather than an aspiration. A reader who meets the same verb applied to property, to conduct and to the soul understands that one process is being described in three settings.

**Who Purifies: The Ambiguity of the Subject**

Some of the earliest commentators read the verse with God as the subject: he whose soul God purifies has succeeded. The reading is supported by verses that attribute purification to God alone, and it guards against a theology of self-sufficient effort. The opposite error is equally guarded against elsewhere: "Indeed, Allah would never change a people's state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺" (13:11). The two statements stand together, and the ambiguity of the subject in this verse holds them in place. A reader who attributes the purification entirely to himself has misread the oaths that precede it, since the soul's Maker is named in the seventh. A reader who waits for the purification without acting has misread the verb, which is in the active voice and describes work.

**What the Work Consists In**

The exegetes describe the process in practical terms: the removal of base traits and the cultivation of their opposites. Stinginess gives way to generosity, cowardice to patience, envy to goodwill, and each substitution requires a decision taken repeatedly rather than once. The verse's verb of growth implies exactly that repetition, since nothing grows by a single act. The Qur'an's legislation supplies the material: prayer at fixed hours, alms from cherished wealth, restraint of the tongue, feeding the poor, keeping pledges. None of these is presented as a means of earning salvation on its own account; all of them are the ordinary work of cultivation. The reader who asks what purifying a soul looks like on a Tuesday is answered by the list of things he was already commanded to do. The work consists in acts rather than in states, and the acts are available today. A reader who waits to feel purified before acting has reversed the order the verse sets out. The purification is the result of the work, and the work is ordinary enough that no excuse remains.

**Success Defined Inwardly**

The word for success in this verse is *falāḥ*, and the Qur'an uses it for a state rather than for an achievement. It appears in the call to prayer, where the muezzin summons the congregation to it, and it appears in descriptions of the believers who will inherit Paradise. The success described is not measurable in the terms the world uses. It is not wealth, health or reputation, and it can coexist with the absence of all three. The verse defines it by its cause rather than by its appearance: whoever purifies his soul has succeeded. A reader who measures his life by visible outcomes will not be able to tell whether he has succeeded, and the verse tells him that the measurement he needs is the one he can perform on himself.

**A Statement Addressed to the Reader, Not to the Prophet**

Unlike the consolations of the two preceding chapters, this verse has no named addressee. It is phrased in the third person and applies to whoever performs the action. The shift is significant for a Meccan chapter revealed to a man under pressure. The chapter's opening oaths concern the whole created order, and its conclusion concerns any person who will do the work. The Prophet is included in the statement rather than singled out by it, and the inclusion is the point: the rule that saved him is available to the person hearing it. The chapter ends its first movement with a promise that requires nothing of the hearer except that he begin.

The reader who asks what purifying a soul requires on an ordinary day is answered by the acts he was already commanded to perform, and the answer is the same for the merchant, the farmer and the messenger.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:10

> and doomed is the one who corrupts it

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dassāhā: To Bury**

The verb in this verse is *dassā*, and its root denotes hiding, burying, pressing something into the ground until it disappears. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the most extreme act of concealment in its pages: the man given the news of a daughter's birth who deliberates "should he keep her in disgrace, or bury her ˹alive˺ in the ground?" (16:59). The verb therefore carries an image, and the image is violent. Applied to the soul, it describes not a failure to improve but an act of interment. The soul is not merely neglected in this verse; it is put under. The choice of verb is the sharpest feature of the statement, and it rules out any reading in which the failure is passive. Burying implies concealment from sight and effort spent on the concealment, and both features belong to the verse. A person does not bury something carelessly; he covers it so that it will not be found. The reader who recognises the effort will recognise the pattern in his own conduct.

**Corruption as Concealment**

The verse pairs two ideas that seem unrelated — corruption and hiding — and the pairing is precise. A soul that is corrupted is a soul whose original proportion has been covered over, so that what was fashioned in the best form can no longer be seen from the outside or the inside. The covering is made of acts. Each repeated wrong adds a layer, and the layers accumulate until the person no longer recognises what is underneath. The Qur'an describes the same process in the language of a heart that becomes sealed through persistence, and the image in this verse is the agricultural form of the same idea: something planted has been pressed back into the soil. The reader is told that the soul he was given can be rendered invisible by his own conduct, and that the rendering is his doing.

**A Mirror With Two Outcomes**

Verses 9 and 10 are constructed in exact parallel, and the parallelism is the point. The same subject, the same soul, the same capacity, and two verbs pointing in opposite directions. The emphatic particle appears in both, so neither outcome is presented as more certain than the other. The structure removes any suggestion that one group was made for one outcome and the other for the other. What separates them is the verb each performed: one grew it, one buried it. The symmetry is what makes the statement fair, and it is what allows the chapter to move immediately to a historical example. The example will show the second verb at work in a whole people. One mirror, two outcomes, and the difference lies in what is done with what is seen. The reader is not given two souls but two responses, and both are within his range. The verse makes the range explicit so that the outcome cannot be blamed on the equipment.

**Ruin That Nobody Else Caused**

Nothing in the verse names another agent. The soul was fashioned by God, and the burying is done by its owner. No tempter is mentioned, no enemy is blamed, and no circumstance is offered as mitigation. The grammar places the whole of the action on the person, and the placement is deliberate. A reader who wishes to explain his state by his upbringing, his society or his difficulties is answered by the absence of any such term in the sentence. The verse describes a self-inflicted condition, and the self-infliction is what makes it a matter of responsibility rather than of misfortune. The capacity to grow the soul was instilled alongside the capacity to bury it, and both were given to the same person. Nobody else caused the ruin, and the statement removes the explanations a person reaches for first. Circumstance, other people and the difficulty of the task are all excluded by the verb's construction. The reader who has been explaining his condition by reference to others has been answered directly.

**The Soul Will Testify**

The concealment described in this verse does not succeed in its aim. The Qur'an states the outcome plainly: "In fact, people will testify against their own souls, despite the excuses they come up with" (75:14–15). The buried soul is not lost; it is present as a witness against the one who buried it. The verse therefore adds a second layer to the warning. A person can cover what he was made with, and the covering will not hold at the moment it matters. The excuses mentioned in that verse are the ordinary ones — circumstance, other people, ignorance — and the verse dismisses them in advance. The reader who is burying his soul is accumulating testimony against himself, and the testimony is his own. The soul testifies rather than being judged by a stranger, and the arrangement is what makes the outcome undeniable. A reader who disputes an external accusation can continue disputing it; he cannot dispute his own knowledge of what he did. The verse places the evidence where it cannot be challenged.

**The Example That Follows**

The chapter moves directly from this verse to a tribe that acted on it. The Thamūd are introduced in the next verse as a people who rejected in their transgression, and their story is the worked example of burying. They were given a messenger, a sign and a set of terms; they sent their worst man to destroy the sign; and they were levelled. The sequence is the second verb at civilisational scale. The placement of the example immediately after the warning is a rhetorical decision: the abstract statement about a soul is followed by a concrete account of a people, so that the reader can see what the verb produces when a community performs it together. Nothing further needs to be argued once the example is in view. The example that follows is not an illustration added afterwards but the demonstration the argument requires. A general statement about buried souls remains theoretical; a tribe destroyed for one act makes it historical. The reader moves from a principle to a record, and the record is what persuades.

**A Warning in the Present Tense**

The verse is not written about the past. It states a rule in the present, and the rule applies to whoever performs the action. The reader of the chapter is inside its scope from the moment he reads the verb. The warning is therefore not about the Thamūd, who are finished, but about the person holding the text. The chapter has given him the equipment, named the two outcomes in symmetrical terms, and then shown him what one of them looks like in history. What remains is his own conduct, and the verse leaves him with it. A soul can be buried quietly, over years, by acts that never feel decisive; the verse says so in one word, and the word is the same one used for burying a child.

**A Burial That Looks Like Living**

The concealment described in this verse is internal, and that is what makes it dangerous. A buried soul does not stop functioning. The person continues to work, speak, trade and worship, and nothing in his outward life announces what has happened underneath. The covering is made of accumulated acts, and acts leave no visible mark on a face. The verse therefore describes a condition that no observer can detect and that the person himself may not notice for years. The Qur'an's warning about it is aimed at exactly that invisibility. A reader who waits for an obvious sign that his soul has been buried will wait indefinitely, because the sign is the absence of feeling rather than the presence of damage. The chapter supplies the test in the verse before it: growth. A soul that is growing produces visible fruit in conduct — generosity where there was stinginess, patience where there was irritation. The absence of such fruit is the only reliable indicator, and the verse asks the reader to check for it.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:11

> Thamûd rejected ˹the truth˺ out of arrogance

**Expanded Commentary**

**From a Rule to a Case**

Ten verses have established a principle: a soul can be grown or buried, and the outcome follows the verb. The eleventh verse stops arguing and starts narrating. The shift is characteristic of the Qur'an's method in the short Meccan chapters, which rarely sustain an abstraction for long. A principle is stated and then embodied in a people, so that the reader sees the principle operating rather than being asked to accept it. The example chosen is not illustrative in a loose sense. It is a case in which every element of the principle is visible: a soul given, a messenger sent, a sign provided, a refusal made, and an outcome delivered. The chapter's argument is now in narrative form, and the narrative is short enough to hold.

**Ṭaghwāhā: The Rejection Had a Manner**

The verse does not simply say that Thamūd rejected. It says they rejected *bi-ṭaghwāhā* — in their transgression, through their overstepping. The addition specifies the character of the refusal. This was not a failure of evidence or a hesitation of intellect. It was an act of a people who knew what was being asked and refused on grounds of their own standing. The Qur'an's account of the same rejection elsewhere supports the reading: "As for Thamûd, We showed them guidance, but they preferred blindness over guidance" (41:17). The verb of preference is decisive. Guidance was shown and blindness was chosen, and the choosing is described as an act of will rather than a state of ignorance. The word in this verse names the will behind it. The manner of the rejection matters because rejection is not a single act. It was excessive, and the excess is what the verse names rather than the fact alone. The reader who has been treating his own refusals as isolated incidents is being shown that refusal has a shape and a direction.

**Who They Were and Where They Lived**

Thamūd were a northern Arabian tribe, and their settlement is named in the Qur'an as the Stone Valley: "Indeed, the residents of the Stone Valley also denied the messengers" (15:80). Their distinguishing achievement is recorded in the challenge put to them by their messenger: "to carve homes in the mountains with great skill?" (26:149). The site lies on the caravan route between the Ḥijāz and Syria, and it was still occupied by ruins in the Prophet's lifetime. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī records that when the army passed through the land of Thamūd called al-Ḥijr, the Prophet ﷺ ordered his companions to pour out the water they had drawn from its wells and to take water only from the well the she-camel used to drink from (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3378). The place was not a legend to the first audience. It was a location they passed.

**The Rejection Recorded Across the Revelation**

The story appears repeatedly, and the repetitions are evidence of its familiarity. The sūrah of the Moon opens its account with the same verb: "Thamûd rejected the warnings ˹as well˺" (54:23). The sūrah of the Ant describes the internal division the messenger produced: "We certainly sent to the people of Thamûd their brother Ṣâliḥ, proclaiming, ‘Worship Allah,’ but they suddenly split into two opposing groups" (27:45). The longer Meccan and Madinan accounts in Sūrahs 7 and 11 expand the narrative with speeches on both sides. The chapter in front of us compresses all of it into five verses, which it can do only because the audience already knows the story. The compression is a sign of confidence in the hearer's memory. The record is spread across the revelation, and the spread is what makes the account reliable. A reader who finds the same event described in several chapters with consistent details understands that he is not reading a passing allusion but a documented case referred to deliberately.

**Why This Example, in Mecca**

The choice of Thamūd for a Meccan audience is not arbitrary. The Quraysh were traders who travelled north, and the carved façades of al-Ḥijr were on their route. A warning drawn from a people whose remains they had seen cannot be dismissed as a story about strangers. The parallel is also structural: a prosperous, technically accomplished people, confident in their buildings, addressed by a messenger from among themselves, refusing on grounds of status. The Meccan situation matched the Thamūdic one at almost every point, and the chapter expects the audience to notice. The verse's function is not to inform but to position the hearer inside a pattern he has already walked through. The example was chosen for its audience. A tribe known to the people being addressed, destroyed within living historical memory and destroyed for rejecting a messenger, fits the situation of the listeners exactly. The reader who asks why this story rather than another has his answer in the setting.

**The Connection to the Buried Soul**

The example is placed immediately after the warning about burying the soul, and the connection is the chapter's argument. A tribe that rejects a messenger with a clear sign is performing the second verb collectively. The individual act described in verse 10 has a social form, and the Thamūd are that form. Their transgression was not a private failing but a public decision taken by a people with the capacity to decide otherwise. The reader who reads the two passages together sees that the soul's burial is not only a personal matter. It can be performed by a society, in which case the outcome is also social, and the chapter's last verse describes what that outcome looked like. The connection to the buried soul is the reason the story appears here. A reader who has just been told about a soul that hid its own corruption is now shown a community that acted on the same impulse at scale. The scale differs; the mechanism does not.

**Why the Story Is Told Again**

Thamūd appear in at least six places in the Qur'an, and each telling serves a different argument. Al-Qamar sets the account inside a sequence of peoples who rejected warnings, with the refrain of punishment repeating between them. Al-Aʿrāf and Hūd expand the narrative with dialogue, so that the reader hears both the messenger and the people. The sūrah of the Ant focuses on the internal division the message produced, and the sūrah of the Poets dwells on their buildings and their boast about them. This chapter's version is the shortest of all, and its brevity is possible only because the audience knew the story. The repetition across the revelation is itself evidence of the story's function: it is the Qur'an's standard case for a society that had everything — a messenger, a sign, a stated warning, a scheduled test — and failed anyway. A reader who meets the name Thamūd anywhere in the Book is meeting a settled reference, and this verse invokes the whole of it in three words.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:12

> when the most wicked of them was roused ˹to kill the she-camel˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ashqāhā: One Man singled Out**

The verse identifies a single individual in the superlative: the most wretched of them. Not a faction, not a council, not a mood — a man, and the worst of the tribe. The identification is remarkable in a chapter that has been dealing in generalities about souls. It says that collective ruin has a point of origin, and that the point is a person. The superlative also carries a judgement in advance of the act. The man is named as the most wretched before he is described doing anything, which means the wretchedness is a condition he already possessed and the act is its expression. The chapter's argument about the buried soul acquires a name. One man is singled out because one man acted, and the singling out is what makes the account precise. The reader is not shown a crowd acting together but a named individual identified within a group, and the precision is what allows the verse to place responsibility accurately.

**Inbaʿatha: Sent Forth by Others**

The verb is *inbaʿatha*, to be roused, dispatched, to rise to a task at the prompting of others. The man did not act alone and he did not act first. The Qur'an states the arrangement explicitly in the sūrah of the Moon: "But they roused a companion of theirs, so he dared to kill ˹her˺" (54:29). The verse in this chapter uses a passive construction that preserves the same fact: he was roused. Somebody sent him. The passive voice is doing moral work, since it records that the tribe produced the act even though one hand performed it. The reader is shown the mechanism by which a community commits a crime through an agent, and the mechanism is why the whole community is punished.

**The Nine Men of the City**

The Qur'an supplies the social context elsewhere. "And there were in the city nine ˹elite˺ men who spread corruption in the land, never doing what is right" (27:48). The description is precise: a small group of notables, corrupt, and opposed to reform. The man who hamstrung the camel came from that milieu, and the verse in this chapter identifies him as the worst among them. The detail matters for the application. Societies are not destroyed by the wickedness of their weakest members but by the wickedness of those with standing, because standing is what allows a private vice to become a public act. The chapter names the ringleader and leaves the reader to look for the equivalent in his own city. Nine men were corrupting in the city and not reforming, and the description is given so that the reader understands the setting of the act. The killing did not happen in a vacuum; it happened in a place already described as disordered. The verse supplies the context before supplying the event.

**The Name and the Report**

The exegetical tradition names the man Qudār ibn Sālif, and the name appears in the earliest commentaries. A report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, in the Book of Commentary, describes the one who went forth to the camel as a strong, mighty man, invincible among his people, and compares him to Abū Zamʿah. The description is not incidental. The man chosen for the task was chosen for his capacity to perform it without being stopped, which means the tribe's selection was practical as well as moral. They sent someone who could not be resisted. The verse's superlative — the most wretched — is therefore matched by a superlative of force, and the combination is what made the crime possible. The name is preserved in the exegetical record, and the preservation is worth noting because the Qur'an itself does not give it. A reader who knows the name from the tradition knows that the identification is transmitted rather than revealed, and the distinction affects how much weight the detail carries.

**A Collective Crime With a Single Hand**

The punishment that follows falls on the whole tribe, and the arrangement has puzzled readers. The explanation is in the verb. A man who is roused acts as the hand of those who roused him, and the tribe's approval is recorded in the account: they called their companion and he took up the task. The crime was planned by many, authorised by many and performed by one. The Qur'an's treatment of such cases is consistent — the responsibility attaches to the decision, not only to the blow. The verse in this chapter makes the point in a single passive verb, and the passive is the reason the collective punishment that follows is just rather than indiscriminate. A collective crime committed by a single hand is the pattern the verse records, and the pattern is what makes the punishment of the whole community intelligible. The reader who wonders how one act implicates a tribe is being shown a community that approved the act rather than restraining it.

**Wretchedness Named Before the Act**

The order of the verse is significant. The man is described as the most wretched before his deed is mentioned. The description is therefore not a conclusion drawn from the act but a fact about the man. The chapter is making a claim about character: a person becomes the most wretched of his people by a series of choices that precede the decisive one, and the decisive one merely reveals the series. That claim is the same one the chapter made about the soul. A soul is not buried in a moment; it is buried by degrees, and the moment of burial is only when the covering becomes visible. The verse supplies a case in which the process reached its term. Wretchedness is named before the act is described, and the order is unusual enough to be significant. The reader is told the standing of the man first, so that when he reaches the killing he is not surprised by it. The verse treats the outcome as the consequence of a condition already in place.

**What the Verse Says About Ringleaders**

The application of the verse extends beyond its historical subject. Every community contains people whose standing allows them to convert private hostility into public action, and the verse identifies them as the point at which a society's condition becomes decisive. The ringleader is not the cause of a people's corruption; he is its instrument and its proof. A tribe that would not have approved the killing does not rouse its worst man to perform it. The chapter therefore indicts the senders as much as the sent, and it does so by naming only the sent. The reader who wants to know where a society's ruin begins is directed to the man who was roused, and to the people who roused him.

**Delegation as the Mechanism of Public Crime**

The verse describes a mechanism that societies use repeatedly: the worst act is performed by an agent, and the community that prompted the agent keeps its hands clean. The delegation accomplishes two things at once. It gets the act done, and it diffuses the responsibility among so many people that no single person can be identified as its author. The Qur'an's account refuses the diffusion. It names the man who was roused and it records that he was roused, which means the senders remain in the sentence even though only one hand is described. The reader is shown how a people can commit a crime collectively while performing it individually, and how the record keeps both facts in view. The application is not limited to ancient tribes. Every society has acts it wants done and does not want owned, and the verse identifies the arrangement by which such acts are arranged. The punishment that follows in the next verse falls on all of them, and it falls justly, because all of them are in the verb.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:13

> But the messenger of Allah warned them, “˹Do not disturb˺ Allah’s camel and her ˹turn to˺ drink!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning Given in Full Elsewhere**

This chapter compresses the messenger's warning into a single sentence, and the longer accounts supply the rest. Ṣâliḥ's words are recorded at length in the sūrah of Hūd: "And O my people! This she-camel of Allah is a sign for you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah's earth and do her no harm, or a swift punishment will overtake you!" (11:64). The sūrah of al-Aʿrāf adds the ground of the claim: "A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is Allah's she-camel as a sign to you" (7:73). The compressed form in this chapter preserves the two essential elements — the animal belongs to God, and her drinking is to be respected — and omits the rest because the audience already had it. The compression is a sign that the story was common knowledge.

**The Division of the Water**

The specific term of the test was an arrangement about water. Ṣâliḥ states it in the sūrah of the Poets: "Here is a camel. She will have her turn to drink as you have yours, each on an appointed day" (26:155). The sūrah of the Moon records the same instruction: "And tell them that the ˹drinking˺ water must be divided between them ˹and her˺, each taking a turn to drink ˹every other day˺" (54:28). The arrangement is precise and modest. Nobody was asked to surrender his water entirely; everybody was asked to alternate. The test was therefore not a demand for sacrifice but a demand for fairness, and the fairness had a schedule. A people who could not keep a rota for a well had failed something much smaller than a religious system. Dividing the water is the clearest possible form of the arrangement, and the verse chooses it because water cannot be shared ambiguously. A reader who has seen a community allocate a scarce resource understands the arrangement immediately, and the understanding is what makes the demand that follows so stark.

**A Sign, Not an Object of Worship**

The camel was not presented as something to be venerated. It was presented as a sign — evidence that the messenger spoke for God. The Qur'an is explicit: "this is Allah's she-camel as a sign to you" (7:73), and in the sūrah of the Moon the sending is described as a test: "We are sending the she-camel as a test for them. So watch them ˹closely˺, and have patience" (54:27). The animal's function was evidential, and the command attached to it was negative: do not harm her. Nothing in the arrangement required belief in the camel, ritual directed at her, or expenditure on her behalf. The whole of the demand was restraint. That is why the crime is so damaging in the chapter's argument. A people who cannot refrain from harming an animal they have been told to leave alone have failed the smallest possible test.

**The Messenger's Title in the Verse**

The verse calls him *rasūl Allāh*, the messenger of Allah, and the title is part of the statement rather than a description added later. By naming him in these terms, the chapter places the crime in its proper frame. The man they defied was not a local reformer or a rival claimant; he was the representative of the One who made the heaven and the earth sworn by earlier in the same chapter. The reader who has followed the oath list knows what the title means, because the list established the Maker's authority over the sky, the ground and the soul. The warning about a camel is therefore a command from the One who fashioned all three, and the defiance is proportionate to the title. The title used in the verse is the one the messenger uses of himself, and the choice is deliberate. He does not present himself as an owner or a ruler but as one sent with a message. The reader who notes the title notes that the demand rests on that office and not on force.

**A Demand Small Enough to Be Refused Only Deliberately**

The structure of the test removes every excuse. The camel was not competing for their water on all days; she was not consuming their pasture; she was not demanding worship or wealth. The instruction was to leave her alone on her appointed day and to drink on theirs. A refusal of such a demand cannot be attributed to hardship, since the hardship was minimal and scheduled. It can only be attributed to unwillingness, and unwillingness of that kind is what the chapter has been describing as transgression. The verse in the previous ayah named the manner of the rejection; this verse supplies the terms that were rejected, and the terms are so light that the rejection becomes inexplicable except as rebellion. The demand was small enough that refusing it required a decision. A reader confronted with a trivial request can comply without cost, and the cost of refusal is therefore visible. The verse constructs the situation so that the rejection cannot be explained by the weight of what was asked.

**The Warning as the Ground of the Punishment**

The verse's function in the narrative is juridical. Before the crime is committed, the prohibition is stated, the sign is identified, and the consequence is named in the fuller accounts. Nothing about the punishment that follows can therefore be described as unannounced. The Qur'an's method is consistent on this point: no people is destroyed without a messenger and a warning, and the warning is recorded so that the reader can see that it was given. The verse in this chapter is that record in compressed form. The reader arrives at the next verse knowing that the tribe had been told, and the knowledge is what makes the crushing that follows intelligible rather than arbitrary. The warning is what makes the punishment just rather than arbitrary. The community was told, in advance and in plain terms, what would follow, and the telling is recorded. The reader who questions the outcome has the warning in front of him, and the warning is the ground of everything that follows.

**A Test That Measured the Whole People**

The sūrah of the Moon describes the sending of the camel as a test, and the test measured the tribe rather than the man who killed her. A scheduled alternation at a well requires the cooperation of everyone who draws water, and the cooperation either holds or it does not. The man roused to kill her was able to act because the arrangement had already lost the tribe's support. The verse in this chapter records the terms of the test; the verse that follows records the result. Between them lies the whole of the chapter's moral argument, which is that a soul — or a society — is not ruined by a single act but by the condition that made the act acceptable. The warning was addressed to all of them, and all of them answered it by sending their worst man.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:14

> Still they defied him and slaughtered her. So their Lord crushed them for their crime, levelling all to the ground

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-ʿaqqarūhā: The Method of the Killing**

The verb describes hamstringing — cutting the tendons of the leg so that the animal collapses and cannot rise. It is not the ordinary word for slaughter, and the choice is significant. Hamstringing is done to a creature that one intends to leave helpless, and it is the method used when the killer wants the animal down before it is finished. The Qur'an uses the same verb elsewhere in the account: "Then they killed the she-camel —defying their Lord's command—" (7:77). The specificity of the word preserves the brutality of the act, and the brutality is part of the chapter's argument. This was not a utilitarian killing for meat. It was a public destruction of a sign, performed in a way that maximised the animal's helplessness before witnesses. The method of the killing is specified, and the specification is not incidental. Hamstringing an animal destroys it without benefit to anyone, and the reader who knows what the method involves understands that the act was destruction rather than necessity. The verse records the manner because the manner is the crime.

**Damdama ʿAlayhim: A Punishment That Covered Them**

The verb used for the punishment is *damdama*, and the exegetes describe it as the most severe form of destruction: something that comes suddenly, annihilates and covers completely. The reduplication in the root conveys intensity and repetition, and the preposition "upon them" completes the image — the punishment came down over them rather than at them. The Qur'an describes the same event in other terms elsewhere. The sūrah of Hūd records the blast: "And the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook the wrongdoers, so they fell lifeless in their homes, as if they had never lived there" (11:67–68). The sūrah of al-Aʿrāf records an earthquake: "Then an ˹overwhelming˺ earthquake struck them, and they fell lifeless in their homes" (7:78). The verse in this chapter compresses all of it into a single verb of crushing.

**Sawwāhā: Levelled, or Distributed Evenly**

The final verb has been read two ways, and both readings are recorded. The first takes it as levelling: God razed their city, or levelled the earth over them. The second takes it as equalising: the punishment was distributed evenly across the tribe, so that none escaped and none received more than the rest. The two readings differ in image but agree in outcome. The first reading fits the archaeological fact that the carved homes of al-Ḥijr were left standing as ruins; the second fits the theological point that collective guilt produces collective punishment. The stronger reading in context is the first, since the verb's object is most naturally the settlement. The second is not excluded, and it sharpens the warning: nobody in such a people is exempt by rank.

**The Same Verb Used for Making and for Flattening**

*Sawwā* appeared in verse 7 as the verb of fashioning the soul, and it appears here as the verb of levelling a civilisation. The repetition is the chapter's most deliberate device. The One who proportioned a human soul with care is the One who flattened a people, and the same word covers both acts. The wordplay makes a claim about power and about ease. What was fashioned with such precision can be levelled without effort, and the levelled thing is not restored by the argument that it was once well made. The reader who has followed the chapter from the oath on the sun to this verse is holding both uses of the verb at once, and the holding is the point. The same verb is used for making and for flattening, and the reuse is deliberate. A reader who has seen the verb applied to the raising of the sky now sees it applied to the levelling of a city, and the pairing tells him that both acts belong to one power.

**The Three Days Between the Act and the End**

The fuller account supplies a detail this verse omits, and the detail matters. After the killing, Ṣâliḥ told them: "You have ˹only˺ three ˹more˺ days to enjoy life in your homes— this is an unfailing promise!" (11:65). The interval was announced, and it elapsed. The punishment was therefore not a surprise sprung on a people who might have repented; it was a dated event, communicated in advance, which they spent in their homes. The three days are the chapter's most severe detail, because they remove the last excuse. A people given a deadline and a warning, who use the time neither to repent nor to flee, have made their position final. The crushing described in this verse arrived at the end of a period they had been told about. Three days is a period the reader can hold in mind, and the period is what makes the account concrete. The delay between the act and the end is stated, and the stating turns an abstract punishment into a sequence of days a person could count. The specificity is the point.

**The Verse as the Chapter's Proof**

The argument of the chapter runs from an oath on the created order to a statement about the soul, and this verse is the evidence for the statement. A soul can be buried, and a people who bury theirs are levelled. The narrative supplies the case that the abstract statement could not: a specific tribe, a specific crime, a specific punishment, at a location the audience could visit. Nothing further needs to be argued. The reader has been shown the second verb operating at its full extent, and the showing is why the chapter can end with a single sentence about consequences. The example does the work that a paragraph of reasoning would do less well. This verse is the chapter's proof because it supplies the case the argument promised. A reader who has been told that souls can be buried and that burial has consequences now has a documented instance. The proof is not theoretical; it is a record of a community that tested the principle and lost.

**The Symmetry of Making and Unmaking**

The chapter began with seven things made — light, a body that follows, a day, a night, a heaven, an earth, a soul — and it ends with one thing unmade. The symmetry is exact and the contrast is severe. Everything in the oath list was fashioned with proportion and purpose; the tribe at the end is levelled. The difference between the two states is not the power of the Maker, which is identical in both, but the conduct of the creature. A reader who takes the chapter as a whole is left with a single proposition: the same hand that fashioned you can flatten you, and what decides which it does is the verb you perform on your own soul. The verse states the flattening without comment, and the absence of comment is what makes it land.

## Sūrah al-Shams 91:15

> He has no fear of consequences

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Final Sentence With Three Possible Subjects**

The chapter ends with a clause whose subject is not stated, and the tradition records three readings. The first takes the subject as God: He does not fear the consequence of destroying them. The second takes it as the most wicked of them: he did not fear the outcome of his act. The third takes it as Ṣâliḥ: he did not fear the outcome, because he had delivered the message and was spared the punishment. All three are grammatically available, and the Arabic's economy is what allows them. The dominant reading in the exegetical tradition is the first, and it is the one that gives the verse its weight as a conclusion. The other two are not merely possible variants; each states a fact that the narrative supports, and both will be considered.

**The Dominant Reading: God Fears Nothing**

On the first reading the verse states that the destruction of a people carries no cost to God. He is not answerable to anyone, exposed to retaliation, or diminished by what He does. The claim is severe, and its severity is deliberate. A human ruler destroys a city and inherits the consequences: the resentment of survivors, the cost of occupation, the judgement of history. The verse denies that God's action has that shape. He levelled them and there is nothing afterwards that He needs to manage. The statement closes the narrative by removing any suggestion that the punishment was reluctant, partial or reversible. What was done is finished, and the One who did it is unaffected by having done it. The dominant reading places the absence of fear on the side of God, and the placement is what gives the ending its weight. A reader who reaches the verse after the account of a destroyed tribe learns that the destruction involved no hesitation and no regret. The reading is severe and it is the transmitted one.

**Why the Verse Is Needed**

A chapter that ends with a crushed people raises a question, and the final verse answers it. The question is whether such an act leaves the actor exposed — to blame, to regret, to a reckoning. The verse answers no, and the answer completes the argument of the whole sūrah. The oaths established that the Maker of the heaven and the earth is the Fashioner of the soul; the two central verses established that a soul can be grown or buried; the narrative showed what burying produces. The final clause establishes that the One who produced the outcome is beyond reproach for producing it. Without that clause the reader might read the destruction as a tragedy in which all parties lost. The verse forbids the reading. The verse is needed because the account would otherwise invite a question. A reader who has followed the destruction might suppose that the end of a whole community gave its maker pause, and the verse removes the supposition before it can settle. The statement closes the account rather than opening a new one.

**The Warning to the Audience in Mecca**

The application to the first hearers is direct. A people who had been given a messenger, a sign and a warning had been destroyed, and the One who destroyed them feared nothing. The Quraysh were in the same position: a messenger among them, a book as the sign, and an opposition that had begun to resemble the transgression of Thamūd. The verse tells them what stands behind the warning. It is not a threat issued by a man who can be resisted or a faction that can be outlasted. It is a statement about a power that has already exercised itself on a people stronger in their buildings than the Quraysh, and that was unaffected by the exercise. The chapter's final sentence is therefore a warning delivered in the grammatical form of a description.

**The Second Reading: The Killer's Heedlessness**

On the second reading the subject is the man roused to hamstring the camel, and the verse describes his state of mind: he did not fear the outcome of what he was doing. The reading fits the narrative, since a man who performs such an act in front of a prophet who has just announced a punishment is acting without any expectation of consequence. It also connects the verse to the chapter's central theme. The burying of a soul is precisely the loss of the sense of consequence, and the most wretched of the tribe is its extreme case. He had reached the point where a stated punishment did not register. The reading is secondary, but it supplies the psychology that the narrative otherwise leaves unstated. The second reading places the heedlessness on the side of the killer, and the placement changes the emphasis without changing the event. A reader who adopts it understands the verse as a description of a man who did not consider the consequences, and the description fits the conduct already recorded.

**The Third Reading: The Messenger's Completed Duty**

On the third reading the subject is Ṣâliḥ, and the verse records that he did not fear the outcome because he had done what he was sent to do. The Qur'an describes the same posture after the punishment in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf: "So he turned away from them, saying, ‘O my people! Surely I conveyed to you my Lord's message and gave you ˹sincere˺ advice, but you do not like ˹sincere˺ advisors’" (7:79). The messenger's freedom from fear is the freedom of a person who has discharged an obligation completely. The reading has the advantage of ending the chapter on the messenger rather than on the punishment, and it fits the pattern of the Qur'an's accounts, in which the prophet is spared and speaks after the destruction. It is the least common of the three readings and the most consoling.

**An Ending With No Consolation in It**

The chapter closes on a statement of finality, and it offers no comfort. There is no call to repentance after the crushing, no note of mercy, and no promise that the pattern can be interrupted. The absence is a rhetorical choice. The consolations belong to the two chapters that preceded this one, which were addressed to a man accused of abandonment; this chapter is addressed to a people being warned, and its ending is shaped for them. The reader who wants the mercy must go back to the middle of the sūrah, where the successful person is named: the one who purifies his soul. That verse is still available, and it is the only exit the chapter offers. The final clause tells him what happens to those who do not take it. No consolation is offered, and the absence is the intended effect. The chapter began with oaths and ended with a soul's ruin or purification; this verse confirms that the ruin was complete. A reader looking for a mitigating clause will not find one, and the search is what the ending is designed to discourage.

**What the Reader Holds at the End**

Fifteen verses leave the reader with a sequence, and the sequence is worth stating. Seven things were sworn by, ending on his own soul. The soul was fashioned and given the knowledge of right and wrong. It can be grown, and the one who grows it succeeds. It can be buried, and the one who buries it fails. A people buried theirs together, sent their worst man to destroy the sign they had been given, and were levelled by a Maker who fears no consequence. Every step in that sequence is stated without qualification, and the last step is not addressed to the dead. It is addressed to whoever is reading, and it is addressed in the present tense, which is the chapter's final act of seriousness. What the reader holds at the end is a choice already made visible in two outcomes. Nothing further is required of him at this point except to recognise which of the two descriptions he has been reading about himself. The chapter closes by leaving the recognition with him.
