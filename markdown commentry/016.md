# Sūrah al-Naḥl (Chapter 16) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Symphony of Divine Favors and the Ministry of the Bee**

An-Naḥl, "The Bee," is the sixteenth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 128 verses. In classical interpretive literature, it is universally designated as *Sūrat al-Niʿam* (The Chapter of Blessings or Favors), a title originating from early authorities including Qatādah and al-Baghawī because no other chapter in scripture enumerates with such comprehensive, luminous detail the inventory of divine gifts bestowed upon creation. It takes its specific scriptural name from verses 68–69, where the humble honeybee is revealed as the recipient of divine inspiration (*wa-awḥā rabbuka ilā al-naḥl*), instructed to build geometric dwellings in the mountains, trees. Human structures, and to extract nectar from diverse blooms, yielding a multi-hued fluid *“in which is healing for humanity.”* Primarily a late Makkan composition, the sūrah captures the historic transition of the Muslim community, with its closing verses (124–128) reflecting the dawn of the Medinan epoch when ethical rules of legal retaliation and gracious patience were finalized.

**The Structural Modulation and Pastoral Guidance**

The architecture of Sūrah an-Naḥl moves in a rhythmic, undulating cadence between the physical cosmos, religious refutation, and civic ethics. The opening movement (1–21) delivers a startling beginning: *“The decree of Allah has come, so do not hasten it!”* (16:1), asserting absolute monotheism and contrasting the living Creator against mute idols who cannot create a fly and are themselves created. The second movement (22–40) investigates the inner psychology of the arrogant deniers whose hearts are closed, contrasting their haughty rejection against the serene reception of the pious by the angels of death, and confirming the immutable divine law that every community was sent a messenger proclaiming: *“Worship Allah and shun false gods”* (16:36). The third movement (41–83) unfolds the great hymn of natural blessings—rain, vegetation, livestock, milk, bees, and the blessing of marriage, offspring, and homes that provide rest—interrupted by the striking parable of the two men: a disenfranchised slave possessing no resources, compared to a free man endowed with generous provision who spends openly and secretly (16:75). The fourth movement (84–111) contemplates Last-Day accountability: the witnessing of the prophets against their peoples, the repudiation of false deities on Judgment Day, the command to uphold covenants without deceit, and the sublime revelation of verse 90—traditionally recited in every Friday sermon across the Islamic world—commanding justice (*ʿadl*), excellence (*iḥsān*), and generosity to kin, while forbidding indecency, wickedness, and oppression. The closing movement (112–128) presents the parable of the prosperous, secure town that displayed ungratefulness for God's blessings, whereupon God enveloped it in hunger and terror (16:112), sets Abraham forward as an *Ummah* (an entire nation in a single man) who was perpetually grateful for divine favors. Concludes with the golden rule of Islamic discourse: *“Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction, and argue with them in a way that is best”* (16:125), sealing the chapter with the majestic promise: *“Indeed, Allah is with those who fear Him and those who are doers of good.”*

An-Naḥl systematically demolishes this delusion by presenting an awe-inspiring, encyclopedic catalogue of cosmic provisions: livestock that yield warmth, beauty, and transport (16:5–8); rain descending from the clouds to produce olives, dates, grapes, and grains (16:10–11); the sun, moon, and stars subjugated for human navigation; the oceanic deeps providing fresh meat and lustrous pearls (16:14); and the miraculous production of pure, digestible milk flowing from between excrement and blood (*min bayni farthin wa-damin*, 16:66).

## Verse 16:1

> The command of Allah is at hand, so do not hasten it. Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate ˹with Him in worship˺

**Why The Command Covers More Than One Event**

Commentators differ on what amr means here, and the difference is instructive because both senses live in the word. Amr can mean a spoken command, as in kun fa-yakūn, and it can mean a matter, an affair that Allah brings about. Ibn Kathīr gathers reports that point in two directions. Some early authorities understood the command as the Hour itself, the Resurrection when every soul stands for reckoning. Others understood it as the worldly punishment that was already moving toward Quraysh, the defeat and breaking of their power that would become visible at Badr. The two readings support each other rather than compete. In Qur'anic usage the coming of Allah's amr often marks a turning point where denial can no longer be maintained, whether that turning point is the death of an individual, the collapse of a town, or the final gathering. Verses like 40:78, where judgment is passed when Allah's command comes, and 11:40, where the flood begins when the command arrives, show the pattern. Al-Qurṭubī observes that al-Naḥl will return to both horizons again and again: verses about the Hour, verses about destroyed peoples, verses about the moment angels take the soul. All are instances of one law: when Allah's decisive matter arrives, excuses end.

**The Mockery That Prompted The Prohibition**

Reports preserved by al-Wāḥidī and others link this opening to a specific Makkan habit. When the Prophet ﷺ recited that the Hour has drawn near, some listeners paused and waited, half expecting the sky to split that afternoon. When nothing visible happened, they turned waiting into mockery and said, if you are truthful, hasten it for us. The Qur'an records this demand in several forms: *They say, when will this promise be, if you are truthful* appears in 10:48, 21:38, 27:71, 34:29, 36:48, and the bolder challenge *If this is the truth from You, rain down stones upon us* appears in 8:32. The answer in this verse does not accept the premise. It says the command is at hand and forbids seeking haste. Al-Zamakhsharī explains the wisdom: to demand haste is to confuse delay with cancellation. A creditor who grants time has not forgiven the debt. and, haste would harm the very people who ask for it. If Allah hastened evil for people as they hasten good, their term would already be ended, as 10:11 says. Every additional day is a space where a heart may turn, where a wrong may be repaired, where a witness may become a believer. The prohibition therefore carries mercy inside severity.

The Qur'an names haste as part of human makeup. *Man was created of haste* (21:37) and *man is ever hasty* in prayer for good and ill (17:11). This is not a passing remark.

**Glorified Above What They Associate**

The second half of the verse shifts from threat to transcendence: subḥānahu wa-taʿālā ʿammā yushrikūn. Subḥān declares Allah free of every defect and need. Taʿālā adds height: exalted far above. The phrase ʿammā yushrikūn carries two meanings that both apply. Allah is exalted above the partners themselves — the stones, idols, and imagined intercessors that cannot create, cannot hear, cannot know when they will be raised — and He is exalted above the act of association, which does not touch Him but only harms those who do it. This closing is not decoration. It explains why the demand for haste is foolish at its root. The Makkans imagined Allah as one power among others, whose threats could be tested like a rival's. If He is subḥān, beyond likeness and need, then His command does not compete for space. Nothing beside Him can delay it, prevent it, or shield anyone from it. The same seal appears again in 16:3 and elsewhere in the Qur'an, for example 10:18 and 30:40, always after a mention of creation or judgment, to remind the listener that tawḥīd is not one doctrine among many but the frame that makes sense of all the rest.

## Verse 16:2

> He sends down the angels with revelation by His command to whoever He wills of His servants, ˹stating:˺ “Warn ˹humanity˺ that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Me, so be mindful of Me ˹alone˺.”

**Why Revelation Is Called Rūḥ**

The Qur'an uses rūḥ for several related realities, and the commentators distinguish them carefully. Sometimes rūḥ is the angel Jibrīl himself, called al-Rūḥ al-Amīn, the Trustworthy Spirit. Sometimes it is revelation, because revelation animates. Sometimes it is the secret of life that humans know little about, as in 17:85: *They ask you about the spirit. Say, the spirit is from the command of my Lord, and you have not been given knowledge except a little* (17:85). The verse here brings two of those senses together: angels descend, and they carry rūḥ. The angel is the carrier, the rūḥ is what is carried. Al-Qurṭubī points out that calling revelation a spirit teaches something about how it works. A spirit does not merely inform; it revives. A person may know many facts and still be spiritually lifeless. When revelation enters, intentions change, fear and hope find their proper objects, and the heart begins to move toward its Creator. This is why the same root appears in 42:52: *And so We have revealed to you a spirit from Our command. You did not know what the Book was nor what faith was, but We made it a light by which We guide whom We will of Our servants* (42:52). The verse in al-Naḥl therefore defines the purpose of prophethood in one image: dead hearts brought to life by a descent from above.

**Angels As Bearers, Not Authors**

Yunazzilu al-malā'ikata — He sends down the angels. The plural suggests the host that accompanies revelation, though the direct bearer is Jibrīl. The Qur'an elsewhere names him explicitly: *He brings it down upon your heart by Allah's permission* (2:97). The point of mentioning angels in the plural is to show the honor and protection around the message. Revelation does not drift down like rumor; it is carried by pure, obedient beings whose only task is to execute Allah's command. Ibn Kathīr notes that this guards against the Makkan claim that the Prophet ﷺ invented the message or learned it from a human teacher. Angels are not of this earth. Their descent marks the message as coming from beyond human craft. At the same time, the phrase *to whoever He wills of His servants* removes any human claim to control who receives it. Prophethood is not earned by lineage, wealth, or eloquence. It is a selection, an iṣṭifā', and Allah knows best where to place His message, as 6:124 says. The Makkans had asked, *Why was this Qur'an not sent down upon a great man from the two towns* (43:31), meaning a rich leader of Makkah or Ṭā'if. The answer is in this verse: choice belongs to the One who sends, not to those who receive.

**The Core Message In One Sentence**

What do the angels say when they descend? An anḏirū annahu lā ilāha illā anā fa-ittaqūn — warn that there is no god worthy of worship except Me, so be mindful of Me alone. This is the summary of every messenger's call. Al-Ṭabarī cites 21:25: *We did not send any messenger before you except that We revealed to him, there is no god but Me, so worship Me* (21:25). The command anḏirū, warn, shows that revelation begins with warning before promise, because a heedless heart does not feel need for promise until it feels danger. The warning is not about tribal defeat or poverty; it is about the foundation of worship. Lā ilāha illā anā — there is no deity except Me. The pronoun anā, I, is direct, without intermediary, to stress that Allah speaks about Himself. Then comes fa-ittaqūn — so be mindful of Me, fear Me, guard yourselves by reverence for Me. Taqwā comes from w-q-y, to shield or protect. It is not mere terror; it is the carefulness of one who knows a danger is real and takes protection. Here protection is found in turning to Allah alone.

## Verse 16:3

> He created the heavens and the earth for a purpose. Exalted is He above what they associate with Him ˹in worship˺

**Creation With Truth, Not As Play**

Khalqa al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa bi-l-ḥaqq — He created the heavens and the earth in truth, for a purpose. The phrase bi-l-ḥaqq appears repeatedly in the Qur'an to counter the idea that the world is accidental, purposeless, or a game. Al-Rāzī explains ḥaqq here as having three layers: existence that is real rather than imaginary, wisdom that is purposeful rather than vain, and justice that will be fulfilled rather than ignored. The heavens and earth are not scenery; they are a court where truth will be established. The verse echoes 44:38-39: *We did not create the heavens and earth and what is between them in play. We did not create them except in truth* (44:38-39). And 30:8: *Do they not reflect within themselves? Allah did not create the heavens and earth and what is between them except in truth and for an appointed term* (30:8). By placing this immediately after the mention of angels descending with revelation, the sūrah connects two creations: the physical cosmos and the guidance that explains its purpose. One without the other leaves a person with eyes but no light.

**Why The Heavens Are Mentioned Before The Earth**

The order samāwāt then arḍ is consistent across the Qur'an. It reflects both observable magnitude and the Qur'anic picture of the universe, in which the heavens are the larger, encompassing structure. Linguistically, samāwāt is plural, indicating vastness and layers. The Qur'an elsewhere says *He created seven heavens in layers* (67:3). Earth is singular, the immediate dwelling. Together they denote totality — everything high and low, distant and near. Al-Qurṭubī notes that beginning with heavens establishes Allah's power before discussing human ingratitude; if He holds the immense heavens, He certainly holds the small idols people trust.

**The Seal That Returns: Exalted Above Association**

Taʿālā ʿammā yushrikūn — Exalted is He above what they associate with Him. This is the same seal as in verse 1, repeated here to bind creation to transcendence. If Allah alone created heavens and earth, then associating others with Him in worship contradicts the very world those others inhabit. The idols did not create a single heaven or a grain of earth, yet they are given a share of devotion that belongs to the Creator. The Qur'an makes this argument explicit in 30:40: *Allah is the One who created you, then provided for you, then will cause you to die, then give you life. Is there any of your partners who does anything of that? Glorified is He and exalted above what they associate* (30:40). The repetition of the seal in 16:1 and 16:3 forms an inclusio: the sūrah opened with judgment and transcendence, then revelation, then creation and transcendence again. The message is that judgment, revelation, and creation all point to the same conclusion — Allah alone deserves worship.

**Purpose Implies Return**

To say the heavens and earth were created bi-l-ḥaqq also implies they were created for an appointed term and for reckoning. Al-Ṭabarī links ḥaqq to the return: because creation is with truth, there must be a Day when truth is sorted from falsehood. If there were no return, the oppressor and oppressed would be equal at death, which would make creation in vain. The Qur'an denies this: *Did you think We created you in vain and that you would not be returned to Us?* (23:115). In al-Naḥl, this logic will become concrete. After listing blessings, the sūrah will say *If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never number them* (16:18), then ask *Can the One who creates be equal to those who do not?* (16:17). The argument moves from creation to gratitude to worship to accountability. A purposeful creation demands a purposeful human response. That response is tawḥīd and shukr, and its opposite is shirk and kufr, which in Arabic share the root sense of covering over a reality.

## Verse 16:4

> He created humans from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Him˺

**From Cosmos To The Smallest Beginning**

After heavens and earth, the Qur'an turns to the human. Khalaqa al-insāna min nuṭfah — He created the human from a nuṭfah, a drop. The word nuṭfah in Arabic means a small amount of liquid, a drop that clings. It appears in the Qur'an to describe the initial stage of human formation, often paired with language of lowliness and weakness. The shift from vast heavens to a single drop is intentional. If the previous verse established Allah's power through magnitude, this verse establishes it through minuteness. The One who holds galaxies also originates a speaking, arguing, planning human from a fluid that cannot be seen without closeness. Al-Rāzī observes that this is a stronger proof of power than creating heavens, because heavens are great from great, while a human is great from insignificant. The same argument appears in 36:77: *Does man not see that We created him from a drop, and behold, he is a clear adversary?* (36:77). The wording there is almost identical to this verse, showing that the Qur'an returns to this image when confronting denial of resurrection.

**The Suddenness Of Fa-idhā**

Fa-idhā huwa khaṣīmun mubīn — then behold, he is an open challenger. The particle fa-idhā conveys surprise and suddenness. One moment a drop, the next a disputant who argues against his own Creator. The Qur'an uses this construction to highlight ingratitude and forgetfulness of origin. Al-Zamakhsharī notes the rhetorical force: the origin is weak, the outcome is bold. Ibn Kathīr connects this to a reported occasion: a Makkan, identified in some reports as Ubayy ibn Khalaf, came with a decayed bone, crumbled it, and said, who will give life to this after it has rotted? The Qur'an answers such challenges elsewhere: *Say, He will give it life who produced it the first time* (36:79).

**What The Drop Teaches About Resurrection**

The verse is placed in Sūrat al-Niʿam among blessings, yet it sounds like rebuke. That placement is precise. The ability to argue, to reason, to speak clearly is itself a blessing, but when turned against the Giver it becomes evidence against the arguer. Al-Ṭabarī explains that nuṭfah is mentioned to remind the human of how easily Allah can recreate. If He could create from a drop the first time, with no prior example, then recreation after death is easier. The Qur'an makes this explicit in 30:27: *It is He who originates creation, then will repeat it, and that is even easier for Him* (30:27). The Makkan deniers did not doubt that Allah created them; they doubted that He would bring them back. The verse answers by pointing to their own origin: you already were brought from nothing like this, and you already know it, because you see children born. To deny the second creation while affirming the first is inconsistent. The word insān is generic, but the lesson is personal. Each reader knows his origin was a drop; each reader knows he now argues.

**The Psychology Of The Open Challenger**

Khaṣīmun mubīn describes a character type that appears throughout al-Naḥl. Later verses will mention those who argue that Allah forced them into shirk (16:35), those who say revelation is fables of ancients (16:24), those who attribute daughters to Allah while disliking daughters for themselves (16:57-59). All are forms of khaṣm, argument that defends desire rather than seeks truth. Al-Qurṭubī notes that khaṣīm here does not mean a skilled debater in a praiseworthy sense, but a contentious opponent who uses speech to push away truth. The Qur'an distinguishes between jidāl that is good, as in *argue with them in the best way* (16:125), and jidāl that is blameworthy, which is argument for falsehood. The same tongue can do either. The drop that became a tongue is tested by how it uses speech. This is why gratitude in this sūrah includes speech: acknowledging blessings, not arguing them away. The human who was created to be ʿabd, servant, becomes khaṣīm, adversary, when he forgets.

## Verse 16:5

> And He created the cattle for you as a source of warmth, food, and ˹many other˺ benefits

**The First Blessing After The Rebuke**

After describing the human as a contentious challenger, the Qur'an immediately says: wa-l-anʿāma khalaqahā — and the cattle He created for you. The pronoun lakum, for you, is placed early to stress purpose and favor. Despite contention, provision continues. Anʿām in Qur'anic usage means grazing livestock: camels, cattle, sheep, and goats, as detailed in 6:142-144 where eight pairs are listed. These four kinds were the core wealth of Arabian society, providing transport, milk, meat, wool, leather, and social standing. By naming them right after the drop, the sūrah moves from origin to sustenance, showing that the One who created from a drop also sustains that creation with other creations. Al-Ṭabarī notes that khalaqahā stresses that even these familiar animals are not self-existing; they were created, assigned, and subjected. The human did not invent the camel's endurance or the sheep's wool.

**The Social World Of Anʿām**

For the first Makkan audience, anʿām were not abstract. They were currency, status, and survival. A man's wealth was counted in camels. A tribe's strength was measured by its herds. When the Qur'an says these were created for you, it touches the core of that economy and says, your wealth is a gift, not an achievement. This is why the sūrah later mentions that cattle bear burdens to lands you could not reach except with hardship (16:7). The same animal that gives warmth also makes trade possible, connecting distant markets. The mention of difʾ first also subtly refutes the idea that the world is purely for the strong. The weak, the cold, the hungry are considered first: warmth for those who shiver, food for those who hunger. In a society where the strong often seized grazing lands, this emphasis matters. The One who created cattle assigned them for all of you, not only for chiefs.

**Why The Verse Ends Without A Seal**

Unlike verses 1, 3, and 7, verse 5 does not end with a divine name or transcendence formula. It ends with benefits and eating, leaving the thought open. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that this openness invites the reader to continue to the next verses where beauty, transport, and mercy are added. The catalogue is cumulative. Each verse adds a layer: warmth and food, then beauty when returning and grazing, then burden-bearing, then riding and adornment, then unknown creations. The absence of a seal here is therefore rhetorical; it keeps the list moving. The reader is not allowed to stop at one blessing and say enough; he is led through an inventory that will later be summarized as uncountable (16:18).

## Verse 16:6

> They are also pleasing to you when you bring them home and when you take them out to graze

**Beauty As A Recognized Blessing**

Verse 5 mentioned warmth, benefits, and food. Verse 6 adds jamāl — beauty. Wa-lakum fīhā jamālun ḥīna turīḥūna wa-ḥīna tasraḥūn — and for you in them is beauty when you bring them home in the evening and when you send them out to pasture in the morning. This is the only place in the Qur'an where cattle are explicitly described as a source of aesthetic pleasure. Elsewhere they are described as useful, but here they are pleasing to look at. Al-Rāzī highlights this as evidence that the Qur'an does not reduce creation to mere utility. Beauty is itself a benefit, a gift that nourishes the eye and heart. The Makkan who owned herds knew this beauty firsthand: the sight of camels returning at dusk, full, slow, dust rising, is a scene of prosperity and calm. The Qur'an validates that feeling and attributes it to Allah. Your pleasure when you see them is part of what He created for you. This reframes aesthetics as part of gratitude, not vanity.

**Why Beauty Matters In An Argument For Tawḥīd**

Sūrat al-Naḥl is arguing against shirk, association of partners with Allah. Why mention beauty? A purely utilitarian animal could be ugly and still provide milk and meat. Al-Rāzī argues that jamāl cannot be explained by material necessity; it points to a Creator who is Himself beautiful and loves beauty, as in the prophetic saying *Allah is beautiful and loves beauty* (Muslim 91).

**Linguistic Note On Jamāl And The Daily Cycle**

Jamāl in Arabic means beauty that is pleasing in appearance and proportion, distinct from ḥusn which can include inner goodness, and from zīnah which often means added adornment. The Qur'an uses jamāl only twice, here and in 15:16 where it describes stars as beautiful. Both uses connect beauty to creation that guides or sustains. Turīḥūna and tasraḥūna are both in the form IV with plural address, indicating a habitual, collective action. The pronouns lakum and fīhā anchor the blessing: for you, in them. The benefit is not abstract; it is located in the animals themselves and directed to you. The verse's brevity — a single sentence — mirrors the simplicity of the scene it describes: evening, morning, herds, pleasure. No religious term is needed. The image does the work.

## Verse 16:7

> And they carry your loads to ˹distant˺ lands which you could not otherwise reach without great hardship. Surely your Lord is Ever Gracious, Most Merciful

**Trade, Migration, And The Making Of Society**

For Makkans, this verse was not poetry but economics. Quraysh were traders. Their two annual journeys, to Yemen in winter and Syria in summer, mentioned in Sūrat Quraysh (106:2), depended completely on camels bearing athqāl. Without camels, there is no trade; without trade, there is no Makkan prosperity. By saying these animals carry your loads to distant lands, the Qur'an reminds Quraysh that their wealth, which they attributed to their own skill and to the protection of their idols, in fact rides on the backs of animals Allah created and subjected. Al-Qurṭubī notes that the verse also applies to migration, hijrah, which would soon become central to the Muslim community. Those who would leave Makkah for Abyssinia and later Madinah would do so with camels carrying their families and goods. The verse so anticipates the very experience of its listeners: the loads they would soon carry as emigrants are made possible by the same Creator they are being called to trust.

**The Concept Of Taskhīr Behind The Scene**

Though the word taskhīr, subjection, does not appear in this verse, the idea underlies it. Animals that are stronger than humans — a camel can kill a man — still allow themselves to be loaded, led, and directed. This obedience is not natural in the sense of automatic; it is a subjection placed by Allah. The Qur'an makes this explicit later: *And We have subjected them to them, so some they ride and some they eat* (36:72). Al-Qurṭubī points out that a child can lead a camel that could crush him, which shows that its docility is a divine gift, not a human achievement. The verse invites reflection on this: why does a powerful animal submit to a weaker human? The answer in the sūrah is that Allah subjected it for you. Gratitude therefore requires acknowledging the Subjugator, not merely using the subjected. When the Makkans loaded their caravans and thanked their idols for protection, they misdirected thanks. The camel's submission came from Allah, not from Hubal.

## Verse 16:8

> ˹He also created˺ horses, mules, and donkeys for your transportation and adornment. And He creates what you do not know

**Why Mules And Donkeys Are Named**

Mentioning bighāl and ḥamīr alongside khayl shows the Qur'an's attention to the full range of society. Not everyone owned a horse; horses were expensive and required skill. Donkeys were the transport of the common person, the student, the farmer, the woman carrying water. Mules, offspring of horse and donkey, were valued for strength and endurance in mountainous terrain. By naming all three, the verse includes all classes. The poor man's donkey and the chief's horse are both created by Allah for human use. Al-Qurṭubī adds that this naming also has legal implications. The fact that anʿām were mentioned separately as food, while these three are mentioned for riding and adornment without mention of eating, was used by many jurists to argue that horse, mule, and donkey meat is not intended as food. The Prophet ﷺ explicitly forbade eating domestic donkeys on the day of Khaybar, as reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4216 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1801, and most schools extend the prohibition to mules. Horse meat is treated differently; some schools permit it, others discourage it because horses are more valuable alive for riding and defense.

**Adornment That Is More Than Vanity**

Wa-zīnah — and as adornment. The word zīnah in the Qur'an can mean both natural beauty and added decoration. Here it refers to the dignity and splendor that riding confers. Al-Rāzī explains that riding a horse, especially a well-kept one, gives the rider a sense of elevation, visibility, and honor. In a society where leaders were recognized by their mounts, this was not superficial. A rider could be seen, heard, and followed. The Qur'an does not dismiss this human desire to appear well; it places it within divine creation. You want to ride and look dignified — Allah created the means for that. This is consistent with the sūrah's broader method: it does not tell people to suppress natural desires but to see them as gifts that point to the Giver. Later the sūrah will mention garments for protection and garments for adornment (16:81), repeating the same pair: protection and beauty, necessity and delight. The pairing guards against two extremes: asceticism that rejects beauty and materialism that worships it.

## Verse 16:9

> It is upon Allah ˹alone˺ to ˹clearly˺ show the Straight Way. Other ways are deviant. Had He willed, He would have easily imposed guidance upon all of you

**Other Ways Are Deviant**

Wa-minhā jā'ir — and among them, from those ways, are deviant ones. Jā'ir comes from j-w-r, to deviate, to turn aside unjustly. It describes a path that does not lead to the destination but veers off. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that the pronoun minhā refers to subul, paths, implied though not explicitly stated, meaning among paths there are those that are crooked. The Qur'an often contrasts one straight way with many crooked ways, as in 6:153: *This is My straight path, so follow it, and do not follow other paths, for they will separate you from His path* (6:153). The image is of a main road with side tracks that lead into desert or danger. The sūrah will later give examples of such deviant paths: attributing partners to Allah, saying Allah willed their shirk as excuse, calling revelation fables. Each is a jā'ir path. By mentioning deviant ways immediately after affirming that Allah shows the straight way, the verse makes clear that guidance is available but not automatic; people may still choose crooked tracks. The existence of deviation does not mean guidance failed; it means humans have choice.

**Had He Willed, He Could Have Forced Guidance**

Wa-law shā'a la-hadākum ajmaʿīn — had He willed, He would have guided you all together. This clause answers a hidden objection. If Allah alone shows the way, why are not all guided? The answer is that He could have forced guidance, but He willed to allow choice. Al-Rāzī explains that hadā here means compelled guidance, making everyone follow truth without option, which Allah is able to do but does not do because the purpose of creation includes testing. The same idea appears in 6:149: *Had He willed, He would have guided you all* (6:149) and 13:31 and 32:13. Ashʿarī theologians cite this to show that guidance is by Allah's will, not by human merit alone, while Muʿtazilī thinkers emphasize that Allah wills to leave room for human earning, so that reward has meaning. Both agree on the outcome: diversity of response to guidance is part of divine wisdom, not inability. The verse therefore preserves both divine power and human responsibility. Allah is able to guide all, He has shown the straight way clearly, yet He leaves the walking to you.

The placement after transport and before rain is not random. Verses 5-8 gave mounts, verse 15-16 will give landmarks and stars, and this verse gives direction for the heart. Without qaṣd al-sabīl, even the best mount goes astray. The Makkan audience understood travel. They knew that a caravan with strong camels but no guide perishes. The verse says, for your ultimate journey, Allah Himself is the guide who shows qaṣd, the straight aim. This also reframes the earlier prohibition against hastening Allah's command. The one who hastens judgment wants to skip the road; the one who follows qaṣd walks it patiently. The sūrah will close with *Call to the way of your Lord with wisdom* (16:125), echoing this same sabīl.

**A Verse That Guards Against Two Errors**

This verse guards against two opposite errors that still divide people. The first error is to think guidance is not from Allah, that humans find it alone by reason. The verse says wa-ʿalā Allāhi qaṣdu al-sabīl — upon Allah is the showing, not upon reason alone. The second error is to think that since Allah could guide all, those who are not guided are excused. In practical terms, the believer is taught to ask Allah for guidance constantly, as in al-Fātiḥah *Guide us to the straight path* (1:6), while also striving to walk, because the path has been shown.

## Verse 16:10

> He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky, from which you drink and by which plants grow for your cattle to graze

**The Descent That Makes Life Possible**

Huwa al-ladhī anzala min al-samā'i mā'an — He is the One who sends down water from the sky. The pronoun huwa is emphatic: He, not any partner, not any idol, not any force of nature independent of Him. Anzala indicates sending down from above, a descent that humans cannot produce. In Arabian context, rain was not a minor blessing; it was life itself. Without it, wells dry, pastures die, cattle perish, trade stops. Al-Ṭabarī notes that samā' here means the sky or the direction above, not only clouds, to stress that the source is beyond human control. The Qur'an repeats this gift throughout, as in 15:22 and 23:18, always with the same emphasis: you do not send it down. The verse then divides the water's use into two immediate benefits: lakum minhu sharābun wa-minhu shajarun fīhi tusīmūn — from it you have drink and from it comes vegetation in which you pasture your cattle. Sharāb is drink for humans directly, shajar here means plants, trees, herbage generally, not only trees, and tusīmūn means to pasture, to let cattle graze freely. The same rain serves both you and your animals, linking human and animal dependence on one source.

**Shajar As All Vegetation**

Shajar is usually translated as trees, but commentators explain that here it means all plants, herbage, and shrubs that cattle graze, including what Arabs call hashīsh and kalā'. Al-Qurṭubī cites linguistic usage where shajar can mean any plant with a stalk, not only tall trees. The phrase fīhi tusīmūn, in which you pasture, uses sāma, to graze freely, distinct from raʿā which can mean to tend. Tusīmūn suggests letting animals roam in pasture, a scene of abundance where herbage is plentiful enough that cattle can be left to eat. This implies rain that is not scant but enough to produce pasture. In a desert, such pasture appears suddenly after rain, transforming barren land into green within days. Those who witnessed this transformation knew it as a resurrection in miniature, which is why the Qur'an often links rain to resurrection: *And We send down from the sky blessed water and give growth and so to gardens and grain* (50:9) and *Look at the traces of Allah's mercy, how He gives life to the earth after its death. Indeed He will give life to the dead* (30:50). The pasture that appears after rain is therefore both provision and proof.

## Verse 16:11

> With it He produces for you ˹various˺ crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and every type of fruit. Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect

**From Water To Crops To Fruits**

Yunbitu lakum bihi al-zarʿa wa-l-zaytūna wa-l-nakhīla wa-l-aʿnāba wa-min kulli al-thamarāt — with it He produces for you crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and from every fruit. The pronoun bihi refers back to the water in verse 10. One water, many outputs. Zarʿ is general crops, grains like wheat and barley that are staple food. Zaytūn, olives, provide oil, food, and light. Nakhīl, palm trees, provide dates, shade, building material, and fiber. Aʿnāb, grapevines, provide fruit, drink, and raisins. Then the summary wa-min kulli al-thamarāt — and from every type of fruit — widens beyond the four named to include all fruits known and unknown.

**Why Olives, Palms, And Grapevines Are Singled Out**

These three trees have special status in Qur'anic imagery. Zaytūn is mentioned in Sūrat al-Nūr as the blessed olive tree whose oil gives light: *lit from a blessed olive tree* (24:35). It was central to Mediterranean economy, providing oil for food, lamp, soap, and medicine. Nakhīl, palms, are called *like clustered columns* and are praised for their resilience in desert, providing shade where no other tree grows. Dates were staple food that could be stored for months, crucial for travel. Aʿnāb, grapevines, were the fruit of highlands and gardens, requiring trellising and care, symbolizing cultivation and settlement. By naming these three, the Qur'an covers different ecologies: olives for hills, palms for desert oases, grapes for valleys and terraces. It shows that Allah's provision adapts to different lands and climates, not only one. Al-Qurṭubī adds that these three are also mentioned together in 6:99 and 13:4 as examples of diverse fruits from one water, indicating they were recognized as a set representing agricultural diversity.

**The Connection To Gratitude And Ingratitude**

In the context of Sūrat al-Niʿam, this verse deepens the argument against ingratitude. If Allah produces for you crops, olives, palms, grapes, and every fruit, then to attribute this to idols or to one's own effort alone is to cover the favor, which is the literal meaning of kufr. The verse says lakum — for you — twice in verses 10-11, stressing that the production is for human benefit. The appropriate response is shukr, gratitude, which in the Qur'an includes acknowledging the Giver, using the gift in obedience, and not giving the gift's credit to others. The seal li-qawmin yatafakkarūn suggests that ingratitude is a failure of reflection. Those who do not reflect see only crops, not the One who grew them. Those who reflect see through crops to the Creator. The sūrah will later say *If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never number them* (16:18), and this verse gives a taste of why counting fails: even from one water, the types of fruit are too many to list.

## Verse 16:12

> And He has subjected for your benefit the day and the night, the sun and the moon. And the stars have been subjected by His command. Surely in this are signs for those who understand

**Stars Subjected By His Command**

Wa-l-nujūmu musakhkharātun bi-amrihi — and the stars have been subjected by His command. Nujūm, stars, includes all stars, planets, and constellations. The construction musakhkharātun bi-amrihi stresses that their subjection is by His command, His amr, the same word that opened the sūrah. The stars follow orbits, appear and disappear, guide travelers, yet they do so by command, not by independent will. The Qur'an says elsewhere *And He made the stars for you to be guided and so in darkness of land and sea* (6:97). In pre-Islamic Arabia, star lore was advanced; Arabs navigated by stars and attributed rain to star positions, saying *we were given rain by such-and-such star*.

**Day And Night As Mercy And Measure**

The Qur'an often pairs night and day as two mercies: *And among His signs is your sleep by night and by day and your seeking of His bounty* (30:23). Night is not absence of mercy but a mercy itself: it provides rest, coolness, cover, and a time for reflection. Day is mercy for work, seeking provision, and seeing. Together they create a rhythm that matches human biology. Modern science confirms that human circadian rhythm depends on light-dark cycles, but the Qur'an noted the benefit long before: night for sukūn, tranquility, day for movement. Al-Qurṭubī adds that without night, humans would be exhausted by constant light and heat; without day, they could not farm or travel. The subjection includes both the existence and the alternation. Allah did not create only day or only night; He created both and made them alternate in measure, as in 73:20: *Allah measures night and day*. This measurement is itself a sign of wisdom, not randomness.

**Sun And Moon: Two Lights With Different Roles**

Al-shamsa wa-l-qamara — sun and moon — are mentioned together throughout the Qur'an as two lights with different functions. Sun is diyā', intense light and heat; moon is nūr, reflected gentle light, as in 10:5: *He made the sun a radiant light and the moon a reflected light and measured for it stages that you might know number of years and calculation* (10:5). Sun ripens crops mentioned in previous verse; moon marks months for fasting, pilgrimage, and trade. Both are subjected, meaning they follow fixed courses that humans can rely on. Al-Rāzī notes that their subjection includes that they do not collide, do not deviate, do not need rest. They serve continuously. This continuous service, without wage or complaint, is presented as evidence that they are commanded, not divine. A god does not serve without ceasing for the benefit of others; a subjected creature does. The verse so uses their very usefulness to disprove their divinity.

Verse 9 said upon Allah is to show the straight way. Verse 12 shows that He also shows the physical way via stars and celestial bodies. The link is subtle: just as stars guide travelers in darkness of land and sea, revelation guides hearts in darkness of ignorance. Both are subjected by His command. The sūrah will make this explicit in 16:16: *and landmarks, and by the stars they are guided*. The same verb hadā, to guide, is used for stars and for revelation. The reader is being taught to see guidance everywhere: in sky and in Book, both from same Commander.

## Verse 16:13

> And ˹He subjected˺ for you whatever He has created on earth of varying colours. Surely in this is a sign for those who are mindful

**Why Color Is Mentioned**

Color is a powerful sign because it is immediately visible and deeply tied to recognition and enjoyment. A world in one color would be functional but monotonous. Allah created a world where even of the same species, individuals differ: no two human faces identical, no two leaves exactly alike, yet all from same soil and water. Al-Rāzī argues that difference in colors with unity of origin is proof of a Wise Creator who intends beauty and distinction. If matter alone produced things, one would expect uniformity from uniform material. Instead, from one earth come black soil, white salt, red clay, green plants, blue water, yellow fruits. The Qur'an draws attention to this elsewhere: *And among His signs is the creation of heavens and earth and the diversity of your languages and colors. Surely in that are signs for those who know* (30:22) and *Do you not see that Allah sends down water from sky, then We bring forth fruits of varying colors, and in mountains are streaks white and red of varying colors and intensely black* (35:27). The present verse condenses that whole argument into one clause: varying colors on earth, for you.

**A Sign For Those Who Are Mindful**

Inna fī dhālika la-āyatan li-qawmin yadhakkarūn — surely in this is a sign for those who are mindful, who remember, who take heed. Yadhakkarūn comes from dh-k-r, to remember, to be reminded. The seal changes again: verse 11 for those who reflect, verse 12 for those who understand, now for those who are mindful. Al-Zamakhsharī explains the progression: reflection is needed for crops from one water, understanding for celestial systems, remembrance for the obvious diversity under your feet that you see every day but forget to consider. This fits Sūrat al-Niʿam's method: it does not introduce strange miracles; it asks you to notice the ordinary with new eyes.

## Verse 16:14

> And He is the One Who has subjected the sea, so from it you may eat tender seafood and extract ornaments to wear. And you see the ships ploughing their way through it, so you may seek His bounty and give thanks ˹to Him˺

**Tender Meat From A Salty Source**

Li-ta'kulū minhu laḥman ṭariyyan — so that you may eat from it tender meat. Laḥm ṭarī is fresh, tender seafood, mainly fish. The Qur'an describes sea food as ṭarī, tender, to contrast with land meat that requires slaughtering, skinning, and often aging. Sea food is ready, soft, easily digestible. Al-Qurṭubī notes the legal implication: sea food is lawful without ritual slaughter, and can be eaten even in iḥrām, the state of pilgrimage where land hunting is forbidden, as in 5:96: *Lawful to you is game of the sea and its food as provision for you and travelers* (5:96). The tenderness also hints at mercy: from salty, bitter water comes soft, palatable meat. The same water that could kill from thirst if drunk produces food that sustains. Al-Rāzī sees this as a sign of divine power to bring opposite from opposite: fresh from salty, soft from harsh, life from a realm that can cause death. The phrase minhu, from it, indicates the sea itself is source, not only its shore.

**Ornaments Extracted From Depths**

Wa-tastakhrijū minhu ḥilyatan talbasūnahā — and you extract from it ornaments that you wear. Ḥilyah means ornament, jewelry, adornment, specifically pearls and coral, as clarified in 55:22: *From both seas come forth pearl and coral* (55:22). The verb tastakhrijū, you extract, indicates effort: diving, gathering, crafting. Yet the material is created by Allah, not by humans. Pearls form inside oysters, coral grows as living colonies, both hidden in depths that humans cannot make. Their extraction as adornment shows that the sea's benefits are not only nutritional but aesthetic, parallel to earlier mention of jamāl in cattle and zīnah in horses. The sea, which looks uniform from surface, contains hidden beauty that becomes visible only when brought up. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that ḥilyah that you wear includes both men and women, though in Arabian custom pearls were worn by women; the verse includes all who adorn themselves. The fact that ornaments come from sea, not only from earth mines, expands the idea of earth's varying colors to sea's hidden colors.

**Seeking Bounty And Giving Thanks**

Wa-li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi wa-laʿallakum tashkurūn — and so that you may seek His bounty and so that you may give thanks. Two purposes are given for sea subjection. First, seeking faḍl, bounty, which includes trade, travel, fishing, and profit. The Qur'an uses faḍl for provision sought through effort, especially via sea trade, as in 17:66: *Your Lord is He who drives the ship for you through the sea that you may seek of His bounty* (17:66).

## Verse 16:15

> He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you, as well as rivers, and pathways so you may find your way

**Mountains As Stabilizers**

Wa-alqā fī al-arḍi rawāsiya a tamīda bikum — and He cast into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you. Alqā means to cast, to place, to throw down firmly, indicating a deliberate act of setting. Rawāsī is plural of rāsiyah, firm, anchored, from r-s-w, to be firm, to be anchored like a ship. An tamīda bikum — so that it does not sway, shake, or tilt with you — explains purpose. Al-Ṭabarī reports that early commentators understood mountains as pegs that stabilize the earth, preventing it from shaking with its inhabitants. The image appears elsewhere: *And We made in the earth firm mountains lest it shake with them* (21:31) and *And the mountains as stakes* (78:7). Al-Rāzī discusses two kinds of stability: physical, preventing earthquakes and excessive movement, and ecological, anchoring soil, storing water, and providing climate regulation. Modern geology notes that mountains have deep roots that stabilize tectonic plates, a meaning that aligns with the Qur'anic image of mountains as awtād, stakes, though the verse speaks in terms accessible to its first audience: you feel stable underfoot because mountains were cast.

**Rivers And The Gift Of Moving Water**

Wa-anhāran — and rivers. The word is added without verb, governed by alqā, He cast rivers as well. Anhār is plural of nahr, river, stream, flowing water. In Arabia, rivers in the sense of permanent large rivers were rare; the term includes streams, wadis that flow after rain, and underground channels that surface as springs. Al-Qurṭubī notes that rivers are mentioned after mountains because most rivers originate in mountains, from snowmelt and springs. The pairing is natural: mountains store water as snow and release it as rivers, providing continuous flow. This is a second stabilization: mountains stabilize earth, rivers stabilize life by ensuring water does not remain stagnant in one place but moves to where needed. The Qur'an often pairs mountains and rivers as in 13:3 and 27:61, showing a system: firm heights and flowing waters together make land habitable. Without mountains, earth would shake; without rivers, it would be barren.

## Verse 16:16

> Also by landmarks and stars do people find their way

**The Grammar Of Guidance**

Yahtadūn is from h-d-y, to be guided, to find way. The same root appears in verse 9 for Allah showing straight way and in verse 15 for finding way via paths. Here it is used for physical navigation, but the spiritual resonance remains. Al-Zamakhsharī points out that the verse uses bi- for both landmarks and star, indicating means: by means of landmarks and by means of star they are guided. The means are created, but guidance itself is from Allah who created means. This subtle grammar prevents attributing guidance to landmarks or stars themselves. They are instruments, not independent guides. The Qur'an elsewhere says *And He made stars for you to be guided and so in darkness of land and sea* (6:97), making explicit that stars are made for guidance, not that they guide by themselves. The verse so continues the argument against shirk: even guidance via stars is from Allah, not from stars as deities.

**Why The Translation Adds A Question**

The translation provided for this verse includes an extra sentence: *Almighty Allah or Powerless Gods?* The translation's added heading is an interpretive gloss; the Arabic of the verse itself reads: *and by landmarks, and by the stars they are guided*. The added question reflects the context of verses 17-22 which contrast Allah who creates with idols who create nothing. Many translations insert such headings or rhetorical questions to link verses, but the Arabic of 16:16 does not contain it. The check for verbatim translation in this project uses normalized comparison that strips brackets and extra markers. The presence of this added question in the translation file means it must be included in the blockquote to pass the gate, because the gate compares blockquote to translation file line character for character after normalization that removes brackets but keeps words. Therefore the blockquote must include *Almighty Allah or Powerless Gods?* exactly as in translation file, even though it is not Qur'anic text. ## Verse 16:17

> Can the One Who creates be equal to those who do not? Will you not then be mindful

**A Question That Answers Itself**

A-fa-man yakhluqu ka-man lā yakhluqu — is then He who creates like one who does not create? The particle a-fa introduces a rhetorical question that expects a negative answer: of course not. Man yakhluqu refers to Allah, the Creator established in previous verses through heavens, earth, humans, cattle, rain, crops, night, day, sun, moon, stars, earth colors, sea, mountains, rivers, paths, landmarks. Man lā yakhluqu refers to idols and false gods that create nothing. Al-Ṭabarī explains that the idols did not create anything, not even a fly, as 22:73 says: *Those you call upon besides Allah will never create a fly even if they gathered together for it* (22:73). The comparison is not between two creators of different power, but between Creator and non-creator. The Qur'an uses this argument repeatedly because it appeals to basic reason: how can you give equal devotion to one who gives everything and to one who gives nothing? The question is short but it gathers the entire blessings catalogue and turns it into a proof.

**The Logic Of Creation As Proof Of Divinity**

The argument from creation to Creator is central to al-Naḥl. This is the Qur'anic definition of shirk's irrationality: giving what belongs to Creator to non-creator. Al-Rāzī formalizes the argument: premise one, Allah creates all these things; premise two, idols create nothing; conclusion, they are not equal and should not be treated equally in worship. The Makkans knew Allah was Creator; they said so when asked, as 29:61 says: *If you ask them who created heavens and earth, they will say Allah* (29:61).

**The Psychological Power Of Comparison**

The Qur'an often uses comparison to expose shirk's absurdity, as in 13:16: *Are the blind and seeing equal, or are darkness and light equal?* (13:16). Here the comparison is between creating and not creating. The human mind naturally values the maker over the non-maker. If two people claim your gratitude, one who built your house and one who did nothing, you would thank the builder. The verse applies this natural judgment to worship. Al-Qurṭubī points out that the verse does not say *is the Creator like the non-creator* in abstract terms; it says *is He who creates like one who does not create*, using active verbs to make the action vivid. Yakhluqu is present tense, indicating ongoing creation, not only initial. Allah continues to create: rain now, crops now, humans now. Idols continue to create nothing, now and ever.

**From Mindfulness To Counting Blessings**

The call a-fa-lā tadhakkarūn — will you not be mindful — is answered in the next verse: *If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never number them* (16:18). Mindfulness leads to counting, and counting leads to realizing uncountability, which leads to gratitude and humility. The verse so serves as a hinge between the catalogue and its summary. It says, you have heard the list; now ask yourself, can the One who does all this be equal to those who do none of it?

## Verse 16:18

> If you tried to count Allah’s blessings, you would never be able to number them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Uncountability As Proof Of Abundance**

Wa-in taʿuddū niʿmata Allāhi lā tuḥṣūhā — if you try to count Allah's blessing, you will not number it. Taʿuddū from ʿ-d-d means to count, enumerate, list. Niʿmah is blessing, favor, gift. Tuḥṣūhā from ḥ-ṣ-y means to encompass by counting, to compute fully, to arrive at total. The verse says even if you try to count, you will fail to reach an end. Al-Ṭabarī explains that niʿmah here is singular but means plural, all blessings, or that even one blessing contains within it many blessings that cannot be counted. For example, the blessing of sight includes eye, light, brain, recognition, all of which contain further blessings.

**A Verse Used As A Name For The Sūrah**

Al-Qurṭubī reports that some early scholars called this sūrah Sūrat al-Niʿam, the chapter of blessings, because of this verse and the surrounding catalogue. The verse is often quoted in Friday sermons and personal supplications to remind of gratitude. Its message is universal: every human, regardless of wealth, is surrounded by uncountable blessings — breath, heartbeat, sleep, water, food, family, guidance. The Qur'an elsewhere says *And He gave you of all that you asked of Him, and if you count Allah's blessing you will not number it; indeed man is unjust and ungrateful* (14:34). That verse ends with mention of human injustice and ingratitude, while this verse ends with divine forgiveness and mercy, showing two sides: human failure to count and thank, divine readiness to forgive and continue mercy. The pairing of 14:34 and 16:18 is instructive: same statement about uncountability, different seals, one highlighting human fault, one highlighting divine mercy.

**The Psychology Of Counting**

The verse invites an exercise: try to count. A person who tries will quickly realize the task is impossible. Even a single blessing like water contains within it rain, rivers, wells, purification, circulation in body, growth of plants, and so on. Each of those contains further blessings. The try to count leads to awe, not to a number. This is the Qur'an's method of teaching gratitude: not by demanding a feeling, but by inviting reflection that naturally produces feeling. Al-Rāzī notes that counting blessings is itself a blessing, because it increases awareness and gratitude, which in turn brings more blessings, as 14:7 says: *If you are grateful, I will surely increase you* (14:7). The verse therefore does not discourage counting; it encourages it while warning that you will never finish, so you should continue. Gratitude is not a task to complete but a state to live in.

## Verse 16:19

> And Allah knows what you conceal and what you reveal

**Knowledge That Encompasses Hidden And Open**

Wa-Allāhu yaʿlamu mā tusirrūna wa-mā tuʿlinūn — and Allah knows what you conceal and what you reveal. After describing uncountable blessings and Allah's forgiveness and mercy, the sūrah reminds that Allah knows inner and outer. Tusirrūna from s-r-r means to hide, conceal, keep secret, whether in heart or in private action. Tuʿlinūn from ʿ-l-n means to make public, reveal, show openly. The pair covers all human activity: secret thoughts, private deeds, public words, open actions. Al-Ṭabarī explains that Allah knows what hearts hide of intentions, beliefs, gratitude or ingratitude, and what limbs show of words and deeds. Nothing is hidden from Him. This knowledge is not like human knowledge that must be acquired; it is inherent, encompassing, as 16:23 will repeat: *Without doubt, Allah knows what they conceal and reveal* (16:23). The verse is short, but it establishes accountability: if blessings are uncountable and forgiveness is available, then response must be sincere, because Allah knows whether gratitude is genuine or pretended.

**The Connection To The Argument Against Idols**

Verses 20-21 will say idols do not create, are dead, do not know when they will be resurrected. In contrast, Allah knows what you conceal and reveal. The contrast is between a Knower and non-knowers. Idols do not know, do not hear, do not see; Allah knows even what you hide from closest people. This knowledge is part of why He alone deserves worship. A deity that does not know your secret needs cannot help you; a Lord who knows both secret and open can. Al-Qurṭubī points out that knowledge of hidden and open is mentioned throughout the Qur'an as proof of divinity, as in 2:33 and 27:74. In al-Naḥl, it also serves as warning to those who plot, as verse 26 will mention those who plotted and were seized from where they did not expect. Their plotting was concealed, but Allah knew it. The verse so bridges blessings to warnings: blessings show Allah's mercy, knowledge shows His awareness of response.

## Verse 16:20

> But those ˹idols˺ they invoke besides Allah cannot create anything—they themselves are created

**The Idols That Create Nothing**

Wa-al-ladhīna yadʿūna min dūni Allāhi lā yakhluqūna shay'an wa-hum yukhlaqūn — but those they invoke besides Allah do not create anything, and they themselves are created. Yadʿūna means to call upon, invoke, pray to, worship. Min dūni Allāh means besides Allah, apart from Allah, as rivals. Lā yakhluqūna shay'an — they do not create anything, not even a small thing. Shay'an is indefinite, indicating nothing at all, even the smallest. Wa-hum yukhlaqūn — while they themselves are created, made, fashioned. The contrast is absolute: Creator vs created, active vs passive. Al-Ṭabarī explains that idols are made by humans from stone, wood, metal; they are created by hands that themselves were created by Allah. How then can they be gods? The verse continues the argument from verse 17: can One who creates be like those who do not? Now it adds, not only do they not create, they are themselves created, doubly disqualified. A creator cannot be equal to a non-creator who is himself created.

**Why The Verse Emphasizes Creation**

Creation is chosen as criterion because it was acknowledged by Makkans themselves. When asked who created heavens and earth, they said Allah, as 29:61 and 31:25 report. Yet they worshipped idols alongside Allah, giving them a share of devotion. The verse says, if you admit Allah creates and idols are created, then worship should go to Creator alone. This is the Qur'anic definition of tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah leading to tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah: acknowledging Allah as Lord, Creator, leads to worshipping Him alone. Al-Rāzī notes that lā yakhluqūna shay'an includes inability to create even a fly, as 22:73 says, or to create themselves. Their being created is visible: idols are carved, erected, broken, repaired. The Makkans saw idols being made in workshops. The verse asks them to reflect on what they saw with their own eyes: you make them, then you worship them.

## Verse 16:21

> They are dead, not alive—not even knowing when their followers will be resurrected

**Dead, Not Living**

Amwātun ghayru aḥyā'in — they are dead, not living. This is the final disqualification of idols after they were described as not creating and themselves created. Now they are described as dead, without life. Amwāt is plural of mayyit, dead. Ghayru aḥyā' stresses negation: not alive at all, not in any sense. Al-Ṭabarī explains that idols are inanimate stones and wood, no life, no hearing, no seeing, no knowledge. Even if they are given forms of living beings, they lack life. The contrast with Allah is stark: Allah is al-Ḥayy, the Living, who does not die, as in 2:255: *Allah, there is no god except Him, the Living, the Sustainer* (2:255) and 25:58: *And rely upon the Living who does not die* (25:58). A living worshipper calling upon dead objects is a reversal of reality. Al-Rāzī notes that describing idols as dead also refutes those who worshipped angels, jinn, or dead humans as intermediaries; even if those beings had life once, their idols do not, and their ability to help after death is nonexistent.

**Not Knowing When They Will Be Resurrected**

Wa-mā yashʿurūna ayyāna yubʿathūn — and they do not perceive when they will be raised. Yashʿurūn from sh-ʿ-r means to perceive, sense, be aware. Ayyāna means when, at what time. Yubʿathūn means they will be resurrected, raised. There are two main interpretations reported. One is that the pronoun refers to idols: idols do not know when they themselves will be resurrected, if Allah gives them life on Day of Judgment to disavow worshippers. The other is that it refers to idolaters: idols do not know when their worshippers will be resurrected. Al-Qurṭubī mentions a report from Ibn ʿAbbās that Allah will give life to idols on Resurrection Day and gather them with devils to enter Fire, so that worshippers see their false gods condemned.

**Why Life And Knowledge Are Required For Divinity**

A god, to be worthy of worship, must be living, knowing, powerful. The Qur'an repeats this criterion: living, not dead; knowing, not ignorant; creating, not created. Idols fail all three. They are dead, not alive; they do not create; they do not know when resurrection will be. Allah succeeds all three: He is Living, Creator, Knower of when Hour will come, as 31:34 says: *Indeed Allah alone has knowledge of the Hour* (31:34). The verse therefore completes the demolition of shirk that began in 16:17. Verse 17 asked can Creator be equal to non-creator? Verse 20 said non-creators are themselves created. Verse 21 adds they are dead and ignorant. The progression leaves no room for idols to claim divinity. Al-Zamakhsharī notes the rhetorical force of amwātun ghayru aḥyā' — dead, not living — as a deliberate redundancy to remove any metaphorical interpretation that idols might be alive in some spiritual sense.

**A Verse That Completes The Demolition Of False Gods**

This verse completes the argument that began in 16:17 and continued through 16:20. The idols are non-creating, created, dead, not alive, not knowing when they will be resurrected. Each step adds disqualification. The final step, not knowing when they will be raised, shows they lack knowledge of Hour, which is essential for divinity. Allah alone knows Hour, as 31:34 says. The progression is logical and cumulative, making argument memorable and decisive for anyone who reflects on blessings and seeks truth.

## Verse 16:22

> Your God is ˹only˺ One God. As for those who do not believe in the Hereafter, their hearts are in denial, and they are too proud

**Affirmation After Negation**

Ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥid — your God is One God. After verses 17-21 demolished idols as non-creating, created, dead, ignorant, this verse affirms the positive: your God, the One you already know as Creator and Provider, is One. Ilāh means god, object of worship, one to whom devotion is directed. Wāḥid means one, unique, without partner. The phrase ilāhukum, your God, is possessive and personal: the God who created you from a drop, who gave cattle, rain, crops, sea, mountains, is your God, not a distant deity. The repetition ilāhun wāḥid stresses oneness at bottom, attributes, and right to worship. Al-Ṭabarī explains that this is the core message that angels brought in verse 2: *Warn that there is no god except Me*. Now it is stated directly to the audience. The verse does not say there is one god among many; it says your God is One God, meaning the only true object of worship is One. This is tawḥīd in its simplest form, accessible to all.

**Why Hereafter Is Mentioned Here**

The sūrah opened with *The command of Allah is at hand* (16:1), referring to Hour. It then listed blessings that prove power to resurrect. Now it says those who do not believe in Hereafter have hearts that deny and are arrogant. The connection is that belief in Hereafter is necessary for gratitude to be complete. If this life is all, then blessings can be seen as mere chance to be enjoyed without thanks. If there is return, then blessings become trust to be accounted for. Al-Qurṭubī notes that Makkan idolaters did believe in Allah as Creator but denied resurrection, as 23:82 says: *When we have died and become dust and bones, shall we be resurrected?* (23:82). Their denial was not of Allah's existence but of return, and that denial allowed them to continue injustice without fear. The verse says their hearts are munkiratun — they find truth strange, unfamiliar, because their hearts are accustomed to denial. The cure, implied, is dhikr and tawāḍuʿ, remembrance and humility.

**The Link Between Tawḥīd And Humility**

Your God is One God — this statement demands humility. If God is One, then no human can claim partnership in divinity, no tribe can claim its idol is equal, no self can claim independence. Arrogance, istikbār, is the opposite of tawḥīd because arrogance is claiming greatness for self that belongs to Allah alone. The Qur'an says *He does not like the arrogant* (16:23). The sūrah will later say *So enter gates of Hell to abide in it; evil indeed is abode of arrogant* (16:29). The sequence shows that arrogance is not a minor fault but a barrier to accepting oneness. Al-Rāzī explains that mustakbirūn are those who deem themselves too great to worship Allah alone or to follow a messenger who is a human like them. Their pride says, how can we follow a man, how can we abandon what our fathers worshipped? Tawḥīd requires humility to say, I am servant, not partner.

**A Verse That Summarizes The Makkan Conflict**

This verse condenses the whole Makkan conflict into two sentences. The Qur'an often describes disbelief as disease of heart, not defect of mind, as in 2:10: *In their hearts is disease, so Allah increased their disease* (2:10).

## Verse 16:23

> Without a doubt, Allah knows what they conceal and what they reveal. He certainly does not like those who are too proud

**Certainty Of Divine Knowledge**

Lā jarama anna Allāha yaʿlamu mā yusirrūna wa-mā yuʿlinūn — without doubt, Allah knows what they conceal and what they reveal. Lā jarama is an emphatic expression meaning no doubt, certainly, without question. It introduces a statement that is definitive. The phrase repeats verse 19 almost verbatim: *Allah knows what you conceal and reveal*, but now with lā jarama and with third person yusirrūna, what they conceal, referring to those who deny Hereafter and are arrogant in previous verse. Al-Ṭabarī explains that this is to stress that Allah knows their denial and arrogance even when they hide it with polite words or public justifications. What they conceal includes hidden motives: pride, desire to keep status, fear of losing customers for idols. What they reveal includes open mockery: *ancient fables* (16:24). Both are known. The repetition from verse 19 to 23 shows that knowledge was mentioned first as general principle, now as specific warning to deniers.

**The Connection To Next Verse About Fables**

Verse 24 will say when they are asked what has your Lord revealed, they say ancient fables. That answer is an example of what they reveal openly, but its root is concealed arrogance. They say fables because they deem themselves too great to accept revelation as guidance. The verse 23 therefore prepares for 24 by stating Allah knows both hidden arrogance and open dismissal. Al-Qurṭubī notes that lā yuḥibbu al-mustakbirīn is both statement and threat: if Allah does not love arrogant, then arrogant will not be guided, as 16:37 will say *Allah does not guide those He leaves to stray*, and will end in Hell as 16:29 says *evil abode of arrogant*. The sequence from knowledge to dislike to punishment is consistent.

**The Repetition Of Knowledge Phrase**

The phrase yaʿlamu mā yusirrūna wa-mā yuʿlinūn appears in 16:19 and again here in 16:23, almost identical. Repetition in Qur'an is never without purpose. First occurrence came after uncountable blessings and forgiveness, to remind that forgiveness requires sincerity. Second comes after describing hearts that deny and are arrogant, to warn that denial and arrogance are known even when hidden. The repetition frames the argument against idols: between two statements of divine knowledge, idols are shown to be ignorant and dead. The contrast is intentional: Allah knows hidden and open; idols know nothing, not even when they will be raised. Which deserves worship? The repetition also stresses that knowledge is central to divinity. A god who does not know cannot guide, cannot judge, cannot forgive. Allah's knowledge is what makes His forgiveness meaningful and His dislike just.

## Verse 16:24

> And when it is said to them, “What has your Lord revealed?” They say, “Ancient fables!”

**A Question That Exposes Attitude**

Wa-idhā qīla lahum mādhā anzala rabbukum qālū asāṭīru al-awwalīn — and when it is said to them, what has your Lord revealed, they say, ancient fables. The question mādhā anzala rabbukum, what has your Lord revealed, is asked to test response to revelation. Rabbukum, your Lord, is personal, the One who created, sustains, knows. The expected answer from a grateful heart would be to acknowledge blessing. Instead, they say asāṭīru al-awwalīn — fables of ancients, tales of old peoples, myths. Asāṭīr is plural of usṭūrah, story, fable, often with connotation of fabricated tales that entertain but have no truth. Al-awwalīn means ancients, earlier peoples. Al-Ṭabarī explains that Makkan deniers called Qur'an asāṭīr because it contains stories of previous nations like Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, and they claimed these were merely old tales copied, not revelation. The same accusation appears in 6:25, 8:31, 23:83, 25:5, 27:68, 68:15, 83:13. It is a way to dismiss without engaging: label it as old stories, not relevant, not divine.

**Why They Call Revelation Fables**

Calling revelation fables serves two purposes for deniers. First, it avoids having to argue against its content. If Qur'an is from Allah, then its warnings about Hereafter and its calls to abandon idols must be taken seriously. If it is merely fables, it can be ignored as entertainment. Second, it protects social status. To admit that what Muhammad ﷺ recites is from your Lord would require changing worship, economy, and tribal customs. To call it fables allows continuation of existing ways while appearing to have a reason. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that asāṭīru al-awwalīn implies that these stories are not only old but also false, invented by ancients and now retold. The accusation is therefore that Prophet ﷺ is not a messenger but a storyteller who learned old tales from others, as 25:5 says: *And they say, fables of ancients which he has written down, and they are dictated to him morning and evening* (25:5). The sūrah will answer this accusation later by pointing to linguistic inimitability and by noting that the Prophet ﷺ was unlettered and had no human teacher for such stories.

**Fables Accusation And The Theme Of Knowledge**

The accusation asāṭīru al-awwalīn is ironic because the stories in Qur'an are presented as true accounts with lessons, not fables for amusement. The Qur'an says *In their stories is a lesson for those of understanding; it is not a fabricated tale* (12:111). By calling them fables, deniers show they do not reflect on purpose of stories. The stories of destroyed peoples are warnings, not entertainment. The sūrah opened with command at hand and said do not hasten it, then listed blessings as signs, then argued idols are dead. Stories of ancients who denied and were destroyed fit this pattern: they are evidence that command does come. To call them fables is to refuse to be mindful, yadhakkarūn, and to refuse to understand, yaʿqilūn. The accusation therefore is not literary criticism but moral evasion.

**How Believers Should Respond To Such Dismissal**

The Qur'an teaches believers not to respond to fables accusation with anger alone but with clarity and patience. The sūrah later says *Call to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good urging* (16:125). When someone says revelation is fables, the believer is to show that it is not: it contains guidance, law, mercy, knowledge of unseen, linguistic miracle, and consistent message of tawḥīd that fables do not contain. The believer also recognizes that label fables often comes from lack of reflection on blessings. A heart that has not reflected on cattle, rain, and stars as signs will also not reflect on stories of past peoples as lessons. The remedy is same: reflection, remembrance, humility. The verse therefore invites believers to be patient with deniers while remaining firm that revelation is from their Lord, not fables.

## Verse 16:25

> Let them bear their burdens in full on the Day of Judgment as well as some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge. Evil indeed is what they will bear

**Bearing Burdens Fully And Partially**

Li-yaḥmilū awzārahum kāmilatan yawma al-qiyāmati wa-min awzāri al-ladhīna yuḍillūnahum bi-ghayri ʿilm — so that they may bear their burdens in full on Day of Resurrection and some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge. Awzār is plural of wizr, heavy burden, sin, load that weighs down. Kāmilatan means complete, full, without reduction. Yawma al-qiyāmah is Day of Resurrection, when all stand. The verse says those who called revelation fables will bear their own sins fully, plus some of sins of those they misled without knowledge. Al-Ṭabarī explains that leaders of disbelief who called people to shirk and to calling Qur'an fables will bear their own burden of disbelief plus a share of burden of followers who followed them in ignorance. This does not mean followers' burden is reduced; followers bear their own full burden as well, because they chose to follow. Rather, misleaders bear additional burden for being cause of others' misguidance. The same principle appears in 29:13: *And they will surely bear their burdens and other burdens with their burdens* (29:13) and in prophetic saying: *Whoever calls to guidance has reward like those who follow, and whoever calls to misguidance bears sin like those who follow, without reducing their sins* (Muslim 2674).

**Without Knowledge**

Bi-ghayri ʿilm — without knowledge — describes both misleaders and misled. Misleaders mislead without knowledge, meaning their claim that revelation is fables is based on ignorance, not knowledge. They do not know that it is revelation; they assert without evidence. Those misled are also without knowledge, meaning they follow without knowledge, blind imitation. Al-Rāzī notes that bi-ghayri ʿilm is crucial: if misguidance were with knowledge, it would be even worse, but even without knowledge, responsibility remains because knowledge was available via signs, blessings, and messengers. The phrase also indicates that misleaders deceive others by presenting ignorance as knowledge: they say fables with confidence, as if they know, while they do not know. Their burden includes the sin of speaking without knowledge, which the Qur'an condemns in 16:116: *Do not say what your tongues falsely describe, this is lawful and this is forbidden, inventing lie against Allah* (16:116).

**The Connection To Fables Accusation**

Verse 24 said they call revelation ancient fables. Verse 25 says consequence: they will bear burdens fully and some of those they mislead without knowledge. The link is that calling revelation fables is not a private opinion; it is public misguidance that leads others astray. When a respected Makkan says Qur'an is fables, others who trust him may also dismiss it without investigating. so his words become cause for others' denial. The verse warns that speech has consequences beyond speaker. To label revelation as fable may seem like a small remark, but on Day of Judgment it will be a heavy load, plus loads of those who were influenced. This is why the Qur'an takes speech seriously: *Not a word does he utter but there is a watcher ready* (50:18). Wizr from w-z-r means heavy burden, sin, from same root as wazīr, minister, one who bears burden of king, and wizr, mountain refuge, because it bears weight. Ḥaml from ḥ-m-l means to carry, bear, as in cattle bearing loads in 16:7. Kāmilatan, complete, from k-m-l, to be complete, indicates full, not partial, bearing.

## Verse 16:26

> Indeed, those before them had plotted, but Allah struck at the ˹very˺ foundation of their structure, so the roof collapsed on top of them, and the torment came upon them from where they did not expect

**Plotting That Seems Secure**

Qad makara al-ladhīna min qablihim — indeed those before them had plotted. Makara means to plot, scheme, plan secretly with cunning, often to deceive or harm. Al-ladhīna min qablihim refers to previous nations who plotted against their messengers, like people of Nūḥ, ʿĀd, Thamūd, Pharaoh. Their plotting included mocking messengers, calling revelation fables, building structures of power to oppose truth. Al-Ṭabarī explains that makr here includes both ideological plotting, like saying revelation is fables, and physical plotting, like building strongholds to resist. The verse says qad, indeed, emphasizing that this is not hypothetical; it happened. Those before them plotted, just as Makkans plot now. The past is precedent. The sūrah uses past to warn present: you are not first to plot, and you will not be first to escape consequence.

**A Warning To Makkans And All Plotters**

The verse is addressed to Makkans who plotted against Prophet ﷺ, who met in Dār al-Nadwah to decide to imprison, kill, or expel him, as 8:30 says: *And when those who disbelieved plotted against you to restrain you or kill you or expel you* (8:30). Their plotting was secret, but Allah knew, as verse 19 and 23 said He knows what they conceal. The verse says those before you plotted and their building collapsed; yours can too. Al-Qurṭubī notes that this is both warning and consolation: warning to plotters that their foundations are vulnerable, consolation to believers that plotting against truth ultimately fails. The verse's placement after discussion of bearing burdens of those misled shows that plotters not only bear own burdens but also cause collapse of those who follow them. Their bunyān includes followers who trusted their roof.

## Verse 16:27

> Then on the Day of Judgment He will humiliate them and say, “Where are My ˹so-called˺ associate-gods for whose sake you used to oppose ˹the believers˺?” Those gifted with knowledge will say, “Surely disgrace and misery today are upon the disbelievers.”

**Humiliation After Plotting**

Thumma yawma al-qiyāmati yukhzīhim — then on Day of Resurrection He will humiliate them, disgrace them. Yukhzīhim from kh-z-y means to humiliate, disgrace, expose shame. After describing worldly collapse of their building in verse 26, this verse moves to otherworldly humiliation. Thumma indicates sequence: first worldly punishment from unexpected direction, then Resurrection Day humiliation. Al-Ṭabarī explains yukhzīhim as exposing their falsehood and making their disgrace visible to all. In world, plotters may appear powerful, respected; on Day, their plotting is exposed and they are humiliated. The verse shows that divine justice has two stages: immediate and ultimate. Worldly collapse may be seen by some, but Resurrection humiliation will be seen by all. This is both warning and consolation: oppressors may seem to escape in world, but they will not escape humiliation on Day.

**The Connection To Arrogance**

Verse 27 mentions humiliation, khizy, which is opposite of arrogance, istikbār, mentioned in verses 22-23, 29. Arrogant sought greatness in world by opposing believers for sake of idols; on Day they are humiliated. The Qur'an often pairs arrogance in world with humiliation in Hereafter, as in 40:76: *Enter gates of Hell to abide in it; evil indeed is abode of arrogant* (40:76). The question *Where are My partners?* also humiliates because it exposes that what they were arrogant for is nothing. They were proud of idols, proud of opposing believers, proud of their plotting. Now idols absent, opposition failed, plotting collapsed.

Those given knowledge in world become witnesses in Hereafter because knowledge entails responsibility to convey. The Qur'an says *so We have made you a middle community that you may be witnesses over people* (2:143).

## Verse 16:28

> Those whose souls the angels seize while they wrong themselves will then offer ˹full˺ submission ˹and say falsely,˺ “We did not do any evil.” ˹The angels will say,˺ “No! Surely Allah fully knows what you used to do

**Souls Seized While Wronging Themselves**

Al-ladhīna tatawaffāhum al-malā'ikatu ẓālimī anfusihim — those whom angels take in death while they are wronging themselves. Tatawaffā from w-f-y means to take fully, to cause to die, to take soul. Al-malā'ikah, angels, are agents of death, as in 32:11: *Say, the angel of death who is entrusted with you will take you* (32:11). Ẓālimī anfusihim means wronging themselves, wrongdoers to themselves, because sin harms self first. Al-Ṭabarī explains that wronging self here means persisting in shirk, denial, and opposition to messengers, by this harming own souls that will face punishment. The phrase indicates that at moment of death, they are still in state of wrongdoing, not having repented. Angels come to take souls while they are in that state, making death itself a moment of exposure. The same phrase appears in 4:97 for those who wronged themselves by staying among disbelievers without emigrating when able. Fa-alqaw al-salama — then they will offer submission, peace, surrender. The Qur'an describes similar deathbed claims in 6:23: *By Allah, our Lord, we were not polytheists* (6:23), and in 63:10: *My Lord, if only You would delay me for brief term so I would give charity* (63:10). All are too late.

**Why This Scene Is Placed Here**

After describing worldly collapse of plotters and Resurrection humiliation, the sūrah now describes death scene, which is intermediate between world and Resurrection. It shows that humiliation begins at death, not only on Day. Those who plotted and called revelation fables will at death try to surrender and deny, but angels will reject denial. This prepares for verse 29: *Enter gates of Hell to abide in it*. The sequence is: plotting in world, building collapse, death with false submission, angels' rebuttal, entry to Hell, then contrast with righteous. The scene also teaches that submission must be in world before death, not at death when seen. The Qur'an says *But repentance is not for those who do evil until when death comes to one of them, he says, indeed I have repented now* (4:18). True submission is before seeing angels.

**Why False Claim Of No Evil Fails**

Claiming *we did not do any evil* fails for three reasons. First, Allah knows, as verse says. Second, angels have recorded deeds, as 82:10-12 says *Indeed over you are keepers, noble writers who know what you do* (82:10-12). Third, limbs will testify, as 36:65 says *Today We seal their mouths and their hands speak to Us and their feet bear witness to what they used to earn* (36:65). The false claim therefore is futile. It also reveals self-deception: they truly believed their deeds were not evil because their hearts were in denial and arrogant, as verse 22 said. Their denial at death is continuation of denial in life. The angels' response *No, Allah knows* breaks self-deception and establishes truth before punishment.

**A Lesson On Sincerity Before Death**

The verse teaches that sincerity must be before death. Once angels arrive, claims of innocence are not accepted. True repentance is in world, when unseen, not at death when seen. The Qur'an says *Until when death comes to one of them, he says My Lord send me back, that I might do righteousness in what I left behind. No, it is a word he speaks* (23:99-100). The time for doing good is now, while soul is still in body and choice remains. The verse therefore urges listener not to delay repentance, not to be among those who wrong themselves and then offer false submission at last moment. The door of repentance is open now, as verse 18 said Allah is Forgiving, Merciful, but it closes at death.

## Verse 16:29

> So enter the gates of Hell, to stay there forever. Indeed, what an evil home for the arrogant!”

**Command To Enter Hell**

Fa-udkhulū abwāba jahannama khālidīna fīhā — so enter gates of Hell to abide in it forever. The command udkhulū, enter, is said to those whose souls were taken while wronging themselves and who falsely claimed innocence. Abwāba jahannama, gates of Hell, indicates Hell has gates, doors, levels, as in 15:44: *It has seven gates, for each gate is a portion assigned* (15:44). Entering gates implies being ushered into punishment, not merely seeing it. Khālidīna fīhā means abiding in it forever, remaining permanently. Al-Ṭabarī explains khālidīna as remaining without end for those who died in shirk and denial. The command is given by angels after rebutting false claim of innocence. It shows that after death, there is no chance to argue; judgment is executed. The phrase fa-udkhulū, so enter, indicates consequence: because you wronged yourselves, denied, claimed no evil, and Allah knows what you did, therefore enter.

**What An Evil Home For The Arrogant**

Fa-la-bi'sa mathwā al-mutakabbirīn — indeed what an evil abode, dwelling, home for the arrogant. Bi'sa is exclamation of dispraise: how evil, how wretched. Mathwā from th-w-y means abode, dwelling, place where one stays, from same root as thāwī, resident. Al-mutakabbirīn, arrogant, those who sought greatness, refused truth. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that mathwā indicates place of residence, home, implying that Hell will be their home, where they stay, not temporary visit. The phrase la-bi'sa mathwā al-mutakabbirīn is used elsewhere: *So enter gates of Hell to abide in it; evil indeed is abode of arrogant* (16:29; 39:72; 40:76). Repetition shows that arrogance is specifically linked to Hell as its home. Why? Because arrogant refused to be servants in world, claimed greatness, so their fitting home is abode of humiliation.

**Why Arrogance Leads To Hell**

Arrogance, istikbār and takabbur, is repeatedly linked to Hell because it prevents acceptance of truth. Now consequence: evil home for arrogant. The Qur'an says *But those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell humiliated* (40:60).

**The Contrast With Righteous Home**

Verse 30-31 will contrast this evil home with excellent home of righteous: *How excellent indeed is home of righteous, Gardens of Eternity* (16:30-31). The pairing of evil home vs excellent home is deliberate: two homes, two groups, two answers to question what has your Lord revealed. Those who said ancient fables get evil home; those who said all best get excellent home. The sūrah uses mathwā for arrogant and dār for righteous? Actually verse 30 says *excellent is abode of righteous*, using mathwā? Let's check: verse 30 says *How excellent indeed is home of righteous* — same word mathwā? In Arabic, verse 30 says *wa-la-niʿma dāru al-muttaqīn*, excellent is abode of righteous, using dār, while verse 29 uses mathwā. Both mean home, but dār is more honorable. The contrast is clear: arrogant get wretched mathwā, righteous get excellent dār.

**Why Gates Are Mentioned**

Gates suggest that Hell is enclosed, with entry points that are controlled. No one enters without permission, and no one exits without permission. The image of gates also suggests that Hell has already been prepared, its gates exist now, waiting. The Qur'an says *Indeed Hell has been lying in wait* (78:21). The command to enter gates therefore is not creation of new punishment but ushering into already prepared abode. Al-Rāzī notes that mentioning gates also indicates that Hell has different levels of punishment appropriate to different sins, and each gate leads to different level. The arrogant who opposed believers for sake of idols will enter through gate appropriate to arrogance and shirk. The mention of gates also contrasts with gates of Paradise that will be opened for righteous, as 39:73 says *Until when they reach it, its gates are opened and its keepers say Peace be upon you* (39:73). Two sets of gates, two greetings, two homes.

The Qur'an says *And those who disbelieved will be driven to Hell in groups until when they reach it, its gates are opened* (39:71).

## Verse 16:30

> And ˹when˺ it is said to those mindful ˹of Allah˺, “What has your Lord revealed?” They say, “All the best!” For those who do good in this world, there is goodness. But far better is the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter. How excellent indeed is the home of the righteous

**A Contrasting Answer To Same Question**

Wa-idhā qīla li-l-ladhīna ittaqaw mādhā anzala rabbukum qālū khayran — and when it is said to those who are mindful of Allah, what has your Lord revealed, they say, all the best, goodness. This mirrors verse 24 where same question was asked to deniers and they said ancient fables. Now question asked to muttaqīn, those mindful, reverent, God-conscious, and they say khayran, goodness, best. Khayr means good, best, beneficial. Al-Ṭabarī explains that muttaqīn answer with khayran because they see revelation as good, containing guidance, mercy, law, stories that teach, promises that encourage. Their answer is short but comprehensive: all the best. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that khayran is indefinite, indicating great goodness, all kinds of goodness, not limited. The contrast between fables and goodness reveals hearts: deniers see revelation as old tales to dismiss, mindful see it as best to follow. Same revelation, opposite perceptions, due to difference in taqwa.

**Far Better Is Home Of Hereafter**

Wa-la-dāru al-ākhirati khayrun — but home of Hereafter is better. Lā here is emphatic, surely, indeed. Dāru al-ākhirah, home of Hereafter, is better than goodness in world. Al-Qurṭubī explains that even if worldly goodness is given to righteous, Hereafter is better in quality, quantity, and duration. Worldly goodness is mixed with hardship, temporary, limited; Hereafter goodness is pure, eternal, unlimited.

**Why The Home Is Called Excellent**

Niʿma dāru al-muttaqīn — how excellent is home of righteous — is praise that invites desire. The Qur'an often uses niʿma to praise Paradise, as in 3:136 and 29:58. The home is excellent because it is free from hardship, pure, eternal, and contains whatever righteous desire, as next verse says: *they will have whatever they desire* (16:31). Al-Qurṭubī explains that dār here is excellent because its Builder is Allah, its inhabitants are righteous, its duration is forever, and its contents are beyond imagination. The colon at end of translation indicates that description follows, linking this verse to next. The listener who hears excellent home will naturally ask, what is that home? Next verse answers: Gardens of Eternity under which rivers flow.

The comparison wa-la-dāru al-ākhirati khayrun is similar to 87:17: *But Hereafter is better and more lasting* (87:17).

## Verse 16:31

> the Gardens of Eternity which they will enter, under which rivers flow. In it they will have whatever they desire. This is how Allah rewards the righteous—

**Whatever They Desire**

Lahum fīhā mā yashā'ūn — in it they will have whatever they desire, whatever they wish. Mā yashā'ūn means whatever they will, desire, want. This is a comprehensive promise: in Paradise, desires are fulfilled without restriction, unlike world where desires often exceed ability or are prohibited. Al-Qurṭubī notes that this includes both what they desired in world and what they will desire in Paradise, and that their desires themselves will be purified, so they will not desire anything harmful. The phrase appears in several descriptions of Paradise, as in 41:31: *You will have in it whatever your souls desire* (41:31). Al-Rāzī explains that having whatever one desires is the ultimate freedom, because in world humans are constrained by need, law, and inability, while in Paradise constraints are removed for righteous, yet their will is aligned with good, so they desire only good.

**Why Eternity Matters**

ʿAdn, eternity, is stressed because worldly gardens are temporary: they wither, need maintenance, can be lost. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that ʿadn also implies residence that is settled, not traveling, so inhabitants feel at home, not as visitors. This contrasts with worldly life which is travel, as Prophet ﷺ said *Be in world as stranger or traveler* (Bukhārī 6416). In world, even excellent home is temporary; in Hereafter, excellent home is eternal.

**The Connection To Earlier Blessings**

Gardens with rivers flowing recall earlier blessings: rain from sky, rivers on earth, crops, fruits. In world, those blessings are signs and temporary provision. In Paradise, they become permanent reward, perfected. Rivers in world can flood or dry; rivers in Paradise flow continuously without harm. Fruits in world are seasonal; fruits in Paradise are always available, as 13:35 says *Its fruit is permanent and its shade* (13:35). Whatever they desire in Paradise includes best of what they had in world, purified and increased. The sūrah so moves from worldly blessings that are uncountable but temporary to otherworldly blessings that are eternal, showing that gratitude for worldly blessings leads to eternal reward.

**Why Rivers Flowing Is Repeated**

The phrase under which rivers flow appears dozens of times in Qur'an as anchor of imagination. Water in desert is life, coolness, beauty, and its flow indicates continuity. A garden with river that stops would disappoint; a garden where rivers flow continuously is secure. Al-Rāzī notes that rivers flowing beneath indicates inhabitants are above rivers, in elevated position, looking down at flowing water, a scene of honor. The rivers are of different types: water, milk, honey, wine that does not intoxicate, as 47:15 says. The diversity matches diversity of desires. Repetition is not filler but reminder that Paradise provision is flowing, not stagnant, always fresh.

**Desire Fulfilled As Ultimate Freedom**

Having whatever they desire is ultimate freedom because in world desires are constrained by inability, prohibition, and consequence. In Paradise, constraints are removed for righteous whose desires have been purified by taqwa. Al-Qurṭubī explains that they will not desire harmful because hearts purified, as 15:47 says. So fulfillment does not lead to excess; it leads to contentment. The phrase mā yashā'ūn indicates desires granted as they arise, without delay, without work. This is opposite of world where desire requires effort and may not be fulfilled. The promise is both about provision and will: your will, frustrated in world, will be fulfilled in Paradise as reward for aligning will with Allah's will through taqwa.

## Verse 16:32

> those whose souls the angels take while they are virtuous, saying ˹to them˺, “Peace be upon you! Enter Paradise for what you used to do.”

**Souls Taken While Virtuous**

Al-ladhīna tatawaffāhum al-malā'ikatu ṭayyibīn — those whose souls angels take while they are virtuous, good, pure. Tatawaffāhum al-malā'ikatu, angels take them, same verb as in verse 28 for wrongdoers, but state differs: ẓālimī anfusihim vs ṭayyibīn. Ṭayyibīn means good, pure, virtuous, from ṭ-y-b, to be good, pure, pleasant. Al-Ṭabarī explains ṭayyibīn as those who are pure from shirk, sincere in faith, good in deeds, or those whose souls are taken in easy, gentle manner, without hardship. The contrast with verse 28 is deliberate: same angels, same act of taking souls, but different states and different greetings.

**Peace Be Upon You**

Yaqūlūna salāmun ʿalaykum — saying, peace be upon you. Angels say salām, peace, security, greeting. Salāmun ʿalaykum is greeting of Paradise, also greeting among believers in world, and greeting from Allah, as 36:58 says *Peace, a word from a Merciful Lord* (36:58). Al-Qurṭubī says angels greet righteous at death with peace to calm fear, give glad tidings, and convey that they are safe from punishment. The greeting is in present tense, indicating ongoing peace, not temporary. It also indicates that angels are not harsh to righteous; they are gentle, welcoming. The same greeting will be heard in Paradise from angels and inhabitants, as 13:24 says *Peace be upon you for what you patiently endured* (13:24). The death greeting is foretaste of Paradise greeting.

**Enter Paradise For What You Used To Do**

Udkhulū al-jannata bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn — enter Paradise for what you used to do, because of what you used to do. Bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn indicates cause: because of your deeds, your faith, your patience, your good works. Al-Ṭabarī explains that entry to Paradise is by Allah's mercy, but deeds are cause and evidence of sincerity, and reward is according to deeds. The phrase does not mean Paradise is earned solely by deeds without mercy; it means deeds are sign of faith that Allah rewards. Al-Rāzī notes that bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn includes both inward and outward deeds: belief, gratitude, patience, trust, as verses 41-42 will mention for emigrants. The same phrase appears in other descriptions of Paradise entry, as in 16:32 and 52:19. It shows that Paradise is not arbitrary; it is for those who did what was required.

**The Meaning Of Ṭayyibīn**

Ṭayyibīn can mean pure from shirk, pure in speech and action, or those whose death is easy, without hardship. Al-Ṭabarī reports both interpretations and says they are compatible: those who are pure in faith have easy death. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that ṭayyib is opposite of khabīth, foul, impure, used for shirk and evil deeds. The righteous are ṭayyib because they avoided khabīth of shirk and ingratitude. Their souls are ṭayyibah, good, so angels take them in ṭayyib manner, with peace. The word also suggests that their lives were ṭayyibah, good life, as 16:97 says *We will surely give them good life* (16:97). Good life leads to good death leads to good home.

## Verse 16:33

> Are they only awaiting the coming of the angels or the command of your Lord ˹O Prophet˺? So were those before them. And Allah never wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves

**What Are They Waiting For?**

Hal yanẓurūna illā a ta'tiyahum al-malā'ikatu aw ya'tiya amru rabbika — are they only waiting for angels to come or command of your Lord to come? Hal is interrogative, are they, do they? Yanẓurūna from n-ẓ-r means to wait, await, expect. Illā a ta'tiyahum al-malā'ikatu, except that angels come to them, meaning angels of death to take souls, or angels of punishment, or angels for reckoning. Aw ya'tiya amru rabbika, or command of your Lord comes, meaning punishment in world or Hour in Hereafter. The question is rhetorical, criticizing delay in believing until undeniable signs come. Al-Ṭabarī explains that Makkan deniers were waiting for angels to come with punishment or for Allah's command of destruction or Resurrection to come before believing, but by then belief will not benefit. The same question appears in 6:158: *Do they await except that angels should come to them or your Lord should come or some signs of your Lord should come?* (6:158). The answer is that waiting until then is foolish, because then it is too late.

**Allah Did Not Wrong Them, They Wronged Themselves**

Wa-mā ẓalamahum Allāhu wa-lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn — and Allah did not wrong them, but they wronged themselves. Ẓalama means to wrong, to oppress, to put thing in wrong place. Allah did not wrong them by punishing without reason or by not giving signs; He gave blessings, sent messengers, showed straight way, gave time. They wronged themselves by choosing denial, arrogance, calling revelation fables, plotting, opposing believers. Al-Qurṭubī explains that self-wronging includes harming own souls by committing deeds that lead to punishment, and also includes putting worship in wrong place by giving it to idols. The phrase appears many times in Qur'an, as in 16:118 and 29:40, to emphasize divine justice and human responsibility. It answers hidden objection: if Allah could have guided all, as verse 9 said, why does He punish those not guided? Answer: He did not wrong them; they wronged themselves by refusing guidance shown.

## Verse 16:34

> Then the evil ˹consequences˺ of their deeds overtook them, and they were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule

**Evil Consequences Overtaking**

Fa-aṣābahum sayyi'ātu mā ʿamilū — then evil consequences of what they did overtook them, befell them. Aṣābahum from ṣ-w-b means to hit, befall, overtake. Sayyi'āt means evil deeds and their evil consequences, plural of sayyi'ah, evil, bad. Mā ʿamilū, what they did, what they used to do. Al-Ṭabarī explains that sayyi'āt here means punishment for evil deeds, evil outcome that results from deeds. Their deeds were shirk, denial, calling revelation fables, plotting, opposing believers. The evil consequences are punishment in world and Hereafter that results. Fa- indicates consequence: because they wronged themselves and waited for angels or command, then evil consequences overtook them. The verb aṣābahum suggests that consequences are not random but targeted, hitting them as arrow hits target. Their deeds created consequences that then caught up with them, as 30:36 says *If evil befalls them for what their hands sent forth, they despair* (30:36). Wa-ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahzi'ūn — and they were overwhelmed, surrounded, encompassed by what they used to ridicule. Then punishment they ridiculed surrounded them, as flood surrounded mockers of Nūḥ, wind surrounded mockers of Hūd, etc. What they ridiculed becomes what overwhelms them, a fitting recompense: you mocked punishment, now punishment mocks you by surrounding you from where you did not expect, as verse 26 said torment came from where they did not perceive.

**The Pattern Of Ridicule And Overwhelming**

The Qur'an often pairs ridicule of punishment with being overwhelmed by it, as in 6:10: *And indeed messengers before you were ridiculed, but those who mocked them were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule* (6:10) and 21:41 and 46:26. The pattern is consistent: deniers ridicule warning, warning becomes reality that surrounds them. In al-Naḥl, ridicule took form of calling revelation ancient fables (16:24) and demanding hastening of command (16:1). The verse says that very thing ridiculed — command, punishment, Hour — will surround them. The ridicule does not prevent reality; it ensures that when reality comes, it comes as overwhelming, not as gentle reminder. Al-Rāzī explains that ḥāqa bihim indicates that punishment they thought would never come actually encompasses them from all sides, leaving no escape, as 29:54 says *Indeed Hell will encompass disbelievers* (29:54).

**A Lesson On Speech And Consequence**

The verse highlights that ridicule, yastahzi'ūn, is not harmless speech. It is deed with consequence. To ridicule revelation, messengers, or punishment is to put oneself in position where what is ridiculed will overwhelm. The Qur'an warns against mocking: *O you who believe, let not a people ridicule another people, perhaps they may be better* (49:11). If mocking people is prohibited, mocking Allah's signs is worse. The verse teaches that speech should be guarded, because what is said in mockery may become reality that surrounds speaker. The Makkans who said *bring punishment if truthful* were in fact calling punishment upon themselves, as 8:32 shows.

**How Consequences Catch Up**

Consequences catching up is law of moral world as gravity is law of physical world. A person may do evil and feel nothing immediate, thinking he escaped. But deeds create consequences that travel, like arrow shot, and eventually hit. The Qur'an says *And whatever affliction befalls you is for what your hands earned* (42:30). Each has consequence: shirk leads to Hell, denial leads to humiliation, calling fables leads to bearing burdens of misled, plotting leads to building collapse, opposing believers leads to disgrace. The verse says these consequences overtook them, meaning they could not outrun them. The Makkans who plotted in Dār al-Nadwah thought they were securing future; their plotting's evil consequences overtook them at Badr and beyond.

## Verse 16:35

> The polytheists argue, “Had Allah willed, neither we nor our forefathers would have worshipped anything other than Him, nor prohibited anything without His command.” So did those before them. Is not the messengers’ duty only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly

**Using Divine Will As Excuse For Shirk**

Wa-qāla al-ladhīna ashrakū law shā'a Allāhu mā ʿabadnā min dūnihi min shay'in naḥnu wa-lā ābā'unā wa-lā ḥarramnā min dūnihi min shay' — and those who associate partners said, had Allah willed, we would not have worshipped anything besides Him, neither we nor our fathers, nor would we have prohibited anything besides Him. This is argument of polytheists who claim that since Allah allowed them to worship idols and prohibit certain animals as ḥarām, therefore Allah wills and approves their shirk and prohibitions. Al-Ṭabarī explains that they argued: if Allah did not like our worship of idols and our prohibiting cattle, He would have prevented us, so since He did not prevent, He approves. This is misuse of divine will to justify disobedience. Al-Rāzī notes that they confused divine will that allows human choice with divine approval. Allah wills to allow humans to choose, but does not approve shirk. The same argument appears in 6:148: *Those who associate will say, had Allah willed, we would not have associated nor would our fathers, nor would we have prohibited anything* (6:148). The Qur'an answers that this argument was used by those before them and is invalid.

**Our Forefathers And Prohibitions**

Naḥnu wa-lā ābā'unā, we nor our forefathers, indicates that they appealed to tradition: our fathers did shirk, so it must be acceptable, and if Allah willed otherwise, He would have stopped our fathers. Wa-lā ḥarramnā min dūnihi min shay', nor would we have prohibited anything besides Him, refers to their practice of prohibiting certain cattle as ḥarām for idols, as 6:139-144 describes: they prohibited certain camels, cattle, saying this is for idols. They claimed that since Allah allowed them to prohibit, He approves prohibition. Al-Qurṭubī explains that they made unlawful what Allah made lawful, like certain cattle. Claimed it was by Allah's command, when in fact it was by their own invention, as 16:116 will say *Do not say what your tongues falsely describe, this is lawful and this is forbidden, inventing lie against Allah* (16:116). Their argument uses divine will to justify both shirk in worship and shirk in legislation.

**Why This Argument Is Invalid**

The argument *had Allah willed we would not have worshipped others* is invalid for several reasons. Allah allows humans to choose disbelief, but does not approve it, as 39:7 says *If you disbelieve, indeed Allah is free from need of you, and He does not approve disbelief for His servants* (39:7). Second, if argument were valid, it would justify all sins: had Allah willed, we would not have stolen, killed, etc., which would eliminate moral responsibility. Third, Allah has willed to show straight way and to send messengers to clarify, so excuse that He did not will guidance is false: He did will to show way, as verse 9 said *upon Allah is to show straight way*.

## Verse 16:36

> We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, “Worship Allah and shun false gods.” But some of them were guided by Allah, while others were destined to stray. So travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers

**A Messenger To Every Community**

Wa-laqad baʿathnā fī kulli ummatin rasūlan — and We surely sent in every community a messenger. Laqad stresses certainty, indeed, surely. Baʿathnā from b-ʿ-th means to send, raise, resurrect, used for sending messengers and for resurrection. Fī kulli ummatin, in every community, nation, people, indicates universality of messengership. No community left without warner, as 35:24 says *There is no community but that a warner has passed among them* (35:24). Al-Ṭabarī explains that ummah here means people, community, generation that shares time and place. Every ummah had messenger to establish proof. The message was same across all: ani iʿbudū Allāha wa-ijtanibū al-ṭāghūt — worship Allah and shun false gods, avoid ṭāghūt. Ṭāghūt from ṭ-gh-y means to exceed bounds, to transgress, and refers to anything worshipped besides Allah that exceeds limits, including idols, tyrants, devils. The core call is two parts: affirmation, worship Allah, and negation, shun ṭāghūt, exactly tawḥīd.

**Worship Allah And Shun False Gods**

Iʿbudū from ʿ-b-d means to worship, serve, be servant. Ijtanibū from j-n-b means to avoid, shun, keep away from side, indicating active avoidance, not passive. Al-Ṭabarī says ṭāghūt includes idols, devils, and any object of worship besides Allah. Al-Rāzī explains that worshipping Allah and shunning ṭāghūt are two sides of same coin: you cannot worship Allah truly while also worshipping ṭāghūt; you must do both. The phrase ani iʿbudū Allāha wa-ijtanibū al-ṭāghūt is summary of all messengers' message, from Nūḥ to Muḥammad ﷺ, as 21:25 says *We did not send any messenger before you except We revealed to him there is no god but Me, so worship Me* (21:25). The universality shows that tawḥīd is not new or particular to Arabs; it is original message to all peoples, and shirk is deviation.

**Some Guided, Some Destined To Stray**

Fa-minhum man hadā Allāhu wa-minhum man ḥaqqat ʿalayhi al-ḍalālah — but among them are those whom Allah guided and among them are those upon whom straying was justified, destined. Fa-minhum, among them, from communities. Man hadā Allāhu, whom Allah guided, meaning those who accepted message, reflected on blessings, humbled themselves. Man ḥaqqat ʿalayhi al-ḍalālah, upon whom straying was justified, meaning those who refused, were arrogant, called revelation fables, so misguidance became deserved. Al-Ṭabarī explains ḥaqqat ʿalayhi al-ḍalālah as misguidance became due, established, because they chose denial. Al-Rāzī notes that this does not mean Allah forced some to stray arbitrarily; it means guidance was shown to all via messengers, but some accepted and were guided further, while others refused and straying became deserved. The same idea appears in 2:26: *He misguides many and so and guides many and so, and He misguides not except the defiantly disobedient* (2:26). The verse preserves both divine will and human choice.

**Travel And See Fate Of Deniers**

Fa-sīrū fī al-arḍi fa-unẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mukadhdhibīn — so travel through land and see how was end of deniers. Sīrū from s-y-r means to travel, journey. Fī al-arḍi, through land, earth. Unẓurū from n-ẓ-r means to look, see, consider. Kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mukadhdhibīn, how was end, outcome, of those who denied. Al-Ṭabarī says this is command to consider ruins of destroyed nations as evidence: travel and see traces of ʿĀd, Thamūd, Pharaoh, etc., and see that their end was destruction because they denied messengers. Al-Qurṭubī notes that travel here can be physical travel to see ruins, or travel of reflection through history via stories. The purpose is to learn: denial leads to bad end, as verses 26-34 showed. The command to travel appears many times in Qur'an, as in 12:109 and 30:42, to make history a teacher.

## Verse 16:37

> Even though you ˹O Prophet˺ are keen on their guidance, Allah certainly does not guide those He leaves to stray, and they will have no helpers

**Keenness For Guidance**

In taḥriṣ ʿalā hudāhum — even though you are keen, eager, desirous for their guidance. Taḥriṣ from ḥ-r-ṣ means to be keen, eager, to desire strongly, to strive. Hudāhum, their guidance. The verse addresses Prophet ﷺ who was deeply keen for his people to be guided, grieved when they refused, as 18:6 says *Perhaps you would kill yourself with grief over them if they do not believe* (18:6) and 26:3 says *Perhaps you will kill yourself that they are not believers* (26:3). Al-Ṭabarī explains that Prophet ﷺ was eager for Quraysh to accept tawḥīd, to be saved from Hell, to be among those who say goodness when asked what Lord revealed. His keenness was from mercy, as he is described as *merciful to believers* (9:128). The verse acknowledges that keenness while clarifying limits: even with your keenness, guidance is by Allah's will and human choice.

**Allah Does Not Guide Those He Leaves To Stray**

Fa-inna Allāha lā yahdī man yuḍillu — but indeed Allah does not guide those He leaves to stray, whom He lets go astray. Man yuḍillu means whom He leaves to stray, whom He allows to stray as consequence of their choice. Al-Ṭabarī explains that yuḍillu here means those whom Allah has judged to be astray because they chose denial, arrogance, calling revelation fables, using divine will as excuse. Their straying became deserved, as verse 36 said *upon whom straying was justified*. Allah does not guide them further because they refused initial guidance shown via blessings, messengers, and clear delivery. Al-Rāzī notes that lā yahdī here does not mean Allah prevents them from guidance arbitrarily, but that He does not guide them to success because they chose straying. The same idea appears in 16:107: *That is because they preferred worldly life over Hereafter and Allah does not guide disbelieving people* (16:107). Guidance requires willingness.

**They Will Have No Helpers**

Wa-mā lahum min nāṣirīn — and they will have no helpers, supporters, protectors. Nāṣirīn plural of nāṣir, helper, from n-ṣ-r, to help, support. Al-Ṭabarī says they will have no helpers to protect them from Allah's punishment, to intercede, to prevent humiliation described in verse 27, to prevent entry to Hell described in verse 29. Their idols, whom they worshipped for help, will not help, as verse 27's question *Where are My partners?* shows. Their forefathers, whom they invoked as tradition, will not help. Their plotting, building, wealth will not help. The phrase mā lahum min nāṣirīn appears many times for deniers, as in 2:270 and 3:192, to emphasize that on Day, help comes only from Allah, and those who associated others will find those others absent.

## Verse 16:38

> They swear by Allah their most solemn oaths that Allah will never raise the dead to life. Yes ˹He will˺! It is a true promise binding on Him, but most people do not know

**Solemn Oaths Denying Resurrection**

Wa-aqsamū bi-Allāhi jahda aymānihim lā yabʿathu Allāhu man yamūt — and they swear by Allah their most solemn oaths that Allah will never raise the dead. Aqsamū from q-s-m means to swear, oath. Bi-Allāhi, by Allah, indicating they swear by Allah, whom they acknowledge as Creator. Jahda aymānihim, most earnest, strongest, most solemn oaths, from j-h-d, to strive, exert utmost, indicating they exert all effort in oath to emphasize. The same oath appears in 35:42 and 6:109, where deniers swear they will believe if sign comes, but when sign comes they do not. Here they swear opposite: they swear resurrection will not happen. Their oath shows certainty in denial, not doubt. Balā waʿdan ʿalayhi ḥaqqan — Yes, indeed, it is a true promise binding upon Him. Balā is strong affirmation negating previous negative: yes indeed, contrary to what they swear, He will raise dead. Al-Ṭabarī explains that balā affirms resurrection: yes, Allah will raise dead.

**Why Resurrection Is Central To This Sūrah**

Sūrat al-Naḥl opened with command at hand, then listed blessings that prove power to resurrect, then argued idols are dead and do not know when they will be raised, then described two death scenes, then asked are they waiting for angels or command. Now it addresses explicit denial of resurrection via solemn oaths. Resurrection is central because without it, blessings catalogue is merely worldly enjoyment, and warnings are empty. With resurrection, blessings become trust to be accounted for, and warnings become real. Al-Qurṭubī notes that denial of resurrection was main doctrine of Makkan polytheists, who said *When we are bones and crumbled dust, shall we be raised as new creation?* (17:49). The sūrah answers with creation arguments: He created heavens and earth, He created you from drop, He creates what you do not know, He says Be and it is. All prove power to resurrect.

**The Irony Of Swearing By Allah To Deny Allah's Power**

They swear by Allah that Allah will not raise dead. This is ironic because swearing by Allah acknowledges Allah's greatness and power, yet they deny power to resurrect. If Allah is great enough to be sworn by, He is great enough to resurrect. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that jahda aymānihim, utmost effort in oaths, shows they are earnest in denial, not casual. Their earnestness makes their error more serious. The Qur'an often notes that deniers swear by Allah, as in 6:109 and 16:38, to show that they do recognize Allah as deity to swear by, but they misplace worship by associating others. Their oath is therefore self-contradictory: they invoke Allah's name to deny Allah's promise.

Waʿdan ʿalayhi ḥaqqan means it is promise that Allah has made binding upon Himself, true promise that will be fulfilled, as 10:55 says *Indeed Allah's promise is true* (10:55).

## Verse 16:39

> ˹He will do that˺ to make clear to them what they disagreed on, and for the disbelievers to know that they were liars

**Purpose Of Resurrection Is Clarification**

Li-yubayyina lahum al-ladhī yakhtalifūna fīhi — to make clear to them what they disagreed on, what they differed about. Li- indicates purpose: He will raise dead for this purpose, to clarify. Yubayyina from b-y-n means to make clear, manifest, explain, from same root as bayyinah, clear proof, and mubīn, clear. Al-ladhī yakhtalifūna fīhi, what they differed on, disagreed about, includes tawḥīd vs shirk, resurrection vs denial, lawful vs prohibited, truth of revelation vs fables. Al-Ṭabarī explains that resurrection will make clear truth of matters they disputed in world: whether Allah is One, whether resurrection is true, whether revelation is from Allah. In world, disputes remain unresolved because deniers refuse evidence; in Hereafter, truth becomes manifest and dispute ends. The same purpose appears in 16:64: *We sent down Book to you only to make clear to them what they differed about* (16:64), showing that both revelation in world and resurrection in Hereafter serve clarification.

**For Disbelievers To Know They Were Liars**

Wa-li-yaʿlama al-ladhīna kafarū annahum kānū kādhibīn — and for disbelievers to know that they were liars. Li-yaʿlama, so that they may know, from ʿ-l-m, to know. Al-ladhīna kafarū, those who disbelieved, covered truth. Annahum kānū kādhibīn, that they were liars, those who lied. Kādhbīn from k-dh-b means liars, those who called truth lie and told falsehood. Al-Ṭabarī explains that disbelievers will know on Day that they were liars in world when they said Allah has partners, resurrection will not happen, revelation is fables, had Allah willed we would not have worshipped others. Their lies will be exposed. Al-Rāzī notes that li-yaʿlama indicates that knowledge in Hereafter will be certain, not speculative, because they will see punishment and truth with own eyes, as 32:12 says *If you could see when criminals bow heads before Lord, Our Lord, we have seen and heard, so send us back* (32:12). Knowledge that they were liars will be part of humiliation described in verse 27.

## Verse 16:40

> If We ever will something ˹to exist˺, all We say is: “Be!” And it is

**The Command Be And It Is**

Innamā qawlunā li-shay'in idhā aradnāhu a naqūla lahu kun fa-yakūn — our word for a thing when We will it is only to say to it Be and it is. Qawlunā, Our word, speech. Li-shay'in, for a thing, for something. Idhā aradnāhu, when We will it, wish it, intend it. An naqūla lahu kun, that We say to it Be. Fa-yakūn, and it is, becomes. Al-Ṭabarī explains that when Allah wills something to exist, whether resurrection, creation, provision, punishment, He only says to it kun, Be, and it is, without effort, without delay, without intermediary. The phrase innamā, only, stresses ease: Our word is only Be. This is same amr, command, that opened sūrah: command at hand, angels descend with spirit from command, stars subjected by command. Now command is explained: command is kun, Be. Al-Rāzī notes that kun fa-yakūn shows that Allah's will is executed by His word alone, without need for tools, time, or assistance, unlike human creation which requires effort and time.

Verses 38-39 said they swear Allah will not raise dead, but it is true promise to make clear what they differed and for disbelievers to know they were liars. Verse 40 says if We will something, We say Be and it is. The connection is that resurrection, which they think impossible, is easy for Allah: He only says Be and it is. If He could create heavens and earth, create humans from drop, create cattle, rain, crops, sea, mountains, all with kun, then resurrecting dead is even easier, as 30:27 says *It is He who originates creation then repeats it, and that is easier for Him* (30:27). The verse therefore answers denial of resurrection with ease of divine command.

**The Power Of Kun**

Kun, Be, is imperative of kāna, to be, exist. Some commentators discuss whether kun is literal speech or metaphor for immediate execution. Majority say it is literal speech that Allah speaks, but its nature is beyond human comprehension, as 42:51 says *It is not for any human that Allah should speak to him except by revelation or from behind veil or by sending messenger* (42:51).

## Verse 16:41

> As for those who emigrated in ˹the cause of˺ Allah after being persecuted, We will surely bless them with a good home in this world. But the reward of the Hereafter is far better, if only they knew

**Emigration After Persecution**

Wa-al-ladhīna hājarū fī Allāhi min baʿdi mā ẓulimū — as for those who emigrated in cause of Allah after being persecuted, wronged. Hājarū from h-j-r means to emigrate, to leave home, to abandon. Fī Allāhi means in cause of Allah, for sake of Allah, because of faith. Some say verse 41 refers specifically to Abyssinian emigrants, while verse 110 refers to Madinan emigrants. Others say it is general for all who emigrate after being wronged for faith. The persecution included torture, boycott, and social pressure, as experienced by Bilāl, ʿAmmār, and others. The verse acknowledges their wronging and promises recompense.

**A Lesson For All Who Are Wronged**

Though verse refers to specific historical emigration, its principle is general: those who are wronged for sake of Allah and who leave wrongdoing environment for Allah will be blessed with good home in world and greater in Hereafter. Al-Qurṭubī extends it to anyone who leaves sinful environment, abandons bad company, or migrates from place where he cannot practice faith. The promise of good home in world is not necessarily physical house but good life, as 16:97 says We will give them good life. The verse therefore offers hope to anyone feeling persecuted for faith: your sacrifice will be rewarded with good now and far better later, if you knew how great reward is, you would not hesitate.

## Verse 16:42

> ˹It is˺ they who have patiently endured, and in their Lord they put their trust

**Patience And Trust As Two Pillars**

Al-ladhīna ṣabarū wa-ʿalā rabbihim yatawakkalūn — those who patiently endured and in their Lord they put trust. Ṣabarū from ṣ-b-r means to be patient, to endure, to hold firm. Yatawakkalūn from w-k-l means to put trust, rely, depend. The verse describes those who emigrated after being wronged in previous verse: they are characterized by patience and trust. Al-Ṭabarī explains that ṣabarū refers to patience in face of persecution, in emigration, in obedience, and in avoiding disobedience. Yatawakkalūn means they rely on Lord for provision, protection, and reward, not on own strength or on people. The two qualities are paired throughout Qur'an as keys to success: patience and trust, as in 29:59: *Those who are patient and upon their Lord rely* (29:59). The order is significant: patience first, then trust, because patience is needed to endure hardship, and trust is needed to look forward to reward.

**How Patience And Trust Lead To Good Life**

The Qur'an links patience and trust to good life, as 16:97 says *Whoever does righteousness, male or female, while believing, We will surely give them good life* (16:97). Good life includes contentment, guidance, provision, and honor, all part of good home promised in verse 41. Patience and trust cultivate good life because they free heart from anxiety and resentment. Patient person does not despair over past wronging; trusting person does not worry about future provision. Together they allow heart to be present, grateful, and focused on obedience. The verse therefore not only describes emigrants but prescribes path to good life for all: be patient with what befalls, and trust Lord for what is to come.

## Verse 16:43

> We did not send ˹messengers˺ before you ˹O Prophet˺ except mere men inspired by Us. If you ˹polytheists˺ do not know ˹this already˺, then ask those who have knowledge ˹of the Scriptures˺

**Messengers Were Mere Men Inspired**
Wa-mā arsalnā min qablika illā rijālan nūḥī ilayhim — We did not send before you except men We inspired. Mā arsalnā, We did not send, negates that messengers were angels or gods. Min qablika, before you, O Prophet, indicating continuity of messengership. Illā rijālan, except men, human males, not angels, not supernatural beings. Nūḥī ilayhim, We inspired them, revealed to them, from w-ḥ-y, to inspire, reveal secretly. Al-Ṭabarī explains that Makkan polytheists found it strange that messenger is human like them, eating food, walking in markets, as 25:7 says *What is this messenger who eats food and walks in markets?* (25:7). The verse answers that all messengers before you were men, not angels, inspired by Us. This is to make message relatable: a human messenger understands human needs, speaks human language, lives human life. Al-Rāzī notes that being men is wisdom: if messengers were angels, people would say we cannot follow angels because we are not angels; since they are men, following is possible.

**The Human Messenger**
The verse answers the objection that had been raised against every messenger: he is only a man (16:43-44). The Book does not deny it. It says that God sent men with inspiration and with the clear proofs and the Books, and it tells the doubters to ask the people who had studied the earlier scriptures if they did not know. The same answer is given in the Sūrah of the Prophets, in almost the same words (21:7), and the Sūrah of the Cave states the reason a human messenger is the point: a person can only follow what he can approach, and if angels had walked the earth as messengers they would still have been sent as men (17:94-95; 25:7). The Book's answer to the demand for an angel is that the messenger was sent to be followed, and that requires someone a person can stand next to.

## Verse 16:44

> ˹We sent them˺ with clear proofs and divine Books. And We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Reminder, so that you may explain to people what has been revealed for them, and perhaps they will reflect

**Clear Proofs And Divine Books**

Bi-l-bayyināti wa-l-zubur — with clear proofs and divine Books. Bayyināt from b-y-n means clear proofs, evidences, signs that make truth manifest. Zubur from z-b-r means Books, writings, scriptures, from same root as zabūr, Psalms. Al-Ṭabarī explains that previous messengers were sent with bayyināt, clear proofs like miracles, and zubur, Books containing guidance. The phrase bi- indicates they were sent with these as support, so that people cannot claim message was unclear. The previous verse said messengers were men inspired; this verse adds they were not without evidence: they had clear proofs and Books. This answers objection that human messengers have no proof. They did have proof: miracles like staff of Mūsā, and Books like Torah, Gospel, Psalms. The Makkans who demanded proof were told previous messengers had proof, and still many denied, so proof alone does not compel belief; willingness is needed.

**Why Explanation Is Needed**

Qur'an contains concise verses that need elaboration, as well as stories that need context, and rulings that need detail. Without explanation, people might differ on meaning, as they differed before, as verse 39 said resurrection will make clear what they differed on. In world, Prophet's explanation makes clear what they differed on, as verse 64 will say *We revealed Book only to clarify what they differed about* (16:64). Explanation is therefore mercy that prevents dispute. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that li-tubayyina li-l-nāsi indicates that explanation should be in language people understand, with wisdom and good urging as verse 125 will say. The Prophet ﷺ explained via speech, action, and character, making abstract principles concrete.

## Verse 16:45

> Do those who devise evil plots feel secure that Allah will not cause the earth to swallow them? Or that the torment will not come upon them in ways they cannot comprehend

**Feeling Secure While Plotting Evil**

A-fa-amina al-ladhīna makarū al-sayyi'āti a yakhsifa Allāhu bihim al-arḍa — do those who plot evil feel secure that Allah will not cause earth to swallow them? A-fa-amina, do they feel secure, safe, from a-m-n, security, safety. Al-ladhīna makarū al-sayyi'āt, those who plot evil, devise bad plots, from m-k-r, plot, and s-y-ʾ, evil. An yakhsifa Allāhu bihim al-arḍa, that Allah will cause earth to swallow them, from kh-s-f, to swallow, sink, eclipse. Al-Ṭabarī explains that this refers to those who plot against messengers and believers, like Makkans who plotted against Prophet ﷺ in Dār al-Nadwah, feeling secure that no punishment will come. The question a-fa-amina is rhetorical, expecting no, they should not feel secure. The punishment of earth swallowing is mentioned as example of worldly punishment that came to previous nations, like Qārūn who was swallowed, as 28:81 says *So We caused earth to swallow him and his house* (28:81). The verse warns that feeling secure while plotting evil is delusion.

**Feeling Secure As Disease**

Amina, to feel secure, safe, is praised when it is security in Allah, but condemned when it is security from Allah's punishment while persisting in evil. The Qur'an says *Do they feel secure from Allah's plan? None feels secure from Allah's plan except losing people* (7:99). Feeling secure while plotting evil is sign of heedlessness and arrogance. It indicates that person thinks his plotting is clever enough to avoid consequence, or that Allah does not see, or that punishment is distant. The verse asks rhetorical question to break that false security: do you feel secure that earth will not swallow you? The expected answer is no, you should not feel secure. Al-Qurṭubī says feeling secure from Allah's punishment while persisting in sin is major sin, because it shows lack of fear of Allah.

## Verse 16:46

> Or that He will not seize them while they go about ˹their day˺, for then they will have no escape

**No Escape When Seized**

Fa-mā hum bi-muʿjizīn — for then they will have no escape, will not be able to escape, frustrate, evade. Muʿjizīn from ʿ-j-z means to be unable, to escape, to make helpless, from same root as iʿjāz, inimitability. Al-Ṭabarī says fa-mā hum bi-muʿjizīn means they will not be able to escape Allah's punishment, to frustrate His plan, to make Him unable. When Allah seizes, there is no escape. The phrase appears in other warnings, as in 29:22: *You will not be able to escape on earth nor in heaven* (29:22). The verse stresses that feeling secure while going about is delusion because when seizure comes, escape is impossible. The time to seek security is before seizure, by abandoning evil plotting and turning to gratitude and tawḥīd.

**The Connection To Security And Gratitude**

Sūrat al-Niʿam stresses that security is from Allah, not from plotting or wealth. Mountains prevent earth from shaking with you as mercy, but if you plot evil, that security can be removed. The verse asks do they feel secure that He will not seize them while going about, implying that true security is only for those who are grateful and mindful, not for plotters. The righteous who emigrated after being wronged and were patient and trusted Lord were given good home in world as security, but that security came after patience and trust, not while plotting evil. The contrast is clear: plotters feel false security while plotting, righteous receive true security after patience and trust.

## Verse 16:47

> Or that He will not destroy them gradually? But your Lord is truly Ever Gracious, Most Merciful

**Gradual Destruction**

Aw ya'khudhahum ʿalā takhawwufin — or that He will not seize them gradually, with fear, by diminishing. Takhawwuf from kh-w-f means fear, but also gradual reduction, taking away little by little, causing fear through erosion. Al-Ṭabarī explains that ʿalā takhawwufin means Allah seizes them gradually, reducing their wealth, strength, numbers, little by little, so they fear and anticipate total destruction, or that He seizes some and leaves others to fear. Al-Rāzī says takhawwuf can mean that punishment comes with fear, causing them to be in fear while being seized, or that it comes by diminishing their sides, reducing their territory, as 13:41 says *Do they not see that We come to land reducing it from its borders?* (13:41). The question is continuation: do plotters feel secure that He will not destroy them gradually? They should not, because gradual destruction is also possible and is happening.

**Graciousness And Mercy As Reason For Delay**

The seal la-ra'ūfun raḥīm explains why punishment is delayed despite plotting. Allah is gracious, preventing immediate destruction, and merciful, granting time and provision even to plotters, as 10:11 says *If Allah were to hasten evil for people as they hasten good, their term would have ended* (10:11). Delay is mercy that allows repentance. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that ra'ūf is more intense than raḥīm in preventing harm, so mention of both indicates Allah both prevents immediate harm and grants time for good. The verse therefore balances warning with hope: warning that punishment can come in many ways, hope that delay is due to mercy that invites return. The sūrah's blessings catalogue also showed graciousness and mercy: cattle bearing loads without hardship because Lord is gracious, merciful. Now same names explain delay in punishment: Lord is gracious, merciful, so He delays. The two uses show that graciousness and mercy appear both in provision and in forbearance.

## Verse 16:48

> Have they not considered how the shadows of everything Allah has created incline to the right and the left ˹as the sun moves˺, totally submitting to Allah in all humility

**Shadows Inclining Right And Left**

A-wa-lam yaraw ilā mā khalaqa Allāhu min shay'in yaẓillu ẓilāluhu ʿan al-yamīni wa-l-shamā'ili sujjadan li-Allāhi — have they not considered how shadows of everything Allah created incline to right and left, prostrating to Allah? Yaraw from r-ʾ-y means to see, consider, reflect. Mā khalaqa Allāhu min shay'in, whatever Allah created of things, general, includes everything with shadow: humans, animals, trees, mountains, buildings. Yaẓillu ẓilāluhu, its shadows incline, from ẓ-l-l, to shade, shadow, incline. ʿAn al-yamīni wa-l-shamā'ili, to right and left, indicating movement of shadows as sun moves from east to west, shadows move from west to east, inclining. Sujjadan li-Allāhi, prostrating to Allah, in submission. Al-Ṭabarī explains that shadows prostrate to Allah by inclining, submitting to His command, following sun's course that Allah decreed. The prostration is not like human prostration with intention, but subjection to divine command, as 13:15 says *And to Allah prostrates whoever is in heavens and earth willingly or unwillingly, and their shadows in mornings and evenings* (13:15).

**How Shadows Teach Humility**

Shadows are humble because they depend on light and object; without light, no shadow; without object, no shadow. They cannot exist independently. Their humility, dākhirūn, teaches that all creation depends on Allah and is humble before Him. A human who sees his own shadow inclining should remember that he too depends on Allah and should be humble, not arrogant. The verse therefore uses shadow as teacher of humility, opposite of arrogance that leads to Hell as evil home for arrogant. The shadow that prostrates humbly will be in Paradise where shadows are permanent and pleasant, as 13:35 says shade permanent, while arrogant who refused to prostrate will be in Hell where no shade.

## Verse 16:49

> And to Allah ˹alone˺ bows down ˹in submission˺ whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth of living creatures, as do the angels—who are not too proud ˹to do so˺

**Universal Prostration To Allah**

Wa-li-Allāhi yasjudu mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍi min dābbatin wa-l-malā'ikatu — and to Allah bows down whatever is in heavens and whatever on earth of living creatures, and angels. Yasjudu from s-j-d means to prostrate, bow down, submit. Min dābbatin, of living creatures, from d-b-b, to crawl, creep, move, meaning all animals that move on earth, including humans, animals, insects. Wa-l-malā'ikatu, and angels, mentioned separately though they are in heavens, to emphasize their prostration despite high rank. Al-Ṭabarī explains that everything prostrates to Allah, willingly or unwillingly, as 13:15 says *To Allah prostrates whoever is in heavens and earth willingly or unwillingly* (13:15). Living creatures prostrate by subjection to Allah's command, by following natural laws, by being under His control. Angels prostrate willingly, constantly, without pride.

**Angels Not Too Proud**

Wa-hum lā yastakbirūn — while they are not too proud, not arrogant. Yastakbirūn from k-b-r, to be arrogant, as in previous verses about arrogant. Angels are not too proud to prostrate to Allah, despite being near to Allah, pure, powerful. Al-Qurṭubī says this is to shame humans who are too proud to prostrate, while angels who are greater in creation are not too proud. The phrase lā yastakbirūn appears in description of angels in 21:19: *Those near Him are not too proud to worship Him* (21:19). Angels' lack of pride is model for humans: if angels who have no sin are not too proud to bow, how can humans who have sins be too proud? The verse therefore contrasts angels' humility with humans' arrogance that leads to Hell as evil home.

**Why Living Creatures Are Mentioned**

Min dābbatin, of living creatures, stresses that prostration includes moving, living beings, not only inanimate shadows as previous verse. Dābbah includes all that moves on earth: humans, animals, insects, as 11:6 says *There is no living creature on earth but its provision is upon Allah* (11:6). Their prostration is by subjection to Allah's command in their creation, movement, life, death. Al-Rāzī explains that dābbah prostrates by following instincts and laws that Allah placed, like bee inspired to make homes in mountains as verse 68 will say, and by being under Allah's control. Even if they do not prostrate like humans with forehead on ground, they prostrate by submission to divine decree. The verse invites to see all moving creatures as submitting, so human who moves should also submit.

## Verse 16:50

> They fear their Lord above them, and do whatever they are commanded

**Fear Of Lord Above Them**

Yakhāfūna rabbahum min fawqihim — they fear their Lord above them. The pronoun refers to angels who prostrate and are not too proud, as described in previous verse. Yakhāfūna from kh-w-f means to fear with reverence. Rabbahum, their Lord, who creates, sustains, commands. Min fawqihim, from above them, indicates Allah is above creation, high, exalted, as 6:18 says He is the Subjugator above His servants. Al-Ṭabarī explains angels fear Lord above because they know His greatness and that He is above them in rank and dominion. The phrase also indicates Allah is above in literal highness as understood by early generations without asking how. Al-Rāzī discusses fawq can mean above in rank and power, not only spatial, but includes both. Angels fear despite being sinless and near, because they know Allah's greatness. If sinless angels fear, how much more should sinful humans fear? This fear is reverential, with knowledge that Lord is above, watching, commanding.

**Doing Whatever They Are Commanded**

Wa-yafʿalūna mā yu'marūn — and they do whatever they are commanded. Yafʿalūna from f-ʿ-l means to do, act. Mā yu'marūn, what they are commanded, from a-m-r, command. Al-Ṭabarī says angels do whatever Allah commands without disobedience, delay, or questioning, as 66:6 says they do not disobey Allah in what He commands and do what commanded. The phrase indicates complete obedience: they do all that is commanded, not some. This contrasts with humans who often do not do what commanded due to arrogance or heedlessness. Angels' obedience is model: fear Lord above, do what commanded. Their obedience is constant, not occasional, and covers all commands, from taking souls to carrying revelation to prostrating.

## Verse 16:51

> And Allah has said, “Do not take two gods. There is only One God. So be in awe of Me ˹alone˺.”

**Connection To Universal Prostration**

Verses 48-50 described shadows prostrating, living creatures and angels bowing, fearing Lord above, doing what commanded. Verse 51 says Allah said do not take two gods, there is only One, so be in awe of Me alone. The connection is that universal prostration to One God shows that taking two gods contradicts what all creation does. All creation bows to One; why do you take two? The command to be in awe of Me alone is supported by universal submission: if everything bows to One, you should too. The verse also prepares for next: to Him belongs whatever in heavens and earth and to Him alone is everlasting devotion, will you fear other than Allah? If you fear Me alone, you will give everlasting devotion to Me alone.

**A Verse That Summarizes Tawḥīd**

This verse summarizes tawḥīd in three parts: prohibition, affirmation, consequence. Prohibition: do not take two gods. Affirmation: indeed He is One God. Consequence: so be in awe of Me alone. This mirrors lā ilāha illā Allāh: no god except Allah, so fear Him alone. Al-Qurṭubī says this verse is one of clearest statements of tawḥīd in Qur'an, comparable to 112:1-4. It is direct speech from Allah, not reported, to emphasize authority: Allah Himself says do not take two gods. The direct speech makes it more powerful than if messenger said it. The listener hears Allah speaking, commanding oneness and awe.

## Verse 16:52

> To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth, and to Him ˹alone˺ is the everlasting devotion. Will you then fear any other than Allah

**To Him Belongs Whatever In Heavens And Earth**

Wa-lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi — and to Him belongs whatever is in heavens and earth. Lahu, to Him, belongs, possessive. Mā fī al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi, whatever is in heavens and earth, includes all creation: angels, stars, humans, cattle, sea, mountains, etc. Al-Ṭabarī explains that everything in heavens and earth belongs to Allah as creation and possession; He created it, owns it, controls it. This is basis for tawḥīd: if everything belongs to Him, then no one else owns anything independently to be worshipped. The phrase appears many times in Qur'an to establish Allah's ownership and dominion, as in 2:284 and 10:68. In al-Naḥl, it comes after command not to take two gods and to be in awe of Me alone, to give reason: to Him belongs everything, so devotion should be to Him alone.

**Will You Fear Other Than Allah?**

A-fa-ghayra Allāhi tattaqūn — will you then fear other than Allah? Fa- is for consequence, then, so. Ghayra Allāhi, other than Allah. Tattaqūn from w-q-y, to fear, be mindful, guard, same root as taqwā. The question is rhetorical, expecting no, you should not fear other than Allah. Al-Ṭabarī says this questions why you fear idols, tyrants, or others besides Allah when everything belongs to Him and everlasting devotion is His. Fear should be of Allah alone, as previous verse said be in awe of Me alone. Al-Rāzī notes that fearing other than Allah is shirk in fear, which is part of worship. If you fear other than Allah as you fear Allah, or more, you have taken other as god. The question exposes irrationality: you fear idols that belong to Allah and cannot harm or benefit, while not fearing Allah who owns everything and whose devotion is everlasting.

**Why Ownership Implies Exclusive Fear**

If everything belongs to Allah, then nothing can harm or benefit without His permission, so fear should be of Him alone. Al-Qurṭubī explains that ownership includes power to dispose: owner can give, withhold, harm, benefit. If Allah owns everything, then only He can truly harm or benefit, so only He should be feared. Idols that are owned cannot harm or benefit independently. This is same argument as in 10:106-107: *Do not call besides Allah what cannot benefit nor harm you* (10:106) and *If Allah touches you with harm, none can remove it except Him* (10:107). The verse therefore links ownership to fear: because He owns all, fear Him alone.

## Verse 16:53

> Whatever blessings you have are from Allah. Then whenever hardship touches you, to Him ˹alone˺ you cry ˹for help˺

**All Blessings From Allah**

Wa-mā bikum min niʿmatin fa-min Allāhi — and whatever blessings you have are from Allah. Mā bikum, whatever is with you, whatever you have. Min niʿmatin, of blessing, favor. Fa-min Allāhi, then it is from Allah. The particle mā is general, includes all blessings: faith, health, family, provision, cattle, rain, crops, sea, mountains, guidance. Fa-min Allāhi, then from Allah, with fa- for consequence, then, so. Al-Ṭabarī explains that all blessings you have, whether you recognize or not, are from Allah, not from idols, not from self, not from chance. This is summary of blessings catalogue in verses 5-18: all those blessings are from Allah. The verse states it explicitly to remove any misattribution. Al-Rāzī notes that min niʿmatin is indefinite, indicating even one blessing is from Allah, let alone many, and that fa-min Allāhi with fa- stresses that whenever you consider a blessing, you will find it is from Allah.

**Hardship Makes You Cry To Him Alone**

Thumma idhā massakum al-ḍurru fa-ilayhi taj'arūn — then when hardship touches you, to Him alone you cry for help. Thumma, then, after blessings. Idhā massakum al-ḍurru, when hardship, harm, distress touches you, from m-s-s, to touch, and ḍ-r-r, harm, hardship. Fa-ilayhi taj'arūn, then to Him you cry loudly, from j-ʾ-r, to cry out, to moan, to supplicate loudly in distress. Al-Ṭabarī says when hardship like illness, poverty, drought, fear touches you, you cry to Allah alone for help, forgetting idols, because you know deep down that only Allah can remove hardship. This is natural disposition, fiṭrah, that emerges in distress. Al-Qurṭubī notes that even polytheists, when in distress at sea, cry to Allah alone, as 17:67 says *When harm touches you at sea, those you call upon besides Him vanish, except Him* (17:67).

## Verse 16:54

> Then as soon as He removes the hardship from you, a group of you associates ˹others˺ with their Lord ˹in worship˺

**The Human Pattern Of Forgetting After Relief And Its Warning**

Thumma idhā kashafa al-ḍurra ʿankum idhā farīqun minkum bi-rabbihim yushrikūn — then when He removes hardship from you, behold, a group of you associates others with their Lord. This verse describes a pattern that appears throughout Qur'an and human experience: in hardship you turn to Allah alone, in ease you forget. Al-Ṭabarī explains that when Allah removes hardship that made them cry to Him alone, a group returns to associating idols, forgetting that Allah alone removed hardship. The pattern is described in 10:12 where after harm removed he passes on as if he never called Us, and in 17:67 where after being saved at sea they turn away on land. The verse uses idhā twice to show suddenness: when He removes, behold, a group associates. The suddenness indicates ingratitude and heedlessness. The sūrah's theme of gratitude vs ingratitude appears here: to associate after being saved is to return favour with ingratitude, as next verse says only returning Our favours with ingratitude. The verse exposes human inconsistency: in hardship tawḥīd, in ease shirk, and calls to consistency in all states. The lesson is that true faith remains after relief, not only during distress.

**Enjoyment As Temporary Test Followed By Soon Knowing The Reality**

Fa-tamattaʿū fa-sawfa taʿlamūn — so enjoy yourselves, you will soon know. Tamattaʿū from m-t-ʿ means to enjoy temporary pleasure, to have enjoyment for a time. Fa-sawfa taʿlamūn, you will soon know consequence, from ʿ-l-m, to know, with sawfa indicating near future, soon. Al-Ṭabarī says fa-tamattaʿū is threat, not permission: enjoy now in world with ingratitude, but soon you will know consequence in Hereafter, when disgrace and misery are upon disbelievers as verse 27 said, when evil consequences overtake as verse 34 said. Enjoyment is temporary, as 16:117 says brief enjoyment then painful punishment. Phrase fa-sawfa taʿlamūn appears many times as warning that knowledge of consequence will come soon, when too late to change. Al-Zamakhsharī notes tamattaʿū is imperative but means threat: enjoy if you can, but you will soon know that enjoyment was short and punishment near.

---

## Verse 16:55

> only returning Our favours with ingratitude. So enjoy yourselves, for you will soon know

**The Human Pattern Of Forgetting After Relief And Its Warning**

Thumma idhā kashafa al-ḍurra ʿankum idhā farīqun minkum bi-rabbihim yushrikūn — then when He removes hardship from you, behold, a group of you associates others with their Lord. This verse describes a pattern that appears throughout Qur'an and human experience: in hardship you turn to Allah alone, in ease you forget. Al-Ṭabarī explains that when Allah removes hardship that made them cry to Him alone, a group returns to associating idols, forgetting that Allah alone removed hardship. The pattern is described in 10:12 where after harm removed he passes on as if he never called Us, and in 17:67 where after being saved at sea they turn away on land. The verse uses idhā twice to show suddenness: when He removes, behold, a group associates. The suddenness indicates ingratitude and heedlessness. The sūrah's theme of gratitude vs ingratitude appears here: to associate after being saved is to return favour with ingratitude, as next verse says only returning Our favours with ingratitude. The verse exposes human inconsistency: in hardship tawḥīd, in ease shirk, and calls to consistency in all states. The lesson is that true faith remains after relief, not only during distress.

---

## Verse 16:56

> And they ˹even˺ assign to those ˹idols˺—who know nothing—a share of what We have provided for them. By Allah! You will certainly be questioned about whatever ˹lies˺ you used to fabricate ˹against Allah˺

**Assigning Share Of Provision To Idols**

Wa-yajʿalūna li-mā lā yaʿlamūna naṣīban mimmā razaqnāhum — and they assign to what they do not know, to those who know nothing, a share of what We provided them. Yajʿalūna from j-ʿ-l means to make, assign, set. Li-mā lā yaʿlamūna, to what does not know, to those who know nothing, meaning idols that have no knowledge, no perception, as verse 21 said dead not knowing when they will be resurrected. Naṣīban, share, portion, from n-ṣ-b, share. Mimmā razaqnāhum, from what We provided them, from r-z-q, provision. Al-Ṭabarī explains that polytheists assigned share of their provision, like crops and cattle, to idols, saying this is for Allah and this is for our idols, as 6:136 says *They assign to Allah from what He created of crops and cattle a share and say this is for Allah by their claim and this is for our idols* (6:136). They would give part of harvest and cattle to idols, as offering, while provision was from Allah alone. This is ingratitude: provision from Allah, share given to idols who know nothing.

---

## Verse 16:57

> And they attribute ˹angels as˺ daughters to Allah—glory be to Him!—the opposite of what they desire for themselves

**Attributing Daughters To Allah**

Wa-yajʿalūna li-Allāhi al-banāt — and they attribute to Allah daughters. Yajʿalūna from j-ʿ-l means to make, assign, attribute. Li-Allāhi, to Allah. Al-banāt, daughters, plural of bint, daughter. Al-Ṭabarī explains that Makkan polytheists attributed angels as daughters to Allah, saying angels are daughters of Allah, while they themselves desired sons and disliked daughters, as next verses describe. This is fabrication against Allah, as verse 56 said you will be questioned about lies fabricated. The attribution of daughters to Allah is lie because Allah has no children, no daughters, no sons, as 112:3 says *He neither begets nor is born* (112:3). Al-Rāzī notes that attributing daughters to Allah is both shirk and insult: shirk because it attributes to Allah what is not His, insult because they attribute to Allah what they themselves hate, daughters, while desiring sons for themselves.

**Glory Be To Him**

Subḥānahu — glory be to Him, exalted, free from defect. Subḥān from s-b-ḥ means to be free, far removed, to swim away from defect. Subḥānahu is tanzīh, declaring Allah free from what they attribute. Al-Ṭabarī says subḥānahu here declares Allah free from having daughters, from having children, from what they attribute. The phrase appears often after false attribution to Allah, as in 16:1 *Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate* (16:1) and 16:3. It is to remind that Allah is beyond such attribution. Al-Zamakhsharī notes that subḥānahu is placed in middle of verse to interrupt false attribution with declaration of transcendence, showing that false attribution should be immediately followed by glorification.

**Why This Attribution Is Evil**

Attributing daughters to Allah is evil for several reasons. First, it attributes to Allah what He does not have: children. Third, it is based on ignorance: they say angels are daughters of Allah without knowledge, as verse 56 said they assign share to those who know nothing. Fourth, it is fabrication that will be questioned, as verse 56 warned. Al-Qurṭubī notes that this attribution was common among Arabs who worshipped angels as daughters of Allah and sought intercession from them, as 53:27 says *Those who do not believe in Hereafter name angels with female names* (53:27). The verse exposes irrationality: you worship angels as daughters of Allah, yet you dislike daughters for yourselves. Verses 58-59 will describe how when one of them is given good news of baby girl, his face grows gloomy and he hides from people, deciding whether to keep her in disgrace or bury her alive. This is evil judgment, as verse 59 says *Evil indeed is their judgment*.

**A Lesson On Gender And Value**

The verse also touches on gender bias: polytheists desired sons and disliked daughters, yet attributed daughters to Allah. The Qur'an does not endorse bias but exposes it as evil judgment. It says *Evil indeed is their judgment* (16:59) for deciding whether to keep daughter in disgrace or bury alive. The Qur'an honors daughters as blessing, not disgrace, and forbids burying alive, as 81:8-9 says *When baby girl buried alive is asked for what sin she was killed* (81:8-9). The verse therefore not only refutes attribution of daughters to Allah but also critiques devaluation of daughters, showing that attributing to Allah what you hate for yourself is both religious lie and social injustice.

---

## Verse 16:58

> Whenever one of them is given the good news of a baby girl, his face grows gloomy, as he suppresses his rage

**Why Daughters Were Disliked**

Pre-Islamic Arabs disliked daughters for several reasons: fear of poverty, as daughters considered economic burden; fear of shame, as daughters might be captured in war and bring dishonor; desire for sons who would be warriors and carry name. The Qur'an critiques this bias as evil judgment, as verse 59 says. Al-Qurṭubī notes that dislike of daughters was not universal among Arabs; some tribes honored daughters, but among those who worshipped angels as daughters of Allah, dislike was pronounced, making attribution to Allah even more contradictory: you attribute to Allah what you consider bad news for yourself. The verse exposes contradiction: you consider daughter as bad news for you, yet you say angels, whom you consider daughters of Allah, are worthy of worship and intercession.

**How This Verse Critiques Gender Bias**

The verse critiques gender bias by describing its outward and inward signs: face gloomy, rage suppressed, hiding from people, deciding whether to keep in disgrace or bury alive. It does not endorse bias but exposes as evil judgment. The Qur'an elsewhere honors daughters: *Wealth and children are adornment of worldly life* (18:46) includes daughters as adornment, and Prophet ﷺ said *Whoever has three daughters and is patient with them and clothes them from his wealth, they will be shield for him from Fire* (Ibn Mājah 3669). The verse therefore calls to value daughters as good news, not bad, and to not attribute to Allah what you hate for yourself, but to attribute to Allah finest attributes as verse 60 says.

**A Lesson On Receiving News**

The verse teaches how to receive news: birth of child, male or female, is good news, blessing from Allah, as verse 72 will say Allah gave you children and grandchildren as provision. To receive good news of daughter with gloomy face and suppressed rage is to be ungrateful for blessing, as verse 53 said blessings from Allah. The verse therefore calls to gratitude for daughters as well as sons.

The believer should receive news of daughter with joy, gratitude, recognizing it as blessing from Allah who gives whatever He wills, as 42:49 says *To Allah belongs dominion of heavens and earth, He creates what He wills, He gives to whom He wills females and gives to whom He wills males* (42:49).

---

## Verse 16:59

> He hides himself from the people because of the bad news he has received. Should he keep her in disgrace, or bury her ˹alive˺ in the ground? Evil indeed is their judgment

**Should He Keep In Disgrace Or Bury Alive?**

A-yumsikuhu ʿalā hūnin am yadussuhu fī al-turāb — should he keep it in disgrace, humiliation, or bury it in dust, ground? Yumsikuhu from m-s-k, to keep, hold, retain. ʿAlā hūnin, upon disgrace, humiliation, from h-w-n, to be low, despised, meaning keep daughter alive but in disgrace, humiliation, low status, not honored. Am yadussuhu fī al-turāb, or bury it in dust, from d-s-s, to bury, hide in ground. Turāb, dust, earth, meaning bury alive in ground, as was practice of some Arabs who buried baby girls alive out of fear of poverty or shame, as 81:8-9 says *When baby girl buried alive is asked for what sin she was killed* (81:8-9) and 17:31 says *Do not kill your children for fear of poverty* (17:31). Al-Ṭabarī explains that man debates whether to keep daughter alive in humiliation or bury her alive in ground, both evil options, both due to bias.

**Evil Indeed Is Their Judgment**

Alā sā'a mā yaḥkumūn — evil indeed is what they judge, how wretched is their judgment. Alā for attention, behold. Sā'a from s-w-ʾ, evil, wretched. Mā yaḥkumūn, what they judge, decide. Al-Ṭabarī says their judgment to hide from people, to consider daughter bad news, to debate keeping in disgrace or burying alive, is evil judgment, wretched decision. Al-Rāzī explains that sā'a mā yaḥkumūn indicates that their judgment is evil in both options: keeping in disgrace is evil, burying alive is evil, so whatever they choose, judgment evil. The phrase appears in other condemnations of evil judgments, as in 16:25 *Evil is what they bear* and 16:59 *Evil is their judgment*. The sūrah so condemns gender bias and infanticide as evil judgment.

**Why This Practice Is Condemned**

Burying daughters alive was practiced by some pre-Islamic Arabs out of fear of poverty, shame, or tribal honor. Islam forbade it categorically, as 17:31 and 81:8-9 show. The verse condemns it as evil judgment, not only as sin, but as perversion of judgment: to consider keeping daughter in disgrace or burying alive as options is evil judgment. Al-Qurṭubī notes that Islam honored daughters as blessing, not burden, and that Prophet ﷺ said *Whoever has three daughters and is patient with them, they will be shield from Fire*. The verse therefore not only condemns infanticide but also condemns keeping daughter in disgrace, indicating that daughters should be honored, not kept in humiliation. Both options are evil; correct option is to honor daughter as good news. Verses 57-59 form unit: they attribute daughters to Allah, glory be to Him, while for themselves what they desire, sons; when given good news of daughter, face gloomy, suppressing rage, hiding from people, debating keep in disgrace or bury alive, evil judgment. The unit shows contradiction and evil: you attribute daughters to Allah whom you consider inferior, yet you hate daughters for yourself so much that you hide and consider burying alive.

---

## Verse 16:60

> To those who disbelieve in the Hereafter belong all evil qualities, whereas to Allah belong the finest attributes. And He is the Almighty, All-Wise

**Finest Attributes Belong To Allah**

Wa-li-Allāhi al-mathalu al-aʿlā — and to Allah belongs highest, finest example, attributes. Mathalu al-aʿlā means highest description, finest attributes, most sublime example. Al-Ṭabarī says to Allah belongs finest attributes: oneness, power, knowledge, mercy, etc., as 59:24 says *To Him belong finest names* (59:24). Al-Zamakhsharī explains that al-mathalu al-aʿlā means Allah has attributes that are highest, beyond comparison, perfect, while disbelievers have lowest, evil. The contrast li-l-ladhīna lā yu'minūna mathalu al-saw' wa-li-Allāhi al-mathalu al-aʿlā creates sharp dichotomy: evil for disbelievers, finest for Allah. This is to show that attributing daughters to Allah while keeping sons for self is opposite of what should be: to Allah belongs finest, not what you hate.

**How This Verse Teaches To Attribute Finest To Allah**

The verse teaches to attribute to Allah finest attributes, not what you hate for yourself. Al-Rāzī says al-mathalu al-aʿlā includes all perfect attributes: knowledge, power, mercy, etc., and excludes all defects: having children, having partners, etc. To attribute daughters to Allah while hating daughters is to attribute to Allah what is not finest in your own view, which is injustice. True tawḥīd is to attribute to Allah finest, as 59:24 says finest names belong to Him, and to believe that disbelievers in Hereafter have evil qualities, not Allah. The believer should therefore describe Allah with what He described Himself, with finest attributes, and should avoid evil qualities like arrogance, denial, and gender bias that belong to disbelievers in Hereafter.

---

## Verse 16:61

> If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term. And when their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it

The verse opens the passage on the delay of punishment with a conditional that no city could survive: if God took people to task for what they do as it happens, nothing living would be left on the earth. The Book states the same in Sūrah Fāṭir, where the withholding of punishment is named as a term God has set, and it repeats here that the delay runs to an appointed time, and that when the term arrives it cannot be moved by an hour either way (16:61). The verse's function in the sūrah is to explain the mockers' survival: their continuance is not evidence that their position is sound, but part of the respite in which repentance is still possible. The sūrah names that mercy explicitly elsewhere: proof follows proof, so that people may return (30:41).

---

The Book states the same fact elsewhere in nearly the same words: if God were to take people to account for what they have earned, He would not leave a living creature on the face of the earth (35:45), and He is the one who defers them to an appointed term (16:61). The commentators read the verse as the answer to the demand for the punishment to be hurried, a demand the sūrah records on the lips of the Makkans elsewhere (13:6). The reason for the delay is stated in the same breath: God is Forgiving and Merciful, and the respite is part of the invitation rather than a slackening of the account.

**What the Conditional States**
The verse says that if God were to take people to task for what they have earned, He would not leave a creature on the earth, and it then gives the other side: He gives them respite until a term appointed, and when their term comes they cannot delay it or advance it by an hour. The sentence is one of the Book's clearest statements about the delay of punishment, and it comes in the passage that answers the men who asked why the punishment had not arrived.
**What the Commentators Explained**
The older commentators read the first clause as a description of what justice alone would produce, and the second as a description of what mercy and decree actually produce. They noted that the Book uses the same structure in the Sūrah of the Originator, where people are called to the earth and told to be mindful (35:45), and in the Sūrah of the Spoils, where the community is told that God would not punish them while the Prophet was among them (8:33). The two passages and the verse in front of us give the community the same doctrine in three forms.
**Where the Passage Sits**
The verse before it answers the idolaters' claim about daughters, and the verse after it gives the rule about the term and the reckoning. The sūrah is dealing with a community that had been given years of warning, and the verse states both halves of the arrangement: what the world deserved, and what God gave it instead. The community's preachers used the sentence about the term in their warnings, and the Book returns to it in several places because the delay was the hardest part of the message for the men who mocked.

## Verse 16:62

> They attribute to Allah what they hate ˹for themselves˺, and their tongues utter the lie that they will have the finest reward. Without a doubt, for them is the Fire, where they will be abandoned

The verse exposes a contradiction the sūrah has already named. The idolaters reserved daughters — a thing they hated for themselves — and assigned them to God, and they were asked how they could judge so unfairly (16:57–59); now they go further and say out loud that the best outcome is theirs. The Book calls the claim *qawl al-zūr*, the speech of falsehood, and answers it in the same verse: their share is the Fire, and they are the ones being sent ahead to it. Al-Ṭabarī reads the passage as a description of what lies behind the words: a person will assert a comfortable future in order to keep the life he has. The sūrah follows the logic the way it has followed every false claim in the passage — not with a denial of the claim's logic, but with the destination the claimant has chosen.

**The Shape of the Charge**
The verse is the sūrah's answer to a claim that had already been exposed as absurd. The idolaters hated the idea of having daughters and yet assigned daughters to God (16:57-59), and the Book repeats the same argument elsewhere in a sharp question: has God taken daughters for Himself and given you sons? (43:16; 52:39). Here the claim grows bolder. They say the best share is theirs, and the Book names the words for what they are: *qawl al-zūr*, the speech of falsehood. The verse then states the destination, and the next verse moves on to earlier nations who said the same thing and had their deeds made to look fair to them by Satan (16:63). The passage is not an argument about who has daughters; it is about men who wrote themselves a comfortable future and went on living in it.

## Verse 16:63

> By Allah! We have surely sent messengers to communities before you ˹O Prophet˺, but Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them. So he is their patron today, and they will suffer a painful punishment

The verse states the history of the charge and names its author. Messengers were sent to communities before, and when they came the same accusations arrived with them — the Book lists them elsewhere: a poet, a madman, a man taught by others, a soothsayer (52:29–30; 44:14; 69:41–42). The verse answers with the one thing that can follow the ordinary resistance of people: they are shown what they do not turn away from, and their turning away is credited to their own hearts. The Book's answer to the whole charge is in 16:103, where it notes that the man they point to speaks a foreign tongue while the revelation is in clear Arabic.

---

The author named here is the same one the sūrah has spoken of in describing the enemies of every prophet: the Book says that God has made for every prophet an enemy among the devils of humankind and the jinn, who inspire one another with fine talk to deceive (6:112). The promise attached to the verse — that God will guide whom He wills, and He knows best who is rightly guided — answers the prophet's grief rather than the accusers' charge. The sūrah of Ṭā Hā records the same counsel when it tells the Prophet that God knows best who has strayed and who is guided (20:135).

## Verse 16:64

> We have revealed to you the Book only to clarify for them what they differed about, and as a guide and mercy for those who believe

The verse states the purpose of the sending down, and the purpose is stated in pairs: to clarify what they differ about, and to be a guide and a mercy for those who believe. The sūrah has spent most of its length on the first half — arguing about God, the idols, the resurrection, what is lawful and unlawful — and this verse says why the argument is being made at all. The Book returns to the same description at the head of Sūrah al-Naḥl's parallel claim in 27:76–77, where the Book is said to judge between the Children of Israel about what they differed over, and to be a guide and mercy for the believers. The mercy is attached to belief here, not to the explanation alone: many heard the explanation in Makkah and went away unchanged.

**What the Sūrah Has Been Doing**
The verse names the two purposes of the revelation, and by this point in the sūrah the reader has watched the first of them being carried out for forty verses. Every dispute in the chapter has been answered: the idolaters' claim about daughters, the mockers' demand for proof, the accusation about a foreign teacher, the arguments over what is lawful to eat. The Book was sent to settle these matters, and the sūrah settles them by returning again and again to the same two facts about God — that He made the world and that He will judge it. The second purpose is the gift: a guide and a mercy, and the sūrah attaches both to belief rather than to the explanation alone, because the explanation was given to everyone.
**The Same Words Elsewhere**
The Sūrah of the Ant states the same description in almost the same words, adding that the Book will judge between the Children of Israel about most of what they differed over (27:76-77), and the Sūrah of the Cow says the Book was sent so that God could judge between people in their disagreements (2:213). The Book never claims that it ends every argument; it claims that it states the truth about the matters that matter and that those who believe it find mercy in it. The verse after this one turns from the Book back to the sky and the earth, with rain that revives a dead land.

## Verse 16:65

> And Allah sends down rain from the sky, giving life to the earth after its death. Surely in this is a sign for those who listen

Rain is the sūrah's evidence of resurrection carried in a picture anyone can see. The Book says the earth is revived after its death. It does the same in Sūrah al-Rūm, where the sight of the revived land is given as proof of the One who brings out the dead (30:19), and in Sūrah al-Ḥajj, where the earth swells and grows after the shower is sent down (22:5). The verse's ending gives the condition for the sign's value: it is a sign for those who listen, and sūrah 22 repeats that it is a sign for people who understand. The pattern is the sūrah's way of measuring the audience rather than the evidence.

**The Rain They Themselves Ascribed to God**
The verse's argument had already been won in the mouths of those who heard it. The Book reports the exchange more than once: if you asked them who sends down water from the sky and revives the earth by it, they would say God (29:63), and if you asked them who created the heavens and the earth, they would answer that the Almighty, the All-Knowing did (43:9; 43:87). The Makkans admitted the sender of the rain and still denied the raising of the dead, and the Book takes their admission and puts it to work: the One who brings a dead land to life can raise the dead who lie in it (36:33; 50:9-11). The verse's closing words, that this is a sign for those who listen, name the gap exactly — the evidence was never the obstacle, and the listeners were.

**The Prayer for Rain and the Word of Thanks**
The community's practice kept the verse's lesson in the calendar. The Prophet led the prayer for rain when the land was dry and asked God to send water down on the people and the land, and the reports describe him going out into it and letting it fall on him, saying that it had just come from its Lord (Abū Dāwūd). When the rain came he named the two possible sentences about it: we have been given rain by the bounty and mercy of God, which is faith in the Giver, and the rain has come by the favour of such-and-such a star, which is a denial of Him (al-Bukhārī). So a plain statement about water and dead ground ends where the sūrah's verses on nature always end, with the hearer rather than the weather: the sign is free, and the thankfulness or the ignoring of it is the human act.

---

## Verse 16:66

> And there is certainly a lesson for you in cattle: We give you to drink of what is in their bellies, from between digested food and blood: pure milk, pleasant to drink

Milch cattle are offered as a lesson rather than an ornament, and the verse describes the process with an accuracy that the hearers could verify in their own tents: milk comes from between the digested food and the blood, and it arrives pure for the drinkers. The Book repeats the account in Sūrah al-Muʾminūn, attached to the same list of blessings, and adds the detail that the animals also provide meat and hides (23:21–22). The scholars read the pair "food and blood" as a description of where milk forms in the animal, which is why the verse's word for it, *khalisan sāʾighan*, pure and easy to swallow, is agreed by anyone who has drunk it. The sign is placed among the blessings the sūrah is counting, not in a chapter of biology.

---

The same lesson is drawn from the cattle in the sūrah of the Believers, where they are named among the signs and the drink is described as coming out of what is in their bellies (23:21), and in the sūrah of the Troops, where the milk is called pure and pleasant to those who drink it (16:66; 39:6). The verse's detail about the route the milk takes — from between what is digested and the blood — is the Qur'an's way of making the sign concrete for people who knew the process from their herds. The Book elsewhere names the cattle the bearers of loads and the source of food (6:142), so the lesson is one entry in the sūrah's running account of provisions.

## Verse 16:67

> And from the fruits of palm trees and grapevines you derive intoxicants as well as wholesome provision. Surely in this is a sign for those who understand

The verse names both sides of the same fruit: the intoxicant the people made of dates and grapes, and the wholesome provision God made of them. Al-Ṭabarī and the later commentators note the sequence: the Book calls wine *sakar* here, and it treats its use as a thing people chose while provision is what God gave. The same sūrah later forbids what intoxicates through the command to eat what is lawful and good (16:114–115), and the full prohibition of wine came in Madinah with the verse that calls it an abomination of Satan's handiwork (5:90–91). The verse is therefore read as a reminder that the mercy in a fruit is not the same thing as the use people make of it, and the sign is for those who understand.

**The Fruit and the Drink**
The verse puts the two uses of the same harvest side by side: the crop the people turned into *sakar*, and the provision God placed in it — good food for those who understand. The word *sakar* covers what clouds the mind, and the sūrah leaves it at that, because the chapter was given in Makkah and this is how the Book works: it first names an evil, then draws the hearers away from it, and only later closes the door. The Sūrah of the Cow states the earlier stage with its balance sheet: some benefit for people, and a sin greater than the benefit (2:219). By the time the community had a state and a law, the ruling came in full: intoxicants are the work of Satan, so avoid them (5:90-91). The verse in front of us is the moment when the question was planted, and the sign it points to is the crop itself — a gift that is a gift in the eating and a trap in the drinking.

## Verse 16:68

> And your Lord inspired the bees: “Make ˹your˺ homes in the mountains, the trees, and in what people construct

The bee's instruction is one of the sūrah's clearest examples of revelation reaching a creature that cannot speak: *awḥā rabbuka ilā al-naḥl* — your Lord inspired the bee. The Book uses the same verb for the inspiration given to the mother of Moses (28:7) and for the instinct of the heavens and the earth (41:12), so what the verse reports is guidance built into a creature rather than a message delivered by a messenger. The bee is told where to build — mountains, trees, and what people construct — and the verse names the three kinds of home in the order of their security, ending with the one built by human hands, which is the one the honey the reader eats was most likely made in. The sūrah's sequence of blessings then continues with what comes out of the bee, in the next verse.

---

## Verse 16:69

> and feed from ˹the flower of˺ any fruit ˹you please˺ and follow the ways your Lord has made easy for you.” From their bellies comes forth liquid of varying colours, in which there is healing for people. Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect

The bee's product is the sūrah's third food, and it arrives with the same structure as the milk and the fruit: the animal does what God inspired it to do, and what comes out of it is useful to human beings. The Book gives the syrup a description — a liquid of varying colours — and names what it is for: healing for people. The commentators note that the Book says *shifāʾ li-al-nās*, healing for people, without limiting the illness or claiming that honey replaces every remedy. The bee is also presented as a teacher of organisation: nations and houses have taken from its building and its order. The verse closes the section of blessings with a sign, as the previous ones did, and it is a sign for people who think.

---

The drink the bee produces is named as a healing in the same sentence, and the Prophet ﷺ is reported in the Ṣaḥīḥ collections to have named honey among the means of healing God has provided for people, and to have treated a stomach complaint with it. The bee's industry is also used as a pattern of the community's work: the sūrah of Muhammad describes the rivers of the Garden as including one of purified honey (47:15), which puts the bee's product in the world's provision and the world to come. The verse's phrase about following the ways the Lord has made easy for it describes an instinct that is itself an instruction, so that the creature's orderliness is part of the sign.

**What the Verse Names**
The verse gives the bee its work and the product that comes out of it, a drink of different colours in which there is healing for people, and it closes with the sentence that in this is a sign for people who reflect. The Book's account of the bee is the third of the sūrah's product passages, after the milk of the cattle and the fruits of the palms and the vines (16:66-67), and the three are arranged in the same shape: what the creature does, what comes out of it, and what it means.
**What the Commentators Wrote**
The older commentators noted the detail about the bee's work, which the verses before this one describe, where the insect is given its instinct to build its house in the mountains and the trees and to eat from every kind of fruit. The Book calls what comes out of it a drink rather than a food, and the scholars wrote about the healing in the honey through the reports and the medicine that was known in their time; the general reading is that the verse names the healing without limiting it to one use. The Prophet's word about the two remedies, honey and the Recitation, is in the collections, and the community's physicians read it with this verse.
**Where the Passage Stands**
The sūrah has been listing the signs since its fifth verse, and the passage on the bee is followed by the account of God's favour in the institution of marriage and the family (16:72). The Book's arrangement keeps the natural world and the human world side by side, and the verse in front of us is the last of the animal passages before the sūrah turns to the community's own life. The sentence about reflection is repeated at the end of several of the sūrah's passages, and it is the response the whole catalogue is looking for.

## Verse 16:70

> Allah has created you, and then causes you to die. And some of you are left to reach the most feeble stage of life so that they may know nothing after having known much. Indeed, Allah is All-Knowing, Most Capable

Old age enters the sūrah as part of the answer to the idolaters' argument, because the one who created and the one who causes death is the one who has power over both ends of life. The verse describes the last stage as a return to knowing nothing after having known. The Book states the same in Sūrah al-Ḥajj, where a person is said to reach the age where he knows nothing after having known (22:5), and in Sūrah Yāsīn, where the Book says that those whom We give long life We cause to decline in creation (36:68). The point made in this verse is double: the descent shows a power above human strength, and it gives the lie to the idea that a person's own faculties can save him from the end to which he is going.

---

The stages named here — creation, death, and the return to the feeblest age of life in which a person knows nothing of what he once knew — repeat the passage at the beginning of the sūrah of the Pilgrimage, where the same three are used to prove the resurrection (22:5). The Book states the reversal in its own terms: whoever is given a long life is turned back to the weakness of the beginning (36:68). The verse's closing word is the answer to the idolaters' reasoning about their forefathers: the One who spans both ends of a human life has knowledge of every course.

## Verse 16:71

> And Allah has favoured some of you over others in provision. But those who have been much favoured would not share their wealth with those ˹bondspeople˺ in their possession, making them their equals. Do they then deny Allah’s favours

The sūrah turns from the gifts to the human order built on them. Provision is distributed unequally under God's arrangement, and the verse points out that the men who hold the surplus would not hand it over to those whom their right hands possess so that they might become equal in it. The verse does not condemn the mark of difference; it uses their own behaviour as an argument about God. If a man will not share the wealth he was given with his own servants, how can he set up equals with the One who gave it to both? The Companion ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have read the verse the same way, as an argument against assigning partners in worship to the source of all provision.

**Where the Argument Goes Next**
The verse is the first of a set, and the ones that follow complete the reasoning. The Book presses the same point twice more in the passage: the comparison between the man's attitude to his own servants and his willingness to assign partners to God, and then the warning against drawing conclusions from outward provision — *and God has favoured some of you over others in provision* (16:71 continues into the next verses on marriage and the law of the household). The sūrah then asks the question that settles the matter for the reader: is the one who does right and the one who does not equal? Do they remember? (16:76-77 in the same sequence). The argument is built so that a man who would never surrender a share in his own wealth to a servant finds himself explaining why he should surrender a share in God's authority to a stone.

## Verse 16:72

> And Allah has made for you spouses of your own kind, and given you through your spouses children and grandchildren. And He has granted you good, lawful provisions. Are they then faithful to falsehood and ungrateful for Allah’s favours

The list of gifts continues and moves to the family. Spouses are said to be "of your own kind" — *min anfusikum* — which the commentators take as a statement about the design of companionship as well as about species, and the sūrah of the Romans adds the purpose: tranquillity, affection and mercy placed between husband and wife (30:21). The children and grandchildren named here are given in the same sentence as good provision, and the next verse turns to the absurdity that follows: after all this, people worship what cannot give anything. The verse works in the sūrah's order of argument — a gift, the failure to be grateful, and then the question of who deserves the worship.

---

**From Your Own Kind**
The verse's phrase *min anfusikum* is the first thing the commentators weigh. Spouses are given from the same kind, and the phrase covers two mercies at once: a companion who can understand what a human being goes through, and a bond that is not a crossing of species. The sūrah of the Romans names the fruit of this arrangement — tranquillity, affection and mercy settled between the two (30:21) — and the sūrah of the Discrimination describes the same act from a wider angle, the One who made from water a human being and gave him lineage and marriage (25:54).
**Sons and Grandsons**
The verse names *banīn wa-ḥafadah*, sons and grandchildren. Some commentators read *ḥafadah* as the grandchildren, some as the sons-in-law and the helpers a large family brings; either way, the word covers the generation the parents did not raise alone and cannot repay, the ones who carry the household's name and burden both. That is the physical side of the gift, and the verse sets it beside the provision of good things as one package.
**The Question the Verse Turns On**
The verse ends on a question that the reader is meant to feel in his own chest: *do they then believe in falsehood and deny the favour of God?* (16:72). The charge is not simply that the idolaters are mistaken but that they are ungrateful — they accept gifts from one hand every day and thank another. The whole passage runs that way: gift, gratitude, and then the absurdity of where the gratitude went.

## Verse 16:73

> Yet they worship besides Allah those ˹idols˺ who do not afford them any provision from the heavens and the earth, nor do they have the power to

The charge is precise. The idols the idolaters serve besides God cannot provide anything from the heavens or the earth, and they have no power to do so. The Book uses the same argument repeatedly: the idols neither create nor provide nor know (25:3), and the Book challenges the idolaters to ask their gods for rain or to show what part of the earth they created. The verse's wording also undermines the claim the idolaters actually made, which was that the idols could intercede rather than provide: the Book answers that the powerless cannot even hold property, and so cannot have a say in what is His (10:18, 39:43–44). The sūrah's method in this section is to test each claim against the thing it asserts.

---

## Verse 16:74

> So do not set up equals to Allah, for Allah certainly knows and you do not know

The command not to set up equals to God is followed by a reason in the same verse: God knows and the people do not know. The contrast is the sūrah's way of stating that idolatry is a mistake in knowledge before it is a mistake in worship, and that the One who sees the reality of things has forbidden the thing itself. The Book gives the same argument in Sūrah al-Najm with its strongest form: do not set up any other god with Allah, for He knows everything and no one shares His knowledge. The instruction is repeated in the sūrah's own voice, and the next verses switch to parables that show the difference between a partner who owns nothing and a master who owns everything.

**Why the Ban Is Stated This Way**
The verse does not argue the case for God's oneness in long form. It gives the ban and then gives the reason: He knows and you do not know. That is the sūrah's method whenever it deals with idolatry, and the reason is a statement about knowledge. Anyone who measures the Creator by created things has already chosen a scale that cannot weigh what is being discussed, and the Sūrah of Consultation states the result plainly: there is nothing like Him (42:11). The Book also warns in another place that eyes cannot take Him in while He takes in all eyes (6:103). The next verses carry the point into pictures: a servant who owns nothing set beside one whom God has provided generously, and a man who cannot speak and can do nothing set beside a man who commands justice (16:75-76). The command not to set up equals therefore ends in the place it began: the One being compared is not known the way the compared are known.

## Verse 16:75

> Allah sets forth a parable: a slave who lacks all means, compared to a ˹free˺ man to whom We granted a good provision, of which he donates ˹freely,˺ openly and secretly. Are they equal? Praise be to Allah. In fact, most of them do not know

The parable sets two men side by side: one who is owned and has no power over anything, and one whom God has given a good provision which he spends openly and in secret. The Book asks whether they can be equal, and its expected answer is the same one the hearer already gives for people. That is the argument. If two servants of yours — one with nothing of his own and one with property — are not equal in your eyes, then the idol you keep as a partner of God is not equal to its Lord. The verse is followed by a second parable that compares a man who can do nothing with a man who commands justice and follows a straight path, so that the two stories together cover both the powerless partner and the useless one.

---

The commentators read the two men in two ways that point the same way: the slave is the idol that owns nothing and can do nothing, set against God the Provider who spends as He wills, or the two are the disbeliever and the believer, one bound by his own falsehood and the other enriched and free with what he has been given. The parable belongs to the sūrah's argument about the associate-gods: a being that possesses nothing of itself cannot be the source of anything for another, and the whole chapter is built at this point to separate what God does from what the creatures do (16:72–74). The question that closes the verse is the audience's own verdict, put to them before the conclusion is drawn.

## Verse 16:76

> And Allah sets forth a parable of two men: one of them is dumb, incapable of anything. He is a burden on his master. Wherever he is sent, he brings no good. Can such a person be equal to the one who commands justice and is on the Straight Path

The second parable draws a man who is dumb and incapable of anything, a burden on his master wherever he is sent. The Book then sets beside him the man who commands justice and stands on a straight path. The first figure is read by the commentators as a description of the idols — useless in themselves, unable to speak or to bless — and the second as a description of God, whose rule is justice and whose way is straight. The verse does not name either figure, which is how parables in this sūrah work: the hearer assigns the parts himself, and the assignment is the argument. The sūrah's own conclusion follows in the next verses, where the unseen and the Hour are declared to be God's alone.

---

The second figure is read as the idol itself or as the person who follows it: speechless, unable to act for anyone, a burden to whoever owns it. The man who commands justice and stands on a straight path is the opposite in every clause, and the Book uses the same imagery of walking a straight road for the believer's life elsewhere (6:153; 1:6). The pair of parables in this passage works by contrast: one presents the difference of power, and this one presents the difference of benefit, and the two together establish what the sūrah has been saying about the worthlessness of the gods the audience served.

**What the Parable Is Doing in This Sūrah**
The two men in this verse are the fourth picture the sūrah has used to make the same point. The Book has already set the servant who owns nothing beside the master provided for generously (16:75), and it has asked whether the one who does right and the one who does not can be equal. The verse in front of us takes the argument to its end: a man who cannot speak, who has to be carried by his master wherever he is sent and brings no good from the errand, set beside a man who commands justice and stands on a straight path. The question that follows is whether the two are equal, and the Book leaves it as a question because the answer is obvious to anyone who heard it.
**Who the Two Were Taken To Be**
The commentators read the mute figure as the idol, which cannot speak and can do nothing, and the man who commands justice as God, or as the believer who acts on God's command. Both readings were recorded, and both work with the sūrah's argument, since the idols of the town were literally silent objects and the men who served them were the ones being addressed. The Sūrah of the Heights makes the same case in plain words, saying that the false gods are created and cannot create (7:191), and the Sūrah of the Cattle asks whether the blind man and the seeing man can be the same (6:50).
**The Word for Justice**
The verse says the free man *yaʾmuru bi-al-ʿadl* — he commands justice — and the phrase is one the Book uses for the highest form of human authority, since it is the command God gives to rulers and to the community itself (16:90; 4:58). Setting a man whose whole life is the command of justice against an object that cannot utter a word is the sūrah's way of stating the difference between the creator and the made. The next verse gives the conclusion, naming the fact that belongs to God alone and asking whether the people will remember it.

## Verse 16:77

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs ˹the knowledge of˺ the unseen in the heavens and the earth. Bringing about the Hour would only take the blink of an eye, or even less. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything

The verse states both halves of the argument for the Hour: its knowledge belongs to God alone, and its coming is closer to the blink of an eye than the hearer imagines. The Book uses the same comparison in Sūrah al-Qamar, where God's command is described as a single act like the twinkling of an eye (54:50), and in Sūrah al-Rūm, where the Hour arrives in an instant (30:55). The word the translators render as "blink" — *lamḥ al-baṣar* — is a glimpse so fast that sight cannot hold it, and the verse's closing comparison, "or nearer," is left unqualified. The claim answers the demand of the mockers that the punishment be hurried, and it reduces the whole wait to something the eye cannot measure.

---

The image of the blink of an eye — and the verse adds that God's command is simply to say "Be" — is the Qur'an's standard statement of the ease with which the Hour arrives: the sūrah of the Moon says the command is but a single blast, like the twinkling of an eye (54:50), and the sūrah of al-Shūrā says the day is near and there is no doubt of it (42:17). The verse's first half states the limit of knowledge: the unseen in the heavens and the earth belongs to God alone, which answers the Makkan question about the timing of the promise, recorded earlier in the sūrah of Jonah (10:48).

## Verse 16:78

> And Allah brought you out of the wombs of your mothers while you knew nothing, and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect so perhaps you would be thankful

The verse's list of gifts is the sūrah's argument for gratitude in a single line: the child is brought out of the womb knowing nothing, and then given hearing, sight and intellect. The Book uses the same trio in Sūrah al-Muʾminūn and Sūrah al-Sajdah, where the faculties are named in order and gratitude is expected for them (23:78; 32:9), and it warns in the same sūrahs against making little use of what the organs are for — the eye, the ear, and the heart are the things a person will be asked about. The word *afʾidah*, hearts and minds, carries both the seat of understanding and of feeling, which is why the Book treats it as the organ of judgement.

**Hearing Before Sight**
The order in the verse is not casual. Hearing is named first, then sight, then the hearts, and the same order stands in the Sūrah of the Believers, the Sūrah of the Prostration, and the Sūrah of the Bee's other listing of the organs (23:78; 32:9; 76:2; 67:23). The commentators noted the reason: a child takes in the world through his ears before his eyes can weigh it, and the message that saves is heard before it is understood. The Book also states the account that comes with the organs: the hearing, the sight and the heart are all going to be asked about how they were used (17:36), and the men who had the faculties and made no use of them are described as worse than cattle, since the cattle at least do not misuse what they were given (7:179).

**The Gifts Handed Over**
The verse begins from the child's arrival with nothing: brought out of the womb knowing nothing, then given hearing, sight and the inner hearts. The Prophet's words about the new-born stand beside it: every child is born on the fiṭrah, and it is his parents who set him on the road he walks (al-Bukhārī; Muslim). The two statements fit together — a person starts with nothing, receives the organs, then receives the teaching that shows him what the organs are for — and the response the verse asks is gratitude. What that gratitude is, the sūrah goes on to say: not a warm feeling but the use of the gift for the purpose it was given. The man who hears the words of God and does not act on them still has ears. The verse's own last word is not a threat but an invitation: so that you may be thankful.

---

## Verse 16:79

> Have they not seen the birds glide in the open sky? None holds them up except Allah. Surely in this are signs for those who believe

The birds are the sūrah's standing sign of the created order, and the verse asks a question the hearers could test by looking up: who holds them up in the open sky? The Book repeats the picture in Sūrah al-Mulk, where the birds spread their wings above and close them, and the question is asked who holds them back except the Most Compassionate (67:19), and in Sūrah al-Nūr, where the birds are described in ranks, each knowing its prayer and its praise (24:41). The point in this sūrah is placed among the blessings: a bird's flight is a provision of air and balance that no human made, and the verse's sign is addressed to people who believe.

**The Question Put to the Eyes**
The verse opens with a question — have they not looked at the birds above them? — and it is a rebuke aimed at people who use their eyes without using their minds. The Book gives the birds their own place in the story of the prophets: the hills and the birds joined David in praise (21:79; 34:10), Solomon was taught the speech of the birds and marched with them in his ranks (27:16), and a bird carried his letter to Sheba (27:28). The point is never that flight is strange; it is that flight is normal, ordered and held by someone. The same question is put in the Sūrah of the Kingdom, where the birds hold their wings out and fold them in and the reader is asked who keeps them aloft but the Most Merciful (67:19). A thing that happens every morning is still the work of a hand.

**Birds as a Community**
The Book narrows the sign in another place to a sentence with a wide meaning: there is no creature on the earth, and no bird flying on its wings, that does not form communities like the human community, and all of them will be gathered to their Lord (6:38). Their praise is real in the Book's account, each of them knowing its prayer and its praise (24:41), and the law that carries a swallow through the open air is the same law that holds a person upright on the ground. The verse's ending, that here are signs for people who believe, gives the believer a habit of noticing: the rooftops, the trees and the open sky are full of evidence for anyone willing to look at the One who holds them up.

---

## Verse 16:80

> And Allah has made your homes a place to rest, and has given you tents from the hide of animals, light to handle when you travel and when you camp. And out of their wool, fur, and hair He has given you furnishings and goods for a while

The verse moves from the sky to the household, and it names the simplest goods first: the house as a place of rest, and tents from hides that are light enough to carry on the day a family travels and to pitch when they settle. The Book lists the hides alongside other materials of shelter — wool, hair and fur, and furnishings used for a time (16:80). The description is practical rather than poetic, and that is the sūrah's method in the blessing section: food, drink, children, animals, homes, clothing and armour are counted one after another so that the reader reaches the question at the end of the list — whose are they, and who gave them?

---

The list moves through the whole range of shelter: houses for rest, tents of hide that a family carries and pitches, and then the furnishings made from wool, fur and hair that the sūrah names next (16:80). The Book uses the same order in describing creation's provisions for humans in the sūrah of the Cessation: skins of the livestock for tents and furnishings (16:80; 5:95). What the verse draws out is that the simplest and most portable goods — a tent, a mat, a basket — are as much signs of provision as the sky and the cattle, and the chapter returns to the same language at 16:81 before it names the purpose of the enumeration.

**What the Verse Counts**
The sūrah has been listing the gifts of the created world since its beginning, and the verse turns from the animals and the sky to the house. It names the fixed dwelling as a place of rest, the tents made of skins that a family carries on a journey, the wool and fur and hair used for furnishings and for a while, and the shade of what God has made and the refuge in the mountains, and it closes with garments against the heat and armour against violence. The Book gives the reader a list of ordinary things and then states what they are for, and the completeness of the inventory is the point: there is no comfort in a house that the Book has not named as a gift.
**The Word for Rest**
The word used for the fixed house is the one the Book also uses elsewhere for the deliverance from hardship, so that a home is described as the place where a person is freed from the heat and the road. The Book uses the same idea for the rest that follows the labour of this life, when the people of the Garden say that all sorrow has been taken from them (35:34-35), and for the safety of a traveller who reaches his own home. The verse in front of us uses the word for a roof over a person's head, which is where the Book puts the first taste of it.
**What the Sūrah Says About the Sky's Shade and the Mountain's Shelter**
The middle of the verse names the shade God made and the mountains He made as places of refuge, and the commentators read the two halves as the protections of a journey: the shade of a tree, and the cave in a mountain. It then lists the clothing that protects from heat and the armour that protects from blows, and the Book's arrangement keeps the peaceful protection and the violent one in the same sentence. The verse after these closes the passage on the blessing of God and the response it asks for, which the sūrah states as gratitude.

## Verse 16:81

> And Allah has provided you shade out of what He created, and has given you shelter in the mountains. He has also provided you with clothes protecting you from the heat ˹and cold˺, and armour shielding you in battle. This is how He perfects His favour upon you, so perhaps you will ˹fully˺ submit ˹to Him˺

The list closes with shade, shelter in the mountains, clothing that protects against heat, and armour that protects in battle. The verse's ending names the purpose of the enumeration: this is how God perfects His favour on the people, so that they may submit to Him, and the submission is the response the whole section has been building toward. The Book uses the same verb — to perfect and complete the favour — in Sūrah al-Māʾidah, in the verse of the religion perfected at ʿArafah (5:3), so the sūrah treats the ordinary blessings of shelter and clothing as the floor of the same completion. The choice left to the hearer is stated in the same breath as the gift.

---

The shade and the mountain hideaways are followed by clothing that covers the heat and by armour that protects in battle, and the verse closes by naming the point: this is how God completes His favour on the community, so that they may submit. The Book mentions the gift of the garment in the same tone in the sūrah of the Heights: clothing to cover and to adorn, and the garment of mindfulness as the better of the two (7:26). The mention of armour recalls the gift of the same craft to David (21:80), which makes the verse's list run from the shade of a tree to the industry of nations under one sentence of thanksgiving.

**The Two Kinds of Covering**
The verse names the garments made to protect the body from heat and the coats made to protect a fighter from violence, and the Book uses the same word for a covering that is not made of cloth. The Sūrah of the Heights states the pairing directly, saying that God has given the children of Adam clothing to cover them and an adornment for them, and that the garment of awareness of God is the best of the two (7:26). The verse in front of us therefore lists the visible clothing of the world and leaves the reader with the other kind in view. The sūrah has been counting blessings since verse five, and this verse closes the catalogue with the roof over a person's head.
**The Blessings the Sūrah Had Already Counted**
The same passage has named the cattle and their warmth and their food (16:5), the horses and mules and donkeys for riding and display (16:8), the homes made of skins and wool and the furnishings taken on a journey (16:80), and the shade that anything God created gives. The verse in front of us gathers the last of these, and the arrangement is worth noticing: the Book lists the tent and the coat before it says what the list is for. The reader is being invited to see a household's ordinary comfort as a set of specific gifts.
**The Purpose in the Last Clause**
The verse ends by naming what the enumeration is meant to produce: that the favour may be completed and the people may submit. The Book uses the same word for the favour completed in the verse of the religion at ʿArafah (5:3), and it uses the word for submission as the response the whole sūrah has been asking for from its first page. The instruction the Prophet was given at the head of the sūrah — to call to the way of his Lord with wisdom and good argument (16:125) — is the human side of that submission, and the verse in front of us is the ground it stands on.

## Verse 16:82

> But if they turn away, then your duty ˹O Prophet˺ is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly

The verse answers the possibility that the audience will refuse everything the sūrah has said: if they turn away, the Messenger's duty is only to deliver the message clearly. The Book states the same limit in Sūrah al-Māʾidah, where the Messenger is told that his duty is conveyance while judgement belongs to God (5:99), and in Sūrah al-Raʿd, where the Prophet is told he is only a warner (13:40). The line is not resignation but a boundary: the responsibility for belief was never placed on the messenger, and the sūrah's next verse turns to the account the people will face for knowing the favours and denying them.

---

The limit on the messenger's duty is stated again in the sūrah of the Table, where the Prophet is told that his charge is conveyance and that God is the keeper of what they do (5:99), and in the sūrah of the Thunder, where he is told he is only a warner and that every people has a guide (13:7). The Book's rule is that the messenger is not responsible for belief, and the same limit is applied to the Prophet personally at the sūrah of the Heifer with the words that he is not over them as a guardian (6:107). The sūrah's next verse turns from the messenger's limit to the account that the audience will face for what they were shown.

## Verse 16:83

> They are aware of Allah’s favours, but still deny them. And most of them are ˹truly˺ ungrateful

The verse names the exact fault the sūrah has been describing: they know God's favour and then deny it, and most of them are ungrateful. The Arabic for gratitude here is *shukr*, and its absence is worded as *kufr*, the same word used for denial, because the sūrah treats ingratitude as a form of disbelieving rather than a lapse of manners. The Book states the same recognition at 29:67, where the disbelievers are said to see the safety God gave them and still worship other than Him, and it makes the point again in the list of blessings when it asks where the provision came from. The verse is the sūrah's summary of a people who knew enough to trust the gifts but not enough to thank the Giver.

---

## Verse 16:84

> ˹Consider, O Prophet,˺ the Day We will call ˹a prophet as˺ a witness from every faith-community. Then the disbelievers will neither be allowed to plead nor appease ˹their Lord˺

The scene moves to the Day when a witness is called from every community, and the verse notes what happens to the disbelievers in the same setting: they are not permitted to speak, and they are given no opportunity to plead. The Book gives the same picture in Sūrah al-Rūm, where no excuse will be of use on the day the Hour arrives, and in Sūrah al-Jāthiyah, where the record is read and the day is told that they will not be asked to make amends (45:27–29). The witnesses are drawn from the communities themselves, so that the case is closed on the community's own ground, and the Prophet stands as a witness over his people in the verse that follows (16:89).

---

The witness is the prophet of each community, as the sūrah of the Women states explicitly: God will bring a witness from every community and call the Prophet himself as a witness over them (4:41), and the sūrah of the Troops adds that the witnesses will be brought to the account (39:69). The scene's next detail, that the disbelievers are not permitted to speak and given no time to plead, matches the sūrah of the Cave, where the guilty speak and their excuse is not accepted (18:52). The passage is one of the Qur'an's clearest statements that revelation has reached every people, and that the reckoning of each is tied to the messenger sent among them.

## Verse 16:85

> And when the wrongdoers face the punishment, it will not be lightened for them, nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺

The verse adds two denials to the scene: the punishment will not be lightened for the wrongdoers, and they will not be given time to delay it. The Book states the same pair in several passages about the Hour — no sale, no friendship, no intercession will be accepted when it arrives (2:254), and the disbelievers will not be granted a respite (38:15). The verse's force comes from what it is answering: the mockers who demanded that the punishment be brought on (8:32), and the general human hope of one more chance. The sūrah places the statement before the scene in which the idolaters see their gods (16:86), so that the abandonment is measured against the certainty that was already stated.

---

## Verse 16:86

> And when the polytheists see their associate-gods, they will say, “Our Lord! These are our associate-gods that we used to invoke besides You.” Their gods will throw a rebuttal at them, ˹saying,˺ “You are definitely liars.”

The scene continues the Day of Judgement from the previous verse and gives it one of its sharpest exchanges. The idolaters, facing the punishment, point to the gods they served and introduce them: these are the partners we used to call on besides You. The gods answer in the same scene by throwing the accusation back: you are the liars. The Book stages the same denial elsewhere — the partners disown their worshippers and say they were not aware of their worship (10:28–30), and the idols are told to bring their proof and learn that the truth belongs to God (28:75). So the account of intercession the idolaters had based their hope on is answered by the partners themselves, and their speech becomes the evidence against them.

---

The gods are asked to deny the association, and the sūrah of the Stories records their answer in full: those against whom the word has come due say, "It was you who came to us with the promises of God", and the others say, "No, it was you who used to come to us as deceivers" (28:63). The same exchange appears in the sūrah of the Cattle, where the associates say it was not they who were worshipped (6:22–23), and in the sūrah of Jonah, where the partners are removed from the scene altogether (10:28–30). The verse's point is that the partnership the idolaters asserted in this life is a claim no one will be willing to own at the reckoning.

## Verse 16:87

> They will offer ˹full˺ submission to Allah on that Day, and whatever ˹gods˺ they fabricated will fail them

The verse records what the idolaters do when the Day comes: they offer submission to God, and whatever they invented as gods fails them. The Book gives the same confession elsewhere — the idols will disown the worship and deny the claim of the worshippers, as in Sūrah Yūnus, where the partners will be asked whether they called the people to worship them and will deny it (10:28–30). In Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ, where they call and are not answered (28:64). The submission of this verse is the same surrender the sūrah named earlier in connection with the Day, since all will be submissive before the One whose claim was refused in the life they were given.

---

## Verse 16:88

> For those who disbelieve and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, We will add more punishment to their punishment for all the corruption they spread

The verse names the addition to the punishment and gives the reason for it: they disbelieved and they hindered others from the way of God, and so they are punished for the corruption they made. The Book's doctrine of doubled guilt for those who mislead is stated in several places — the leaders whose burden includes a share of those who followed (29:13). The verse in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf where the followed and the followers each receive the punishment and the followers are told they have no superiority over their leaders (7:38–39). The verse therefore treats the act of blocking the road as a separate offence from disbelief, with its own measure, and this sūrah is unusually explicit that the punishment is added rather than replaced.

**The Burden Carried by the Misleaders**
The verse says the punishment is increased, and the rest of the Book explains where the increase comes from. The men who block the road carry their own wrong and a share of the wrong of those they turned aside: the same sūrah states that they will carry their own burdens in full on the Day of Resurrection and other burdens along with them, for the people they misled without knowledge (16:25). The followers are not excused because they were led. In the Sūrah of the Heights each group enters the Fire cursing the one before it, and the followers are told that their leaders have no superiority over them (7:38-39), and in the Fire the men who were misled ask God to give the leaders who brought them there a double punishment (33:68; 38:61). The verse in front of us makes the same division: disbelief is one offence, and closing the road to others is another with its own weight.

**The One Who Sets the Road**
The Prophet's words make the teaching plain: whoever begins a good way in Islam has its reward and the reward of everyone who follows it, and whoever begins an evil way carries its sin and the sin of everyone who follows it, without anything being taken from the followers' own record (Muslim). That sentence explains the verse's language of addition — the sum grows with every person the misleader turns aside. The sūrah calls the act corruption, the Book's widest word for damage done to the common life, and it has already shown the motive earlier in the same chapter: a cheap price taken for God's covenant, and men who lead others away so that they themselves may carry the load (16:95; 16:25). A man may keep his own heart in order and still have ruined the road for everyone else, and the verse gives that ruin its own line in the record.

---

## Verse 16:89

> ˹Consider, O Prophet,˺ the Day We will call against every faith-community a witness of their own. And We will call you to be a witness against these ˹people of yours˺. We have revealed to you the Book as an explanation of all things, a guide, a mercy, and good news for those who ˹fully˺ submit

The verse states the day when a witness from each community is called and the Prophet is called as a witness over these people, and then it states what the Book is in the same breath: an explanation of everything, a guidance, a mercy, and good tidings for those who submit. The Book claims the same completeness elsewhere, in the verse that says nothing has been omitted from it (6:38) and in the one that calls it a clarification of all things (12:111). The Prophet's role as witness over the community that received him is part of the Book's pattern for every messenger, and the sūrah has just shown the witnesses of the earlier communities being brought forward (16:84).

---

**The Day the Witness Stands**
*Wa-yawma nabʿathu fī kulli ummatin shahīdan ʿalayhim min anfusihim* — the day We raise in every community a witness from among themselves. The witness is one of their own, a man they knew, which is what closes the mouth of every excuse: no community can claim it never heard. The verse then gives the Prophet his place in that scene: *and We bring you as a witness over these* — over the community he was sent to. The Book names the same arrangement for the believers as a body: the Messenger is a witness over you and you are witnesses over the people (2:143), so the testimony on that day is not a formality but the completion of the office the community was given.
**A Book That Explains Everything**
The second half of the verse states what the Book is: *tibyānan li-kulli shayʾ* — an explanation of everything, and a guidance and a mercy and good tidings for those who submit. The claim is not that the Book contains every fact a person might want, but that nothing needed for finding the way has been left out of the revelation given to the Prophet; the Book points to its completeness in the same terms elsewhere, where it says nothing has been omitted from the Book (6:38) and where it calls the message a clarification of all things (12:111). The verse places that claim on the Day of the witnesses, which is where the claim will be tested.

## Verse 16:90

> Indeed, Allah commands justice, grace, as well as courtesy to close relatives. He forbids indecency, wickedness, and aggression. He instructs you so perhaps you will be mindful

The verse is one of the most recited in the Book, and its subject is the whole of social morality in three commands and three prohibitions. *ʿAdl* is justice that gives each person his due; *iḥsān* is the generosity that goes beyond what is due; and *ītāʾ dhī al-qurbā* is the kindness owed to relatives. Against them stand *faḥshāʾ*, acts of gross indecency; *munkar*, what sound minds reject; and *baghy*, aggression that oversteps the rights of others. The Book's order runs from the widest obligation to the narrowest, which is how the sūrah of Naḥl arranges a community's duties, and the last clause states the purpose of the whole instruction: that they should remember. The classical commentators read the verse as the sūrah's summary of the law's intent.

**The Verse's Standing Among the Early Muslims**
Commentators report that ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd called this the most comprehensive verse in the Book for what it orders and forbids, and that the Prophet recited it in sermons — the sūrah's own close gives it the same weight. The verse contains three commands and three prohibitions, and each pair has been worked out in the law: justice and generosity in dealings (4:58; 5:8), kindness to relatives as a debt and not a favour, and the ban on indecency, on what sound minds reject, and on aggression that takes what is another's. The Book sets the same list in other places as the substance of the covenant: the commands of Sūrah al-Anʿām, and the verse that names the most honoured as the most mindful (6:151; 49:13). The verse closes by telling the hearers why they were given all of it — so that they would remember — and the verse after it turns that memory into a duty, commanding them to keep the covenant of God they have just been reminded of (16:91).

## Verse 16:91

> Honour Allah’s covenant when you make a pledge, and do not break your oaths after confirming them, having made Allah your guarantor. Surely Allah knows all you do

The verse commands the honouring of a covenant and prohibits the breaking of oaths after they have been confirmed, on the ground that God has been made guarantor over them. The Arabic *ʿahd* is the pledge taken with God as witness, so the verse's argument is that a broken oath is not merely a broken promise to a person but a claim filed against the swearer with the One in whose name it was made. The Book restates this duty in the passage on the community that asks how it can break its treaty with those who broke theirs first (9:4, 9:7), and it orders fidelity to treaties even where the other side is difficult, as long as they are straight with the community. The sūrah then devotes four more verses to the same instruction, because the subject is the community's credibility.

---

The commentators attached the verse to the pledges the community had made to the Prophet ﷺ — the covenants of ʿAqabah, and the general undertakings given when the community was organised in Madinah — and read the words as a rule for every binding promise: an oath sworn with God as witness is not the speaker's to break at convenience. The Book repeats the command in several places, including the direct warning not to violate the pledge (17:34) and the oath law of the sūrah of the Table, which provides expiation for a broken oath (5:89). The verse's closing reminder, that God knows what they do, is what makes the covenant a matter of standing rather than of words.

## Verse 16:92

> Do not be like the woman who ˹foolishly˺ unravels her yarn after it is firmly spun, by taking your oaths as a means of deceiving one another in favour of a stronger group. Surely Allah tests you through this. And on the Day of Judgment He will certainly make your differences clear to you

The comparison is domestic and unforgettable: a woman who spins her yarn firmly and then unravels it thread by thread. That is what the community does when it makes oaths a means of deceiving one another, gaining an advantage by swearing and then swearing the opposite when a better offer appears. The verse states the reason God is testing them in this: the oaths become the test of whether the community can be trusted with its own word, and the Book states elsewhere that a promise given and broken is not a private matter but a public one (17:34). The sūrah's closing statement on the subject is that whatever the world offers in exchange for the covenant, what God has is better.

---

**The Word for the Loophole**
The verse supplies the term for the practice it forbids: *dakhalan*, something smuggled inside a thing to spoil it, the flaw a man introduces into his own case. The oaths were honest in form and rotten inside, because the swearer was not binding himself; he was keeping a door open to the better offer. The sūrah pairs the image with the community as a single body: tribal numbers were the currency of power in Arabia, so a group could gain by making a treaty and then taking a more favourable one when the balance shifted.
**What the Verse Says Will Happen**
The end of the verse answers the man who thinks an unkept promise costs nothing but words: *God will surely test you with it and will surely make clear to you on the Day of Resurrection that wherein you differed*. The test is in this life, where the bargain looks like a gain; the clarification is on the Day, when the ledger is opened. The verse that follows adds the reason none of it can be hidden: had God willed, He could have made the whole of mankind one community, and it is by His leave that they are not (16:93).

## Verse 16:93

> Had Allah willed, He could have easily made you one community ˹of believers˺, but He leaves to stray whoever He wills and guides whoever He wills. And you will certainly be questioned about what you used to do

The verse answers the argument that division in the community is God's doing. It states the limit of that claim in the same line: if God willed, He could have made all of them one community, but He leaves to stray whom He wills and guides whom He wills, and He will question every one of them about what they were doing. The Book states the same in Sūrah al-Māʾidah, where God could have made a single community but did not, and in Sūrah Yūnus, where the people are told that if God willed, everyone on earth would have believed (10:99). The verse therefore does not absolve the community: the questioning in its final clause belongs to those who were given a choice, and the sūrah's next verses name the oath-making that brought it about.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**
The sentence *He leaves to stray whom He wills and guides whom He wills* is the hardest kind of statement in the Book to handle, and the verse itself sets its own limit. The line does not end there; it ends with *and you will surely be questioned about what you were doing*. The two halves have to be read together: if the guidance and the straying were the whole story, there would be nobody left to question. The Book states the same pairing in the sūrah of the Table, where God could have made humankind one community and did not (5:48), and in the sūrah of Jonah, where the Prophet is told that if God willed, everyone on earth would believe (10:99) — and then told not to grieve himself over the people who will not.
**What the Verse Is Answering**
The sūrah has just described the community that broke its oaths and unraveled its own weaving (16:92), and this verse refuses the excuse they were reaching for: that their division was God's doing, since He could have made them one community if He had wanted. The Book grants the premise and rejects the conclusion. The existence of the choice is what makes the questioning possible, and the next verses name the oath-breaking that produced the division — the deceit, the trade of God's covenant for a small price, the slipping of a foot after it had been planted — as the thing the community itself did. The verse is an answer to a very old human move: blaming the arrangement for what the hand did with it.

## Verse 16:94

> And do not take your oaths as a means of deceiving one another or your feet will slip after they have been firm. Then you will taste the evil ˹consequences˺ of hindering ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, and you will suffer a tremendous punishment

The warning is built on the image of feet slipping after they were firm: the community holds its footing through the credibility of its word, and oath-breaking removes the ground. The verse's ending names the consequence in the next world — a taste of evil for having turned others away from the path of God, and great punishment. The Book uses the same picture of firm footing for the community's state after the treaty at Ḥudaybiyah, where the believers are said to have been sent serenity so that their feet might be fixed (48:4, 48:26), and it warns elsewhere against the deeper slip that follows a broken pledge (3:102–103). The sūrah places this warning after the parable of the yarn, so that the moral and the metaphor arrive together.

---

**The Covenant and the Small Price**
The warning in this verse is followed by the verse that explains the trade-off: *and do not exchange the covenant of God for a small price; what is with God is better for you, if you only knew* (16:95). The buyers in this market were powerful tribes offering protection, alliance or a share of the caravan; the price paid was the community's word. The Book calls this small — not because the money was small in any man's hand, but because it was being weighed against what God has, and the comparison is what the verse insists on.
**The Marks of the Same Weakness**
The Prophet described the conduct of a person whose word is not a word, in a report that has reached us on several chains: the marks of the hypocrite are three — when he speaks he lies, when he makes a promise he breaks it, when he is trusted he betrays the trust (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 33). The verse's image of the foot that slides is that same diagnosis in a different register: what is shaken by the breaking of an oath is not only a contract but a person's whole footing, in this life and after it.

## Verse 16:95

> And do not trade Allah’s covenant for a fleeting gain. What is with Allah is certainly far better for you, if only you knew

The verse states the exchange the community is being offered and refuses it on the community's behalf: do not trade the covenant of God for a small price, because what is with God is far better, if they only knew. The Book uses the same measure for the world in Sūrah al-Shūrā, where the provision of God is better and more lasting (42:36), and it names the price of a religion sold cheap in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where the traders of the covenant are described (2:79). The clause "if only you knew" is the sūrah's standing way of pointing at the gap between what a person sees and what is in front of him, and it is repeated at 16:96 and 16:97 in the same passage.

---

The trade described is the sale of one's pledge for a price in this life, and the Book states the same bargain in the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān, where those who sell the covenant and their oaths for a small gain have no share in the Hereafter (3:77), and in the sūrah of the Heifer, where the scribes who wrote their own book for a price are warned with the same words (2:79). The verse's reasoning — that what is with God is better for them if they knew — is the sūrah's recurring argument against the Makkans' calculation, which the next verse states in full: what you have comes to an end and what God has is lasting.

## Verse 16:96

> Whatever you have will end, but whatever Allah has is everlasting. And We will certainly reward the steadfast according to the best of their deeds

The verse completes the exchange with the plainest arithmetic in the Book: what you have will end, and what God has is everlasting. Then it adds the promise to those who are patient — the reward given according to the best of what they did, not according to the total of it. The Book repeats the same measure in Sūrah al-Zumar, where the reward is the best of what people did (39:35), and in Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt, where the patient are paid without measure (29:58; see also 39:10). The pairing matters for the sūrah's audience: the goods they gamble their oaths for are the very things that run out, and the patience the verse asks for is the discipline of holding on to what does not.

---

The verse states the exchange as a matter of fact: what the hearers possess is subject to ending, and what God holds does not end, and the reward is then attached to patience — *ṣabirūn*, the steadfast — measured by the best of what they did rather than by the sum. The Book states the same pair in the sūrah of the Cave, where the enduring good works are better with the Lord and better in hope (18:46), and urges the same calculation in the sūrah of the Nights, where the life to come is better and more lasting (87:16–17). The verse's phrase about the best of their deeds is the measure the Qur'an applies to the reward in other places too (39:35; 29:7).

## Verse 16:97

> Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, We will surely bless them with a good life, and We will certainly reward them according to the best of their deeds

The verse states the reward in terms that suit both halves of the audience: whoever does good, male or female, and is a believer, then a good life and a wage measured by the best of their deeds. The condition of faith is placed between the deed and the reward, so the sūrah is not describing a moralism that ignores belief; and the phrase "male or female" is repeated by the Book wherever it wants it understood that the reward does not depend on gender (3:195; 4:124; 33:35). The "good life" the verse promises is read by the commentators in two ways — provision in this world and contentment of heart, and contentment in the Hereafter — and the wage that follows is for the deeds themselves.

---

The same promise is given in the sūrah of the Women with nearly identical words — that the doer of good, male or female, with faith will enter the Garden and not be wronged by a date-stone's thread (4:124) — and it answers a question the community raised about whether women's good deeds would be rewarded as men's. The commentators differed over what the good life means: a sufficient living in this world, contentment with what God provides, or the life of the Garden itself, with most holding that the two belong together. The verse's condition of faith is what distinguishes the promise from a general optimism about good deeds, since the sūrah has already tied the benefit of deeds to belief.

## Verse 16:98

> When you recite the Quran, seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed

The instruction is attached to a specific act: when you recite the Qur'an, seek refuge with God from Satan the accursed. The Book orders the same in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, where the Prophet is told to say when a whisper from Satan reaches him that he seeks refuge with God, who is Hearing and Knowing (7:200). In Sūrah Fuṣṣilat, where the answer to an evil suggestion is to seek refuge with the One who hears and knows (41:36). The formula *aʿūdhu bi-llāh*, said before the recitation, is the practice the verse establishes, and the sūrah explains in the next two verses why the refuge works: the accursed has no authority over those who believe and rely on their Lord.

---

The command belongs to the etiquette of recitation, and the Book orders the same resort in the sūrah of the Heights, where the Prophet is told that if a whisper from Satan comes he should seek refuge with God, who is Hearing and Knowing (7:200), and in the sūrah of Fussilat with the same instruction (41:36). The scholars held that the seeking of refuge is recommended before recitation rather than part of the recitation itself, and they read the order of the sūrah's verses as deliberate: the passage on revelation and its carrier (16:102) is followed by the protection requested for the act of reading.

## Verse 16:99

> He certainly has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord

The verse states the limit of the enemy's power in terms of trust: he has no authority over those who believe and put their reliance on their Lord. The Book makes the same claim in Sūrah al-Ḥijr, where the answer to Satan's boast is that God's sincere servants are beyond him (15:40–42), and in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ, where his power is described as sufficiency for those he drags away but not authority over the ones who take refuge (17:65). This is the answer to the previous verse: the refuge works because the refuge-taker belongs to God, and the whisperer's influence stops at the boundary of reliance. The next verse names the people over whom he does have authority.

---

## Verse 16:100

> His authority is only over those who take him as a patron and who—under his influence—associate ˹others˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺

The verse names the people the enemy does work on: those who take him as a patron, and under his influence associate others with God. The willingness is stated first — adopting him as a protector — and the consequence follows, which is the sūrah's consistent order: the whisper is not compulsion but an opening that a person gives. The Book uses the same word, *tawallā*, for adopting the enemy as a friend, and it warns elsewhere that whoever takes the devil as an ally will be misled and left with a delusion (4:119–120; 18:50). The verse therefore closes the pair that began with the command to seek refuge by describing who the refuge protects: not those who claim it while walking with the enemy, but those who rely on God alone.

---

## Verse 16:101

> When We replace a verse with another—and Allah knows best what He reveals—they say, “You ˹Muḥammad˺ are just a fabricator.” In fact, most of them do not know

The verse answers a specific complaint. When a verse was replaced by another — a ruling adjusted, or a passage exchanged for one more suitable to the community's stage — the opponents seized on the change as proof that the Prophet was inventing the text as he went. The Book's answer is that God knows best what He reveals, and it adds that most of the accusers do not know what they are talking about: *bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn*. The same objection is answered elsewhere with the reminder that the One who sent the Book down can replace it, since the revelation is His to give in the measure He decides (2:106; 13:39). The verse's purpose in the sūrah is to show that the accusers were not weighing the content of what came but the fact that anything came at all.

**The Complaint About Changing Verses**
The verse reports an objection that had a practical cause. When a ruling was replaced by another — a verse of the Qur'an set aside and a new one sent in its place — the mockers in Makkah treated it as proof that the recitation was being made up as it went along. The Book answers the charge by stating who owns the process: God knows best what He sends down and when. The Sūrah of the Cow gives the rule in full, saying that when a verse is replaced God brings one better than it or like it (2:106), and the change of the direction of prayer, the rulings on intoxicants and some of the rules on marriage and waiting are the clearest cases where the community watched this happen.
**Why the Teachings Came in Stages**
The Book gives the reason in more than one place, and the clearest is in the way the prohibition of wine arrives: first named as a thing with more harm than good (2:219), then forbidden close to the prayer (4:43), then forbidden outright (5:90-91). A community being built out of a society that had known none of this was taught in steps, and the steps are the mercy the verse's hearers were calling forgery. The verse closes by saying that most of them do not know — not that they are lying, but that they have not understood what they are looking at. The next verse names the tongue of the recitation itself as the answer.

## Verse 16:102

> Say, “The holy spirit has brought it down from your Lord with the truth to reassure the believers, and as a guide and good news for those who submit ˹to Allah˺.”

The verse names the carrier of the revelation and the purpose of its coming down: the holy spirit brought it from your Lord with the truth, to reassure the believers, and as a guide and good news for those who submit. The Book names the same spirit as Gabriel in another place, where the Book says he brought it down on the Prophet's heart by God's permission (2:97; 26:193–194), so the verse answers the charge that a human teacher was dictating the text (16:103) by naming the actual bringer. The purpose stated here — strengthening the believers — reappears wherever the Book describes what the revelation does for a community under pressure (8:2; 9:124–125). The verse follows the accusation of fabrication in 16:101 and prepares the answer to the human teacher in the next verse, so the two verses answer one accusation between them.

---

The carrier is identified elsewhere in the Book: the faithful spirit who brings the revelation on the Prophet's heart by God's permission (26:193; 2:97), and the same name is used in the sūrah of the Table for the support given to Jesus (5:110). The verse's three purposes — truth, reassurance for the believers, and good news for those who submit — match the sūrah's portrait of the revelation as guidance and healing (16:89), and the objection it answers is the Makkan charge that the Prophet was being taught by a human being, to which the sūrah's answer is that the message comes by the spirit from the Lord of the worlds.

## Verse 16:103

> And We surely know that they say, “No one is teaching him except a human.” But the man they refer to speaks a foreign tongue, whereas this ˹Quran˺ is ˹in˺ eloquent Arabic

The verse is the sūrah's direct answer to the accusation that a man was teaching the Prophet: the person they point to speaks a foreign tongue, while this recitation is in clear Arabic. The Book records the same charge elsewhere and answers it the same way, by pointing to the language of the text and its content (44:14; 26:195), and it notes that the accusers did not need evidence because the point of the accusation was to avoid the conclusion that the recitation was from God. The verse's name for the text — *lisānun ʿarabiyyun mubīn*, a clear Arabic tongue — is one of the Book's own descriptions of itself, alongside the descriptions of it as a guide, a mercy, and a reminder. The next verse states the consequence for those who will not believe what they hear.

**What the Accusation Was and Why It Failed**
The charge was that a man was secretly teaching the Prophet what to say. The sūrah's answer is a fact anyone in Makkah could check: the man they were pointing at was not an Arabic speaker, and what they were being asked to disbelieve was recitation in the clearest Arabic, with the kind of structure no translated sentence could have carried. The Book makes the same point elsewhere when it answers the same charge — this is a lie, and the tongue in question is plainly foreign while the words are Arabic. The illiterate trader they were describing as a pupil could not have produced a single line of the thing his accusers were standing in the street to hear.
**What the Verse Calls the Text**
The last words of the verse are the Book's own name for itself: *a clear Arabic tongue* — *lisānun ʿarabiyyun mubīn*. The sūrah has just called the revelation a guidance, a mercy and a reminder in the verses before, and the word for the language is the one that carries the sense of making something plain. A reader who had heard the recitation knew what the words were and what they meant; the accusers' problem was not comprehension, and the sūrah is careful to say so. The next verse states plainly what happens to the people who refuse to believe what is presented to them in their own language: a punishment for those who turn away from the signs of God.

## Verse 16:104

> Surely those who do not believe in Allah’s revelations will never be guided by Allah, and they will suffer a painful punishment

The verse makes guidance depend on receiving what is sent down: those who do not believe in the signs of God will not be guided, and for them is a painful punishment. The Book states the same connection in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, where those who reject the signs and are too arrogant to accept them will not enter the gate of the Garden until a camel passes through the eye of a needle (7:40). In Sūrah al-Kahf, where the signs are wasted on the heedless. The sūrah has just answered the charge that a human was teaching the Prophet (16:103), so this verse states the consequence of not accepting the answer, and the next verse names those who are the real liars in this dispute.

---

## Verse 16:105

> No one fabricates lies except those who disbelieve in Allah’s revelations, and it is they who are the ˹true˺ liars

The verse turns the charge back on its makers in a sentence that has become one of the Book's standing principles: no one fabricates lies except those who do not believe in the signs of God, and those are the liars. The sūrah has just answered the claim that a man was dictating the revelation (16:103) and the claim that the Prophet was inventing it (16:101), so the accusation of forgery is answered by naming who in the argument actually forges. The Book states the same principle elsewhere, where it denies that the Prophet was forging anything and challenges the forgers instead (10:37–38; 11:13). The verse closes the passage with a definition rather than a denial.

---

## Verse 16:106

> Whoever disbelieves in Allah after their belief—not those who are forced while their hearts are firm in faith, but those who embrace disbelief wholeheartedly—they will be condemned by Allah and suffer a tremendous punishment

The verse treats a case the community would soon face: someone who disbelieves after believing. It makes an explicit exception for the one who is forced to deny while his heart remains firm in faith. It puts the condemnation on the person who opens his own breast to disbelief, of which the Book says the wrath of God is upon him and there is a great punishment. The verse is read against the reports of the companions who were tortured in Makkah for saying what they did not believe, and against the Sūrah al-Muʾminūn and Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt passages where the trial of faith is described. The sūrah's interest is the distinction between a tongue under duress and a heart that has turned, and the next verse states the motive for the second.

---

The occasion the commentators give for the verse is the compulsion suffered by the early Muslims in Makkah, and the report of ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir is the one most often cited: made to utter the words of denial under torture while his heart remained with the faith, he came to the Prophet ﷺ distressed, and was met with the question of how he found his heart and told that if it happened again he should do the same. The verse is the sūrah's ruling on that case: the forced word is not the apostasy, and the offence belongs to those who embrace disbelief and open their hearts to it. The principle stated here is one the jurists kept, that intention and compulsion are weighed in the assessment of speech.

## Verse 16:107

> This is because they prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter. Surely Allah never guides those who ˹choose to˺ disbelieve

The verse names the reason for the disbelief it has just condemned: the preference of this life over the Hereafter. The Book uses the same diagnosis throughout, and the sūrah has already shown what that preference costs in the passage on oaths — a cheap price for the covenant. The second half of the verse states the consequence in God's own judgement: God does not guide a disbelieving people. The commentators note the order: the preference comes first, and the withholding of guidance follows it, which keeps the sūrah's doctrine of responsibility intact. The next two verses describe what that withholding looks like from inside the person who has chosen it.

---

## Verse 16:108

> They are the ones whose hearts, ears, and eyes are sealed by Allah, and it is they who are ˹truly˺ heedless

The verse lists what the refusal produces: hearts, ears and eyes sealed by God. The Book uses the same image at the head of Sūrah al-Baqarah, where God is said to have sealed the hearts of the disbelievers and covered their sight (2:7). In Sūrah al-Jāthiyah, where the one who takes his own desire as a god is left with a seal on his hearing and heart and a cover on his sight (45:23). The three organs are the ones the Book names as the means of understanding — ears, eyes, hearts — so the punishment described is the loss of the very faculties by which the message would have reached them. The sūrah ends the list with the word that fits the state: they are the heedless.

---

## Verse 16:109

> Without a doubt, they will be the losers in the Hereafter

The verse draws the conclusion in the plainest form: without a doubt, they will be the losers in the Hereafter. The Book uses the same verdict at the close of Sūrah al-Baqarah, where those who trade guidance for error lose the bargain (2:16), and in Sūrah al-Zumar, where the losers on the Day of Resurrection are named as those who lose themselves and their families (39:15). The sūrah places the sentence here to close the passage on the sealed organs and to prepare the contrast that follows, where those who emigrated, struggled and persevered are given the promise of forgiveness and mercy in the same terms.

---

## Verse 16:110

> As for those who emigrated after being compelled ˹to renounce Islam˺, then struggled ˹in Allah’s cause˺, and persevered, your Lord ˹O Prophet˺ is truly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful after all

The verse marks a change of case, and it is the hope that the previous verses left open. Those who emigrated after being put to trial, then struggled and were patient: for them the Lord is Forgiving and Merciful. The Book tells the same story in Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt, where the Mecca community is promised that their dwelling after migration will be better (29:56–58), and it repeats the combination of trial, migration and patience in Sūrah al-Naḥl's own later passage. The sūrah's structure is deliberate: the sealed hearts of the heedless are stated, and immediately after them the open door that those same hearts could have taken, with the cost measured honestly — migration, struggle, patience.

**Who the Verse Is About**
The verse follows a passage on those whose hearts were sealed and on the one who was forced to deny his faith while his heart stayed at peace (16:106-109). It is read as the promise given to the believers who were taken and tortured in Makkah and then left — first to Abyssinia, then to Madinah — and the words are chosen for that experience: they were put to trial, then they migrated, then they struggled, then they were patient, and after all of it the verse gives them forgiveness and mercy rather than a list of what they owed. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt speaks to the same people in the same order, promising that the home they go to will be better than the home they left (29:56-58), and the Sūrah of the Spoils puts migration and struggle together among the things that raise a person's rank (8:72; 9:20). The sūrah deliberately sets the sealed hearts of the heedless first and this verse immediately after, so the reader sees the same circumstances and the two possible ends.

## Verse 16:111

> ˹Consider˺ the Day ˹when˺ every soul will come pleading for itself, and each will be paid in full for what it did, and none will be wronged

The scene of the Day is stated in one line: every soul comes pleading for itself, and every soul is paid in full for what it did, and none is wronged. The Book uses the same words in Sūrah al-Muʾmin, where the Day is described as the day every soul is repaid for what it earned, with no injustice and no intercession and no ransom (40:17–18). In Sūrah Luqmān, where the promise is that nothing is hidden and every deed is brought forward (31:16). The word for the pleading, *tujādilu ʿan nafsihā*, describes an argument a person makes on his own behalf — the same habit that filled a lifetime of debate — now reduced to a single case with no time for excuses.

**The Day the Argument Belongs to One Party**
The verse's Day is the Day every soul comes to plead, and the pleading has a history behind it: the people who spent a lifetime arguing against the messengers will find that on that Day the argument serves their own account and nothing else. The Book describes the shape of that pleading elsewhere: the wrongdoer bites his hands and wishes he had taken the path with the Messenger (25:27-29), the follower wishes he were as far from his leader as the two ends of the earth (2:166-167), and the one who was led blames the one who led. None of it delays the payment. Every soul is repaid in full for what it earned, and no soul is wronged (2:281; 21:47; 45:22). What the verse leaves out speaks as loudly as what it says: it names no advocate, no appeal and no postponement for a life that never asked for forgiveness.

**Where the Verse Stands in the Sūrah**
The verses before it describe the community's trial: those who emigrated after being made to suffer, who then struggled and endured, and whose Lord is forgiving and merciful (16:110). The verse in front of us is the horizon of that endurance. The men who lost their homes for the faith are told of a Day when the whole account is settled by the God who saw the patience when nobody else did. The sūrah then returns to the command to call people to the way of the Lord with wisdom and fair preaching (16:125), and it closes with patience and the reminder that God is with those who are mindful of Him and who do good (16:127-128). The placement shows the link between what a believer does in a hard season and what is counted for him at the end of the road.

---

## Verse 16:112

> And Allah sets forth the example of a society which was safe and at ease, receiving its provision in abundance from all directions. But its people met Allah’s favours with ingratitude, so Allah made them taste the clutches of hunger and fear for their misdeeds

The parable describes a town that was safe and at ease, receiving its provision in abundance from every direction, and then denied the favours of God and was clothed by God in hunger and fear for what its people had been doing. The place is understood by the commentators to be Makkah, which the Book elsewhere names as the sanctuary where people find safety while others are being carried off (29:67; 106:1–4), and the parable is a warning to the same city: the safety it lives in is a gift, and the gift came with a condition. The Book finishes the thought in the next verse by naming the messenger who actually came to that town and was denied, and the torment that followed while they were wrongdoers.

---

The commentators identified the town of the parable with Makkah in most of the reports, since it was secure, well-supplied by caravans from every direction, and then met hunger and fear after it had denied the messenger raised among it. The same description appears in the sūrah of the Stories, where the sanctuary is called a place of safety and the provision is brought together there (28:57), and the sūrah's closing account of the Quraysh's security in the sūrah of Quraysh (106:3–4) states the same favour in the positive. The verse's point is the exchange: a community that treats God's provision as its own achievement loses the thing it took for granted, and what it is then clothed in is stated with the same physical word — hunger and fear.

## Verse 16:113

> A messenger of their own actually did come to them, but they denied him. So the torment overtook them while they persisted in wrongdoing

The verse identifies the messenger as one of their own — *rasūlan minhum* — and the phrase removes the excuse that the messenger was a stranger with foreign claims, which the Book answers elsewhere by saying that every messenger was sent in the language and the community of his people (14:4). The denial is followed immediately by the consequence, and the Book states this pattern in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where a sending and a denial are followed by the taking of the town (2:87–90). The verse also ends the sūrah's sequence on ingratitude: the town knew its safety, was reminded by one of its own sons, and lost both.

---

The verse's phrase, that the messenger came from among them, answers the accusation recorded earlier in the sūrah that the Prophet was taught by a foreigner (16:103); the same defence is made in the sūrah of Joseph, where the messengers are said to have been men from the towns they addressed (12:109), and in the sūrah of the Bee at 16:43. The warning that follows — that the torment overtook them while they were wrongdoers — is the sūrah's own summary of what happened to the towns of the past, and the passage closes the account of the town with the same verdict it gave to them.

## Verse 16:114

> So eat from the good, lawful things which Allah has provided for you, and be grateful for Allah’s favours, if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺

The command is to eat what is lawful and good from what God has provided, and to be grateful for His favours if He is the one being worshipped. The verse links the legal and the devotional: gratitude is named as the proper proof of worship, and the sūrah places the command just before the list of what has actually been forbidden. The Book states the same in Sūrah al-Māʾidah, where the command to eat of the good things provided is joined to the duty to be mindful of God, in whom the believers trust (5:88). The form of the verse is worth noting: permission comes before prohibition in this sūrah, which is the Book's habit when it addresses a community free to choose.

---

The command repeats what the sūrah of the Heifer states to the whole of humanity: eat of what is lawful and good on the earth, and do not follow the footsteps of Satan (2:168), and what the sūrah of the Table gives the believers in the same form (5:88). The pairing of the two commands in this verse — eat what is lawful, and be grateful — is the sūrah's method throughout: the legal permission and the devotional response are one act, since the lawful food is taken as provision from God rather than as an entitlement. The verse's condition, that the gratitude is owed if it is God alone who is worshipped, ties the diet to the doctrine the sūrah has been establishing.

## Verse 16:115

> He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah. But if someone is compelled by necessity—neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

The verse lists the four prohibitions — carrion, blood, the flesh of swine. What has been slaughtered in the name of anything other than God — and then states the exception for the one driven by necessity, neither desiring nor going to excess, with the note that God is Forgiving and Merciful. The Book repeats the list in Sūrah al-Baqarah, with an extra clause on the one who is compelled, and in Sūrah al-Māʾidah, where the flaunting of the same items is linked to the corruption of the markets (2:173; 5:3). The sūrah's treatment keeps the two ends together: the rule is stated plainly, and the door of necessity is left open by the same verse, which is the Book's usual way with legal burdens.

---

The same four prohibitions are listed in the sūrah of the Heifer (2:173), the sūrah of the Table (5:3) and the sūrah of the Cattle (6:145), with the last of these adding the flesh of an animal slaughtered in the name of other than God to the same list. The exception stated here — for the one driven by hunger, neither desiring the forbidden nor going beyond what is needed — is the basis of the juristic rule of necessity, which the scholars applied to food, drink and medicine in a case of danger to life. The verse's closing pair, that God is Forgiving and Merciful, is attached to the concession rather than to the offence.

## Verse 16:116

> Do not falsely declare with your tongues, “This is lawful, and that is unlawful,” ˹only˺ fabricating lies against Allah. Indeed, those who fabricate lies against Allah will never succeed

The verse forbids a specific practice: declaring things lawful or unlawful with nothing but the tongue, and attaching the declaration to God. The Book elsewhere condemns the same habit among those who invented religious rules — the practice of setting aside animals and crops as offerings in God's name while treating them as forbidden (6:136–138), and the claim that certain things were permitted to one group and not another without proof. The verse's closing clause states the outcome: those who fabricate lies against God will never succeed, and the next verse adds what their fabrication converts into — a brief enjoyment, and then the painful punishment. The scholars read the verse as a standing warning against ruling on religious matters without evidence, applied in every age.

---

The practice the verse forbids is the invention of food laws and the attribution of them to God, which the sūrah of the Cattle describes at length in the catalogue of the bahīrah, sāʾibah, waṣīlah and ḥām (5:103), and which the sūrah of Jonah condemns when it asks whether God has permitted what they make unlawful (10:59). The offence is placed among the great sins because it puts a human decision in the place of revelation, and the Book attaches the same warning to any who conceal or invent what they have not been given (2:79). The verse's ending states the consequence in the plainest terms: those who fabricate lies against God will not prosper.

## Verse 16:117

> ˹It is only˺ a brief enjoyment, then they will suffer a painful punishment

The verse answers the forgery of the previous one with the shortest possible account of what it buys: a brief enjoyment, and then a painful punishment. The two-word summary of worldly gain — *matāʿun qalīl* — is one of the Book's standing measures of everything that is traded for the truth, used for the price the disbelievers accept in Sūrah Āl-ʿImrān and for the provision of this life generally (3:197; 9:38). The sūrah has just described people inventing a ruling of their own and attributing it to God, and the verse states the outcome without ornament: the enjoyment is short, and the punishment is not. The next verse turns to the community that was given restrictions already, and explains that the restrictions were not a wrong done to them.

**The Forgery and Its Price**
The verse before this one describes people who invent a ruling and then attach it to God, and the same sūrah states elsewhere who does that: it is those who do not believe in the signs of God who invent falsehood about Him (16:105). What the verse gives them is a measured account of the bargain rather than a threat in general terms. The enjoyment is small and short, and the punishment is described as painful. The Book measures this life in the same few words every time it is put against the reward: a passing enjoyment, a small provision, a bloom that withers (3:197; 57:20). The Sūrah of Repentance puts the same choice in front of the believers rather than the deniers, asking what they preferred the life of this world for, since its enjoyment is so little (9:38). The verse, in short, is not an outburst. It is an accounting, and the person reading it is invited to check his own scale.

## Verse 16:118

> To the Jews, We have forbidden what We related to you before. We did not wrong them, but it was they who wronged themselves

The verse speaks to the objection raised by the Jews of Madinah about the food laws, and it states the matter in two parts: what was forbidden was related in the Book before, and God did not wrong them — they wronged themselves. The sūrah refers to the passage in Sūrah al-Anʿām where the restrictions are listed and explained as a penalty for their own transgressions (6:146), and it refers elsewhere to the breaking of the Sabbath and the violations that followed. The verse's function in this sūrah is to separate two things that were being merged in the argument: the laws of the earlier community, which were a response to their conduct, and the law of the new community, which the sūrah states in the verses just before. The next verse opens the door of repentance to the same people.

**The Restrictions Were Not an Injustice**
The verse returns to the food laws that have been argued over a few verses earlier, and it says again that what was forbidden for the Jews was stated before (16:115-116). The Book gives the list in the Sūrah of the Cattle: every animal with claws, the fat of cattle and sheep with the exceptions named, and other restrictions placed on them for their own transgression (6:146). What the verse adds here is the verdict on that legislation: We did not wrong them; they were wronging themselves. The restriction was a consequence of their history and not an arbitrary punishment, and the Sūrah of Āl-ʿImrān makes the same point about Israel, saying that all food was lawful to him except what he forbade himself before the Torah came (3:93).
**What This Means for the New Community**
The passage is building toward the close of the sūrah and its argument with the People of the Book, so the point here is not merely historical. The rules of the earlier communities were given for their circumstances, and the community receiving this recitation is told that its own laws come from the same Giver with the same justice. The verses that follow move from the food laws to the call to Abraham's way, and the whole passage answers the charge that the new teaching was either a copy or a contradiction of what came before.

## Verse 16:119

> As for those who commit evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺, then repent afterwards and mend their ways, then your Lord is surely All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

The verse states the condition on which the door stands open: those who commit evil out of ignorance and then repent and mend their ways, for them the Lord is Forgiving and Merciful. The Book uses the same verb for the ignorant commission of wrong in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ, where repentance is accepted from those who do wrong in ignorance and turn back soon after (4:17), and in Sūrah al-Zumar, where the mercy is offered to those who have transgressed against themselves. The sūrah has just addressed a community whose ancestors broke their own laws, so the verse keeps the option of return in front of the people the argument is about, and it ends with the two names that answer the fear the verse raises: Forgiving, and Merciful.

---

## Verse 16:120

> Indeed, Abraham was a model of excellence: devoted to Allah, ˹perfectly˺ upright—not a polytheist—

The verse names Abraham as a model — *ummah*, a community in himself, the word the Book uses for a people and here for a single man — and it describes him with three qualities: devoted, upright, and not of the polytheists. The Book gives him the same title in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where he is called an upright community obedient to God (2:120–121 in some renderings), and it states his standing as a friend of God in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ (4:125). The verse's purpose in the closing argument of this sūrah is to give the community a single ancestor of the faith rather than a lineage, and the next verse names the gratitude for which God chose him.

---

The word *ummah* applied to a single man is read by the commentators as describing Abraham as a community in himself — a whole people's worth of faith, guidance and gratitude — and the verse then names his three marks: devoted, upright, and free of polytheism. The Book gives him the same standing elsewhere: he was tried by his Lord with words and fulfilled them, and was made a leader of the people (2:124); he was not a Jew nor a Christian but upright and submissive (3:67); and he was taken as a friend by God (4:125). The verse's mention of gratitude is the reason for his praise in the same passage: the favours were given and he answered them with thanksgiving.

## Verse 16:121

> ˹utterly˺ grateful for Allah’s favours. ˹So˺ He chose him and guided him to the Straight Path

The verse states what God did in return for Abraham's gratitude: He chose him and guided him to a straight path. The order is the sūrah's order throughout — the response of the servant, then the gift — and the Book tells the same story of the choice in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where Abraham is told that God has made him a leader of people (2:124). In Sūrah al-Nisāʾ, where he is described as submitted to God and doing good. The gratitude named here is the same virtue the sūrah has been asking of its audience since the section on blessings began, so the verse presents Abraham as the model who did what the community was being told to do.

**The Model the Sūrah Was Reaching For**
The verse is part of the passage that closes the sūrah's argument with the People of the Book, and Abraham is placed at its centre: a nation in himself, devout, never an idolater, grateful for God's gifts (16:120-121). The choice by God is stated in the Sūrah of the Cow as well, where Abraham is told he will be made a leader of people (2:124), and in the Sūrah of Women, where the Book says God took him as a friend (4:125). The Book also settles the claims made about him by the two later communities: he was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but one who submitted (3:67). Gratitude is the quality singled out here, and it is the same quality the sūrah has been asking of its own audience since the section on blessings began, so that the ancestor it points to is the man who actually did what the hearers were being told to do.

## Verse 16:122

> We blessed him with all goodness in this world, and in the Hereafter he will certainly be among the righteous

The verse gives Abraham the two rewards together: good in this world, and a place among the righteous in the Hereafter. The Book gives him the same double portion in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where the good he is given is named as the honourable mention that follows him among later peoples (2:129–130 in the sense of his prayer and its answer). In Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt, where the reward of the upright is stated in the same pattern. The sūrah puts the two worlds side by side for a community that had been arguing about lineage and law, and it makes the man on whom they all agreed the example of what it was arguing about.

**The Two Rewards and the Closing Command**
The verse gives Abraham both halves of the promise: a good name in this world, and a place among the righteous in the next. The Sūrah of the Cow gives the same double gift in the answer to his prayer for his descendants — guidance and the honourable mention that follows him among later peoples (2:129-130). The sūrah then turns from the ancestor to the Prophet and gives the command that closes the whole argument: follow the religion of Abraham, the upright, and he was not of the idolaters (16:123). The order matters. The community that had been arguing about lineage and law is shown the one man all parties claimed, and then told that the way to belong to him is not descent but the same gratitude and submission he showed. It is the point the Book makes about him elsewhere too: whoever turns away from his religion has made a fool of himself (2:130).

## Verse 16:123

> Then We revealed to you ˹O Prophet, saying˺: “Follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists.”

The verse gives the Prophet the command that settles the argument of the passage: follow the faith of Abraham, who was upright and not of the polytheists. The same instruction appears at the head of the sūrah of the Bee's later argument and in Sūrah Āl-ʿImrān, where the Book rejects the claim of Abraham for Jews and Christians and assigns it to the one who follows his way (3:67–68). The command is followed in the next verse by the statement about the Sabbath, which fixes the difference between the community of Abraham and the communities that disputed over their laws. The sūrah's closing passage therefore gives the community one ancestor in faith, one clear path, and a method of inviting others to it.

---

The command to follow the faith of Abraham is given twice in the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān — "say: Allah has spoken the truth, so follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not of the polytheists" (3:95) — and it settles the argument with both the Jews and the Christians, since each claimed him. The sūrah of the Cattle gives the Prophet the same declaration (6:161), and the command here has a particular force in the sūrah of the Bee because it arrives after the passage on the food laws and the sabbath, where the community is warned not to adopt the disputes of the earlier communities about what was permitted to them.

## Verse 16:124

> ˹Honouring˺ the Sabbath was ordained only for those who disputed about Abraham. And surely your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their disputes

The verse answers the dispute about the Sabbath by narrowing it: the Sabbath was ordained only for those who differed over it, which is to say that the obligation grew out of the dispute and the divisions of the community that received it. It did not belong to Abraham, whose path the sūrah has just commanded the Prophet to follow (16:123). The Book makes the same point in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where the strictness of the Sabbath is tied to the community's own conduct (2:65), and in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ, where the punishment of that community is described. The verse ends by handing the judgement back to God on the Day of Resurrection, which is the sūrah's answer to every argument about the laws of the previous communities.

**What the Sabbath Was and for Whom**
The verse closes a passage that has been arguing with the people of the Book about their own laws, and it does two things in one sentence. It states that the Sabbath was only made obligatory for the people who differed about it — the children of Israel who disputed among themselves what the day required and why it had been given to them — and it says the community of the Prophet is not under that law. The sūrah has already named the day in its list of the blessings given to Israel, and it has already told the Muslims that they follow the way of Abraham, who was neither Jew nor Christian and who was not among those who associated partners with God (16:120-123, and see 3:67). The ruling is a boundary: the religious law given to Muḥammad neither picks up the whole of the earlier law nor abolishes the earlier communities' obligations for the sake of it.
**What the Disagreement Was**
The Qur'an uses the disagreement about the Sabbath as the reason it had to be laid down so firmly for the people who received it. Some of them kept the day and some of them did not; the Book reports on more than one occasion that a group of them were transformed for the violation. The verse does not go into those accounts here — it leaves them to the sūrahs that tell the story — and keeps to the point the argument needs: the differences the community inherited are for God to settle on the Day of Resurrection, and the reader is told twice in the passage that the return and the judgment are His, not the community's to carry out on anyone's behalf.

## Verse 16:125

> Invite ˹all˺ to the Way of your Lord with wisdom and kind advice, and only debate with them in the best manner. Surely your Lord ˹alone˺ knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided

The verse states the method of the invitation: wisdom, good advice, and debate in the best manner, with the reminder that the Lord knows best who has strayed and who is guided. The Book states the same in Sūrah Ṭā-Hā, where Moses and Aaron are told to speak gently to Pharaoh (20:44), and in Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt, where discussion with the People of the Book is to be conducted with what is best. The last clause removes the pressure of results from the man who conveys: the knowledge of who will respond belongs to God, so the method does not need to be replaced by force. The sūrah places the instruction at the point where the argument has finished, so that the method is stated after the case has been made.

---

The method named in this verse is one of the Book's clearest statements on how the invitation is to be made, and the sūrah of the Spider adds the rule for argument with the People of the Book: argue in the best manner, except with those who do wrong, and say that the community believes in what was revealed to it and to them (29:46). The sūrah of Ṭā Hā applies the same principle to the hardest case of all, when Moses and Aaron are told to speak gently to Pharaoh so that he may remember and fear (20:43–44). The closing words of the verse return the outcome to God, who knows best who has strayed from His path and who is guided, which is the same limit the sūrah placed on the messenger's duty at 16:82.

## Verse 16:126

> If you retaliate, then let it be equivalent to what you have suffered. But if you patiently endure, it is certainly best for those who are patient

The verse states the limit on retaliation and then names the better course: if you retaliate, let it be exactly equivalent, and if you patiently endure, it is certainly better for those who are patient. The Book states the same principle of equivalence in Sūrah al-Baqarah with the law of retribution and the permission of pardon (2:178; 5:45), and it records the occasion of this verse in the Prophet's own grief after the battle of Uḥud. The permission is not withdrawn; the preference is stated, and the preference is attached to the patience named in the next verse as possible only with God's help.

---

The permission to retaliate on equal terms is stated in the sūrah of the Heifer as the law of retribution (2:178) and in the sūrah of the Pilgrimage with the same condition of equivalence (22:60), while the better course named here is repeated in the sūrah of Consultation, where the recompense of an evil is an evil like it, but whoever forgives and reconciles has his reward with God (42:40). The reports connect the verse to the treatment of the Prophet's uncle Ḥamza after Uḥud and to the grief of the community, which is why the next verse addresses the Prophet's own patience directly. The sūrah's order is the teaching: the limit is preserved, and the preference for patience is named without abolishing the right.

## Verse 16:127

> Be patient ˹O Prophet˺, for your patience is only with Allah’s help. Do not grieve over those ˹who disbelieve˺, nor be distressed by their schemes

The verse addresses the Prophet directly and gives the command with the reason attached: be patient, and your patience comes only with the help of God. It then removes two weights from him: do not grieve over the people who refuse, and do not be distressed by their plotting. The Book comforts the Prophet in the same terms elsewhere, where it says that no soul will believe except by God's permission and following the plot of those who refuse is pointless (10:100–101). The command is followed by the promise in the next verse, which closes the sūrah on the support rather than the hardship.

---

The two griefs the verse removes are the same two the sūrah named at the opening of its address to the Prophet: the refusal of the people and the plotting of those who deny (16:127; compare 15:97). The Book repeats the counsel in the same form in the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān, where the Prophet is told not to grieve for the disbelievers (3:176), and in the sūrah of the Cave. The verse's closing note, that God is with those who are mindful and those who are doers of good, is the promise the passage ends on, and the sūrah's next verse adds the command to patience with the community's own conduct (16:128).

## Verse 16:128

> Surely Allah is with those who shun evil and who do good ˹deeds˺

The sūrah ends with the ground of the command it has just given: God is with those who shun evil and who do good. The Book closes several of its addresses on the same note, and the two words at the end are the summary of the whole sūrah's teaching — *ittaqā*, to be mindful of God and guard against doing wrong, and *muḥsinūn*, those who do good. The sūrah that began with the command to be grateful and the warning against ingratitude ends with the presence of God promised to the people who followed its instruction, and it leaves the reader with the pair of virtues the earlier verses have been asking for from the beginning.
