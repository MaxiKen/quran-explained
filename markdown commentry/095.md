# Sūrah al-Tīn (Chapter 95) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Tīn 95:1

> By the fig and the olive ˹of Jerusalem˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Opens With an Oath, Not an Argument**

Three of the eight verses of al-Tīn are oath clauses, and they arrive before any claim is made. Arabic rhetoric treats the *qasam* — the sworn statement — as a way of arresting attention and staking a truth the speaker does not intend to prove in the ordinary way. The oath sūrahs use the device at scale: the sun and its brightness (91:1), the morning light (93:1), the winds that scatter (51:1), the racing war-horses (100:1). What this sūrah swears by is edible. A fig and an olive are not cosmic bodies; they are what a household keeps in a jar through a lean season. That choice sets the register for the whole chapter. The sūrah that will speak of human beings made in the best form, and then reduced to the lowest of the low, begins with what a human body needs in order to keep standing. Two small plants are placed in the witness box, and the witness list is about to grow.

**Two Plants That Sustained an Entire Region**

The fig and the olive were the staple fruits of the eastern Mediterranean, and the pairing is not decorative. A fig tree produces a heavy, sweet, energy-dense fruit that keeps for months once it is dried; an olive tree produces oil, and in a pre-industrial economy oil is food, lamp fuel, medicine and soap in a single substance. Together the two describe an agrarian life in which a family can eat through a difficult year. The Qur'an treats both as named blessings rather than as scenery, and the olive elsewhere carries explicit theological weight: the parable of light in Sūrat al-Nūr describes a blessed tree whose oil would almost glow even if no fire touched it (24:35). Nobody in seventh-century Arabia needed the value of these plants explained. The oath draws its force from an ordinary economic fact — these are among the most useful things God has put in the ground — and only afterwards from whatever symbolism later readers find in them.

**Reading the Two Trees as Two Lands**

Most exegetes take the words at face value: God swears by the fruits themselves because of the blessing contained in them. A second school reads them as place names. On that reading the fig stands for Damascus — some say the mount the city is built on, others the place of prostration there — while the olive stands for Jerusalem, or the mount on which Jerusalem stands. A variant makes the fig the region of the Companions of the Cave, whose story occupies Sūrat al-Kahf (18:9–26). What supports the geographical reading is the shape of what follows. The next two oath clauses are unmistakably places: Mount Sinai, and this secure city, which the earliest readers identified without hesitation as Mecca. Three places, then, and three revelations behind them. The literal reading remains the simpler one and needs no symbolism to work, so the two positions are less rivals than a fruit and the orchard it grew in.

**The Fig Leaf and the Olive's Light**

A third line of interpretation ties the two plants to the human story the sūrah is about to tell. The fig supplies the leaves Adam and his wife sewed over themselves after they disobeyed (7:22); the olive supplies the blessed tree of the light parable (24:35). Set side by side, those images describe one human being twice: once in shame, covering himself, and once illuminated by something whose oil needs no fire. The pairing anticipates the movement of the sūrah's middle verses, which will describe human beings at their height and at their collapse. The reading is attractive because it makes the oath load-bearing rather than ornamental, yet it rests on association rather than on anything the text states. It deserves to be held lightly. The defensible claim is the modest one: here are two plants a person can live on, and God has sworn by them before saying anything about the person.

**Why Swearing by a Small Thing Works**

An oath borrows weight from what it swears by, so swearing by a fig might look like a weakening of the device. The opposite is what happens. When the sworn thing is enormous — the sun, the night, the Day of Resurrection — the listener is overpowered into agreement. When it is small, the listener is made to look, and looking is what actually produces conviction. Whoever stops to consider how a fig tree converts rain and dust into sugar has already been moved part of the distance toward the claim the sūrah is preparing: the one who arranged this arrangement also arranged human beings, and gave them a form that can be lost. The rhetorical economy is exact, and it costs nothing. Two plants, then two mountains, then one city, then the sentence the oath was sworn to deliver.

**The Oath Formula and What It Demands of the Listener**

Arabic oaths of this type are not decorative intensifiers; they are compressed arguments. The particle *wa* before the sworn noun signals that something is being staked, and the listener's job is to ask why this particular thing was chosen. The Qur'an repeatedly expects that question to be asked, and sometimes answers it: after swearing by the Day of Resurrection it asks whether humanity supposes its bones will never be gathered (75:1–3). The structure is a wager followed by its payoff. In al-Tīn the payoff is deliberately withheld for two more clauses, so that by the time the response clause arrives the listener has assembled three witnesses from three different regions of the prophetic map. Nothing has been asserted yet. What has happened is that the ground has been prepared for a statement about human beings which the sūrah intends to be received as settled fact rather than as opinion.

## Sūrah al-Tīn 95:2

> and Mount Sinai

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Mountain Enters the Oath, and the Register Shifts**

The first clause of the oath named food. This one names a mountain, and the shift is the point. Sinai is not a mountain a person eats from; it is a mountain a person is addressed from. God spoke to Mūsā there, called him, and gave him the law, and the Qur'an returns to that meeting repeatedly: the right side of the valley in the blessed ground by the tree (28:30), the valley of Ṭuwā where he was told to remove his sandals (20:12), the night he said to his family that he perceived a fire and would bring back news or a burning branch (20:10). By placing the mountain second in the list, the sūrah moves the oath from provision to revelation. A body needs a fig; a community needs a commandment. Both are gifts, and both come from the same giver, but the second one is the reason the first kind of gift can be understood at all.

**The Three Names of One Mountain**

The Qur'an knows this mountain by more than one name, and the variation is deliberate rather than careless. Here it is *Ṭūr Sīnīn*; in Sūrat al-Mu'minūn it is the tree that grows on Mount Sinai, which yields oil and relish for those who eat (23:20); in eight other places it appears simply as *al-Ṭūr*, the Mount, including in the oath that opens Sūrat al-Ṭūr (52:1). The word *ṭūr* itself is not native Arabic in the ordinary sense but a loan, which is itself telling: the mountain came into Arabic speech already carrying the memory of an older revelation. Some early exegetes held that *Sīnīn* is drawn from a non-Arabic word meaning beautiful or blessed, so that the phrase would mean the blessed, beautiful Mount. Others took *Sīnīn* to denote any mountain bearing fruit trees, which would make the term generic rather than proper. The specificity of the context favors the proper reading: a mountain singled out for a covenant, not a category of hills.

**The Mountain Under Which a Covenant Was Made**

Sinai is the site of an oath taken by a whole people, and that is why it belongs in an oath. When the law was given, the mountain was raised over the heads of the Children of Israel and they were told to hold firmly to what had been given to them (2:63; 7:171). The image is one of the most striking in the Qur'an: a covenant accepted under a lifted mountain, with the alternative presented in plain sight. Whatever one makes of the physical description, its function is clear. The community that received the Torah did so as a community, under pressure, with the weight of the obligation made visible. Swearing by that mountain in al-Tīn recalls not only the geography but the obligation attached to it. A human being who has been addressed by God is not in the same position as one who has not, and the second clause of this oath is a reminder that being addressed carries consequences.

**Why the Sequence Moves From Moses to Mecca**

Read the three oath clauses in order and a map appears. The fig and the olive, on the geographical reading, point to the Levant and to Jerusalem, the land of Jesus. Sinai points to Moses. The third clause names Mecca, the city of Muḥammad ﷺ. On that reading the sūrah swears by the three stations of the last great line of revelation, and does so in a sequence that ends where the listeners are standing. The device is quietly argumentative. If a person accepts that God spoke at Sinai and that God sent a messenger in Jerusalem, the third term is not a novelty but the completion of a pattern already conceded. The listeners being addressed in Mecca are not being asked to believe something unprecedented. They are being asked to recognise the shape of what they already claim to accept, and to notice that the shape has come round to them.

**The Mountain, the Fruit, and the Human Being**

Sinai also does something else in this list: it introduces scale. A fig tree is small, a mountain is not, and the third term — a city — sits between the two in size but above both in human consequence. The progression moves from what sustains a body, to what addresses a community, to what shelters a civilisation. Each term is a kind of security. The fruit secures food, the mountain secures law, the sanctuary city secures life itself. The sūrah is assembling the conditions under which a human being can exist at all before it says anything about what a human being is. That order matters for the verses that follow. When the sūrah declares that human beings were created in the best form, the declaration lands on a listener who has just been walked through provision, commandment and shelter — three provisions of a God who has not been careless with this creature.

**A Sworn Thing Must Be Great in Some Respect**

Commentators note a general principle behind the Qur'an's oaths: God swears only by what is great in some respect, either in itself or in what it points to. Sinai qualifies in the second way and, arguably, in the first. It is where speech came down to a human being, and it is where a covenant was made binding on a nation. The mountain is also the setting of one of the most austere scenes in the Qur'an, when Mūsā asked to see his Lord and the mountain crumbled to dust at the disclosure, leaving Mūsā fallen unconscious (7:143). That scene is the measure of the mountain's dignity and of human limits in the same stroke. Placed in an oath list that begins with food and ends with a sanctuary, Sinai stands as the term that carries the most explicit reminder that God is not a human being, and that being addressed by Him is a serious business.

## Sūrah al-Tīn 95:3

> and this secure city ˹of Mecca˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath Ends Where the Listener Is Standing**

Two clauses ago the oath was in orchards; one clause ago it was in the desert of Sinai. Now it comes home. The demonstrative *hādhā* — "this" — does real work, because it points at something both speaker and hearer can identify without explanation. The secure city is Mecca, and every early reader took it that way. The rhetorical effect is to close a distance the first two clauses had opened. After the Levant and after Sinai, the listener is returned to his own streets, and the oath lands as a local fact rather than a distant memory. A Qurayshī hearing this verse is being sworn at, in the place he defends, by the privilege he considers his own. That is why the clause carries an exclamation in the translation and why the sūrah can afford to be terse with him afterwards. The oath has already done the work of establishing that God has a claim on this city.

**What Made Mecca Secure**

The security was real, and it was exceptional. Pre-Islamic Arabia was a place of raid, blood feud and seasonal war, yet Mecca held a recognised sanctuary in which fighting was forbidden and even a man with blood on his hands could not be touched inside its bounds. The Qur'an makes the contrast explicit: have they not seen that We established a secure sanctuary while people are snatched away all around them (29:67). The city's safety was not produced by its walls — it had none worth mentioning — but by a shared recognition that the place was God's. That recognition is exactly the kind of social fact an oath can rest on. To swear by the secure city is to swear by a blessing the listeners accept daily and would find impossible to deny, since the alternative would be to explain why caravans come to them in safety while other towns are raided.

**Ibrāhīm's Prayer and the City's Debt**

The security is not presented as an accident of geography. It is traced to a request. Ibrāhīm prayed that God make this city secure and provide its people with fruits (2:126), and in another verse he describes settling his offspring in a valley with no cultivation, by God's sacred house, so that they might establish prayer (14:37). The city's condition is therefore a granted petition, and the petition was made by the ancestor the Quraysh claimed as their own. The argument implicit in the oath is uncomfortable for its audience: the safety you enjoy was asked for, given, and given for a purpose. A sanctuary whose inhabitants then deny the God who made it sanctuary is a contradiction in terms. This is why the sūrah's later turn is so abrupt. The clause is not simply scenery for an oath; it is the establishment of an obligation the city has been living off without acknowledging.

**The Three Witnesses, Assembled**

With this clause the list is complete, and the pattern behind it becomes visible. A fruit-bearing land, a mountain of covenant, a sanctuary city: provision, law and safety, in that order, with a messenger associated with each. On the reading that the first clause points to Jerusalem, the sequence runs Jesus, Moses, Muḥammad ﷺ, ending with the listeners' own prophet. Even without that reading, the three terms describe three kinds of divine care, and they are sworn together because the claim that follows needs all three. A human being is not merely made; a human being is fed, addressed and sheltered. When the sūrah says next that human beings were created in the best form, the statement is not floating free. It arrives after a witness list that has already established that this creature has been the object of sustained, specific attention.

**Security That Can Be Forfeited**

Mecca's privilege is conditional in the Qur'an's telling, and the condition is stated in the same breath as the blessing. The Qur'an offers the parable of a town that was secure and at ease, its provision coming to it in abundance from every side, and that then showed ingratitude for God's favours, so God made it taste hunger and fear (16:112–113). The sūrah of Quraysh makes the same point in commercial terms: their winter and summer journeys were possible only because of the Lord of this House, who feeds them against hunger and secures them from fear (106:1–4). Security, in other words, is a loan rather than a possession. An oath sworn by the secure city is therefore also a warning addressed to it. The privilege named in this clause is the same privilege that can be withdrawn, and the sūrah's final verse will ask who is entitled to judge when it is.

**An Exclamation, and the Answer It Is Waiting For**

The clause ends the oath with a force the earlier clauses lacked, and the reason is structural rather than emotional. Everything sworn so far has been a gift; what follows is a verdict about the recipient. Oaths in Arabic are completed by a response clause, and the tension between the three clauses and the response is what carries the sūrah. Three regions, three blessings, three revelations, and then one sentence about what human beings were made to be. The exclamation signals that the oath is finished and that the answer is now owed. Nothing in the clause itself argues; it simply holds up the city as evidence. What the listener is expected to supply is the obvious inference: if God has taken this much care over provision, commandment and shelter, then the creature at the centre of all that care cannot have been made for nothing.

## Sūrah al-Tīn 95:4

> Indeed, We created humans in the best form

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath's Answer Arrives in a Single Sentence**

Four words of Arabic carry the response to three oath clauses, and their flatness is deliberate. *Laqad khalaqnā al-insāna fī aḥsan taqwīm* — We created the human being in the best form — states a completed fact in the perfect tense, with the emphatic particle *laqad* foreclosing argument. There is no reasoning offered, no appeal to observation, no list of proofs. The oath did that work; this sentence collects on it. The effect is that the dignity of the human being is not established here but recalled. The sūrah assumes its listeners already know that human beings stand upright, speak, calculate, bury their dead and build, and it treats that knowledge as sufficient grounds for the claim. The verse is not flattering humanity so much as reminding it of something it can verify by looking at itself. What the sūrah will add is not evidence but a condition attached to the gift.

**Taqwīm: Made Right, Not Merely Made**

The key term is *taqwīm*, a verbal noun from *qawwama*, to straighten, raise, proportion and set right. It is not the ordinary word for making something; it denotes making something into what it is supposed to be. A bow that has been *qawwama* is not just manufactured, it is trued. Applied to a human being, the word carries both a physical and a moral sense, and the commentators have always read it that way. Physically, the human being stands erect while other creatures go face-down toward the ground (67:22); he has hands that oppose the thumb, a face that faces forward, a voice capable of articulate sound. Morally, the same word implies that the creature has been set right for a purpose. The two senses are not alternatives. In Qur'anic usage the body's uprightness is a sign of an inner capacity, and the sūrah's later verses will treat the loss of that capacity as a fall to a lower level of the same scale rather than as a change of subject.

**The Scope of the Statement Includes Every Human Being**

*Al-insān* is generic, and the commentators are explicit that it covers male and female, believer and disbeliever, pious and wicked. That is a significant claim and easy to miss. The verse is not describing an achieved state that some human beings reach and others fail to reach; it is describing what every human being was made in. A tyrant and a saint begin from the same form. The distinction the sūrah will draw later is not a distinction in manufacture but in use. This matters for how the verse can be deployed. It rules out any reading of human dignity that makes it a reward for virtue, and it equally rules out any reading that makes the later fall a defect in the design. The design is good for everyone. What differs is what people do with it, and the sūrah's exception clause will locate the difference precisely there.

**The Angels Were Ordered to Bow to This Form**

The dignity asserted here is not a private opinion of the sūrah. It has a narrative behind it that the Qur'an tells several times: when God announced that He was placing a vicegerent on earth, the angels asked how He could place there one who would spread corruption and shed blood (2:30); when He had fashioned the creature and breathed into it of His spirit, He commanded them to prostrate, and they did (15:29; 38:72). The command to bow is the strongest available testimony to the creature's rank, since it comes from beings who do not sin and who had just raised an objection on the record. Adam was also taught the names of all things, a knowledge the angels did not possess (2:31). Read against that background, this verse is the summary line of a long argument: the creature made of clay was raised above the creatures made of light, and the raising was announced in the heavenly assembly before it happened on earth.

**Created in the Image: A Verified Report and Its Limits**

The exegetes support the verse with a report the Prophet ﷺ made in a practical context. He forbade striking a person's face, and gave as his reason that God created Adam in His image, adding that Adam was sixty cubits tall; the report is transmitted by Abū Hurayrah and recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6227 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2841. Muslim scholars have been careful about what the words can bear. The Qur'an states plainly that there is nothing like Him (42:11), so the phrase cannot mean resemblance of form. What it has been taken to mean is that human beings possess, in a limited created way, capacities that belong to God without limit: life, knowledge, will, hearing, sight, speech. The report is used in this verse's commentary not to describe anatomy but to explain why the form is honoured: the creature carries reflections of the divine attributes, which is why striking his face is not merely cruelty but a kind of insult.

**Best Form, and the Fall That Has Not Yet Been Named**

The verse is a summit, and a summit implies a slope. The sūrah does not spell this out here, but the superlative *aḥsan* does some of the work: to say the best form is to imply that lesser forms exist and that a position can be lost. The commentators read the verse together with what follows, since a superlative about origin makes little sense unless the origin can be departed from. The moral consequence is immediate. If the best form is a gift rather than an achievement, then it is held on terms, and the terms will be stated. Nothing in this verse threatens anyone. It is an affirmation, unqualified and generous, and it is the reason the sūrah can be severe two verses later without being cruel: the fall it will describe is measured against a height that was given, not against a standard invented after the fact.

## Sūrah al-Tīn 95:5

> But We will reduce them to the lowest of the low ˹in Hell˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Verb Reverses the Whole Verse Before It**

The turn is carried by *thumma* and by *radadnāhu*: then We return him, or send him back. Both words matter. *Thumma* marks a distance between the two states, not merely a sequence, so the fall is not automatic; time and choice occupy the interval. The verb *radda* means to send back, to restore to a previous condition, and it implies that the destination is not new ground but a descent into something lower than where the creature started. The construction is not "We will destroy him" but "We will put him below." The dignity of verse four was a station; this verse describes a demotion from that station. That is why the verse can be so short. No mechanism is described, no process is narrated. A creature made at the top of a scale is placed at the bottom of the same scale, and the whole force of the sentence lies in the fact that it is the same scale.

**Asfala Sāfilīn: The Grammar of the Lowest**

The phrase *asfala sāfilīn* is a superlative followed by a plural genitive: the lowest of the low. Arabic superlatives of this construction place the subject inside a class rather than above it. He is not merely low; he is a member of the lowest group, one among many. The same construction appears in the respite granted to Iblīs, who is told that he is *min al-munẓarīn*, among those deferred (7:15), and the effect there is the same: the individual is enrolled in a crowd. Some early readers took *sāfilīn* as a second adjective rather than a genitive plural, giving the sense of the lowest and the lowly, which would emphasise the abasement of their condition rather than their membership in a group. The genitive reading is the more natural, and it carries a sharper point. Falling to the bottom of the moral scale means joining everyone else who fell, and the company is the humiliation.

**Old Age or the Fire: The Two Readings and Their Grounds**

Two interpretations divide the exegetes, and each has a real argument. The first reads the verse physically: the lowest of the low is decrepit old age, when strength, memory and sense withdraw. The Qur'an supports the description elsewhere — God created you, then will cause you to die, and some of you are returned to the most debilitated age so that they know nothing after having known (16:70); and whomsoever We grant long life, We reverse in creation (36:68). The second reads it morally and eschatologically: the verse describes the human being who falls below his nature and is cast into the Fire. The grounds for preferring the second are internal. The very next verse makes an exception for those who believe and do good, and an exception of that kind makes no sense against old age, which comes to the believer and the disbeliever alike. A verse that excepts the righteous cannot be describing an unavoidable biological process. The moral reading also fits the sūrah's closing question about judgment, which presupposes a verdict rather than a decay.

**Below the Cattle: How Low the Floor Actually Is**

The Qur'an supplies its own comparison for this floor. It describes people who were given hearts they did not use to understand, eyes they did not use to see, and ears they did not use to hear, and calls them like cattle — no, further astray (7:179). The comparison is severe because it inverts the order of verse four. A creature granted the best form and then living below the level of an animal has not merely failed to rise; he has fallen past his starting point, past the beasts, into a place where the faculties exist and are unused. That is what makes the fall a moral event rather than a natural one. An animal is not in the lowest of the low, because it never had the capacity it is failing to use. The human being in this verse is defined by the gap between what he was made capable of and what he has done with it, and the gap is the measure of his descent.

**A Descent That the Creature Chooses**

Nothing in the verse absolves the person described. The subject of the demotion is God — We will reduce them — but the sūrah never suggests that the demotion is arbitrary. The exception clause that follows identifies who is not demoted, and it identifies them by what they did: believed and acted rightly. The logic is that of a sentence in law rather than a law of nature. People are not thrown down by a force they could not resist; they are assigned a place that their own conduct determined, and God is the one who assigns it. The Qur'an states the principle elsewhere in plainer terms: what would Allah do with your punishment if you are grateful and believe (4:147). Punishment in the Qur'an is not a need in God but a response in a person. That is why this verse can be so calm about so severe a thing. It is reporting the outcome of a choice already made.

**The Sūrah's Own Argument Against Despair**

Read alone, the verse is bleak; read in its place, it is the hinge of an argument about hope. A fall from the best form to the lowest of the low would be unbearable if the fall were final and unconditional, and the sūrah immediately makes clear that it is neither. The exception is broad — those who believe and do good — and it is stated without restriction of time, tribe or prior record. The verse therefore functions as the pressure that makes the exception meaningful. Without the possibility of falling, being made in the best form would be a fact with no consequences; without the exception, the possibility of falling would be a threat with no exit. Both halves are needed, and the sūrah keeps them in a single movement: a height that is given, a depth that is chosen, and a way back that is open to anyone who takes it.

## Sūrah al-Tīn 95:6

> except those who believe and do good—they will have a never-ending reward

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Exception Is the Point of the Whole Sūrah**

Four verses of oath and two of judgment are gathered up by one particle: *illā*, except. Grammatically the clause is an exclusion from the demotion described before it, but rhetorically it is the sūrah's actual destination. Everything earlier — the fig, the olive, Sinai, the secure city, the best form, the lowest of the low — was preparation for a distinction, and this verse draws it. The distinction is not between tribes, or between those born inside a covenant and those born outside it. It is between two ways of using the same gift. Both groups were made in the best form. Both groups had access to the same provisions, the same revelation, the same sanctuary. What separates them is belief and action, named together, in that order, in three words.

**Belief and Good Deeds Are Named Together, and Neither Works Alone**

The formula *āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt* — those who believe and do righteous deeds — recurs throughout the Qur'an as a fixed pair, and the pairing is not incidental. Belief without action is a claim; action without belief is a habit. The Qur'an insists on both because each one protects the other from a particular failure. The sūrah of the Afternoon makes the same pairing and then adds two more conditions: those who believe, do good, urge one another to truth, and urge one another to patience (103:3), with the flat statement before them that humanity is in loss (103:2). Al-Tīn's version is shorter but not weaker. It locates the rescue from the lowest of the low in the same two acts, which tells us that the fall described in the previous verse was not caused by ignorance alone but by a life that did not match a belief, or by a belief that never became a life.

**Ajr Ghayr Mamnūn: A Reward That Is Not Cut Off**

The phrase *ajrun ghayru mamnūn* is usually rendered as a reward unceasing, and the word deserves more than a gloss. *Mamnūn* comes from *manna*, which has two related senses: to cut off or diminish, and to taunt someone with a gift given. Both senses are live here, and both are excluded. The reward is not reduced over time, and it is not given with reproach. The Qur'an uses the same phrase for the believers in Sūrat Fuṣṣilat: those who believe and do good will have an unceasing reward (41:8), and again for those whose deeds are accepted (84:25). The pairing of the two senses produces a picture of a gift that neither runs out nor embarrasses its recipient. That is a specific promise, not a general one. A reward that ended would make the believer's patience a bad investment; a reward given with a taunt would leave the recipient in debt to the giver. Neither is offered here.

**The Unceasing Reward in This World Before the Next**

Commentators apply the phrase to the present life as well as to the Garden, and the application is practical rather than sentimental. When a person who maintains his worship becomes ill, or travels, or is otherwise prevented, the Law does not punish the interruption but provides for it: prayer may be shortened on a journey, with no blame attached (4:101), and the sick or travelling person may defer the fast and make it up on other days (2:184). The ruling expresses the same principle the verse states: the reward for a practice is not suspended by a legitimate interruption of the practice. A believer who cannot stand in prayer because of fever has not lost the reward of standing. The point is theological before it is legal — God's accounting does not treat an unavoidable gap as an abandonment — and it gives the phrase *ghayr mamnūn* a present-tense meaning for anyone who has had a practice broken by circumstances.

**Why the Exception Proves the Previous Verse Was Moral**

The clause settles a question left open by the verse before it. If the lowest of the low meant decrepit old age, the exception would be meaningless, since faith does not exempt anyone from ageing. The fact that belief and good deeds are what removes a person from the demotion shows that the demotion is a moral verdict, and that the sūrah is describing a fall caused by conduct. This is one of the cleanest examples of a later verse governing the meaning of an earlier one. The reading is not imposed from outside; it is required by the structure of the sentence. It also explains why the sūrah ends where it does. A chapter that distinguishes people by their deeds must end with a judge, and this one ends by asking whether God is not the most just of judges.

**A Reward Described by Its Duration, Not Its Contents**

The verse says nothing about what the reward consists of. No rivers, no gardens, no descriptions of pleasure appear here, though the Qur'an supplies them in abundance elsewhere. What is promised is a quality of the reward rather than an inventory: it does not end. The choice is instructive. For a listener being warned about a fall to the lowest place, the most valuable feature of the alternative is not its luxury but its permanence. Loss is what makes worldly good feel inadequate; everything here is *mamnūn* in the first sense, cut off by death or accident. The verse answers that anxiety directly. The believer is being told that what he is working toward cannot be taken from him, cannot be reduced, and cannot be thrown back in his face. That is the promise that makes the previous verse bearable, and it is why the sūrah keeps it to four words.

## Sūrah al-Tīn 95:7

> Now, what makes you deny the ˹final˺ Judgment

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question of Astonishment, Not a Request for Information**

The Arabic is *fa-mā yukażżibuka baʿdu bi-l-dīn*, and the interrogative particle here does not ask for information. It expresses astonishment: what could possibly make you deny this? The form is the second person singular, which turns a general proposition into a direct address, and the particle *baʿdu* — after this, in the face of all this — points backwards at everything the sūrah has just established. The question presupposes that the grounds for denial have been removed. A person who denies the Judgment after being walked through his own creation is not making an intellectual error so much as refusing a conclusion he can see. The tone is closer to a prosecutor's closing than to a teacher's question, and the sūrah can afford that tone because it has spent six verses laying out evidence the listener already accepts about himself.

**Dīn: Judgment First, Religion Second**

The word *dīn* is translated here as Judgment, and the choice is defensible on the evidence of Qur'anic usage. The same word appears in the opening sūrah as Master of the Day of Judgment (1:4), where *yawm al-dīn* can only mean the day of reckoning. It also carries the wider sense of religion or way of life, as in the statement that the religion with God is submission (3:19). Both senses are available, and the commentators have taken the verse both ways. The eschatological reading fits the immediate context better: the sūrah has just described two final outcomes, a demotion and a reward, and both require a judge to assign them. The religious reading is not excluded, since denying that there is a reckoning and denying that there is an obligation amount to the same refusal in practice. What the two readings share is the idea that *dīn* involves being answerable, and that this is the specific thing being denied.

**The Argument From the Drop to the Standing Man**

The strongest force of the question comes from what it leaves unsaid. The Qur'an's standard argument against denial of resurrection is biological, and it is developed at length elsewhere: does humanity not see that We created him from a drop, and then he becomes an open disputant (36:77); was he not a drop of emitted fluid, then a clinging clot, then He fashioned and proportioned him (75:37–38). Al-Tīn compresses the same argument into the two words of verse four. A being who was nothing, became a drop, became a clot, became a proportioned upright creature capable of speech and thought, is being asked how he can suppose that the process stops. The denial is not merely wrong; it is inconsistent with the denier's own biography, which he can verify at any moment by remembering where he came from.

**Addressed to the Denier, or to the Prophet?**

The second person singular raises a question of addressee, and the commentators differ. The majority take it as an address to the human being who denies — a rhetorical "you" aimed at the audience in Mecca who rejected the resurrection. Others, including al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī among those who record it, read it as an address to the Prophet ﷺ, meaning: after these proofs, who could still deny you? On that reading the verse consoles him rather than challenges the audience. The grounds for the majority view are stronger. Nothing in the sūrah has been directed to the Prophet personally up to this point, the oaths are addressed to a plural audience by implication, and a consolatory reading would require the verse to shift register without signal. The grammatical objection to the prophetic reading — that *yukażżibuka* would then have to mean "make you a liar" rather than "make you deny" — reinforces the same conclusion.

**What Denial Actually Costs the Denier**

The verse treats denial as an act with a direction. To deny the Judgment is not to hold a neutral opinion about cosmology; it is to remove the consequence from one's own conduct, and the Qur'an says so elsewhere in blunt terms: those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them, since they are only given more time to increase in sin (3:178). Without a reckoning, patience has no payoff and restraint has no reward, so the rational course becomes accumulation. That is why the denial addressed here is not an error of the mind but a decision about how to live. The sūrah's previous verses have already supplied the alternative — a reward that is not cut off — and this question closes the case by asking the listener to explain why he would prefer the version of the world in which nothing he does finally matters.

**The Astonishment Is Genuine, and That Is the Rebuke**

What gives the verse its edge is that the astonishment is presented as sincere. The sūrah does not say the denier is stupid or wicked; it says it cannot understand how he manages to deny. The rebuke lies in the gap between the evidence and the conclusion. Consider what the listener has been shown in eight short verses: fruit that sustains him, a mountain where God spoke, a city made safe for him, a form that is the best of forms. He accepts every item on that list — he eats the fig, he honours the sanctuary, he knows he stands upright — and then denies the only conclusion that follows from them. The question is designed to make that inconsistency audible. It is the sūrah's way of saying that the denial is not a shortage of evidence but a refusal to follow evidence the denier already concedes.

## Sūrah al-Tīn 95:8

> Is Allah not the most just of all judges

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Ends With a Question It Has Already Answered**

A negative interrogative in Arabic of this form — *a-laysa Allāhu bi-aḥkam al-ḥākimīn* — expects agreement. It is not an open question; it is a conclusion put in the shape of a question so that the listener pronounces it himself. The sūrah has earned the move. Two groups of people have been described, one demoted to the lowest place and one given a reward that is not cut off, and both descriptions presuppose someone who assigns places. If there is no judge, the distinction is not merely unfair, it is incoherent. The closing verse therefore does not introduce a new idea. It names the one who has been operating silently through the whole chapter, and it asks the listener to confirm what the previous verses have already assumed. The answer, in the grammar of the question, is not optional.

**Aḥkam al-Ḥākimīn and the Language of Judging**

The phrase is a superlative of *ḥakam* inside a plural: the most just, or the wisest, of those who judge. The root *ḥ-k-m* covers both adjudication and sound judgement, so the phrase gathers the two meanings that English separates. A *ḥakīm* is one who decides correctly and one who decides with wisdom, and the Qur'an uses the pair as divine names: He is the All-Knowing, the All-Wise. God is called the best of judges in several places — judgement belongs to God alone, He declares the truth and He is the best of those who decide (6:57), with the same formulation at 7:87, 10:109 and 12:80. The last of those is particularly close in spirit: Joseph, wronged by his brothers and then exalted above them, says that God will judge between them. The theme is consistent. Human judgement is partial and slow; the superlative here exists to say that the case will not finally rest with human judges.

**Why Justice Requires the Two Outcomes of Verses Five and Six**

Al-Ṭabarī's reading of the connection is the plainest: people differ, because some leave the station of the best form and descend to the lowest of the low while others remain in it, and it follows that they differ in recompense. The argument is structural. A judge is only necessary where two parties have different claims, and the sūrah has spent three verses establishing that the two parties do. If the tyrant and the person he crushed ended in the same place, the difference between them would have no meaning, and the sūrah's whole moral vocabulary would collapse. Justice here is not retribution for its own sake; it is the recognition of a difference that already exists. The verse is answering the objection that a merciful God would simply forgive everyone equally, by pointing out that such a forgiveness would erase the very distinction the previous verses were built on.

**The Report of the Answer, and Its Grade**

There is a well-known report about how this verse should be answered in recitation. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that when one of you recites Sūrat al-Tīn and reaches its end — *Is Allah not the most just of judges?* — he should say: *balā, wa-anā ʿalā dhālika min al-shāhidīn*, "Yes indeed, and I am one of those who bear witness to that." The report is recorded in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 887, and the same narration extends the practice to the closing questions of two other short sūrahs: after *Is not Allah the most just of judges?* here, and after *Is He not able to give life to the dead?* at 75:40. It should be cited honestly: al-Albānī graded this narration *ḍaʿīf*, weak, in his assessment of Abī Dāwūd. Many jurists nevertheless treat the response as recommended, because a question of this shape invites assent and the words of the answer are uncontroversial in meaning — the listener simply declares that he is among the witnesses to God's justice. What is not legitimate is presenting the report as firmly established when its chain has been graded weak, nor is it legitimate to drop the practice silently because the chain is disputed. The correct citation states the number, the collector, and the grade together, and leaves the reader able to weigh it.

**The Judge Named After a Chapter About Human Dignity**

The placement of this verse is worth attending to. A sūrah that opened with a fig and an olive ends with God as the most just of judges, and the distance between those two poles is the sūrah's subject. Nothing in the chapter suggests that judgement is an afterthought bolted onto a meditation about human nature. The nature is what makes the judgement possible. A creature without the best form could not be held to account, and a creature left permanently in that form without consequence would have been given a dignity with no weight. The sūrah holds the three things together — origin, fall, verdict — in eight verses, and the final question is the one that makes the other two matter. God's justice is not added to human dignity from outside; it is what human dignity looks like when it is taken seriously.

**What the Question Asks of the Listener**

The verse ends the sūrah without answering itself, which is unusual and purposeful. Most of the Qur'an's closing verses make a statement; this one requires a response. The listener is left holding the question and must supply the assent, and in supplying it he does something he could not do passively: he agrees that the difference between the two groups described earlier is real, and that it will be recognised. The report cited above formalises exactly that move, which is why it exists even in its weak transmission. The practice is not a ritual addition to the text; it is the text's own demand made audible. A chapter that began by swearing on provision, covenant and sanctuary closes by making the listener a witness to the justice that governs all three.
