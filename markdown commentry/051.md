# Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt (Chapter 51) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:1

> By the winds scattering ˹dust˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Oath Is Withheld**

The chapter opens inside an oath whose content has not arrived. Arabic builds such statements in two parts, the swearing itself, *qasam*, and the thing sworn to, its *jawāb*, and normally sets the two close together. Here three further oaths are piled on the first before the claim surfaces four verses later: what you are promised is true (51:5). A listener hears four agents at work in the created order, ranked and rising, and only then is handed the conclusion they were being led toward. That order does real work. Stated first, the claim would stand as a bare assertion; placed after four oaths it arrives as something the world has already testified to. The opening verse also declines to name its subject. It offers only *adh-dhāriyāt*, a feminine plural participle with no noun attached, and the hearer must supply the winds, the dust, or both. The small gap between the word and its object is the hook that holds the ear.

**Scattering Is Separation, Not Mere Motion**

The root behind the participle means to lift and disperse: winnowing grain so that chaff goes one way and seed another, or a wind taking dust off a dry plain and carrying it somewhere far. Winnowing is the decisive image, because winnowing sorts two things that looked mixed together. The chapter takes its name from this very word, and the naming is not arbitrary. What is sworn by is a force that separates; what is sworn to is a day of separation. Dust is also the stuff of the human being in the Qur'an account of origins, so the scattering wind is busy with the same material people are made of. A current able to lift dust, hold it in the air, and set it down again elsewhere is a daily, ordinary picture of bodies dispersed in the ground and called back from it. The verb keeps both faces at once: dispersal now, and gathering afterward.

**Four Participles Climbing a Single Scale**

Every one of the four oaths is an active participle in the feminine plural — *dhāriyāt, ḥāmilāt, jāriyāt, muqassimāt* — an agent engaged in an act, not an object at rest. The reading carried by the commentaries takes them as the winds, the rain-laden clouds, the ships gliding (or the planets running their courses), and the angels who distribute what God has decreed. The scale climbs as it goes: dust at your feet, water massed above you, vessels crossing the surface of the sea, and finally the messengers who carry the command down. A version of this explanation attributed to the Prophet ﷺ is on record, though Ibn Kathīr judged that attribution weak and accepted it only as the statement of ʿAlī and of ʿUmar. The chain matters less than the shape of the answer it gives. Two rival readings survive in the exegetical tradition: some took the four as stages in human generation, others read the third oath as stars instead of ships. Both preserve the ascent, and neither displaces the plain sense.

**The Wind Is a Standing Proof Elsewhere in the Book**

This appeal is not exotic or newly invented. The catalogue of signs in al-Baqarah already lists the shifting wind among the evidence offered to people of understanding, and the same list names two of the other things sworn by here, ships and clouds: *“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth; the alternation of the day and the night; the ships that sail the sea for the benefit of humanity; the rain sent down by Allah from the skies, reviving the earth after its death; the scattering of all kinds of creatures throughout; the shifting of the winds; and the clouds drifting between the heavens and the earth—˹in all of this˺ are surely signs for people of understanding.”* (2:164). The same wind that scatters also fertilises and feeds, which is why it can be sworn by without embarrassment: *“We send fertilizing winds, and bring down rain from the sky for you to drink. It is not you who hold its reserves.”* (15:22). An oath is credible only when the thing sworn by is genuinely great, and this one is.

**A Current Under Orders, Never a Free Force**

Oaths here are taken by servants, and the servant's status is part of the argument rather than a limitation on it. The wind scatters because it is sent: *“It is Allah Who sends the winds, which then stir up ˹vapour, forming˺ clouds, which He then spreads out in the sky or piles up into masses as He wills, from which you see rain come forth.”* (30:48). That sending can be withheld, and it can be turned; when it is turned, the identical force destroys: *“And as for ’Âd, they were destroyed by a furious, bitter wind”* (69:6). Nothing scatters on its own authority, and nothing refrains from scattering on its own authority either. This is what makes the oath an argument instead of an ornament. An agent that obeys points to a Commander who is obeyed, and the fourth oath in the series names that administration directly (51:4). The dust lifted off the plain is evidence of government, not of weather alone.

**What the Opening Word Commits the Whole Chapter To**

By beginning with scattering, the chapter states its subject in its first syllables. Things dispersed will be gathered; the power that disperses them is the power that gathers them again; and the interval between the two is not emptiness but a term already set. The wind carries seed and rain, dust and cloud, and it also carries a city's end. One force, opposite results, one Commander behind both. The resurrection sworn to in verse 5 is therefore not a rupture inserted into the natural order from outside. It is the natural order viewed end-on, the same obedience that lifts the dust now doing what it is told on the appointed day. Anyone who has watched a wind lift a dry field and drop it elsewhere has seen the mechanism of that promise at work, though usually without noticing. The next three oaths add the water, the crossing, and the command.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:2

> and ˹the clouds˺ loaded with rain

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Burden Carried Through the Air**

The second oath names a load. *Al-ḥāmilāt* are the ones carrying, and *wiqran* is a burden of the kind a beast bears — the word used for a pack-animal's freight, not for a featherweight. Applied to cloud, it produces a striking picture: water held aloft, enormous in mass, resting on nothing visible, moving steadily across the sky. The image works because everyone in a dry land knows what a loaded animal looks like and what happens when it kneels. The cloud kneels over a chosen place and puts its freight down. Arabic keeps this sense of weight in the word itself, and the commentators take the burden as the moisture the cloud carries from one region to another. Read alongside the first oath, the pairing is exact: a wind scatters, and what it gathers up becomes a load. Dispersal and carriage are two operations of one system, and the oath treats them as equally worthy of being sworn by.

**The Load Is Not the Cloud's Own Property**

Nothing carries itself. The cloud is driven, joined, and stacked by another agent: *“Do you not see that Allah gently drives the clouds, then joins them together, piling them up into masses, from which you see raindrops come forth? And He sends down from the sky mountains ˹of clouds˺ loaded with hail, pouring it on whoever He wills and averting it from whoever He wills.”* (24:43). Note how closely that verse matches this oath. It uses the same vocabulary of loading, and it locates the decision not in the cloud but in the will that directs it — on whoever He wills, withheld from whoever He wills. The rain that falls on one valley and skips the next is a distribution, and distribution implies a distributor. That is precisely the point the fourth oath of this series will make explicit when it swears by the angels administering affairs by command (51:4). The middle oath already assumes the conclusion: a burden carried is a burden assigned.

**Rain Is the Book's Own Proof of Return**

The Qur'an uses the falling of rain as its standing analogy for resurrection, and it does so bluntly: *“And it is Allah Who sends the winds, which then stir up ˹vapour, forming˺ clouds, and then We drive them to a lifeless land, giving life to the earth after its death. Similar is the Resurrection.”* (35:9). The same argument runs through another Meccan chapter: *“˹as˺ a provision for ˹Our˺ servants. And with this ˹rain˺ We revive a lifeless land. Similar is the emergence ˹from the graves˺.”* (50:11). The logic is a comparison of difficulty. Something dead and dry is restored to life by water that arrives from outside itself; if that happens every year to the soil, the claim that it will happen once to bodies is not an extravagance but the same event on a larger scale. An oath by the loaded cloud is therefore an oath by the most familiar evidence for the promise stated in verse 5.

**The Rival Reading: Those Who Carry a Child**

A second interpretation survives, recorded by al-Bayḍāwī and al-Qushayrī among others, which takes *al-ḥāmilāt* as women carrying pregnancy. On that reading the four oaths of verses 1 to 4 describe the generations of humankind: scattering seed, carrying the child, moving through life with ease, and apportioning affairs. The reading is not frivolous. It keeps the grammatical form intact, since the feminine plural participle fits women naturally, and it links the oath to the human story the chapter keeps returning to. Against it stands the sequence. The first oath is almost universally read as wind, and wind belongs with cloud; the third is read as ships or stars, which belong with a natural, not a human, scale; and the fourth is read as angels, above humanity altogether. A chain that begins in the atmosphere and ends in the angelic order loses its coherence if one link is swapped for a human being. The clouds remain the stronger sense, and the other reading is best kept as a permissible allusion rather than the meaning.

**Sequence Built Into the Grammar**

Three of the four oaths are joined by the letter *fāʾ*, which in Arabic marks sequence and consequence, not simple addition. The construction suggests a chain: wind scatters and lifts, cloud is loaded, ships glide, affairs are distributed. Each stage depends on the one before it. Wind moves vapour; vapour becomes water; water fills rivers and seas that carry vessels; and over the whole apparatus stands an administration. A reader who takes the oaths as four unrelated objects misses the argument, which is that the world's life-support runs as a single connected process with a single ordering intelligence at its head. The same connectedness appears in a sign-verse that names wind and ship together: *“And one of His signs is that He sends the winds, ushering in good news ˹of rain˺ so that He may give you a taste of His mercy, and that ships may sail by His command, and that you may seek His bounty, and perhaps you will be grateful.”* (30:46).

**A Held Weight Is a Trust, Not a Possession**

Water in the sky is the clearest example of a blessing nobody owns. Reserves of rain are not stored in any human warehouse: *“We send fertilizing winds, and bring down rain from the sky for you to drink. It is not you who hold its reserves.”* (15:22). The loaded cloud passes over a region and decides nothing itself; it is turned toward some places and away from others, and the people below can only watch. That helplessness is exactly what makes the oath persuasive to its first hearers, who lived on rainfall and knew how narrow the margin was. A year without the burden arriving was a year of famine. To swear by the loaded cloud, then, is to swear by the most precarious and most necessary thing in their world, and to say that its precariousness is managed. What arrives on time, in measure, and to the right place has been measured by someone. The next oath moves from the sky to what the sky makes possible on the water.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:3

> and ˹the ships˺ gliding with ease

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ease That Is Not Human Effort**

The third oath adds a single qualifier, *yusran*, meaning with ease, and that one word carries the argument. A loaded vessel crosses a hundred miles of open water without engine, without oar, without any visible means, because two elements cooperate: something buoyant beneath and something moving above. The human being on board contributes a rudder and a sail. The ease belongs to the arrangement, not to the passenger. This is why the participle *jāriyāt* — those gliding, running, flowing — is chosen rather than a word for travelling or crossing. It describes continuous, unresisted movement, the way water runs downhill. A merchant watching a heavy boat keep pace with a light one feels the point directly: the effort he can see is trivial compared with the support he cannot. The oath swears by that support, not by the boat.

**The Sea Is Subdued, Never Mastered**

Every ship in this Book sails by permission. The formula is repeated so often it becomes a signature: *“It is your Lord Who steers the ships for you through the sea, so that you may seek His bounty.”* (17:66), and *“Allah is the One Who has subjected the sea for you so that ships may sail upon it by His command, and that you may seek His bounty, and that perhaps you will be grateful.”* (45:12). The subjection is revocable, and the Qur'an says so in the same breath: *“If He wills, He can calm the wind, leaving the ships motionless on the water. Surely in this are signs for whoever is steadfast, grateful.”* (42:33). A becalmed ship is the identical vessel that yesterday glided with ease. The difference is entirely on the side of the wind, which is the first oath of this very series. Ease is therefore a gift held out to people who can neither produce it nor demand it, and the third oath is taken by that gift.

**Why a Ship Argues for the Resurrection**

The first hearers of this chapter were traders whose income moved by caravan and, on the coastal routes, by sea. Their wealth was literally floating. A ship that returns is a promise kept; a ship that does not is a household ended. To swear by ships gliding with ease was to swear by the thing they trusted least and needed most. The Book exploits the same fact elsewhere, in a scene of storm: *“He is the One Who enables you to travel through land and sea. And it so happens that you are on ships, sailing with a favourable wind, to the passengers’ delight. Suddenly, the ships are overcome by a gale wind and those on board are overwhelmed by waves from every side.”* (10:22). In that moment the denier prays with total sincerity. The chapter's argument is that the same dependence which surfaces in a storm is present in calm weather too, and the calm is what should have convinced him.

**The Rival Reading: Stars Running Their Courses**

Some exegetes take *al-jāriyāt* as the planets, moving through their stations with unforced regularity. That reading has real support in the text of the Qur'an itself, which repeatedly describes heavenly bodies as travelling: *“And He is the One Who created the day and the night, the sun and the moon—each travelling in an orbit.”* (21:33), and *“It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day. Each is travelling in an orbit of their own.”* (36:40). The verb in both places is of the same family as *jāriyāt*. The ascent argument favours the stars, since the series would then climb from dust to cloud to heaven to angels without a detour down to the sea. The sequence argument favours the ships, because the letter *fāʾ* joins each oath to the last, and wind and cloud are exactly what fill a sail. Both readings may stand together, but the chain of causes makes ships the plainer sense.

**A Prepared Medium Is the Real Sign**

What makes gliding possible is a set of properties that had to be fixed before any boat was built: water dense enough to float a hull but fluid enough to part before it, wind that blows in reliable patterns, and a coastline where trade can land. None of these were arranged by the navigator. The Qur'an names the pattern in a verse that ties wind and ship into a single sign: *“And one of His signs is that He sends the winds, ushering in good news ˹of rain˺ so that He may give you a taste of His mercy, and that ships may sail by His command, and that you may seek His bounty, and perhaps you will be grateful.”* (30:46). The gratitude at the end is the intended outcome of the whole arrangement, which means the ease of the ship is not an accident of physics but part of a design aimed at a response. A medium this well matched to its user is evidence of the user's Maker.

**The Argument Moves From Sky to Human Work**

Two oaths have now covered the atmosphere: dust lifted and water carried. The third brings the argument down to the surface where people earn their living, and the fourth will rise above the sky to the angels who administer affairs. Human labour sits between them, dependent on both. That placement is deliberate. The denier's objection to resurrection is not really about bones; it is about a life spent trading, planting, and planning that he is unwilling to see as provisional. By swearing by his own ship, the chapter asks him to notice how little of his livelihood is actually his. The ease with which a heavy vessel moves is the same ease with which a scattered body could be gathered. The next oath names the administration behind it all.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:4

> and ˹the angels˺ administering affairs by ˹Allah’s˺ command

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Highest Witness in the Series**

The four oaths end above the sky. Dust, cloud, and sea were all visible to the first hearers; the fourth witness is not. Its inclusion changes the character of the series, because the last three are things God governs, while the fourth is one of the governors. The participle *muqassimāt* means those who divide and distribute, and *amran* is the affair, the command, the whole business of running creation. What is sworn by, then, is not a natural process but an agency — a body of servants entrusted with carrying out decisions made elsewhere. That is why the verse closes with the word command, and why the whole series ends with an exclamation. The oath has climbed from the ground to the throne's attendants, and having reached the top it can say what all four witnesses were being assembled to say.

**Distribution Is Execution, Not Decision**

The angels apportion; they do not determine. A parallel series in another chapter makes the same point with almost the same words: *“and those conducting affairs ˹obediently˺!”* (79:5). The added qualifier there, obediently, removes any doubt about where authority sits. The Qur'an states the subordination explicitly: *“In fact, those ˹angels˺ are only ˹His˺ honoured servants, who do not speak until He has spoken, ˹only˺ acting at His command.”* (21:26–27). Nothing in this verse grants the distributors a share in the deciding. They carry revelation, carry rain, carry souls, carry records, and carry the punishment of cities — all as delivery, all on instruction. The distribution described here is thus the mechanism by which a single will reaches a million particular places at once: one command, many couriers, no committee.

**Administration Implies a Record**

A system that apportions affairs also keeps accounts. The Qur'an ties the two together in short, flat sentences: *“while you are certainly observed by vigilant, honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺. They know whatever you do.”* (82:10–12). The same idea appears with more detail: *“As the two recording-angels— ˹one˺ sitting to the right, and ˹the other to˺ the left—note ˹everything˺, not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺.”* (50:17–18). This is the link between the fourth oath and the sworn statement that follows it. If affairs are distributed and registered by servants who never forget and never err, then a day of settlement is not an addition to the system but its completion. An administration that records without ever auditing would be incoherent. The oath by the distributors is, in effect, an oath by the ledger.

**The Order Above Mirrors the Order Below**

Rain falls in measure, provision is measured out, and the measuring is done through the same channel. The angels who carry sustenance are described elsewhere as bearing the throne and interceding for the believers: *“Those ˹angels˺ who carry the Throne and those around it glorify the praises of their Lord, have faith in Him, and seek forgiveness for the believers.”* (40:7). There is a further verse that widens the scope: *“And the angels glorify the praises of their Lord, and seek forgiveness for those on earth.”* (42:5). The unseen order is therefore not a cold bureaucracy. It distributes, records, glorifies, and prays for the people below it. When the chapter swears by it, it swears by a reality that is attentive to human beings specifically — which is exactly what a promise of judgment requires. Judgment presumes that somebody has been paying attention.

**By Command: The Clause That Saves the Verse From Shirk**

Without the final phrase the oath could be misread as elevating the angels into independent powers, which is a mistake the Qur'an corrects repeatedly and bluntly: *“He sends down the angels with revelation by His command to whoever He wills of His servants.”* (16:2). By command, not by initiative. The whole economy of the heavens and the earth runs through a single conduit: *“He conducts every affair from the heavens to the earth, then it all ascends to Him on a Day whose length is a thousand years by your counting.”* (32:5). Note that this verse places the ascent of affairs on a Day — the same Day the present chapter is about to swear will come. The administration described in the oath is not merely how the world is kept running; it is how the world is being brought somewhere. Every distributed affair is also a filed one.

**Four Witnesses, One Verdict**

The series is now complete, and its shape can be read off. Wind scatters, cloud carries, ship glides, angels distribute: two elements, one human artefact, one unseen agency. Each is doing something it cannot do for itself, and each is doing it in a coordinated way that keeps human beings alive. The verdict that follows in the next verse is therefore not a leap. It is what these four things have been saying all along, in the only language they have. To swear by them is to call the created order into the witness box and ask it what it knows about its Maker's intentions. Its answer, given in two clauses, is that the promise is true and the reckoning will occur. Nothing about that answer requires the hearer to leave the world he is already standing in.

**The Same Channel Carries the Promise**

Revelation itself arrives through this administration, which brings the fourth oath very close to the chapter's own subject. The delivery of a message from God is described as a descent by command, sent to whichever servant God chooses (16:2). The book the deniers are arguing about came down the same route as the rain that loads the cloud and the affairs apportioned to every creature. If that channel is reliable enough to carry water to a valley and to record what a man says, it is reliable enough to carry a warning. The argument here asks nothing exotic of the hearer. He is not required to speculate about angelic natures or to accept a private vision; he is asked to notice that the world he lives inside is already run by an unseen service of delivery, and that the recitation in his ears was delivered by it. A courier system with an unbroken record on weather, on provision, and on records has earned a hearing on the last item it brought.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:5

> Indeed, what you are promised is true

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Arrives After Four Oaths**

This is the *jawāb*, the sworn content that the opening verses have been building toward. It begins with *inna*, the particle of emphasis, and it lands on a single adjective: *ḥaqq*, true. Four witnesses were called before it, and the sentence itself is only five words long in Arabic. The disproportion is the point. A claim that needs four oaths to support it and five words to state it is a claim whose difficulty lies not in its complexity but in people's refusal to accept it. Nothing here argues, reasons, or persuades in the ordinary sense. The verse simply announces, after the swearing, that the announcement is certain. Grammatically the oath and its answer are now closed: the chapter has taken its stand and can proceed to deal with the objections raised against it, which it does immediately in verse 8.

**"What You Are Promised" Is Left Unnamed**

The verse does not specify the promise. *Mā tūʿadūn* — that which you are promised — covers the resurrection, the reckoning, the reward, the punishment, and the Hour itself, all at once. The vagueness is deliberate and it does two jobs. First, it refuses to let the hearer pick which part of the promise he will dispute; a general statement cannot be answered by denying one detail. Second, it assumes a shared history. The Meccan audience had been hearing this promise for years from the same mouth, and the definite reference works only because both parties know what is meant. The same technique appears in other chapters: *“˹This is˺ the promise of Allah. ˹And˺ Allah never fails in His promise. But most people do not know.”* (30:6), and *“To Him is your return all together. Allah’s promise is ˹always˺ true.”* (10:4). In each case the promise is named by its source rather than by its content.

**Ḥaqq Means It Will Happen**

Truth in Arabic is not only correctness of a statement. *Ḥaqq* carries the sense of the fixed, the established, the thing that has a right to be. When the verse says the promise is *ḥaqq*, it says the promised event has the same ontological standing as an object already present — it is real, merely not yet arrived. This is why the very next verse does not argue but states occurrence: the Judgment will certainly come to pass (51:6). The two verses are not repetition. The first says the news is not false; the second says the event will not fail. Something could be truly announced and then cancelled, and the chapter closes that loophole in the following line. Read together, the pair removes both escape routes available to a denier: that the report is invented, or that the report is real but will be revoked.

**Why the Oath Was Necessary At All**

God does not need to swear. The oath exists for the one who doubts, and its witnesses are chosen from the doubter's own experience. The catalogue in al-Baqarah shows the method at work: the shifting winds, the rain reviving dead land, the ships, and the clouds are listed as signs *“for people of understanding”* (2:164). An oath by these things is an appeal to evidence the audience already accepts, used to establish a conclusion it does not. The logic runs from the visible order to the invisible promise: a world this carefully administered is not administered for nothing. If the four witnesses of verses 1 to 4 are genuine agents under genuine command, then the announcement they carry is genuine too. The oath is not rhetorical pressure. It is a compressed argument whose premises are the everyday operations of wind, water, sea, and unseen messengers.

**A Promise Addressed to Two Audiences At Once**

The pronoun is plural and inclusive: what *you* are promised. Denier and believer stand under the same sentence, and the same words do opposite work in each of them. For the one who refuses, the statement is a threat; the promise he calls a fairy tale is here declared real and dated. For the one who accepts, it is a comfort; the suffering he endures without recompense now has a guaranteed settlement. The Qur'an elsewhere marks this double effect explicitly: *“Those who disbelieve in it ˹ask to˺ hasten it ˹mockingly˺. But the believers are fearful of it, knowing that it is the truth.”* (42:18). One promise, two opposite reactions, and the difference lies entirely in the hearer. That is why the chapter moves at once from the truth of the promise to the state of those who dispute it.

**Believing Before Arrival Is the Whole Test**

A promise's truth can only be accepted before it is fulfilled; afterward, acceptance costs nothing. The chapter is addressed to people standing in that interval, when nothing visible supports the claim except the four sworn witnesses and the character of the one who made it. The Qur'an is candid about the difficulty: most people do not know (30:6). Knowledge of the promise is therefore not an intellectual achievement but a moral position, taken while the evidence is indirect and the payoff unseen. Anyone who waits for arrival before agreeing has not believed anything; he has merely witnessed. The verse's brevity matches that demand. It offers no proof to examine, only a fact to accept, and leaves the hearer to decide whether the four oaths behind it were sufficient. The next verse removes the last possibility of postponement.

**A Promise Whose Delay Does Not Weaken It**

Most doubt is manufactured inside the interval between announcement and arrival, so the verse has to be read together with what the Book says about that interval. The objection about slowness is answered in one sentence: *“They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment. And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting.”* (22:47). Another chapter gives a larger figure still, describing an ascent made on a day of fifty thousand years (70:4). Measured against scales like these, a human lifetime is not the test period but a moment inside it. The truth of the promise is untouched by the years, and the years themselves are part of what was promised, since a term had to be allowed for the message to travel and for people to answer it. Delay belongs to the promise, and never counts against it.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:6

> And the Judgment will certainly come to pass

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wāqiʿ Describes an Impact**

The word chosen for coming to pass is *wāqiʿ*, from a root meaning to fall, to land, to strike a surface. It is not the neutral vocabulary of occurrence. A thing that is *wāqiʿ* arrives with weight and consequence, the way a stone arrives. The same word opens another chapter devoted to the same event: *“When the Inevitable Event takes place, then no one can deny it has come.”* (56:1–2). There the falling is paired with the impossibility of denial, and the pairing explains the choice here. Once the thing has landed, argument is over. A neighbouring chapter states it in two flat lines: *“Indeed, the punishment of your Lord will come to pass— none will avert it—”* (52:7–8). The chapter of the scattering winds thus borrows the vocabulary of impact to describe a day that cannot be argued away, only endured.

**Two Clauses Making Two Different Claims**

Verse 5 said the promise is true. Verse 6 says the judgment will occur. The distinction is worth holding. A statement can be true in itself and yet the event it describes might be deferred indefinitely, or replaced by mercy, or forgotten. A second clause is needed to close those gaps, and this is that clause. Together the two verses assert both the reliability of the report and the inevitability of the arrival. Nothing further is added about timing, location, or manner. The chapter has stated its case in ten words and now turns to the state of the people who dispute it. That economy is characteristic of Meccan argument: the claim is planted firmly, then the objections are answered one by one rather than the claim being restated. Restating it would concede that the first statement was insufficient.

**Dīn Carries the Idea of a Debt Being Settled**

The Judgment named here is *ad-dīn*, the same word that gives the day its standing title throughout the Qur'an: the Day of *Dīn*. The term's root sense is a due, an obligation, something owed that must be paid or collected. That is why the day is described as a reckoning rather than a ceremony. Every act is a small debt, and the day is when accounts close. The chapter that takes its name from the day makes the finality explicit: *“˹It is˺ the Day no soul will be of ˹any˺ benefit to another whatsoever, for all authority on that Day belongs to Allah ˹entirely˺.”* (82:19). No intercession from an equal, no payment by a proxy, no appeal to a higher court. Authority on that day is undivided, and an undivided authority settles rather than negotiates. The word *dīn* therefore names both the obligation and its discharge.

**Emphasis Stacked Because the Matter Is Doubted**

Arabic has several ways to intensify a statement, and this verse uses two at once: the particle *inna* and the prefixed *lām* of emphasis, producing *la-wāqiʿ* — it will certainly fall. The doubling is not stylistic excess. It is calibrated to the audience, who treated the promise as a story. Where a fact is accepted, a plain verb suffices; where it is denied, the language loads up. The same pattern runs through the chapters that deal with the Hour: the assertion is reinforced precisely because the denial is loud. Note also that nothing in the verse is qualified. No condition, no exception, no *unless*, no *perhaps*. The certainty of the grammar mirrors the certainty of the event, and the chapter offers no version of this sentence in which the falling might not happen.

**No Postponement Is Available**

The mocker's implicit hope is delay, and the Qur'an closes that hope wherever it appears. Noah's preaching puts it in one line: *“Indeed, when the time set by Allah comes, it cannot be delayed, if only you knew!”* (71:4). The reply to those who ask for a date is equally blunt: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘A Day has ˹already˺ been appointed for you, which you can neither delay nor advance by a ˹single˺ moment.’”* (34:30). Both directions are blocked, which removes the two comforts available to a person who does not want the day to arrive: that it can be pushed back, or that it can be brought forward and got over with. What remains is a fixed point that the hearer is moving toward at the speed of his own life. The certainty of arrival is the practical content of this verse.

**Why the Falling Makes the Present Life Serious**

An event that must land converts the time before it into preparation. The Qur'an draws that conclusion in the shortest possible terms: *“So whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it. And whoever does an atom’s weight of evil will see it.”* (99:7–8). Nothing is too small to be seen, which means nothing is too small to have been worth doing. This is the moral weight the verse adds to the promise of verse 5. Truth plus inevitability produces obligation: if the settlement is real and certain, then the conduct of the interval matters in a way it would not otherwise. The chapter immediately demonstrates the point by setting the condemned against the reverent, describing what each group did with the time before the falling. The day does not create the difference between them. It publishes one that already exists.

**Falling Ends the Argument About Dates**

Nearly every objection in the verses that follow is one question wearing different clothes: when? The chapter answers by refusing the category. A thing that falls does not require its schedule to be published in advance, because the falling is the schedule. The day has an appointment that is fixed and concealed at the same time: *“Indeed, the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision is an appointed time—”* (78:17). That arrangement is not a gap in the message. It is the structure of the test. A date known in advance would turn preparation into arithmetic, and the moral weight of the interval would disappear; a day that never arrives would make conduct meaningless. What is given instead is certainty about arrival and silence about timing, which is the only combination that produces a life actually lived in readiness. The verse therefore does more than announce an event. It sets the conditions under which human beings can be judged fairly at all.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:7

> ˹And˺ by the heavens in their marvellous design

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Oath Series Begins**

The first oath and its answer are finished, and the chapter starts again from a swearing. This time only one witness is called, the heaven, and the answer that follows is not a promise but a diagnosis: you are in conflicting views (51:8). The shift tells the reader how the chapter is organised. The first series established what is true; the second addresses why people fail to see it. A single oath is enough for that purpose, because the object of the diagnosis is not the natural world but the human mind, and the sky is invoked as the one thing everyone looks at and nobody disputes. Structurally the verse stands alone between two blocks, closing the argument about the resurrection and opening the argument about disagreement. The exclamation at its end marks the pivot.

**Ḥubuk Means Streaks, Ripples, and Paths**

The word translated as design is *ḥubuk*, the plural of *ḥibāk*. Its base sense is the pattern a wind leaves on sand or water: parallel ridges, fine lines, ripples. The same word is used of the streaks visible on a mountainside and of the tight, even weave of good cloth. Al-Ṭabarī gathers these senses and relates them to the verb *ḥabaka*, to make a thing well and firmly. Understood of the sky, *ḥubuk* becomes the paths along which the heavenly bodies travel, or the layered structure of the clouds, or simply the visible orderliness of the vault overhead. All of these meanings share one idea: a surface that is not blank but ruled, arranged, and finished. The translation "marvellous design" keeps that idea, but the Arabic is more concrete. It says the sky has lines in it, like water combed by wind.

**A Design With No Flaw In It**

The Qur'an invites inspection of exactly this feature and predicts the result: *“˹He is the One˺ Who created seven heavens, one above the other. You will never see any imperfection in the creation of the Most Compassionate. So look again: do you see any flaws? Then look again and again— your sight will return frustrated and weary.”* (67:3–4). The challenge is repeated, and the repetition matters. A single look might miss a defect; a hundred looks that return empty-handed establish something. The verse in front of us swears by that result. When God takes an oath by the heaven's *ḥubuk*, He is swearing by a structure that has been examined and found sound — not by size or splendour, which can be admired without thought, but by the absence of contradiction in its arrangement.

**The Paths Are Travelled**

Order in the sky is not static decoration; it is a set of courses being kept. *“And He is the One Who created the day and the night, the sun and the moon—each travelling in an orbit.”* (21:33). The precision is stated even more sharply elsewhere: *“The sun and the moon ˹travel˺ with precision.”* (55:5). Neither body overtakes the other, and the alternation never breaks: *“It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day.”* (36:40). What the *ḥubuk* describes, then, is a road system in use, and the traffic on it has never once been late. The support structure is invisible — the heavens are raised without pillars anyone can see (13:2) — and yet the loads never fall. An oath by this is an oath by sustained, measured obedience on a scale no human being can interrupt.

**Why the Sky Is Called as a Witness**

The sky is the one piece of evidence no listener can decline. It is above every tribe's territory, unchanged by politics, and available to the poor and the rich equally. The Qur'an uses it as a comparative test: *“Which is harder to create: you or the sky? He built it.”* (79:27). If the greater structure was built and finished without defect, the smaller one's reconstruction is not beyond the same builder. That inference runs underneath the oath here, and it prepares the way for the chapter's later appeal to signs on the earth and in human selves (51:20–21). Another short sūrah makes the same appeal as a set of direct questions: *“and the sky—how it was raised ˹high˺”* (88:18). The raising, the mountains, and the levelling of the earth are put to the hearer as things to look at, not things to believe on report.

**Beauty Here Is Evidence, Not Ornament**

The aesthetic dimension of *ḥubuk* is not incidental. A weave is tight because someone made it tight; ripples are regular because the medium and the force are constant. The Qur'an treats beauty as a signature of intention: *“Who has perfected everything He created.”* (32:7), and *“And ˹He subjected˺ for you whatever He has created on earth of varying colours. Surely in this is a sign for those who are mindful.”* (16:13). Colour, pattern, and finish appear as evidence rather than as pleasing extras. When the sky is sworn by for its design, the argument is that nothing in it looks accidental, and accidental things do not produce ruled patterns that hold for centuries. The verse therefore does more than admire. It sets up the accusation that follows: people who cannot deny the order overhead have no excuse for the confusion they carry in their heads.

**Looking Up Is an Act of Reasoning**

The sky in this Book is material for thought rather than an object of admiration, and the people who do the thinking are named: *“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the day and night there are signs for people of reason.”* (3:190). They are then caught in the act: *“˹They are˺ those who remember Allah while standing, sitting, and lying on their sides, and reflect on the creation of the heavens and the earth.”* (3:191). The oath here addresses the same audience and asks for the same operation — inspection of a ruled, finished surface rather than awe at its size. The canopy overhead is also functional and protective: *“And We have made the sky a well-protected canopy, still they turn away from its signs.”* (21:32). That last clause carries the sting. The turning away described two verses earlier is now located exactly where the evidence is hardest to avoid.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:8

> Surely you are ˹lost˺ in conflicting views ˹regarding the truth˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath's Answer Is a Diagnosis**

An oath by the heaven's design should, by ordinary expectation, be followed by a claim about the heaven or about God. Instead the answer is about the people listening: you are in differing claims. The mismatch is the message. The sky is ruled, consistent, and free of contradiction; the minds beneath it are not. By swearing on order and answering with disorder, the verse puts the two side by side and lets the contrast do the work. Nothing is said about why the sky is well made; the emphasis falls entirely on the fact that the audience is confused in the presence of something perfectly clear. The bracketed addition "regarding the truth" in the translation makes explicit what the Arabic leaves to context — the object of the disagreement is the message itself and the one who brought it.

**Three Things They Are Arguing About**

The exegetes identify three objects of the dispute, and all three are supported by the Qur'an's own record of Meccan conversation. First, the book: some accept it and some deny it. Second, the person who brought it: poet, sorcerer, possessed, a man taught by another, a liar. Third, the resurrection and the judgment, which is the specific claim the first oath series just established. The three are connected. A disagreement about a messenger usually rests on a prior disagreement about whether the dead rise, and a disagreement about a book usually rests on a view of its author. The verse compresses all of this into one word, *mukhtalif*, differing. It does not say they are wrong about one point; it says their position is internally at odds, which is a stronger and more useful criticism.

**Contradiction Is the Diagnostic the Qur'an Itself Uses**

Elsewhere the Book turns inconsistency into a test of origin: *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies.”* (4:82). The same measure is applied here to the objectors. A group that says, in the space of a few years, that the reciter is a poet and a madman and a magician and a liar has not produced four theories; it has produced four incompatible guesses. Each one excludes the others. Poetry requires craft and control; madness excludes both. Magic presumes a teacher and a technique; lying presumes an intention and a plan. The verse names this state without bothering to refute any single item in it, because a set of mutually exclusive claims refutes itself.

**The Catalogue of Accusations Is On Record**

The Qur'an preserves the contradictory charges verbatim, which is what makes this diagnosis checkable. *“Yet they say, ‘This ˹Quran˺ is a set of confused dreams! No, he has fabricated it! No, he must be a poet!’”* (21:5) — three options offered in one breath, each cancelling the last. *“And the disbelievers say, ‘This is a magician, a total liar!’”* (38:4). *“The disbelievers would almost cut you down with their eyes when they hear ˹you recite˺ the Reminder, and say, ‘He is certainly a madman.’”* (68:51). *“and argued, ‘Should we really abandon our gods for a mad poet?’”* (37:36). Note that last pair: mad poet, two words that cannot both be true of one man. The record itself confirms what verse 8 asserts.

**Argumentativeness as a Standing Human Trait**

The confusion described here is not an accident of one city in one century. The Qur'an generalises it in a single sentence: *“We have surely set forth in this Quran every ˹kind of˺ lesson for people, but humankind is the most argumentative of all beings.”* (18:54). Clarity of the message and multiplicity of objections are stated together, so that the reader does not mistake the volume of dispute for evidence against the message. Where every lesson has been set out and argument still proliferates, the cause is not a shortage of material. This is why verse 8 is followed by an explanation of the mechanism rather than by further evidence: the problem is not that the case is weak, and the next verse says so by describing the kind of person who can be turned away from a clear thing.

**Disputing Is Not the Same as Asking**

The verse does not condemn difference of opinion as such. Islamic law and exegesis are built on disagreement conducted with method, evidence, and a willingness to be corrected. What is condemned here is *ikhtilāf* without direction: competing assertions, none of them tested, none of them abandoned when shown to fail. The Qur'an marks the difference by describing the epistemic ground of such speech: *“˹O Prophet!˺ If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah’s Way. They follow nothing but assumptions and do nothing but lie.”* (6:116). Assumption plus contradiction is the state named in verse 8. The following verse explains that this state is not imposed from outside on an innocent mind; it is where a mind ends up after it has been turned.

**Unresolved Confusion Turns Into Hostility**

A disagreement that cannot be settled by evidence tends to settle itself by force, and the Qur'an records the escalation as it happens. Clarity does not calm the objectors; it drives them further out: *“We have surely varied ˹the signs˺ in this Quran so perhaps they may be mindful, but it only drives them farther away.”* (17:41). The accusations grow stranger rather than sharper, and one group settles on a theory of conspiracy: *“The disbelievers say, ‘This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but a fabrication which he made up with the help of others.’ Their claim is totally unjustified and untrue!”* (25:4). This is the practical content of the diagnosis in verse 8. People who cannot agree on a single charge do not become quieter as the charges fail; they become angrier, because each new guess collapses like the last and the only remaining object is the man who will not collapse with them.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:9

> Only those ˹destined to be˺ deluded are turned away from it

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yuʾfak: To Be Turned Around**

The verb here is from the root *a-f-k*, whose basic sense is inversion: turning a thing away from its proper direction, flipping it over, diverting it. Applied to a person and to truth, it means being turned away from what is straight. The Qur'an uses the same root as a rebuke elsewhere: *“That is Allah! How can you then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?”* (6:95). The image is physical, and it is worth holding on to. A person under this verb has not merely failed to arrive at the truth; he has been rotated so that the truth is now behind him and he is walking away from it while facing forward. That is why the verse can say only those turned away are turned away: the direction of the movement is the description of the state.

**Lying Lives in the Same Root**

*A-f-k* also produces *ifk*, a lie, and *affāk*, a habitual liar. The double meaning is not a coincidence but a single idea seen from two sides. To lie is to turn speech away from what is; to be turned away from truth is to have one's judgement inverted so that it produces falsehood. The commentators exploited this by reading the verb actively instead of passively: whoever lies shall be turned away from it. On that reading the verse states a moral law rather than a fact about particular people — dishonesty in speech trains the mind to miss the truth, and the missing becomes permanent. Both readings can stand, and they support each other. The passive describes what happens to a person; the active describes the habit that made him that kind of person.

**Weak Judgment Turns a Person Away From Religion**

Mujāhid's explanation, preserved in the classical commentaries, is blunt: whoever is weak in intellect and in judgment will be turned away from religion by that weakness. The point is not that stupidity is punished. It is that the truth of this message requires a person to hold several things at once — order in creation, obligation in conduct, a settlement to come — and a mind that cannot hold them will drop one and rebuild the rest around the gap. What drops out is usually the inconvenient item. This explains why verse 8's chaos of contradictory accusations is possible at all. The accusations are not rival theories competing for evidence; they are the debris left by a judgment that could not carry the weight of a single coherent position.

**The Brackets Around "Destined"**

The translator's insertion marks a genuine difficulty. The Arabic verb is passive — he is turned — and it does not say by whom, though the surrounding text leaves no doubt that God is the ultimate agent. The Qur'an states this without softening: *“We turn their hearts and eyes away ˹from the truth˺ as they refused to believe at first, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance.”* (6:110). Note the reason clause in that verse: as they refused at first. The turning is a response to a refusal already made. The same balance appears in another passage: *“Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious—”* (2:26). The two halves are held together everywhere in the Book: God turns, and the one turned had already chosen the direction. To read verse 9 as pure fatalism is to drop the second half.

**Habits Harden Into Seals**

Turning away, repeated, stops being an event and becomes a structure. The Qur'an describes the endpoint: *“Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god? ˹And so˺ Allah left them to stray knowingly, sealed their hearing and hearts, and placed a cover on their sight. Who then can guide them after Allah?”* (45:23). Three organs are named — hearing, heart, sight — and all three are the faculties a person would need in order to respond to the message. The sealing is described as knowing, which means it is not an accident or an oversight; it is a consequence that fits the case. Verse 9 describes the beginning of that process, and this description of its end explains why the verse speaks of a class rather than of individuals: the turned-away are those for whom turning has become their fixed condition.

**The Door Remains Open While the Turning Is Incomplete**

Nothing in this verse closes repentance. The category described is a present state, not a final verdict pronounced in advance on a person who has not yet died. The Qur'an's widest invitation is addressed to people who have already exceeded every limit: *“Say, ˹O Prophet, that Allah says,˺ ‘O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.’”* (39:53). The turn described here can be reversed by the one who performed it, and the chapter assumes throughout that its hearers are still in a position to listen — otherwise there would be no point addressing them. What verse 9 removes is a comfortable illusion: that confusion is a neutral condition, a mere difference of opinion, harmless so long as it is sincere. It is not neutral. It is a direction, and it leads somewhere.

**The Word "Only" Makes the Class Exclusive**

The restriction cuts in two directions. If nobody but the turned-away is turned away, then nobody else is shut out: the door closes from inside, not from above. The same limitation is stated from the side of the evidence: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Consider all that is in the heavens and the earth!’ Yet neither signs nor warners are of any benefit to those who refuse to believe.”* (10:101). The failure sits with the one who refuses, not with the material placed before him. For a believer this verse is a reassurance rather than a threat. The confusion he meets in argument is not proof that the case is unclear; it is proof that his opponent has been rotated away from it, and that rotation is something the opponent did. For the one arguing, the verse is a summons to check his own direction before adding one more claim to the pile he already cannot hold.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:10

> Condemned are the liars—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qutila Is a Curse Shaped Like a Killing**

The verb is literally "killed" or "may he be killed," used in Arabic as an imprecation: ruined, destroyed, cut off. It carries no legal content and no reference to actual violence. It is the language of invocation, and the Qur'an uses the same idiom elsewhere in the same way: *“Condemned are ˹disbelieving˺ humans! How ungrateful they are ˹to Allah˺!”* (80:17), *“Condemned are the makers of the ditch—”* (85:4), and *“May he be condemned! How evil was what he determined!”* (74:19). In each case the form is passive and the sense is a prayer for ruin against a described behaviour, not against a named individual. The weight of the word comes from its bluntness. After four oaths, two solemn assertions, and a diagnosis of confusion, the chapter stops analysing and pronounces. That change of register is itself an argument: some states are past description.

**Kharrāṣūn Are the Guessers**

The word translated as liars is *kharrāṣūn*, from a root meaning to conjecture, to estimate, to guess — originally to calculate something roughly, as one estimates a harvest by looking at it. The intensive pattern *faʿʿāl* turns the act into a profession or a fixed habit: not a person who once guessed, but a person whose normal mode of speech is guessing. The commentators add two further senses: one who states his guess as though it were established knowledge, and one who fabricates accusations against another. Both fit the context. The men who called the Prophet ﷺ a poet, a madman, and a magician were not reporting observations. They were producing estimates of a thing they had not examined and presenting them as findings.

**What They Are Guessing About**

The classical reading takes this verse as aimed at those who say human beings will not be resurrected and who deny that certainty about such matters is possible. That matches the chapter's opening, which has just sworn that the promise is true and the judgment will fall (51:5–6). The guessers are therefore contradicting a sworn statement, and doing so without evidence of their own. Their position is not knowledge opposed to knowledge; it is conjecture opposed to testimony. This is why the verse does not argue with them. Argument presumes two people weighing the same kind of material. A man who asserts the impossibility of resurrection on no grounds has not entered a dispute; he has made a noise, and the response to noise is a curse rather than a syllogism.

**Assumption Cannot Carry the Weight of Truth**

The Qur'an makes the epistemic point in terms that could not be plainer: *“Most of them follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions. ˹And˺ surely assumptions can in no way replace the truth.”* (10:36). The same sentence recurs almost word for word: *“although they have no knowledge ˹in support˺ of this. They follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions. And surely assumptions can in no way replace the truth.”* (53:28). The repetition is deliberate, since the claim being made is about method rather than about content. Whatever a person believes by guesswork, he holds it in a way that cannot support weight. When the question is the resurrection of bodies and the settlement of accounts, the required support is certainty, and certainty does not come from estimating. The word *kharrāṣ* names precisely the man who offers estimates in place of it.

**A Sentence That Deliberately Runs On**

The verse ends unfinished. In Arabic the dash is real: the next verse continues as a description of the same people, those steeped in ignorance and totally heedless (51:11). The construction is apposition, and its effect is to hold the curse in the air while the character of the condemned is drawn. The reader is not allowed to hear "condemned are the guessers" and move on. He must wait through the description to find out what guessing looks like from the inside. That structure also protects the verse from being read as a general insult. The condemnation is attached to a specified condition — immersion in ignorance combined with inattention — and the following verse supplies the specification. A curse without a description would be mere anger; with one, it is a verdict.

**The Opposite of Guessing Is the Certainty Already Sworn To**

The chapter has set two things against each other and named both. On one side is *ḥaqq*, the true and the fixed, sworn to by four witnesses in verses 1 to 6. On the other is *kharrāṣ*, the estimate offered as a finding. Everything between verse 7 and verse 14 works out that opposition: conflicting views, minds turned away, immersion in ignorance, mockery about the date of the day. The verse in front of us is the pivot where the description stops being clinical and becomes judicial. The Book elsewhere describes the same substitution at the level of scripture: *“And among them are the illiterate who know nothing about the Scripture except lies, and ˹so˺ they ˹wishfully˺ speculate.”* (2:78). Wishful speculation is the exact activity of the *kharrāṣūn*: forming a view because it is comfortable, then defending it as if it had been earned.

**The Rule That Replaces Guessing Is Verification**

Every condemnation of conjecture in the Qur'an has a stated alternative, and naming it keeps the curse from sounding like mere anger. The instruction to believers is procedural: *“O believers, if an evildoer brings you any news, verify ˹it˺ so you do not harm people unknowingly, becoming regretful for what you have done.”* (49:6). Verification is demanded before speech produces harm, which is precisely the step the guessers skip. A second verse locates the source of fabrication: *“No one fabricates lies except those who disbelieve in Allah’s revelations, and it is they who are the ˹true˺ liars.”* (16:105). The two together define the practice being condemned and the cure for it. Guessing is asserting without checking, and the remedy is checking before asserting. A man who stops estimating and begins examining steps out of the category this verse names, no matter what he said while he was inside it.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:11

> those who are ˹steeped˺ in ignorance, totally heedless

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ghamra Is Water Over a Man's Head**

The word rendered as steeped is *ghamra*, from a root meaning to cover completely, to submerge, to overwhelm. Arabic uses it for deep water that closes over a person's head, and by extension for any state a man is inside and cannot see out of. The picture is not of a lack but of an excess: too much around him, no air left. The Qur'an applies the same image to a people lost in their own confusion, swearing by the Prophet's life that they wandered blindly, intoxicated (15:72). Blindness here is not the absence of sight but its suspension inside something stronger. That is the condition verse 11 names. Ignorance in this sense is not a blank page waiting to be written on; it is a flooded room, and the difficulty is not information but the water.

**Sāhūn Names Inattention, Not Absence**

The second term, *sāhūn*, is the active participle of *sahā*, to be unmindful, to let the attention drift. It does not describe someone who is absent from a place; it describes someone present but not attending. The Qur'an uses it inside a warning addressed to people at prayer: *“So woe to those ˹hypocrites˺ who pray yet are unmindful of their prayers”* (107:4–5). Those people perform the action and miss the action. The same word fits verse 11 exactly. The heedless are not away from the message; they are in the room while it is recited, arguing about it, and none of it reaches the part of them that could respond. Heedlessness is therefore a posture of attention rather than a quantity of knowledge, which is why it pairs so naturally with immersion.

**Two Defects That Close Each Other's Exit**

Taken separately, each condition has a way out. An ignorant person can be taught, and an inattentive person can be startled into attention. Joined, they lock. A man who does not know cannot recognise the moment worth attending to, and a man who does not attend cannot acquire what he is missing. Verse 11 is not listing two complaints about the same group; it is describing a single mechanism with two interlocking parts. This is why the previous verse's curse is not merely severe but exact. The guessers are cursed because they have arranged themselves in a state where no further evidence can operate. The chapter's diagnosis, complete over three verses, moves from contradiction to inversion to submersion: differing claims, a mind turned away, and finally a person under water who is not looking for the surface.

**The Sentence of Verse 10 Is Completed Here**

Grammatically this verse continues the previous one rather than starting fresh. The cursed are named in verse 10, and verse 11 supplies their description, functioning as an apposition that explains who the word refers to. The effect on the reader is deliberate. The curse is suspended while the character of the cursed is drawn, so that nobody can hear the imprecation as a burst of anger against an unmentioned enemy. By the time the sentence closes, the hearer knows exactly which condition is condemned: guessing stated as knowledge, sustained by inattention, inside a confusion the person has no interest in leaving. Any reader can test himself against that description. The verse is structured to make the test easy to apply and uncomfortable to pass.

**Heedlessness Is the Ordinary Human Failure**

The chapter treats this as the common case, not an extreme one. Another Meccan sūrah opens with the same observation: *“˹The time of˺ people’s judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away. Whatever new reminder comes to them from their Lord, they only listen to it jokingly, with their hearts ˹totally˺ distracted.”* (21:1–3). Proximity of the event and distraction of the heart are stated in the same breath, and the distraction is described as total. The instruction that follows such passages is sometimes simply to leave the people in their heedlessness for a while (23:54) — not as a punishment but because attention cannot be forced on someone who has arranged not to give it. Verse 11 belongs to that tradition of description, which is why it reads as calm rather than furious.

**The Veil Is Lifted on the Day**

Heedlessness has an end, and the end is sudden. The scene is stated in the second person, addressed to the one who was not attending: *“˹It will be said to the denier,˺ ‘You were totally heedless of this. Now We have lifted this veil of yours, so Today your sight is sharp!’”* (50:22). The word used for heedless is from the same family as *sāhūn* here, which ties the two passages together. Sight becomes sharp at precisely the moment when sharpness can no longer change anything. That is the real content of the warning in verse 11. The problem is not that these people will never see; it is that they will see later, when seeing has stopped being useful. The verse is a statement about timing rather than about ability.

**Attention Can Be Recovered Now**

Nothing here declares the condition permanent for any living person. The Qur'an describes the opposite movement in the same vocabulary: the guided are *“those who listen to what is said and follow the best of it”* (39:18). Listening and selecting are acts of attention, and they are presented as achievable. The difference between the heedless of verse 11 and the listeners of that verse is not intelligence or education; it is whether the person is presently looking at what is in front of him. This is the practical edge of the verse. It asks a reader to notice which of the two descriptions currently fits — a man submerged in a confusion he enjoys, or a man who hears a statement and asks whether it is true. The chapter gives no third category, because there is none.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:12

> They ask ˹mockingly˺, “When is this Day of Judgment?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Is Not a Request for Information**

The word *when* looks like an inquiry and is not one. The classical commentaries are explicit that this question and its variants are posed in a spirit of derision and denial rather than curiosity. A man who wants a date asks a different way; he wants the answer to be useful. Here the question is a challenge: produce the schedule, or admit there is none. The translation marks this with the bracketed "mockingly," which is an interpretation of tone supported by the surrounding verses — the same people are described a few lines earlier as steeped in ignorance and heedless (51:11), and a few lines later they are told to taste what they sought to hasten (51:14). Mockery is the register that connects those descriptions. The question is a weapon shaped like a sentence.

**A Formula Repeated All Through the Book**

This exact challenge appears again and again, sometimes in identical words. Another chapter puts it as: *“asking ˹mockingly˺, ‘When is this Day of Judgment?’”* (75:6) — the same phrasing, with the same bracketed tone. The deniers of other prophets ask it too: *“They ask ˹the believers˺, ‘When will this threat come to pass if what you say is true?’”* (10:48). The formula recurs in 21:38, 27:71, 34:29, 36:48, and 67:25. The repetition is evidence about the objectors as much as about the message. A question asked in every generation, by every audience, in the same words, is not a research question. It is the standard move of a person who has decided the answer will be "never" and is using the interrogative form to say so politely.

**"When" Assumes a Calendar God Does Not Publish**

The challenge carries a hidden premise: that a promise is only serious if it is dated. The Qur'an rejects the premise and states where the knowledge sits: *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the Hour, ‘When will it be?’ Say, ‘That knowledge is only with my Lord. He alone will reveal it when the time comes.’”* (7:187). The same verse adds that it will come as a surprise, which answers the mocker on his own ground — a date published in advance would allow preparation, and preparation on demand is not what the test is for. A further reply fixes the appointment without revealing it: *“A Day has ˹already˺ been appointed for you, which you can neither delay nor advance by a ˹single˺ moment.”* (34:30). Certainty about the event and concealment of its hour are held together deliberately.

**Asking "When" Is a Way of Saying "Never"**

The position underneath the question is stated openly by the people who hold it: *“And they argue, ‘There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time.’ Yet they have no knowledge ˹in support˺ of this ˹claim˺. They only speculate.”* (45:24). Given that belief, the question about the date has no possible answer, since the asker has already excluded the event. This is what connects verse 12 to the condemnation of the guessers two verses earlier. The man who asks "when" while holding that there is no day is not seeking; he is performing. His scepticism is not a method but a conclusion worn as a question, and the chapter identifies it as speculation dressed up as inquiry.

**Two Audiences Hear the Same Question Differently**

The Book records both reactions to the Hour in one verse: *“Those who disbelieve in it ˹ask to˺ hasten it ˹mockingly˺. But the believers are fearful of it, knowing that it is the truth.”* (42:18). The same event produces derision in one group and fear in the other, and the difference is knowledge rather than temperament. Fear here is not terror of an enemy; it is the alarm of a person who has an account to settle and knows the settlement is real. Mockery is the sound of someone who believes there is no account. The verse in front of us therefore does more than report a question. It marks which of the two groups the asker belongs to, and the following verses answer him in the terms his own group has chosen.

**The Answer Refuses the Date and Gives the Content**

What follows is instructive as a method. The chapter does not supply a date, explain why none can be supplied, or rebuke the questioner for asking. It answers with a description: it is the day they will be tormented over the fire (51:13). The reply changes the subject from timing to substance, and in doing so it removes the ground the question was standing on. A mocker asks "when" because the answer will be embarrassing either way — too soon to be plausible, too late to be feared. A description of what the day contains cannot be mocked in the same way, because it is not a claim about a calendar. This is a pattern in the Qur'an's handling of hostile questions: answer the assumption behind the question rather than the question's form.

**Mockery Is Also a Form of Hastening**

The same people who ask when are described elsewhere as trying to bring the punishment forward. Their prayer is recorded: *“And ˹remember˺ when they prayed, ‘O Allah! If this is indeed the truth from You, then rain down stones upon us from the sky or overcome us with a painful punishment.’”* (8:32). The reply to that posture is a statement of jurisdiction: *“That ˹torment˺ you seek to hasten is not within my power. It is only Allah Who decides ˹its time˺.”* (6:57). Mockery and hastening are two faces of one thing: an attempt to force the promise into the mocker's own timeframe, either by producing it now or by proving it never comes. Verse 12 is the polite version of that attempt. Verse 14 returns it to him in his own words.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:13

> ˹It is˺ the Day they will be tormented over the Fire

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yuftanūn Comes From Assaying Metal**

The verb is from the root *f-ṭ-n*, and its original technical sense is precise. Al-Azharī records in his *Tahdhīb al-Lugha* that the word comes from the phrase *fatantu al-fidda wa'l-dhahab*, "I assayed the silver and the gold," meaning to melt metal in order to separate the bad from the good, and he cites this very verse as an instance of the sense "burning with fire." Ibn Fāris says the same thing in *Maqāyīs al-Lugha*: *f-ṭ-n* is a sound root indicating testing or trial. The commentators explain the word here accordingly — the people of this day are tried upon the fire, which is to say punished and burned in it. The metallurgical origin matters because it carries an idea of exposure: fire shows what a substance actually is.

**Over the Fire, Not Yet Inside It**

The preposition is *ʿalā*, upon or over, and the commentators read it as presentation to the fire. The Qur'an describes that presentation elsewhere in a daily rhythm: the people of Pharaoh are exposed to the fire morning and evening in the interval before the Hour (40:46). Then the entry itself is described with a different verb: *“˹It is˺ the Day they will be fiercely shoved into the Fire of Hell.”* (52:13). The two stages are distinct, and verse 13 belongs to the first. There is a moment of being held over the thing, which is worse than ignorance and worse than arrival, because the person now sees exactly what he denied. The preposition therefore does small grammatical work with a large effect: the day is described as proximity before it is described as contact.

**The Day Is Named After What Happens in It**

Arabic frequently labels the Day of Judgment by its content, and this verse is an example of the technique. *Yawm yuftanūn* — the day they are assayed — makes the event its own name. The same method appears in the description of a day of decision fixed at an appointed time (78:17), and in the title of the chapter of the Inevitable Event (56:1). Naming the day after its operation removes any suggestion that it is a ceremony or a proclamation. It is a process performed on people, and the name tells you what the process is. This is also the answer to the question in verse 12. The mockers asked for a date; the chapter gives them a definition. A definition cannot be postponed, and it cannot be laughed at in the same way, because it describes something that will be done rather than something that will be announced.

**The Assay Cannot Purify Anything Here**

In the metallurgy the word comes from, fire improves the metal: dross is removed and the gold survives better than it was. That half of the image is deliberately withheld on this day. Nothing is refined, because refinement requires a subject capable of change and a time in which to change it. The fire here discloses and consumes. The Qur'an describes the Fire's relation to its inhabitants in terms of permanence: *“But as for those who are rebellious, the Fire will be their home. Whenever they try to escape from it, they will be forced back into it.”* (32:20). Escape is attempted and reversed, which is the exact opposite of purification. The word *yuftanūn* therefore keeps its etymology and inverts its purpose: the test is real, the result is known, and no benefit accrues to the one tested.

**The Metaphor Explains Why Fire Is the Instrument**

Fire is chosen throughout the Qur'an not as an arbitrary cruelty but as the element that reveals and the element that cannot be argued with. It is described as kindled by God and raging over hearts: *“˹It is˺ Allah’s kindled Fire, which rages over the hearts.”* (104:6–7). The location is significant. The heart is where the mockery of verse 12 was produced, where the guessing of verse 10 was stored, and where the heedlessness of verse 11 was maintained. An instrument applied to the organ of decision answers the offences committed by that organ. The same logic runs through the earlier verses of this chapter: those who would not attend with their hearts are held over a fire that reaches the heart.

**The Answer Given to the Mocker's Question**

Read as a reply, this verse is exact. The question was "when," and the answer is a description that makes the date irrelevant: the day is the day of your assay. Whether it comes in a year or a thousand changes nothing about what it will be. The chapter has already established the point twice — the promise is true, and the judgment will certainly fall (51:5–6) — and now it adds what falling consists of. This is how the Book handles a hostile question in general. It refuses to argue on the questioner's terms and restates the matter in terms the questioner cannot rearrange. A date can be disputed; a process cannot. The mocker who wanted to embarrass the Prophet ﷺ with a calendar is handed a furnace instead.

**Sight Arrives When It Can No Longer Help**

The day of assay is also the day of knowledge, and the two are described together: *“˹It will be said to the denier,˺ ‘You were totally heedless of this. Now We have lifted this veil of yours, so Today your sight is sharp!’”* (50:22). Confession follows immediately in another scene: when asked whether the Hereafter is not the truth, the answer is "Absolutely, by our Lord!" and the reply is to taste the punishment for that disbelief (6:30). The knowledge comes; it is certain; it is useless. That is the final argument of verse 13 against the posture of verse 12. The mocker's advantage was that the event had not arrived, and the verse removes the advantage by pointing out that its arrival will produce the very certainty he lacked, at the only moment when certainty cannot be acted on.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:14

> ˹They will be told,˺ “Taste your torment! This is what you sought to hasten.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Taste Is the Verb of Direct Experience**

The command is *dhūqū*, taste, and the Qur'an uses it consistently for recompense. It appears in the address to those who disbelieved after believing: *“So taste the punishment for your disbelief.”* (3:106), in the scene at the Sacred House (8:35), at the taking of souls (8:50), in the Fire itself (22:22), and in the exposure of the disbelievers on the Day (46:34). The word is chosen because tasting admits no intermediary and no report. A person can be told about bitterness; he cannot taste it through someone else. Recompense described as tasting is recompense experienced at first hand, without narration, discount, or representation. The shift from the mock question of verse 12 to this command is a shift from talk about an event to participation in it. The mocker asked for information; he is given sensation.

**Their Own Fitna, Returned to Them**

The word translated as torment here is the same root as the verb of the preceding verse, where the people are assayed over the fire (51:13). What they are told to taste is their own *fitna*. The connection is exact and it is the verse's central device: the trial they were subjected to is handed back to them as a portion. In the language of the assay, the metal is what remains after the fire, and here nothing remains but the fire. The Qur'an uses the same reflexive construction elsewhere: *“Taste the Fire’s torment, which you used to deny.”* (32:20). Denial and tasting are joined as cause and consequence, with no third term between them. The punishment is not an external penalty attached to a belief; it is the belief's own content, finally experienced.

**Hastening Is Answered Literally**

The second half of the sentence quotes their own posture back at them: this is what you sought to hasten. The demand for acceleration is documented repeatedly. They asked for stones from the sky if the message was true (8:32); they challenged the Prophet ﷺ to bring the torment, and were told that the timing is not his to command (6:57); and when a torment actually approached a people in the form of a cloud they welcomed it as rain, they were told it was the very thing they had tried to hurry (46:24). The reply to all of it is a statement about the scale of time: *“And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting.”* (22:47). Verse 14 is the moment the hastening is granted, and the grant is not a favour.

**The Sentence Uses Their Own Grammar**

Justice here is executed through irony, and the irony is structural rather than decorative. The mockers asked a question in verse 12; they are answered with an imperative. They wanted a date; they receive a command to taste. They tried to force the promise into their timeframe; the promise arrives on its own timeframe and describes itself as the thing they hurried. Nothing new is introduced into the sentence — no unfamiliar charge, no unexpected standard. Every element is drawn from what they already said. That is what makes the verse read as exact rather than as merely harsh. The reply is the questioner's own position, completed.

**Tasting Implies Duration as Well as Contact**

A single taste would end. The verse's context forecloses that reading, since the day it belongs to is described as a home rather than a visit: *“the Fire will be their home. Whenever they try to escape from it, they will be forced back into it”* (32:20). Escape and return are built into the description, and both are activities of duration. The command to taste therefore opens an experience that does not close, which is the sharpest edge of the verse. The mocker's advantage had been time — the event had not arrived, so his position looked safe. The reply takes time away entirely. What he hurried into has no exit and no end, and the haste he showed is now the reason he cannot say he was surprised.

**Two Postures Toward One Event**

The chapter sets this scene against another that has not yet been described in detail but is already implied: the reverent, who receive what their Lord gives them (51:15–16). The Book states the contrast explicitly: *“Those who disbelieve in it ˹ask to˺ hasten it ˹mockingly˺. But the believers are fearful of it, knowing that it is the truth.”* (42:18). Both groups face the same day. One tried to drag it forward by mockery; the other lived in awareness of it and prepared. The difference is not in the event but in the relation to it, and verse 14 shows what the first relation produces. Hastening is not confidence. It is a way of managing fear by pretending the thing is not real, and the verse records the moment the pretence is collected.

**The Block of Argument Closes Here**

Verses 7 through 14 form one unit: an oath by the heaven's design, a diagnosis of contradiction, an account of how minds get turned away, a curse on guessing, a description of immersion and heedlessness, the mock question, the day of assay, and the command to taste. The unit moves from a claim about order in the sky to a claim about disorder in people, and ends with the consequence of refusing to correct it. Nothing in the sequence requires the hearer to accept anything he cannot check in himself. The chapter now turns, without transition, to the opposite case — the reverent in gardens and springs. The turn is the argument's other half, and it exists to show that the day described here has more than one outcome.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:15

> Indeed, the righteous will be amid Gardens and springs

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Turns to the Other Outcome**

Without any connective phrase, the scene changes. Verses 7 to 14 described the mockers and ended with a command to taste; verse 15 begins with the reverent and ends with gardens. The absence of a transition is itself a device. Both outcomes are presented as facts of equal grammatical standing, introduced by the same particle of emphasis, and neither is argued for at this point. The hearer is not invited to compare reasons; he is shown two finished pictures. That structure makes the choice practical rather than theoretical. The chapter has already established that the promise is true and the judgment will fall (51:5–6), so what remains is not whether the day comes but which of these two descriptions will fit the person hearing it.

**Muttaqūn Are People Who Put Something in Between**

The word translated as righteous is *muttaqūn*, from the root *w-q-y*, whose basic sense is to shield or to guard. A *muttaqī* is a person who has placed a protection between himself and something harmful, and in the Qur'an the harm is divine displeasure and its consequences. The definition is functional rather than emotional: taqwā is not primarily a feeling of dread but a practice of keeping distance from what destroys. The Qur'an ties the practice directly to this reward: *“And as for those who were in awe of standing before their Lord and restrained themselves from ˹evil˺ desires, Paradise will certainly be ˹their˺ home.”* (79:40–41). Two acts are named — awe of the standing and restraint of desire — and both are descriptions of behaviour across a lifetime, not of a moment. The gardens of verse 15 are the settled outcome of that pattern.

**Amid Means Resident, Not Visitor**

The preposition is *fī*, inside. The righteous are not brought to gardens for a viewing; they are located in them. The Qur'an uses the same construction elsewhere and adds the terms of entry: *“Indeed, the righteous will be amid Gardens and springs. ˹It will be said to them,˺ ‘Enter in peace and security.’”* (15:45–46). Another chapter names the quality of the location first: *“Indeed, the righteous will be in a secure place, amid Gardens and springs.”* (44:51–52). Permanence and safety are the two features that separate this description from every pleasure available in the world, where both are temporary. The doubled reward for the one who fears standing before his Lord is stated in the same terms: two gardens, for a single standing (55:46).

**Gardens and Springs Name the Two Necessities First**

The pairing is not decorative. Food and water are what a desert dweller means by sufficiency, and the verse lists them before anything else — before silk, before company, before the specific delights detailed elsewhere. The fuller inventory comes later in the Book: *“The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that in it are rivers of fresh water, rivers of milk that never changes in taste, rivers of wine delicious to drink, and rivers of pure honey.”* (47:15). Verse 15 gives the category rather than the catalogue, and the choice of category is telling. It answers the mocker's materialism on its own ground: what he wanted from this world and could not keep is described as available there in a form that does not run out.

**The Springs Flow at Their Will**

Water in the world is a matter of location, season, and luck. In the description given elsewhere it becomes a matter of preference: *“˹from˺ a spring where Allah’s servants will drink, flowing at their will.”* (76:6). The same chapter names the drink itself, flavoured with camphor (76:5). The detail matters because it reverses the ordinary relation between a person and his water. Here the supply is directed by the one drinking. That reversal is the general character of the gardens: the constraints of scarcity, distance, and labour that define provision in this world are simply absent, and the verse signals their absence by naming springs rather than wells or cisterns. A spring is water that comes on its own, continuously.

**Security Is Mentioned Before Enjoyment**

Wherever the Qur'an describes the destination of the reverent, safety appears alongside or ahead of pleasure. Entry is in peace and security (15:46); the place itself is secure (44:51); the deliverance is from the horror of the day, and radiance and joy are what replace it: *“So Allah will deliver them from the horror of that Day, and grant them radiance and joy.”* (76:11). The order explains something about the audience. A person living under threat does not enjoy provision, however abundant; enjoyment presupposes that the abundance will not be taken. The verse therefore gives its hearers the two things they lack in this world, and gives them in the order that makes the second possible.

**The Reward Is Stated Before Its Cause**

Verse 15 announces the outcome and says nothing about how it was earned. The reason arrives afterwards, in the description of what these people did before: they received what their Lord gave them because they had been good-doers (51:16), and the following verses itemise the doing. The arrangement is deliberate. The gift is presented first so that it is received as a gift, and the qualification is added so that it is not received as an entitlement available to anyone regardless of conduct. Both halves are needed, and their order matters. A reader who takes only the first verse hears a promise without conditions; a reader who takes only the later verses hears a list of duties without a destination. The chapter holds them together in the sequence it chose. Stating the reward before its cause also shapes how the verse that follows is read. The picture arrives first — gardens, springs, reception — and the account of the good-doers comes afterwards as its explanation, so the reader meets an outcome before meeting the conduct behind it. That ordering makes the conduct look like the reason for something already given rather than the price of something still owed.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:16

> ˹joyfully˺ receiving what their Lord will grant them. Before this ˹reward˺ they were truly good-doers ˹in the world˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Receiving Is Described as an Activity**

The participle *ākhidhīn* means taking, holding, receiving — an agent engaged in an act, not a passive recipient. The construction places the people of the gardens in the middle of receiving, and the tense implies continuation. What is received is *mā ātāhum rabbuhum*, what their Lord has given them, an indefinite phrase that leaves the content open. The effect is a scene rather than an inventory: people actively taking delivery of something from someone they know. The verse refuses to turn the reward into a list of objects, and in doing so keeps the relation at the centre. A list would describe a place; a receiving describes a continuing transaction between a giver and those who take from him.

**The Giver Is Named as Their Lord**

The title used is *rabb*, the one who nurtures, sustains, and owns — not merely a distributor of prizes. The word choice connects this reward to the whole history of the people receiving it. The same Lord who apportioned their provision in the world, who sent the winds and loaded the clouds sworn by at the start of this chapter (51:1–4), is the one now handing them what they take. Nothing has changed except the medium. This is why the reward can be described without specification: it is continuous with a relationship that already existed. The gardens are not a new arrangement with a stranger; they are the same lordship operating without the obstacles of scarcity and death that limited it before.

**Joy Is Part of What Is Given**

The translator's bracketed "joyfully" is supported by the wider description of the same scene. Deliverance from the horror of the day is paired with radiance and joy: *“So Allah will deliver them from the horror of that Day, and grant them radiance and joy.”* (76:11). Elsewhere the address to the people of the gardens is explicitly joyful: *“˹They will be told,˺ ‘Eat and drink joyfully for what you did in the days gone by.’”* (69:24). Note the reason given there — for what you did — which is the same reason verse 16 supplies. Joy in these descriptions is never idle pleasure; it is the affect of a person whose work has been accepted. The bracket is therefore an interpretation, but a sound one, and it is required to make sense of the receiving as anything other than consumption.

**Nothing in Imagination Covers the Reward**

The Qur'an marks the limits of its own description: *“No soul can imagine what delights are kept in store for them as a reward for what they used to do.”* (32:17). The verse is about the same people doing the same things, and it explicitly states that the stored reward exceeds imagination. That has a consequence for how verse 16 should be read. The gardens and springs of the previous verse are not the whole of the reward but the part that can be said in words addressed to people who know what a garden is. Language here functions as a pointer, and the pointer's honesty consists in admitting that the thing pointed at is larger. Any attempt to complete the picture from the descriptions would be building a ceiling where the text has left one open.

**"Before This" Closes Their Account**

The clause *min qablu* — before this — locates the qualifying conduct in the finished past. Their good-doing is not ongoing at the moment of receiving; it is complete, recorded, and behind them. The tense matters morally. A reward corresponding to an unfinished life would be an advance, and an advance can be revoked. What is described here is settlement after the work has stopped, which is the only form of payment that cannot be disputed. The verse therefore contains the whole structure of accountability in three words: something was done before, something is given now, and the giving refers back to the doing. Nothing intervenes to alter the relation between them.

**Muḥsinūn Are People Who Do a Thing Well**

The word translated as good-doers is *muḥsinūn*, the active participle of *aḥsana*, to do well, to make something good or beautiful. The term covers both the moral quality of an act and its craftsmanship: the *muḥsin* performs the act correctly and performs it well. This is the Qur'an's own vocabulary for the standard it sets, and the correspondence it promises is stated as a rhetorical question: *“Is there any reward for goodness except goodness?”* (55:60). The reply is contained in the question. Another verse makes the same link with the condition attached: *“But no! Whoever submits themselves to Allah and does good will have their reward with their Lord.”* (2:112). Submission plus doing well is the qualification; the reward with the Lord is the outcome. Verse 16 states that outcome as already underway.

**The General Claim Is Then Itemised**

Verse 16 ends on a colon in the translation, and the Arabic construction does the same work: the statement that they were good-doers is followed by the specification of what the good-doing consisted in. Three practices are named in the verses that follow — sleep taken little in the night, forgiveness sought before dawn, and a recognised share of wealth assigned to the beggar and the poor (51:17–19). The method is characteristic of this Book. A general category is asserted, and then it is cashed out in concrete, checkable acts, so that nobody can satisfy the description by approving of it in the abstract. The itemisation also fixes the scale: the good-doing that earns gardens is described in hours of the night and portions of property, which are units any person possesses. Itemising a claim converts praise into a list, and a list can be checked against a life. The three items that follow carry times and quantities with them — sleep given up in the night, forgiveness sought before dawn, wealth shared with the one who asks — which is why the verse reads as instruction rather than as compliment.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:17

> they used to sleep only little in the night

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Short Phrase With Two Readings**

The Arabic can be parsed in two ways, and the commentators preserve both. The first takes "little of the night" as the object of sleeping: they slept a small part of the night and spent the rest standing. The second takes the little as a quantity of people: they were few, those who slept — meaning either that few of them slept at all or that such people are rare in number. Al-Ṭabarī records both, and the first is the plainer reading of the words. The second has an advantage of its own: it makes the verse a statement about scarcity, and scarcity is a theme the Qur'an returns to when describing the truly devout. On either reading the practical content is the same. The night of these people was mostly given to something other than sleep, and the verse is praising that arrangement.

**Which Hours Were Kept**

Many of the commentators specify the interval between the sunset and night prayers, the time when the day's work is finished and the body is tired but the obligations are not yet complete. Others take it as the last part of the night, which the following verse supports by naming the hour before dawn (51:18). The two need not be alternatives. What matters is that the hours chosen are the ones a person would naturally spend asleep, and that the choice was repeated. The practice being described is not an occasional vigil but a schedule. This is why the verb is in a form that indicates a continuing habit rather than a single act: they used to sleep little. The description is of a life arranged around the night, not of a night that happened once.

**The Night Prayer Holds a Named Rank**

The tradition gives this practice a specific standing. Abū Hurayrah reports the Prophet ﷺ as saying that the most virtuous prayer after the obligatory ones is the night prayer (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1163). The ranking is comparative and unambiguous: after what is required, nothing outranks what is done at night. That judgement explains why the Qur'an selects this practice first when listing the qualities of the people of the gardens. Of all the acts a person could be praised for, the one named is the one performed when nobody requires it, nobody watches it, and the body prefers otherwise. The rank assigned to it in the tradition corresponds to its difficulty, and the difficulty is precisely what makes it evidence of sincerity.

**The Prophet's Own Measure Is Recorded**

The Qur'an describes the practice in the Prophet's own household and gives the quantities: *“Surely your Lord knows that you ˹O Prophet˺ stand ˹in prayer˺ for nearly two-thirds of the night, or ˹sometimes˺ half of it, or a third, as do some of those with you.”* (73:20). Two-thirds, half, a third — the same range this verse describes as "little sleep." The command that opened that earlier chapter is equally specific: *“Stand all night ˹in prayer˺ except a little—”* (73:2), and the later instruction ties the practice to a promised station: *“And rise at ˹the last˺ part of the night, offering additional prayers, so your Lord may raise you to a station of praise.”* (17:79). The people described in verse 17 are following a pattern already set and quantified, not inventing an asceticism of their own.

**Leaving the Bed Is the Physical Description**

Another verse gives the same practice in a single image: *“They abandon their beds, invoking their Lord with hope and fear, and donate from what We have provided for them.”* (32:16). Three elements appear together — the abandoned bed, the invocation mixing hope and fear, and the donation from provision — and all three recur in this passage of adh-Dhāriyāt. The bed is the concrete object; abandoning it is the concrete act. Nothing about the description is interior or vague. A reader can check whether it happened last night. The physicality is what makes the praise meaningful, since an inward state that never costs anything cannot be distinguished from its absence.

**Sleep Is Not the Enemy**

The Qur'an does not treat sleep as a defect. It is listed among the provisions of the created order: *“and made your sleep for rest, and made the night as a cover, and made the day for livelihood”* (78:9–11), and *“He is the One Who has made the night for you as a cover, and ˹made˺ sleep for resting, and the day for rising.”* (25:47). Rest is designed and good. What verse 17 describes is a reduction, not an abolition: little sleep, not none. The measure matters, because a practice that destroys the body cannot be sustained and cannot be commanded of ordinary people. The praise attaches to proportion — a person who takes the rest he needs and gives the remainder to standing, night after night.

**Night Removes the Audience**

The hour chosen is the hour in which ostentation is impossible. Nobody sees a man praying at three in the morning, and nobody can be impressed by it. The Qur'an warns about the opposite case in the sharpest terms: *“So woe to those ˹hypocrites˺ who pray yet are unmindful of their prayers, those who ˹only˺ show off”* (107:4–6). Prayer performed to be seen is named as a defect, and the night removes the possibility. Another verse makes night worship a standing instruction: *“and prostrate before Him during part of the night, and glorify Him long at night.”* (76:26). The length is specified, which again points to habit rather than feeling. Verse 17 praises little sleep for the same reason: what is done in the dark is done for the one who sees in the dark.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:18

> and pray for forgiveness before dawn

**Expanded Commentary**

**Asḥār Names a Specific Hour**

The word is the plural of *saḥar*, the last part of the night before the morning twilight — not the middle of the night and not after sunrise, but the narrow interval where darkness is at its thinnest. The Qur'an uses the same term when listing the qualities of the patient and devout: *“˹It is they˺ who are patient, sincere, obedient, and charitable, and who pray for forgiveness before dawn.”* (3:17). The two verses agree on both elements: the hour and the act. What is being described is not a general penitential mood but a scheduled practice performed at a fixed time by people who have been awake. The specificity is the point. An hour that has to be waited for, after a night of standing, is an hour that cannot be faked or performed incidentally.

**The Hour Is Marked in the Tradition**

This time carries a distinction stated in the most widely transmitted of reports. Abū Hurayrah reports the Prophet ﷺ as saying: "Our Lord Almighty descends to the lowest heaven in the last third of every night, saying: Who is calling upon Me that I may answer him? Who is asking from Me that I may give him? Who is seeking My forgiveness that I may forgive him?" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1145; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 758). The three questions in that report cover supplication, request, and the seeking of forgiveness, and the last is exactly the act named in verse 18. The hour is therefore not chosen by preference; it is the hour in which the request for forgiveness is met with an offer. That correspondence is what gives this verse its weight as a description of the reverent.

**Forgiveness Is Sought After Worship, Not Instead of It**

The sequence of verses 17 and 18 is instructive. First the night is spent standing; then, at its end, forgiveness is requested. The order tells us what the request is for. It is not a substitute for the prayer and not a postponement of it. It is the seal placed on a completed act, and its content is the defect in that act: the wandering attention, the tiredness, the vanity that creeps into even solitary worship, the falling short of what the hour deserved. A person who ends his night by asking pardon is making a statement about the night's quality, and the statement is that it was not good enough. The Qur'an's own instruction to the Prophet ﷺ follows the same pattern: recite, pray, give, *“And seek Allah’s forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”* (73:20).

**The Plural Form Describes a Habit**

*Asḥār* is a plural, and the plural is doing work. One dawn would be an occasion; many dawns are a practice. The verse describes people for whom this hour was familiar, which is consistent with the habitual form of the preceding verb — they used to sleep little. Both verses are in the register of custom rather than event. The Qur'an praises patterns, and a pattern is what survives the loss of mood. Anyone can stay awake once, in grief or in enthusiasm. To be at the same place at the same hour for years requires an arrangement of the whole life around that hour, and it is the arrangement the verse is praising rather than the feeling that accompanied it on any given night.

**Dawn Is a Boundary the Book Takes Seriously**

The Qur'an repeatedly treats first light as a legal and practical limit. Fasting begins when the light of dawn is distinguishable from the darkness: *“˹You may˺ eat and drink until you see the light of dawn breaking the darkness of night, then complete the fast until nightfall.”* (2:187). The night prayer belongs to the time before that line, and the request for forgiveness is placed at its edge. There is a fitness in this. The last act of the night, performed at the moment the night legally ends, closes a period rather than interrupting one. What follows belongs to the day and its livelihood (78:11). The hour is therefore both the end of the worshipper's night and the beginning of everybody else's work, which is why it is quiet in a way no other hour is.

**Ending With Forgiveness Keeps the Doer Out of the Picture**

A night of worship generates a claim, and every claim is a risk. The person who has stood for hours can begin to regard the hours as a credit, and the Qur'an treats that turn as one of the failures of worship — prayer performed for an audience, even an audience of one's own estimation (107:6). Asking forgiveness at the end removes the credit. It says that the act performed was inadequate, that its acceptance is a matter of mercy, and that the worshipper is not in a position to present a bill. This is why the description in verse 18 belongs where it does, immediately after the standing and immediately before the giving of wealth. Each of the three acts is paired with its own safeguard, and the safeguard here is a request for pardon at the moment of completion.

**Two Verses Describing One Night**

Read together, verses 17 and 18 give the shape of a single night: sleep taken sparingly, standing through the hours, and forgiveness sought as the light arrives. The description is complete without any reference to what was recited, how many units were prayed, or what was asked for besides pardon. That economy is deliberate, and it sets up the third quality named in the following verse, which moves from the night to property. Worship of the body and worship of wealth are then presented as one continuous account of the same people. The chapter will not describe their belief in abstract terms; it describes their hours and their money, because those are the two things a person actually has.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:19

> And in their wealth there was a rightful share ˹fulfilled˺ for the beggar and the poor

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Right, Not a Courtesy**

The word used is *ḥaqq*, a right or a due — the same word that carries the sense of an obligation owed and the sense of truth established. Applied to property, it means the recipient is not being favoured; he is being given what already belongs to him. The Qur'an states the same idea in another chapter's list of the faithful: *“and who give the rightful share of their wealth to the beggar and the poor”* (70:24–25). The parallel is close enough to be treated as the same institution described twice. Language matters here, because charity spoken of as a favour makes the giver generous, while charity spoken of as a right makes him merely honest. The verse chooses the second framing, and the choice removes any credit from the transaction. What the *muḥsinūn* did with their money was to stop withholding a share that was not theirs.

**Inside the Wealth, Not Beside It**

The preposition is *fī*, in: the share existed within their property. This is a stronger statement than saying they gave from their property. Something inside a thing belongs to its structure, and the description implies that a portion of every accumulation was already assigned to someone else from the moment the accumulation began. The owner holds it in trust rather than in full possession. The Qur'an's wider legislation works the same way, treating the alms-tax as a claim on wealth rather than a gift from it, and listing the categories of claimant in detail (2:177). The verse in front of us describes the inner disposition that matches that legislation: people who regarded part of what they had as somebody else's, and who organised their finances accordingly rather than deciding after the fact.

**The Beggar and the One Who Does Not Beg**

Two categories are named, *sāʾil* and *maḥrūm* — the one who asks and the one who is deprived. The commentators explain the second as the person in need who does not beg, either from shame or because his condition is not visible. The distinction is confirmed in the tradition with unusual precision. Abū Hurayrah reports the Prophet ﷺ as saying: "The poor person is not the one who goes around among people, turned away by a morsel or two, or a date or two. Rather, the poor person is the one who does not find sufficient means to make him independent, is not noticed so that charity may be given to him, and does not stand up to ask people." (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1479). The report redefines poverty away from visibility, and verse 19 does the same work by naming both the asker and the silent one in a single phrase.

**A Share That Is Known, and What That Fixes**

The adjective attached to the right is *maʿlūm*, known, determined, measured. The commentators differ on whether this means the obligatory alms or includes voluntary charity as well. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī read it as the required *zakāh*, and the argument for that reading is the adjective: a fixed, known amount is what obligation looks like, while supererogatory giving has no measure. The wider reading, that both are included, is supported by the list in al-Baqarah, which names alms-tax and also charity to relatives, orphans, travellers, and beggars (2:177). The stronger sense keeps the fixity, because fixity is what makes the verse a description of character rather than of mood. A man with a known due in his wealth has decided the question in advance; a man without one decides it each time he is asked, and usually decides it badly.

**The Deprived May Not Be Able to Speak At All**

One reading pushes the category further. Al-Rāzī notes that *al-maḥrūm* can include living creatures who cannot voice their need — animals whose hunger nobody announces. On that reading the obligation to notice is not limited to human beings and not limited to those who can be heard. The verse acquires a practical consequence: the duty falls on the owner of the wealth to look, since the claimant cannot present his claim. This is consistent with the prophetic redefinition of poverty quoted above, where the decisive feature is that nobody notices. A system in which the needy must advertise their need fails precisely the people the verse names second. The inclusion of creatures who cannot ask is the logical extension of the same principle.

**A Fixed Due Protects the Recipient From the Giver**

Because the share is known and sits inside the property, the recipient does not depend on the giver's feelings, his day, or his estimate of who deserves help. This is a structural protection, and the Qur'an's treatment of wealth repeatedly aims at it. A person who gives by impulse can stop giving by impulse; a person discharging a due cannot decide that nobody qualifies. The same logic explains why the verse is placed among the qualities of the *muttaqūn* rather than in a legal passage. It describes a habit of mind in which property is already divided before it is spent. The next verse that mentions the reward of such people, in another chapter, ties giving to self-purification rather than to reputation (92:17–18), which is the same inward arrangement named here.

**Three Acts Cover the Whole of a Person's Resources**

Verses 17 to 19 describe three practices, and each draws on a different resource. The night takes the body and its sleep; the hour before dawn takes the tongue and its request; the share of wealth takes property. Nothing is left outside the account, which is why the summary in the previous verse could be so brief — they were good-doers (51:16). The itemisation is not a rule for one type of piety but a map of the resources a human being actually has. Time, speech, and money are the three things a person can spend, and the chapter describes the reverent as having spent all three in the same direction. The argument now turns outward again, from what these people did to what the world shows to whoever looks at it.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:20

> There are ˹countless˺ signs on earth for those with sure faith

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Returns to Evidence**

After the description of the reverent and their three practices, the chapter turns back to proof. The movement is purposeful. Verses 15 to 19 showed what belief produces in a life; verse 20 returns to what produces belief. Nothing about the reward described earlier is available to inspection, so the chapter grounds it in material that is. The earth is offered as the field of evidence, and the offer is not conditional on travel, study, or special access. Whoever is standing on the ground is already inside the evidence. That is the structural function of this verse in the argument: it moves the case from testimony about the unseen to observation of the seen, and it does so at the moment when the reader has just been told what the unseen testimony costs a person to accept.

**The Signs Are Where People Walk**

The preposition is *fī*, in or upon — the signs are located in the earth itself. The Qur'an describes the same field repeatedly and in concrete terms: *“And He is the One Who spread out the earth and placed firm mountains and rivers upon it, and created fruits of every kind in pairs. He covers the day with night. Surely in this are signs for those who reflect.”* (13:3). Rain and growth are listed in the same register: *“He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky, from which you drink and by which plants grow for your cattle to graze.”* (16:10). What unifies these descriptions is proximity. The evidence for the resurrection is not stored in a distant heaven; it is underfoot, in the soil, the water, the plants, and the animals that depend on them. No expedition is required to reach it, which removes the usual excuse for not looking.

**Āyāt Means Both Signs and Verses**

The plural *āyāt* is the Qur'an's word for a verse of revelation and for a sign in creation, and the double use is not an accident of vocabulary. Both are things that point past themselves to a speaker. A verse carries meaning; a mountain, a river, and a sprouting seed carry meaning of the same kind, though in a different medium. When the chapter says there are signs on the earth, it is saying the earth is legible. That is why the audience is defined by certainty rather than by education. A text requires a reader, and the reader required here is one who treats what he sees as something said rather than as something that merely happened. The parallel between the two uses of the word is what allows the Qur'an to argue from nature at all.

**The Qualifier Explains Why Some See and Others Do Not**

The signs are specified *li'l-mūqinīn*, for those possessing certainty. The addition answers an obvious objection: if the earth is full of signs, why does the earth's population disagree about them? The verse's answer is that the sign is addressed to a capacity, and the capacity is certainty. The same material produces conviction in one person and nothing in another, not because the material differs but because the receiving condition differs. The Qur'an states this mechanism plainly elsewhere: *“Yet neither signs nor warners are of any benefit to those who refuse to believe.”* (10:101). Signs do not compel. They address a person already disposed to accept what evidence shows, and the disposition is called certainty rather than intelligence.

**What the Earth's Signs Actually Include**

The commentaries enumerate them, and the enumeration is worth keeping concrete: the mountains that hold the ground, the waters that run through it, the plants and animals it supports, the alternation of dead and living seasons, and the variation within a single species. The Qur'an isolates one case with unusual precision: *“And on the earth there are ˹different˺ neighbouring tracts, gardens of grapevines, ˹various˺ crops, palm trees—some stemming from the same root, others standing alone. They are all irrigated with the same water, yet We make some taste better than others. Surely in this are signs for those who understand.”* (13:4). Same soil, same water, adjacent plots, different fruit. That single observation is an argument about design, and it can be checked by anyone who has eaten from two trees.

**Yaqīn Is Knowledge That Has Removed Doubt**

The certainty named here has a defined sense in Arabic. *Yaqīn* is knowledge so settled that doubt no longer moves it, and the Qur'an distinguishes degrees of it. One short chapter marks the difference between knowing a thing and seeing it: *“Indeed, if you were to know ˹your fate˺ with certainty, ˹you would have acted differently˺. ˹But˺ you will surely see the Hellfire. Again, you will surely see it with the eye of certainty.”* (102:5–7). Knowledge of certainty and the eye of certainty are two stages, and the second arrives only when the thing is present. Verse 20 speaks of people at the first stage who live as though at the second. That is the qualification being described: not proof held in the hand, but conviction held in the conduct.

**A Dead Earth Revived Is the Argument for Return**

The signs on the earth point specifically at the promise sworn to at the start of the chapter. The method is stated openly: *“And it is Allah Who sends the winds, which then stir up ˹vapour, forming˺ clouds, and then We drive them to a lifeless land, giving life to the earth after its death. Similar is the Resurrection.”* (35:9), and *“˹as˺ a provision for ˹Our˺ servants. And with this ˹rain˺ We revive a lifeless land. Similar is the emergence ˹from the graves˺.”* (50:11). The comparison is drawn by the text itself, so the reader is not asked to invent it. The ground he stands on has been dead and revived every year of his life. Verse 20 places that cycle in the category of signs for the certain, and the next verse moves the same argument from the ground to the person standing on it.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:21

> as there are within yourselves. Can you not see

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Field of Evidence Moves Inside**

Verse 20 located signs in the earth; this verse adds a second location and it is closer. The conjunction is short and the addition is enormous: the same category of evidence that covers the ground also covers the person standing on it. The Qur'an names both fields together elsewhere and states the purpose of showing them: *“We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth.”* (41:53). The movement from outside to inside is not a change of subject but a tightening of the argument. Signs in the earth can be dismissed as unfamiliar territory; signs in one's own body cannot be dismissed at all, since the person dismissing them is made of the material in question.

**Qatādah's Reading of the Body's Purpose**

The ḥadīth scholar and commentator Qatādah is reported to have drawn a specific conclusion from this verse: whoever reflects on his own creation recognises that he was created for worship, and that his joints were made flexible for worship. The observation is anatomical and moral at once. A hand that can grip, kneel, and raise; a knee that bends; a spine that inclines — each of these permits the postures of prayer, and none of them is required by mere survival. Qatādah's point is not that the body is a machine for ritual. It is that the design of the body already contains an orientation, and reflection on that design produces knowledge of what the body was for. The verse's command to see is therefore a command to inspect one's own equipment for its evident use.

**Difference Among People Is One of the Signs**

Another reading takes "within yourselves" as the variation inside the human species. The Qur'an lists it as a sign in its own right: *“And one of His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the diversity of your languages and colours. Surely in this are signs for those of ˹sound˺ knowledge.”* (30:22). Language and colour are named because both are inherited without choice, both vary endlessly, and neither has any bearing on a person's worth. A single chapter elsewhere pairs the same observation with the creation of spouses and the compassion placed between them (30:21). The argument from diversity is that a species this internally varied, yet unified in structure, is not the product of accident. Accident does not produce variety within a pattern; it produces noise.

**The Lowliness of the Body Is Also a Sign**

A third reading, recorded by al-Ghazzālī among others, takes "in your selves" as a reference to the body's undignified processes — the fact that food and drink pass through it and leave it as waste. The purpose of the observation is not disgust but the demolition of pride. A man who remembers what his body is cannot sustain the self-importance that blocks reflection. The Qur'an makes the same move by describing human origins in material terms: *“Let people then consider what they were created from! ˹They were˺ created from a spurting fluid, stemming from between the backbone and the ribcage. Surely He is fully capable of bringing them back ˹to life˺”* (86:5–8). The last clause states the argument outright: the one who assembled this from such a beginning has no difficulty reassembling it.

**"Can You Not See?" Is a Question About Capacity**

The closing challenge, *afalā tubṣirūn*, is not asking whether the eyes function. It asks whether the seeing has reached the level where it registers as evidence. The two are distinct operations, and the Qur'an distinguishes them constantly: the faculties are given, and then their use is demanded. *“And Allah brought you out of the wombs of your mothers while you knew nothing, and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect so perhaps you would be thankful.”* (16:78). Three faculties are listed, gratitude is named as their purpose, and the verse in front of us asks whether the equipment is being used for it. The rhetorical form makes refusal conspicuous. A question that can only be answered "yes I can see" is a question that condemns the person who does not then act on what he sees.

**Two Fields, One Witness**

The pairing of earth and self has a structural logic. The external world is vast, impersonal, and available to everyone; the self is small, immediate, and available only to its owner. Evidence placed in both cannot be avoided by distance or by privacy. The classical tradition summarises the relation by calling the human being a small copy of the larger order — the same kinds of creation present in both, at different scales. That idea is supported by the verse's own grammar: the signs are of one kind in both locations, which is why one verb of seeing covers them. A person who has learned to read the earth's signs already possesses the skill needed for his own, and a person who refuses his own has no excuse for refusing the earth's.

**Reflection on the Self Has Its Own Command**

The Qur'an issues this command directly elsewhere, and the phrasing is close to a rebuke: *“Have they not reflected upon their own being? Allah only created the heavens and the earth and everything in between for a purpose and an appointed term. Yet most people are truly in denial of the meeting with their Lord!”* (30:8). Reflection on the self and the appointed term appear in the same sentence, which connects this verse to the chapter's opening oaths. The person who examines himself finds two things: a body designed for a purpose, and a life with a set end. Both are exactly what the promise sworn to in verses 5 and 6 requires. The chapter will now move from these signs to the provision they depend on, and then to an oath that closes the first argument.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:22

> In heaven is your sustenance and whatever you are promised

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sustenance Is Placed Above, Not Below**

The verse relocates provision. What people work for on the ground is described as stored in the sky, and the commentators give the meaning in two registers. In the concrete sense, the heaven holds the rain, the light, and the wind — the three inputs without which no field, no herd, and no journey exists. In the wider sense, it holds what God has destined for each person, apportioned and brought down. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Rāzī both preserve the second reading, and al-Qushayrī notes that provision arrives through the angels, which connects this verse directly to the fourth oath of the chapter (51:4). The statement is therefore not poetic. It is a claim about where the machinery of supply actually sits, and the claim reverses the ordinary assumption that a man's income originates in his own effort.

**A Report That Fixes the Quantity**

The practical consequence of a fixed supply is stated in the tradition with unusual directness. Jābir ibn ʿAbdillāh reports the Prophet ﷺ as saying: "O people, fear Allah and be moderate in seeking a living, for no soul will die until it has received all its provision, even if it is slow in coming. So fear Allah and be moderate in seeking provision; take that which is permissible and leave that which is forbidden." (Sunan Ibn Mājah 2144, graded ṣaḥīḥ by Dārussalām). The report contains both halves of the doctrine: the provision is complete and cannot be missed, and because it cannot be missed, the pursuit of it must not become immoderate or unlawful. That is exactly the instruction implied by verse 22. A supply already located in heaven does not need to be seized by any means available on earth.

**The Guarantee Is Stated for Every Living Thing**

The Qur'an generalises the point beyond human beings: *“There is no moving creature on earth whose provision is not guaranteed by Allah. And He knows where it lives and where it is laid to rest. All is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record.”* (11:6). Three features are named — the guarantee, the knowledge of each creature's dwelling, and the written record — and all three reappear in verse 22's claim that provision is in heaven. Another passage describes the mechanism of surplus and restriction: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Surely ˹it is˺ my Lord ˹Who˺ gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills of His servants. And whatever you spend in charity, He will compensate ˹you˺ for it. For He is the Best Provider.’”* (34:39). The compensation clause matters here, since it answers the fear that giving reduces what is left.

**The Promise Is Stored in the Same Place**

The second half of the verse puts "whatever you are promised" alongside provision, in the same location. The commentators read the phrase as everything God has apportioned for each person, which includes the resurrection and the judgment sworn to at the chapter's start (51:5–6). Placing the two together does important work. A man anxious about next month's food and a man doubting the Day of Judgment are, on this verse, making the same category error: assuming that what he cannot see does not exist or has not been arranged. Provision arrives from a place he cannot enter; the promised day will arrive from a place he cannot inspect. Both are fixed, both are recorded, and both are outside his control. The verse treats the second doubt as no more reasonable than the first.

**Why This Verse Follows the Description of Giving**

Verses 17 to 19 described people who gave away a recognised share of their wealth. Verse 22 answers the unspoken objection to that practice. Giving looks like loss when supply is thought to originate in the purse; it does not look like loss when supply originates in heaven. The Qur'an makes the same point in the language of a loan: *“And donate from what We have provided for you before death comes to one of you, and you cry, ‘My Lord! If only You delayed me for a short while, I would give in charity and be one of the righteous.’”* (63:10). The provision is described as given by God before it is described as spent by the owner, which is the same ordering as verse 22. The man who gives from a supply located in heaven is not reducing his store; he is drawing on it in the direction it was meant to flow.

**The Heaven Sworn By Earlier Is the Same Heaven**

Verse 7 took an oath by the sky in its design, and verse 22 returns to it as a storehouse. The two descriptions belong together. A structure this finely ordered is not ordered for appearance alone; it carries something. The Qur'an states the functional side of the sky plainly: *“And We have made the sky a well-protected canopy, still they turn away from its signs.”* (21:32). A canopy protects, and what it protects is the supply beneath it — the water cycle, the light, the climate, the seasons on which every harvest depends. The verse therefore does not introduce a new subject. It completes the argument begun with the oath: the same heaven whose *ḥubuk* was sworn by is the heaven holding what people eat and what they have been promised.

**One Sentence Removes Two Anxieties**

Read as pastoral instruction, this verse is among the most efficient in the chapter. It addresses fear about survival and doubt about the future in a single clause, by placing both objects in the same custody. The person who accepts the placement changes his behaviour in two ways at once: he stops grasping at provision by unlawful means, and he stops treating the promised day as a story. Neither change requires new information about the world. Both require a change in the location assigned to what matters. That is why the verse is followed immediately by an oath, since a claim this consequential, made about things nobody can inspect, is exactly the kind of claim the chapter has been preparing to swear to.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:23

> Then by the Lord of heaven and earth! ˹All˺ this is certainly as true as ˹the fact that˺ you can speak

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath by God Replaces the Oaths by Creatures**

The first four oaths of this chapter were taken by created things: winds, clouds, ships, angels. Verse 7 was taken by the heaven. This oath is taken by the Lord of both heaven and earth, and the escalation is the point. Once creatures have been sworn by, the argument reaches the limit of what creatures can support, and the guarantee is transferred to the one who owns them. The particle *fa* at the start ties the oath to what precedes it: given the signs on earth, given the signs in yourselves, given provision located in heaven — therefore, by the Lord of all three. Nothing in the construction asks the hearer to accept a new premise. It asks him to accept the conclusion that the premises he has already been shown require.

**Lord of Heaven and Earth Names Everything Just Cited**

The two titles are not generic. The heaven has just been named as the location of provision and promise (51:22); the earth as the field of signs (51:20); and the self, which belongs to the earth's material, as the second field (51:21). Swearing by the Lord of heaven and earth is swearing by the owner of the entire evidential base the chapter has assembled. The same pairing appears in the oath series that opens another chapter (37:5), and in the description of God as the one who gives authority over whom He wills (59:6). A title that covers both domains is the only title adequate to guarantee a claim about both, since the promise concerns bodies made of earth and an outcome administered from heaven.

**The Comparison Is With the Act of Speaking**

The standard the verse chooses is astonishingly modest: as true as that you speak. Al-Bayḍāwī's gloss states the logic plainly — just as you have no doubt that you speak, you should have no doubt of this reality. Nothing could be more immediate. A person does not infer that he is speaking; he is aware of it while doing it. The verse takes the most indubitable item in a human being's experience and sets the promise beside it. The effect is not to make the promise easier to prove but to make denial harder to explain. Doubt about the resurrection is not the mild agnosticism it presents itself as. On this comparison it is a man doubting something while standing closer to it than he stands to his own voice.

**Why Speech and Not Sight**

Verse 21 ended with a challenge to see, and this verse shifts to speaking. The shift is deliberate. Sight can fail — distance, darkness, a defective eye, an object hidden. Speech cannot be doubted by the one performing it, because there is no gap between the act and the awareness of the act. The verse selects the faculty with the shortest distance between experience and certainty, which is why the comparison works as a standard rather than as a metaphor. It also quietly answers the mockers of verse 12, who had been talking. Their question "when is this day" was itself an act of speech, performed with total confidence in its own occurrence. The verse takes that confidence and turns it into the measure of what they refuse.

**Three Candidates for What "It" Refers To**

The pronoun in "this is certainly true" has no stated antecedent, and the commentators supply three. First, the promise of verse 22 — provision and the apportioned outcome. Second, the Qur'an as a whole. Third, the judgment, as in verse 6. The three are not really competitors, since the promise of verse 22 includes the judgment and the Qur'an is what announces it. The absence of an antecedent is a device: whatever the hearer has been resisting during the preceding verses is the thing now sworn to. A man doubting the resurrection hears the resurrection; a man doubting that his food is provided hears provision; a man doubting the book hears the book. The verse closes over all three at once, which is why it can be this short.

**Emphasis Stacked Three Times**

The sentence carries an oath, the particle *inna*, and the emphatic *lām*: "by the Lord... it is indeed true." Arabic reserves this accumulation for statements the audience denies. Where a fact is accepted, a plain verb is enough; where it is contested, the language loads. The same stacking appears in verse 6 concerning the falling of the judgment, and in the opening oath series. The chapter's density of emphasis is a measure of the resistance it was addressing. Note also what is absent: no condition, no qualification, no concession to the possibility that the matter might turn out otherwise. Three layers of confirmation around an unqualified statement is the strongest form of assertion the language offers, and it is spent on a claim about the future.

**The First Argument Block Closes Here**

Verses 1 to 23 form a complete unit: oaths by the created order, the sworn statement that the promise is true and the judgment will fall, a diagnosis of the mockers, the description of the reverent and their three practices, signs on the earth and in the self, provision in heaven, and now an oath by the Lord of both. The unit moves from nature to conduct to nature again to God Himself. What follows changes register entirely — the chapter turns to narrative, beginning with the honoured guests of Abraham (51:24). The turn is not a departure from argument but a change of evidence: after signs that can be observed, the chapter produces history that can be checked, in the same land, along the same trade routes. The oath of verse 23 is the hinge between the two.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:24

> Has the story of Abraham’s honoured guests reached you ˹O Prophet˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Turns From Signs to Stories**

After twenty-three verses of oath, diagnosis, and evidence, the register changes without warning. The chapter now narrates. The change is not a relaxation of the argument but a second line of it. Signs in nature can be debated by anyone willing to debate them; a story about a known people, in a known region, with a ruin still standing on a known road, is harder to argue with. The Qur'an uses this method throughout its Meccan chapters, placing narrative immediately after theological assertion so that the assertion arrives with witnesses. Verse 24 is the doorway into the longest narrative block of the chapter, which runs through the destruction of Lot's people and then moves to Moses, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Noah.

**"Has It Reached You?" Is a Recognised Opening**

The formula *hal atāka ḥadīth* appears at the head of several passages and always introduces a report the hearer is about to be given in full: *“Has the story of Moses reached you ˹O Prophet˺?”* (79:15), *“Has the news of the Overwhelming Event reached you ˹O Prophet˺?”* (88:1), *“Has the story of the ˹destroyed˺ forces reached you ˹O Prophet˺—”* (85:17), and the same question about Moses at 20:9. The interrogative is not a real question. It signals that what follows is a communication, and it puts the hearer in the position of a recipient rather than a judge. Some commentators note the verse can equally be read as a statement: you have heard tell of Abraham's honoured guests. Either way the effect is to place the story inside a known tradition before any detail is given.

**Who the Honoured Guests Were**

The commentators record the guests as angels in human form, with estimates of three, ten, or twelve, and one of them identified as Gabriel. The parallel accounts are consistent with this: *“And surely Our messenger-angels came to Abraham with good news ˹of a son˺.”* (11:69), and *“They reassured ˹him˺, ‘Do not be afraid! We are ˹angels˺ sent ˹only˺ against the people of Lot.’”* (11:70). Nothing in the narrative announces them as angels at the outset, which is why Abraham's reaction develops as it does — a greeting, a meal, then fear when the meal is not touched. The concealment is the point of the story's structure. Had they arrived visibly as angels, there would have been no hospitality, no suspicion, and no lesson in either.

**Honoured by Two Parties**

*Mukramūn*, honoured, can be read in two directions at once, and both are supported. They are honoured by Abraham, who gives them the best greeting and the best food he owns without knowing who they are. They are also honoured by God, being chosen messengers on an errand of both mercy and judgment. The double sense enriches the adjective rather than blurring it. The chapter's audience is being shown a man who honours strangers as though they were noble, and who turns out to have been right. The same word elsewhere describes the honoured servants who do not speak until God has spoken (21:26–27), which fixes the rank of the guests from the divine side.

**Hospitality Is Tied to Belief in the Tradition**

The practice displayed here is not presented in the Qur'an as a cultural courtesy of ancient Arabia alone; the tradition attaches it to faith itself. Abū Hurayrah reports the Prophet ﷺ as saying: "Anybody who believes in Allah and the Last Day should not harm his neighbor, and anybody who believes in Allah and the Last Day should entertain his guest generously, and anybody who believes in Allah and the Last Day should talk what is good or keep quiet." (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6018). The three items are joined to the same condition of belief, and hospitality sits between neighbourliness and speech. Abraham's conduct in the following verses is the narrative form of that instruction, which is one reason the chapter selects this episode at all.

**Why This Story, in This Chapter**

The chapter's subject is certainty about an unseen promise. Abraham is the Qur'an's standing example of a man who believed before arrival — and the specific promise given him is a child to a barren, elderly wife. That is a promise as physically impossible as resurrection, and it was fulfilled. The story therefore functions as a demonstration of verse 5 rather than as a moral interlude. A God who brings a son from a dead womb brings bodies from dead ground, and the Qur'an draws exactly this kind of comparison when it likens resurrection to the reviving of dead land (35:9). The narrative is argument by precedent, addressed to an audience that already honoured Abraham's name.

**The Same Story Told Elsewhere With Different Emphasis**

This account is close to the versions in Hūd and al-Ḥijr, and other aspects of Abraham's story appear in 14:35–41 and 37:83–109. The variations are purposeful rather than inconsistent. In Hūd the wife's laughter is mentioned and Abraham's pleading for Lot's people follows the news (11:71, 11:74); in al-Ḥijr the emphasis falls on Abraham's astonishment at his own old age (15:54); in aṣ-Ṣāffāt the glad tidings are given directly by God (37:112). Here in adh-Dhāriyāt the emphasis is on the honour shown to the guests and on the mission they carry. Each version selects what its chapter needs, and this chapter needs a story about a promise received by a man who served his guests first.

**A Household Pattern for the One Being Addressed**

The question is put to the Prophet ﷺ directly, and the address matters. A man receiving visitors he cannot identify, feeding them before questioning them, and then learning that they carry a decree of destruction — that sequence is not remote from the situation of a messenger in Makkah whose own visitors were hostile and whose own message carried a warning. The story gives him a precedent for both halves: courtesy that does not wait for the guest's credentials, and certainty that does not waver when the news is impossible. The chapter selects the episode for the same reason it selects the accounts that follow. Each one shows a promise believed before it arrived, and a punishment executed after warning was refused.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:25

> ˹Remember˺ when they entered his presence and greeted ˹him with˺, “Peace!” He replied, “Peace ˹be upon you˺!” ˹Then he said to himself,˺ “˹These are˺ an unfamiliar people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Greetings in Two Grammatical Cases**

The exchange is recorded with a detail that only Arabic preserves. The angels' greeting arrives in the accusative, which reads as "we wish you peace" — a salutation directed at another. Abraham's reply comes in the nominative, "peace be upon you," a statement about peace itself. The commentators point out that the second form is fuller and firmer than the first. The Qur'an legislates precisely this movement: *“And when you are greeted, respond with a better greeting or at least similarly. Surely Allah is a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner of all things.”* (4:86). Abraham's answer is the better greeting, returned to visitors he has not identified and has no reason to flatter. The scene therefore opens with a small act of moral precision that sets the character of everything that follows.

**The Greeting Given Is the Greeting of Paradise**

The words exchanged here are not a local custom that happens to appear in scripture. They are the currency of the destination the chapter has been describing. The people of the gardens are greeted with the same word: *“Those who believe and do good will be admitted into Gardens, under which rivers flow—to stay there forever by the Will of their Lord—where they will be greeted with ‘Peace!’”* (14:23). Speech in that place is described as consisting of nothing but good and virtuous words (56:26). The believers are greeted with it in this world too: *“When the believers in Our revelations come to you, say, ‘Peace be upon you!’”* (6:54). Abraham's house in this scene is behaving like the Garden, which is why the episode can carry theological weight rather than being a mere anecdote about manners.

**They Arrived Unannounced**

No appointment precedes the entry, no message explains the visit, and no introduction identifies the visitors. They simply enter and greet. In the world of the story this is an ordinary occurrence — desert hospitality existed precisely because travellers appeared without notice — and Abraham's response treats it as such. The absence of announcement is what makes the scene a test. A host who has been warned can prepare; a host who has not been warned reveals what he already is. The Qur'an's narrative selects this moment deliberately: the man's character is displayed under conditions that give him no time to arrange an appearance. What comes out is immediate, uncalculated courtesy, followed at once by the private recognition that something about these guests is unusual.

**"An Unfamiliar People" Names a Real Problem**

The phrase *qawm munkarūn* means people not recognised, unknown. The word belongs to the same root that produces *munkar*, the wrong or improper thing, and its basic sense here is simply "not known to me." The exegetes differ on whether Abraham said this aloud to his guests or to himself and to his household. The self-addressed reading is supported by what happens next, since he turns away quietly to his family rather than confronting the visitors (51:26). The other reading makes the remark a polite inquiry into their identity. Both are coherent with the narrative, and the ambiguity may be intentional: the verse records an observation about strangers whose identity is genuinely unknown, and leaves its addressee where the story leaves it.

**Courtesy Is Not Suspended by Uncertainty**

The order inside the verse is instructive. The greeting comes first and the unease second, and the unease does not retract the greeting. Many readers would reverse the sequence: establish who these people are, then decide how to treat them. Abraham's conduct treats the guests well before he knows whether they are safe. The Qur'an's legislation on greeting runs the same way, requiring the better response without a prior investigation of the greeter's worth (4:86). This is not naivety. The man notices the strangeness and acts on it — he goes quietly to his family, which is the behaviour of someone alert. What he refuses to do is make his alertness the guests' problem.

**Strangers Are the Ordinary Form of Angels**

The concealment is consistent with how angels appear throughout the Qur'an. They come to Abraham as messenger-angels bearing good news and are not recognised at first (11:69); they come to Lot in a form that puts him in anguish because he cannot protect guests he believes to be men; they come to Mary in human shape (19:17). A visible angel would end the story before it began, since no host could behave naturally in front of one. The narrative needs the disguise to produce two things at once: genuine hospitality, and genuine fear when the hospitality is not accepted. Both reactions are evidence about Abraham, and neither could have been obtained from a visitor announced as a messenger of God.

**The Greeting Sets Up the Meal**

Everything in this verse is preparation for the next two. A better greeting returned, strangers acknowledged as unknown, and a host who has committed himself to their comfort — that combination makes the meal inevitable and makes its refusal significant. The chapter is building toward a moment in which the ordinary signals of peace between host and guest break down: food placed before visitors who do not eat it. Had the verse opened with the meal, the breakdown would have meant little. Because it opens with the greeting, the reader knows exactly how much trust had been established before it was disturbed. Abraham has already said peace to these people, and they have accepted the word. The next verse shows what he does with that peace.

**The Greeting Is Legislated for Every House**

The exchange recorded here does not remain a story. The Qur'an turns the same practice into law for believers: *“O believers! Do not enter any house other than your own until you have asked for permission and greeted its occupants. This is best for you, so perhaps you will be mindful.”* (24:27). Permission and greeting are joined, and both precede entry. A second verse carries the rule into ordinary social life: *“However, when you enter houses, greet one another with a greeting ˹of peace˺ from Allah, blessed and good.”* (24:61). The greeting exchanged in Abraham's tent is therefore the ground of a standing etiquette, and the description attached to it in that verse — blessed and good — matches the speech of the Garden, which consists of nothing but good and virtuous words (56:26). A scene about unknown visitors becomes the basis of a rule governing every door a believer enters.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:26

> Then he slipped off to his family and brought a fat ˹roasted˺ calf

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rāgha Means He Went Quietly**

The verb translated as slipped off is *rāgha*, from a root carrying the sense of turning aside, departing stealthily, moving without drawing attention. It is not the ordinary word for going. The choice describes a host who leaves his guests without announcing that he is leaving to feed them. The etiquette is exact: a guest told "I am going to slaughter something for you" is placed under an obligation and may protest, while a guest whose host quietly disappears is simply presented with food. The commentators note the parallel formulation elsewhere, where Abraham hastens to bring a roasted calf (11:69), and both features — speed and silence — belong to the same practice. The verse records not merely what was given but how the giving was managed.

**The Best Thing He Owned**

What is brought is a fat calf, roasted. Three qualities are packed into the phrase: it is young and therefore tender, it is fattened and therefore the finest meat available, and it is cooked and therefore ready immediately. In a pastoral household a fattened calf is capital, not leftovers. It is the animal kept for an occasion, and the occasion here is unannounced strangers whose identity is unknown. The parallel verse says the same thing with the addition of haste: *“Then it was not long before he brought ˹them˺ a ˹fat,˺ roasted calf.”* (11:69). The measure of hospitality in both accounts is not quantity but quality — what the host keeps for himself is what the guest receives.

**Haste Is Built Into the Description**

The sequence moves fast: they greet, he answers, he notes their strangeness, he goes to his family, he returns with food. Nothing in the verse suggests consultation, deliberation, or negotiation about whether these visitors deserve a meal. The Qur'an's account in Hūd marks the interval as short. Speed in hospitality is a moral quality rather than a logistical one, because delay invites the guest to feel like a burden. A host who is slow teaches his guest to apologise. Abraham's household produces a roasted calf in the time it takes to have a conversation, which tells the reader that this was a house where such things were expected and prepared for.

**He Did Not Ask Who They Were First**

The verse's placement after the remark about unfamiliar people is significant. Having noted that these are unknown visitors, Abraham's next recorded act is feeding them. He does not interrogate them, does not require a purpose for the visit, and does not condition the meal on an explanation. The order is a rule in itself: identify the need, then satisfy it, and ask questions afterwards if questions remain. This is what separates hospitality from bargaining. A host who feeds in order to learn something is conducting an exchange; a host who feeds and then learns something is practising hospitality. Abraham's later question about their mission comes only after the meal is placed before them (51:31).

**A Whole Calf for Guests of Unknown Number**

The commentators differ on how many visitors there were — three, ten, or twelve — and Abraham did not know the number either. A single fattened calf is proportioned to a large meal, and preparing one for an uncertain party is the practical form of generosity. It also risks waste, which is the ordinary reason households serve less. The verse records the risk being taken. Nothing in the narrative suggests he scaled the meal to what he estimated the guests would eat; he brought what honoured them. This detail is why the later refusal to eat lands so heavily: the offering was not a token, and its rejection is not a small matter.

**The Quiet Departure Protects Everyone**

Going to his family rather than issuing an order in front of the guests has a second effect. The household is not put on display, and the guests are not made aware of the cost being incurred on their behalf. Hospitality that announces its own expense converts the guest into a debtor. The verb *rāgha* covers both sides of that discretion: he leaves without ceremony, and the arrangement is made without the visitors hearing it arranged. The Qur'an's praise of Abraham elsewhere summarises the character displayed here: *“Truly, Abraham was forbearing, tender-hearted, and ever turning ˹to his Lord˺.”* (11:75). Forbearance and tenderness are exactly what the quiet departure shows.

**The Meal Becomes the Test**

A roasted calf placed before guests carries an expectation, and the narrative is about to break it. In the world of this story, refusal to eat is not a dietary preference; it is a signal. Abraham has done everything the custom requires, at speed and at cost, and the next verse will show him offering the food with the polite urgency of a host who wants his guests at ease. What follows — their untouched plates and his resulting fear — depends entirely on the fact that the offering was real and generous. Had the meal been modest or delayed, the refusal would have meant nothing. The verse therefore spends its whole length establishing the weight of what is about to be declined.

**The Guest Holds a Claim, Not a Hope**

Hospitality in the tradition is an obligation attached to belief rather than a kindness left to mood. Abū Hurayrah reports the Prophet ﷺ binding three behaviours to one condition: whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day should entertain his guest generously, should not harm his neighbour, and should say what is good or keep silent (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6018). The adverb carries the weight — generously, not adequately. Abraham's conduct matches it exactly: a fattened calf, prepared at once, for visitors he cannot identify and who have claimed nothing. The same report ties hospitality to belief in the Last Day, which places it inside this chapter's subject. A household that expects a reckoning feeds strangers before asking who they are, because the feeding is part of the account being kept. Nothing in the verse suggests a receipt was issued to the guests, and that absence is the practice in its pure form.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:27

> and placed it before them, asking, “Will you not eat?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**He Brought It Close Rather Than Setting a Table**

The verb is *qarraba*, to bring near, to place within reach. Abraham does not leave the food at a distance for the guests to approach; he moves it to them. The posture is that of a servant rather than a host presiding. Nothing in the verse indicates that he sat down, called them over, or waited for them to help themselves. The action completes the pattern established in the previous verse: the best available meat, prepared quickly, delivered quietly, and now presented directly. Each element removes a barrier between the guest and the food. The cumulative effect is that refusing this meal requires an active decision rather than a passive oversight, which is what makes the refusal meaningful in the narrative that follows.

**"Will You Not Eat?" Is an Invitation, Not an Instruction**

The question form is deliberate Arabic courtesy. A host who says "eat" issues a command and puts the guest under pressure; a host who says "will you not eat?" offers and leaves the answer open. The negative interrogative carries a further shade of urgency — it expresses the host's hope that the guest will eat, without demanding it. The verse therefore records both the generosity and the restraint of the same man in one clause. He has spent a fattened calf and does not intend to extract gratitude for it. The phrasing is the linguistic form of the discretion already shown by the quiet departure: the guest's freedom is preserved even at the moment of greatest investment by the host.

**The Question Also Functions as Alarm**

Read in sequence with what follows, the same words carry a second edge. The guests have entered unannounced, accepted the greeting of peace, and now sit before untouched food. A host's polite question in that situation is also a probe. The Qur'an's parallel account makes the resulting unease explicit: *“And when he saw that their hands did not reach for the food, he became suspicious and fearful of them.”* (11:70). The suspicion is generated by the untouched plate, not by anything the visitors said. Verse 27 is therefore the hinge of the scene: the moment at which hospitality is offered in its fullest form, and the moment at which the host begins to suspect that his guests are not what they appear.

**Refusing Food Was a Declaration in That World**

The commentators explain Abraham's fear by reference to a custom of the ancient world, including Arabia: to refuse food offered by a host was to signal hostility or ill intent. A guest who eats is under protection and at peace; a guest who does not eat has not accepted the relation. On this reading the fear is not irrational and not a failure of faith. It is the correct response to a genuine signal, given the code both parties are assumed to share. A second explanation is also recorded: Abraham may have suspected that these were angels sent with punishment, since angels arriving in human form and refusing food were not visitors with ordinary business. Both explanations rest on the same fact — the untouched meal — and both make his fear a rational inference.

**The Narrative Pauses on the Plate**

Verse 27 is short, and its brevity is a device. The chapter has moved quickly through the arrival, the greeting, the private remark, the quiet departure, and the return with food. Here it stops on a single question and lets the silence around it stand. Nothing happens. The food sits. The reader is held in the interval between an offer and an answer, which is where the scene's tension lives. When the next verse opens, the answer has already been given without words: they did not eat. The economy of the narration means the refusal is felt rather than reported, and the fear that follows it needs no explanation.

**Nothing Is Asked in Return**

Abraham's question seeks no payment, no news, no service, and no introduction. The hospitality of the two preceding verses is unconditional in the strict sense: it is extended before the guests' identity, purpose, or ability to reciprocate is known. The Qur'an elsewhere frames prophetic service in the same terms, with the messenger asking no wage from his people. The narrative here makes the point concretely, through food rather than doctrine. A man who feeds strangers and then asks only "will you not eat?" has set the standard against which the later request for information — what is your mission? (51:31) — can be seen as secondary. He gave first, and he asked afterwards.

**The Untouched Food Discloses What the Guests Are**

The refusal is the moment the disguise begins to fail. Angels do not eat, and their not eating is the first piece of evidence available to Abraham about their nature. The chapter does not narrate his reasoning; it presents the fear and the reassurance that follows. The structure teaches something about how divine visitors operate in these accounts: they arrive concealed, they are treated as human, and they disclose themselves through a detail that human custom makes significant. The meal Abraham spent his best animal on is the instrument of the disclosure. The next verse supplies the reassurance and the news that the visit was actually for.

**The Same Formula Is Used Against the Idols**

One other place in the Qur'an contains this exact question, and the contrast could not be sharper. Standing before the images his people set out with food, Abraham turns to them and says: *“Will you not eat ˹your offerings˺? What is wrong with you that you cannot speak?”* (37:91–92), and then strikes them with his right hand (37:93). Addressed to idols, the polite interrogative is irony: a meal is placed before them and nothing happens, because nothing is there. Addressed to guests in his own tent, the same words are sincerity. The two uses of one formula measure the distance between a living visitor and a dead image. In this scene the refusal to eat falls nearer the second case than Abraham can yet know, and the fear described in the next verse is the alarm of a man who has found that his guests do not behave like men.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:28

> ˹They did not eat,˺ so he grew fearful of them. They reassured ˹him˺, “Do not be afraid,” and gave him good news of a knowledgeable son

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fear Has Two Explanations and Both Are Sound**

The verse states the fear without giving its cause, and the commentators supply two. The first is cultural: in the ancient world, and in Arabia, refusing a host's food signalled hostility, so Abraham read the untouched plate as a threat. The second is theological: he suspected that these were angels sent with punishment, since messengers arriving unannounced in human form and declining food were not on ordinary business. The parallel account supports the first by describing his suspicion directly (11:70). The two are not rivals; they run together, because in that world an unusual visitor was plausibly an agent of judgment. What matters is that the fear is presented as reasonable. The verse does not correct Abraham for feeling it, and the reassurance that follows treats the fear as something to be removed rather than as a fault to be rebuked.

**"Do Not Be Afraid" Is the Standard Angelic Opening**

The reassurance is a formula the Qur'an uses whenever angels appear to people who cannot yet identify them. In Hūd it arrives with the reason attached: *“They reassured ˹him˺, ‘Do not be afraid! We are ˹angels˺ sent ˹only˺ against the people of Lot.’”* (11:70). In al-Ḥijr it is joined immediately to the good news: *“They reassured ˹him˺, ‘Do not be afraid! Surely we give you good news of a knowledgeable son.’”* (15:53). The repetition across chapters shows the pattern: fear, removal of fear, then the message. The order is not arbitrary. A terrified recipient cannot receive news, so the news waits until the fear has been addressed. Verse 28 in this chapter compresses the same sequence into a single sentence.

**The Good News Names Knowledge, Not Strength**

The son promised is *ghulām ʿalīm*, a boy who will be knowing. The quality selected is intellectual and spiritual rather than physical or political. Nothing is said about his strength, his wealth, his descendants, or his rank, and the adjective is the same one used in the parallel account (15:53). Elsewhere the promise to Abraham is described with a different quality: *“So We gave him good news of a forbearing son.”* (37:101), where forbearance — *ḥilm*, the capacity to hold one's temper under provocation — is named. The two descriptions belong to the two sons in the traditional reading and together give the character of the promised lineage. In this verse the emphasis falls on knowledge, which fits a chapter whose argument has just moved through signs, certainty, and reflection.

**Which Son Is Promised**

The majority of commentators identify the knowledgeable son here as Isaac, and the identification is well supported. The parallel in Hūd names him explicitly and adds a grandson: *“And his wife was standing by, so she laughed, then We gave her good news of ˹the birth of˺ Isaac, and, after him, Jacob.”* (11:71). A later chapter confirms the sequence by giving Isaac as a distinct, subsequent glad tiding: *“We ˹later˺ gave him good news of Isaac—a prophet, and one of the righteous.”* (37:112). A minority reading takes the son here as Ishmael, which requires the promise to be a separate announcement. The stronger position is Isaac, since the scene is continuous with the accounts in Hūd and al-Ḥijr that name him, and the wife's reaction in the next verse belongs to Sarah.

**God Gives the News Through Intermediaries**

Verse 28 says the angels gave him good news, while aṣ-Ṣāffāt says God gave it: *“We ˹later˺ gave him good news of Isaac—a prophet, and one of the righteous.”* (37:112). The commentators reconcile the two by noting that God gave the tidings through the angels as His messengers. Both statements are therefore true at different levels, and the pattern is consistent with the chapter's fourth oath, which swears by the angels administering affairs by command (51:4). Here is the administration at work on a single household: a decree from above delivered by couriers below. The same mechanism operates in the announcement to Mary, delivered by a messenger in human form (19:19–21).

**The News Is Impossible, and the Verse Says So Only by Implication**

Nothing in verse 28 mentions the obstacle. The reader learns it in the following verse, from Sarah's own mouth: a barren, old woman (51:29). The narrative arrangement is effective because it lets the promise be stated in its full, unqualified form before the impossibility is raised. The good news arrives as good news, not as a problem to be negotiated. When the objection comes, it comes from the human side and is answered by a statement about God's decree rather than by a biological explanation (51:30). This ordering matters for the chapter's larger argument, which is that promises sworn by God are true before they are feasible. The promise of resurrection operates on exactly the same terms.

**Reassurance Before the Gift**

The sequence inside the verse is a small lesson in how divine communication is conducted. Fear is addressed first; the gift comes second. Neither is withheld, but their order is fixed. A person in fear cannot receive a promise as a promise — he will hear it as another test, or as the calm before a blow. The angels remove the ground of the fear by identifying themselves, and only then deliver the news. The same order appears in the announcement to Mary and in the sending of Moses to Pharaoh, where the instruction is to speak gently so that the hearer may become mindful or fearful in the right way (20:44). Verse 28 records the pattern in its shortest form: an untouchable meal, a frightened host, a word of reassurance, and a son promised to a household that could not produce one. The order is the point. Fear is removed before good news is given, because news delivered to a frightened man is not received as news at all. Abraham's household had just been alarmed by hands that never reached the food, and the reassurance arrives inside the same sentence as the promise of a knowledgeable son.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:29

> Then his wife came forward with a cry, clasping her forehead ˹in astonishment˺, exclaiming, “˹A baby from˺ a barren, old woman!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Cry and a Gesture**

Two physical details are recorded, which is unusual for Qur'anic narrative. The verb *ṣarrat* describes a sharp cry or wail, and the gesture — striking or clasping the face, specifically the forehead — is the conventional expression of overwhelming astonishment in that culture. The verse does not explain the gesture or interpret the cry; it reports them, and the report is what makes the scene vivid. Sarah is not delivering a theological objection in calm terms. She is a woman who has heard something that her whole life has taught her to be impossible, and her body answers before her mind composes a sentence. The narrative allows this without reproach, which tells the reader how the Qur'an distinguishes astonishment from denial.

**She Was Standing By**

The parallel account in Hūd places her in the room and records a different first reaction: *“And his wife was standing by, so she laughed, then We gave her good news of ˹the birth of˺ Isaac, and, after him, Jacob.”* (11:71). The commentators discuss the laughter, some reading it as relief after the fear, others as the astonishment of a woman whose body had returned to fertility, others simply as joy. The two accounts are not in conflict so much as in sequence: a woman present at an extraordinary announcement reacts with a sound and a gesture, then with words. Adh-Dhāriyāt records the physical reaction; Hūd records the laughter and the naming of the son and grandson. Between them the scene is complete.

**The Objection Is Made of Facts**

Her exclamation contains no argument, only two descriptions: *ʿajūz*, an old woman, and *ʿaqīm*, barren. Both are true and both are known to her from her own history. The second is the stronger of the two, since age alone does not exclude childbirth, while a lifetime of barrenness establishes a pattern. She is not disputing God's power; she is reporting the evidence of her own body against the announcement. That is why the reply she receives does not engage with the evidence. It refers her to the one who made the body in the first place. A statement built entirely from facts can still be answered only by a statement about authority, and that is the answer given in the next verse.

**Her Husband Is Included in the Impossibility**

The fuller version of her objection names both parties: *“She wondered, ‘Oh, my! How can I have a child in this old age, and my husband here is an old man? This is truly an astonishing thing!’”* (11:72). The addition matters, because it moves the case from a personal anomaly to a joint one. Two people beyond the age of childbearing cannot produce a child by any ordinary route, and the verse in Hūd makes her say so. Her astonishment in adh-Dhāriyāt is compressed into a single phrase, but the compressed form carries the same content: a baby from a barren, old woman. The impossibility is stated from inside the household rather than by an outside observer, which is what gives it weight.

**The Ages Traditionally Given**

The commentators record Abraham at about one hundred and twenty years old at this point, and Sarah at about ninety-nine. Those figures come from transmitted accounts rather than from the Qur'anic text, and their value here is not precision but proportion: the promise concerns two people at the extreme end of a long life, one of them barren throughout it. The Qur'an itself makes the age explicit in another version, where Abraham responds to the news with his own astonishment: *“He wondered, ‘Do you give me good news despite my old age? What unlikely news!’”* (15:54). The point of the ages is that the birth cannot be explained by any natural recovery of fertility. It is a creation, not a cure.

**Astonishment Is Not Disbelief**

The angels' answer in the parallel account shows how the reaction is judged: *“They responded, ‘Are you astonished by Allah’s decree? May Allah’s mercy and blessings be upon you, O people of this house. Indeed, He is Praiseworthy, All-Glorious.’”* (11:73). The question is a rebuke, but it is a gentle one, and it is immediately followed by a blessing on the household. Nothing in either account suggests Sarah is charged with unbelief. Astonishment at an impossible announcement is the natural response of a person who takes the world seriously; denial would be a refusal to take the announcer seriously. The distinction is the reason this scene can appear in a chapter about certainty without damaging its subject.

**The Same Reaction at Mary**

A later announcement produces the same shape of response. Mary asks: *“How can I have a son when no man has ever touched me, nor am I unchaste?”* (19:20). Her objection, like Sarah's, is built entirely from facts about her own situation, and it includes a defence of her character. The answer is identical in structure: *“He replied, ‘So will it be! Your Lord says, ‘It is easy for Me. And so will We make him a sign for humanity and a mercy from Us.’ It is a matter ˹already˺ decreed.’”* (19:21). Two women, two impossible births, two objections made of evidence, and the same reply in both cases. The pairing is one reason this narrative block sits in a chapter whose subject is the certainty of a promise that has not yet arrived.

**The Same Word Describes the Wind Later in This Chapter**

Sarah calls herself *ʿaqīm*, barren, and the chapter reuses that exact adjective a few verses later for a punishment. The wind sent against ʿĀd is *rīḥ ʿaqīm*, rendered here as the devastating wind (51:41), and its literal sense is a barren wind — one that carries no rain, pollinates nothing, and leaves no good behind it. The word names a thing that produces nothing, whether the thing is a womb or a weather system. The chapter then reverses both cases in opposite directions. The barren woman conceives; the barren wind destroys a people who thought themselves productive and permanent. That single shared adjective ties the household narrative to the destruction narrative, and it makes the point the chapter has been arguing since its oaths: fruitfulness and sterility are not properties of things but conditions assigned by the one who administers them.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:30

> They replied, “Such has your Lord decreed. He is truly the All-Wise, All-Knowing.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Decree Instead of an Explanation**

The reply gives no mechanism. Nothing is said about the restoration of fertility, about the reversal of age, or about how the birth will occur. The angels answer with the fact of a decree: thus has your Lord said. The technique is deliberate and it recurs throughout the Qur'an whenever an impossible event is announced. Explanation would place the event inside a chain of causes the hearer already understands, and the whole point of the announcement is that it sits outside that chain. What is offered instead is the identity of the one who ordered it. A person who cannot follow the method can still assess the authority, and the authority is the only thing adequate to the case.

**Two Names Close the Sentence**

The reply ends with *ḥakīm ʿalīm* — All-Wise, All-Knowing — and the choice of these two names is precise. Knowledge answers the objection about the body: the one who decreed this knows its condition, its age, and its history of barrenness better than the woman describing it. Wisdom answers the objection about the purpose: the decree is not arbitrary, and there is a reason for it that the household cannot yet see. Neither name is a general compliment. Both address a specific difficulty raised in the previous verse. The pairing is one of the most frequent in the Qur'an, and it typically appears where an arrangement is being asserted against an appearance that contradicts it.

**The Same Answer Given to Mary**

The parallel is close enough to be treated as a fixed form. When Mary raises her objection, the reply runs: *“Your Lord says, ‘It is easy for Me. And so will We make him a sign for humanity and a mercy from Us.’ It is a matter ˹already˺ decreed.”* (19:21). Three elements recur — ease for God, a purpose in the form of a sign and a mercy, and a matter already settled. The adh-Dhāriyāt version compresses all three into one clause plus two names. What neither version does is argue. Both refer the questioner to the decree and to the character of the one who issued it. That consistency across two separate narratives, separated in the revelation and addressed to different figures, shows the Qur'an treating impossible announcements as a single class of event.

**"How" Is Answered With "Who"**

Sarah's objection was a question about process, and the answer is a statement about agency. This is a general feature of the Qur'an's handling of the resurrection, which is the chapter's main subject. When asked how dead bones will be restored, the reply points to the one who made them first and knows every creation (36:78–79). When the reviving of land is offered as an analogy, the emphasis falls on the one who sends the wind and drives the cloud (35:9). The method is never supplied because the method is not the difficulty. The difficulty is always whether the agent is capable, and the answer to that is a description of the agent rather than of the process. Verse 30 is that answer in its shortest form.

**A Rebuke Delivered as a Blessing**

The parallel account adds the tone of the reply: *“Are you astonished by Allah’s decree? May Allah’s mercy and blessings be upon you, O people of this house. Indeed, He is Praiseworthy, All-Glorious.”* (11:73). The question is corrective and the greeting that follows it is warm. The household is addressed collectively and blessed, which tells the reader that the astonishment has not damaged the standing of the house. This is how the Qur'an corrects a believer's surprise: it names the surprise as misplaced, then confirms the relationship. In adh-Dhāriyāt the correction is compressed into the two divine names, which do the same work by implication — a person who knows and orders wisely is not to be measured against a household's expectations.

**These Are the Only Names the Case Requires**

Other divine names would have been possible: All-Powerful, Originator, Giver of Life. The reply chooses wisdom and knowledge instead, and the choice is worth noticing. Power alone would answer the objection but leave the purpose open — a powerful agent can do anything, including anything pointless. Wisdom supplies the reason, and knowledge supplies the awareness of the particular case. Together they assert that this birth is both possible and intended. For a household that has waited a lifetime, the second half matters more than the first. The verse thus gives Sarah more than the answer she asked for: not only that the child will come, but that his coming means something.

**The Story Now Turns to Its Second Errand**

With the announcement settled, the narrative returns to the reason the visitors came. Abraham's next question is about their mission (51:31), and the answer concerns a people marked for destruction. The shift is abrupt and it is characteristic of these accounts: a household receives a promise of life at the same moment as a region receives a decree of death, and both decrees are delivered by the same messengers in the same visit. The chapter holds the two together because its argument requires both. A God who gives a son to a barren woman and destroys a city of transgressors is administering one order, and the administration is the same one sworn by in the fourth oath of the chapter's opening (51:4).

**A Birth From a Barren Womb Is the Argument for Bodies From the Ground**

The chapter's subject is resurrection, and this scene supplies its most intimate evidence. The objection to resurrection is always the same: decayed material cannot be reassembled. The Qur'an answers it by pointing at origins: *“And they argue with Us— forgetting they were created—saying, ‘Who will give life to decayed bones?’ Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being.’”* (36:78–79). The story of Abraham's household is that answer acted out. A womb closed for a lifetime is opened by decree, and the mechanism is no more explained than the mechanism of resurrection will be. The same logic runs through the account of human beginnings elsewhere: *“We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop”* (22:5), and *“No female ever conceives or delivers without His knowledge.”* (35:11).

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:31

> ˹Later,˺ Abraham asked, “What is your mission, O messengers?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Comes After the News**

The order of the scene is carefully arranged. Fear is removed, the promise is delivered, the wife's astonishment is answered, and only then does Abraham ask about the visitors' business. A man who had asked first would have spent the whole encounter in anxiety, and the good news would have arrived under the shadow of an unexplained errand. The narrative reverses that: the gift comes before the interrogation, and the interrogation is conducted by a host who is no longer afraid. This sequencing has a practical lesson in it. When an unusual visitor brings both mercy and judgment, receiving the mercy first changes how the judgment is heard, and Abraham's subsequent conduct confirms the difference.

**He Now Calls Them Messengers**

The address *ayyuhā al-mursalūn* — O messengers — shows that the disguise has ended. At the start of the scene they were an unfamiliar people (51:25); after the reassurance and the promise they are recognised by their function. The title is not a guess. Angels who announce a birth that cannot occur naturally, and who speak with the authority of "thus has your Lord decreed" (51:30), have identified themselves sufficiently. Abraham's question therefore proceeds from knowledge rather than curiosity, and the respect in the vocative marks the rank he now assigns them. The same title appears in the parallel account in al-Ḥijr (15:57), where the question is worded almost identically.

**Khaṭb Means the Matter You Have Come On**

The word translated as mission is *khaṭb*, which carries the sense of a significant affair, a weighty matter, a case that has brought someone. It is not the ordinary word for a task or an errand. The choice implies that Abraham understands these visitors to be carrying something consequential, and the narrative justifies the implication: their mission is the destruction of a city. The same word appears in the story of Moses when Pharaoh's magicians and court are confronted with a momentous matter, and in the description of the Day of Judgment as a grave announcement. Its use here signals that the question is not social. Abraham is asking what these messengers are about to do.

**What He Did With the Answer**

The parallel account records the consequence of the reply, and it is the reason this question matters: *“Then after the fear had left Abraham, and the good news had reached him, he began to plead with Us for the people of Lot.”* (11:74). The verse supplies both conditions — fear gone, news received — before describing the pleading, which confirms the significance of the order noted above. Only a man at peace and in possession of a mercy of his own argues for the salvation of others. The pleading is presented as an act of the same character the Qur'an praises in him elsewhere: forbearing, tender-hearted, ever turning to his Lord (11:75).

**Pleading Against a Decree Already Issued**

Abraham's intercession runs into a limit, and the limit is stated without softening: *“˹The angels said,˺ ‘O Abraham! Plead no more! Your Lord’s decree has already come, and they will certainly be afflicted with a punishment that cannot be averted!’”* (11:76). The exchange shows two things at once. Compassion for people under a decree of destruction is legitimate and is exercised by the Qur'an's greatest exemplar of tenderness. And a decree that has come cannot be argued back. The sequence is not a failure of Abraham's pleading but its correct termination: he pleads until he is told the matter is closed, and then he stops. The chapter needs both halves, since its subject is the certainty of decrees.

**The Question Is a Model for Receiving the Unseen**

Abraham does not panic at the presence of angels, does not demand a sign, and does not speculate about their purpose. He asks. The question is short, direct, and respectful, and it is put after the household's own affairs have been settled. This is the conduct of a man whose certainty is operational rather than emotional. The chapter has spent twenty verses arguing that signs require a receiver with certainty (51:20), and it now shows one. Abraham sees messengers where another man sees strangers, and he asks them their business where another man would either flee or worship them. Neither fear nor awe prevents the question.

**Why This Chapter Includes the Question**

The narrative block in adh-Dhāriyāt exists to demonstrate that divine promises and divine punishments are both executed. The question is the hinge that moves the story from the promise to the punishment. Without it, the visit would be a private blessing to one household and would carry no argument for the chapter's opening oaths. With it, the same messengers who announce a birth become the agents of a city's end, and the reader is shown a single administration operating in both directions. That is precisely what the fourth oath described: angels administering affairs by command (51:4). The question Abraham asks is the question the chapter has been answering since verse 5.

**Asking About an Announced Mission Is Not Prying**

The Qur'an draws a line around inquiry, and Abraham's question sits on the permitted side of it. Believers are told: *“O believers! Do not ask about any matter which, if made clear to you, may disturb you. But if you inquire about what is being revealed in the Quran, it will be made clear to you.”* (5:101). The forbidden question is the one that fishes in hidden matters and then cannot bear the answer. Abraham's is different in kind. His visitors have already identified themselves as messengers and have already delivered one part of their errand to his own household. Asking what remains is not curiosity about the unseen; it is completing a communication the other party opened. The verse models a discipline that believers need: ask about what has been raised, and do not manufacture questions about what has not.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:32

> They replied, “We have actually been sent to a wicked people

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mujrimūn Describes a Settled Condition**

The word translated as wicked is *mujrimūn*, from the root *j-r-m*, whose basic sense is to commit a crime or to acquire guilt. The form is that of a person who has made a practice of the act: not someone who stumbled, but someone whose conduct has accumulated into a character. The Qur'an uses the term as a standing contrast with belief: *“Is the one who is a believer equal ˹before Allah˺ to the one who is rebellious? They are not equal!”* (32:18). The two categories are treated as settled and distinct, and the distinction is what justifies differential outcomes. Verse 32 therefore opens the mission statement with a verdict already formed, before any detail of the crime is given.

**The People Are Not Named in This Version**

Adh-Dhāriyāt leaves the target unidentified at this point. The parallel accounts name them immediately: *“We are ˹angels˺ sent ˹only˺ against the people of Lot.”* (11:70). The omission here is a feature of the chapter's compression. Verses 32 to 34 describe the mission in general terms — a wicked people, stones of clay, marked for the transgressors — and the identification arrives only indirectly, through the evacuation of the believers and the single house of submitters found in the city (51:35–36). A reader who knows the story supplies Lot at once; a reader who does not receives a description of a punishment whose logic is stated before its location. Either way the argument is unaffected, since the point is the pattern rather than the place.

**Sent, Not Acting on Their Own**

The reply begins with *innā mursalūn* — we have been sent. The passive is the standing description of angels in the Qur'an and it excludes initiative. They do not choose the target, the method, or the timing. The same subordination is stated doctrinally elsewhere: the angels are honoured servants who do not speak until God has spoken and act only at His command (21:26–27). The chapter has already sworn by this fact in its opening verses, where the fourth oath names the angels administering affairs by command (51:4). Verse 32 is that oath in operation: couriers describing their delivery. The punishment they carry is as much an act of administration as the rain and the provision described earlier.

**Wickedness Here Is Public and Collective**

The mission is directed at a people, not at a list of individuals. The crime being punished is a social condition — a community organised around a practice its own messenger condemned. Lot's address to them is preserved: *“when their brother Lot said to them, ‘Will you not fear ˹Allah˺? I am truly a trustworthy messenger to you.’”* (26:161–162). The charge and the warning both precede the mission, which is why the destruction can be described as just rather than as calamity. The Qur'an states the general rule that makes this necessary: *“And We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺.”* (17:15). A people with a messenger who was rejected is the precise case the rule contemplates.

**The Same Vocabulary Describes Pharaoh**

The chapter's next narrative block uses closely related terms for its target. Pharaoh is described as having transgressed all bounds (79:17), and the mission against him is a seizure rather than a stoning. Elsewhere he is named as a tyrant and a transgressor (44:31). The vocabulary of guilt runs through all four accounts that follow in this chapter — Lot's people, Pharaoh, ʿĀd, Thamūd — and in each case the guilt is established before the punishment and is described in moral rather than legal terms. The repetition is the chapter's method: four cases, one pattern, warning refused and consequence executed. Verse 32 states the pattern in its opening clause.

**A Mission Carried by Messengers of Mercy Too**

The same visitors have just delivered a promise of life to a barren household. Nothing in their description distinguishes the two errands in terms of rank or difficulty; both are deliveries. The Qur'an's presentation of angels refuses the popular division between agents of mercy and agents of wrath. There is one administration and many tasks, and the task assigned determines the outcome, not the nature of the messenger. This is why the reassurance "do not be afraid" (51:28) was necessary at all: the same beings who bring a son can bring a city's end, and a host cannot tell from their appearance which errand they are on.

**The Method Is Specified Next**

Verse 32 announces the target; the two verses that follow describe the instrument and its precision. Stones of clay, marked by the Lord for the transgressors (51:33–34). The narrative moves from the general charge to the particular means with no transition, which is characteristic of these accounts: the guilt is stated, the punishment is specified, and no argument intervenes. The absence of debate is itself the point. By this stage in the Qur'an's telling, the case against these people has already been made in their own lifetime, by their own messenger, and rejected. What remains is execution, and the chapter describes it in the language of an order being carried out.

**Two Conditions Could Have Stopped the Punishment**

The Qur'an states the circumstances under which a community is spared, and neither applied here. *“But Allah would never punish them while you ˹O Prophet˺ were in their midst. Nor would He ever punish them if they prayed for forgiveness.”* (8:33). The presence of a messenger and the practice of seeking forgiveness are the two shields, and the people described in verse 32 had rejected the first and abandoned the second. Their messenger had called them to fear and identified himself as trustworthy (26:161–162), and the reply was that he was bewitched (26:153). The general rule is stated without exception: punishment follows a warning that was refused (17:15). Verse 32 therefore opens the mission statement with a people who have already exhausted the two possibilities that could have kept the stones in the sky.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:33

> to send upon them stones of ˹baked˺ clay

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sijjīl Is Clay That Has Been Fired**

The word used for the stones is *sijjīl*, and it denotes clay baked hard — material that begins as mud and ends as something with the density and edge of ceramic. The commentators take it as stones of hardened clay, and the same word appears in the other accounts of this destruction: *“When Our command came, We turned the cities upside down and rained down on them clustered stones of baked clay”* (11:82), and *“And We turned the cities ˹of Sodom and Gomorrah˺ upside down and rained upon them stones of baked clay.”* (15:74). The material is deliberately ordinary. It is not iron, not fire from the sky, not a meteor of exotic composition. It is earth, altered by heat, and the alteration is the only thing supernatural about it.

**The Cities Are Turned Over Before the Stones Fall**

The order in the parallel account matters. The command comes, the settlements are inverted — their uppermost made their lowermost — and then the stones rain down on what is left. The two operations are distinct and sequential. Verse 33 in this chapter mentions only the stones, because its purpose is to describe the mission the messengers were sent on rather than the whole catastrophe. The fuller sequence appears in Hūd and in al-Ḥijr, and another chapter compresses it into a single image: *“We unleashed upon them a storm of stones. As for ˹the believers of˺ Lot’s family, We delivered them before dawn”* (54:34). Read together, the accounts describe a total reversal of a settled landscape followed by a bombardment of its remains.

**A Rain From Above, Where the Roof Was Thought Safe**

The verb used for the stones is one of raining or sending down. The punishment arrives from the direction people least expect danger — overhead. The cities in question were prosperous, walled, and confident; their security was architectural and horizontal. A stoning from the sky bypasses walls entirely, and the inversion of the settlements removes the ground itself. The Qur'an notes elsewhere that the people of the Stone Valley carved homes in mountains and felt secure, and were overtaken by a blast in the morning (15:82–83) — a different people, but the same structure of false confidence. Verse 33 belongs to that pattern: the instrument of destruction comes from where no defence has been built.

**The Same Word Appears in the Chapter of the Elephant**

*Sijjīl* occurs one other place in the Qur'an, and the context is instructive. Describing the birds sent against the army that came to destroy the Kaʿbah, the text says they pelted them with stones of baked clay (105:4). Two events separated by centuries use the identical material. In both cases a small, unimpressive projectile destroys an army or a city that considered itself invulnerable, and in both cases the destruction is total. The shared vocabulary ties the two accounts into one category of divine action: measured punishment delivered by an unlikely instrument. A Meccan audience that had heard of the Year of the Elephant — an event within living memory of their grandparents — would recognise the reference immediately.

**The Stones Are Clustered, Not Random**

The Hūd account describes them as *manḍūd*, clustered, layered, arranged. The adjective excludes a scattering of debris or a natural rockfall. Stones that are stacked and ordered are ammunition, and ammunition implies a supply and a purpose. The next verse confirms this by describing them as marked (51:34). The combination of clustered and marked produces an image of punishment that is organised rather than chaotic: quantity without waste, and precision without exception. This is the same administration described in the chapter's opening oath — affairs distributed by command — now applied to a single city's end.

**Earth Used as the Instrument of Judgment**

There is a fitness in the material. These were people whose civilisation rested on their land: its cities, its agriculture, its roads, its buildings of stone and mud-brick. The instrument of their destruction is the same substance, fired. The Qur'an often punishes a people through the element they trusted or abused — Pharaoh's army through the sea it pursued Israel into (51:40), ʿĀd through the wind it boasted of, Thamūd through the rock it carved. In this case the ground beneath the cities is turned over and the sky above them throws back hardened clay. The message is that no element belongs to a people. Everything they stand on is on loan.

**Delivered Before Dawn, With the Believers Already Gone**

The timing is specified in another account: the storm of stones came after the believers had been delivered before dawn (54:34). The instruction to Lot's household was to travel in the dark of night, and their appointed hour was the morning (11:81). The punishment and the rescue are coordinated to the minute, and the coordination is the reason verse 33's mission statement can be read as justice rather than catastrophe. Stones fall on a city that has already been emptied of everyone who was not part of the crime. What remains is not a mixed population caught in a disaster but a targeted execution carried out on a cleared site.

**The Mission Executes What the Chapter Swore To**

Verses 5 and 6 of this chapter swore that the promise is true and that the judgment will certainly come to pass. Verse 33 shows a judgment arriving inside history, in a named place, against a named crime, by a stated instrument. The chapter's argument depends on this. A promise about the last day could be dismissed as unverifiable, but a promise pattern already executed four times — Lot's people, Pharaoh, ʿĀd, Thamūd — cannot be dismissed in the same way. The mockers of verse 12 asked when the day would come. The narrative block beginning at verse 24 answers that a version of it has come repeatedly, on schedule, with the instruments described in advance. The stones are the chapter's historical exhibit.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:34

> marked by your Lord for the transgressors.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Musawwama Means Branded or Distinguished**

The participle describes the stones as *musawwama*, from a root meaning to mark, to brand, to set a distinguishing sign on something. The word is used of animals marked so they can be identified in a herd, and of things set apart for a specific use. Applied to stones, it says that each one carried an identification. The commentators explain the marking in two ways, and both are recorded: that each stone bore the name of the person it was destined to strike, and that each fell with a divine purpose attached to it. The first reading supplies a vivid image; the second states the same fact in more general terms. Neither reading leaves room for accident, and the difference between them is only about how much detail the marking contained.

**Precision Excludes Collateral Damage**

A punishment delivered by marked stones cannot strike the wrong person. This is not a small detail in a scene where the guilty city has just been emptied of its believers (51:35). The marking guarantees that the evacuation and the bombardment are two halves of one operation rather than a rescue followed by an indiscriminate disaster. The Qur'an insists on this feature of divine punishment repeatedly: no soul burdened with sin bears the burden of another (17:15), and the record of the day is itemised to the weight of an atom (99:7–8). Verse 34 places the same principle inside a historical event. Stones addressed to individuals are the physical form of a reckoning that is personal rather than collective in its execution.

**"By Your Lord" Turns to the Man Hearing It**

The parallel verse makes the address explicit: *“marked by your Lord ˹O Prophet˺. And these stones are not far from the ˹pagan˺ wrongdoers!”* (11:83). The marking is attributed to the Lord of the listener, not of the ancient city, and the second sentence extends the warning across the centuries. The stones are not far from the wrongdoers now being addressed. This is the mechanism by which a narrative about a destroyed town becomes a present-tense threat. The audience of the chapter is Meccan, and Meccan caravans passed within sight of the region in question. The verse tells them that the same instrument remains available and that the qualification for it has not changed.

**Musrifūn Names the Specific Crime: Excess**

The people the stones are marked for are *musrifūn*, from a root meaning to exceed a limit, to spend or act beyond measure. The word covers extravagance in wealth and in appetite, and it is used of moral excess generally. Its application here is precise: the charge against Lot's people in the Qur'an is not a single act but a settled practice that overstepped what the messenger had set before them. The same vocabulary describes Pharaoh as a tyrant and a transgressor (44:31), and the command to go to him is justified by the fact that he has transgressed all bounds (79:17). Excess is the chapter's word for the state that precedes destruction, in every case it narrates.

**The Two Sides of a Marked Stone**

A marking is a promise to someone and a threat to someone else, and the verse holds both. For the transgressors it is a sentence already written on the instrument of execution. For the believers in the same city it is a guarantee: the stones have names, and theirs are not among them. The evacuation described in the next verse is therefore not a lucky escape but the other half of the marking. This is why the chapter can describe destruction in such administrative language without sounding brutal. The precision is what makes the event just. An unmarked bombardment would be a natural disaster; a marked one is a verdict.

**Marking Implies a Prior Register**

Stones do not acquire names by themselves. The marking presupposes a list, and the Qur'an speaks of such lists constantly: everything written in a perfect record (11:6), the two recording angels noting every word (50:17–18), the affair conducted from heaven to earth and ascending back on a day of reckoning (32:5). Verse 34 puts the same doctrine into a physical object. The stone falling on a specific roof was specified before it fell, which means the register existed before the crime reached its limit. The chapter's fourth oath described the angels as apportioning affairs by command (51:4); this verse shows the apportionment in its most severe application.

**The Verse Ends the Angels' Speech**

Verses 32 to 34 form the complete mission statement: a wicked people, stones of baked clay, marked for the transgressors. Three clauses, no elaboration, no argument, and no offer of negotiation. The brevity matches the situation. The case against these people was made during their lifetime by their own messenger and rejected with an accusation of bewitchment (26:153). Nothing remains to be said to them, and nothing is said. The narration now passes from the messengers' report to God's own account of what happened next, beginning with the evacuation. The shift in speaker marks the end of the announcement and the start of the execution.

**The Same Precision Appears in the Chapter's Other Cases**

The four destructions narrated in this chapter are all described with the same attention to the instrument and its exactness. ʿĀd receives a wind that leaves nothing it passes over unruined (51:41–42). Thamūd receives a single blast while they look on (51:44). Pharaoh and his soldiers are seized and thrown into the sea (51:40). In each case the punishment fits the crime, uses the element the people trusted, and arrives at a stated time. Verse 34 is the clearest statement of the principle behind all four: the punishment is designated, not general. That is what allows the chapter to present destruction as evidence of order rather than as evidence of chaos.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:35

> Then ˹before the torment˺ We evacuated the believers from the city

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rescue Is Described as an Extraction**

The verb is one of bringing forth or causing to exit. The believers are not shielded in place, protected under a roof while the stones fall around them; they are removed from the location before the event. The distinction matters theologically. A shielded believer inside a destroyed city would be a miracle of protection; an extracted believer outside it is a miracle of separation. The chapter chooses the second. Its subject is the sorting of people into two outcomes, and the narrative enacts that sorting physically, in space and time, before the punishment arrives. What happens to the city afterwards happens to a city that no longer contains the people God intended to save.

**The Timing Is Given to the Hour**

Another account fixes the moment: *“We unleashed upon them a storm of stones. As for ˹the believers of˺ Lot’s family, We delivered them before dawn”* (54:34). Before dawn — the same hour this chapter elsewhere names as the time when the devout seek forgiveness (51:18). The coincidence of hours is not accidental in Qur'anic narrative; the last part of the night is repeatedly the time of deliverance and of response. The instruction given to the household matches it: *“So travel with your family in the dark of night, and do not let any of you look back, except your wife.”* (11:81). The departure, the journey, and the arrival are all placed inside a single night, and the morning is named as the appointed time of the city.

**Do Not Look Back**

The condition attached to the evacuation is a prohibition on turning around. The instruction is practical and symbolic at once. Practically, a family fleeing at night through a doomed region gains nothing by looking and loses time by stopping. Symbolically, the look back is attachment to the place being destroyed, and the one person exempted from the command is the one already marked for the same fate: except your wife. The Qur'an does not explain her attachment, and the commentators differ about its nature, but the narrative treats her exclusion as settled in advance. She will suffer the fate of the others (11:81), and the determination is described as already made (15:60).

**Punishment Waits for the Believers to Leave**

The principle is stated elsewhere in general terms: *“But Allah would never punish them while you ˹O Prophet˺ were in their midst. Nor would He ever punish them if they prayed for forgiveness.”* (8:33). The presence of a righteous person in a community delays its destruction. In the case of Lot's city the delay runs to its logical end: the righteous are removed first, and only then does the punishment fall. The same sequence appears in the account of ʿĀd, where the believers are saved and the disbelievers uprooted (7:72). What the pattern establishes is that divine punishment is never a disaster that sweeps up the innocent along with the guilty. The innocent are relocated before it begins.

**Here They Are Called Believers, and Next Submitters**

Verse 35 uses *muʾminūn*, believers; verse 36 uses *muslimūn*, those who have submitted. The commentators read the pairing as describing people who combine faith with action, and the two terms are used elsewhere in the Qur'an with a deliberate distinction between inward conviction and outward compliance (49:14). In this passage the sequence moves from the inward designation to the outward one, which fits the narrative: what is being described is a household that left a city at night on an instruction, an act of submission performed on the basis of belief. Both words are needed to describe them completely, and both are supplied in consecutive verses.

**A Whole City Emptied of One Household**

The scale of the rescue is the striking feature. The evacuation is not of a community, a tribe, or a neighbourhood; it is of a single family. Verse 36 states that nothing else was found in the city. That emptiness is what makes the punishment total, and it is also what makes the rescue remarkable. God removes one household from a doomed settlement with the same care another narrative gives to a whole nation crossing a sea. The attention paid to a single family is the practical demonstration of the marking described in the previous verse: the stones have names, and so does the rescue.

**The Rescue Is Also a Warning to the Rescued**

Nothing in the verse suggests that leaving the city ends the test. The household travels at night under instruction, with a prohibition attached and one member already excluded. Deliverance here is conditional and demanding, and it requires obedience at each step. The Qur'an's accounts of saved peoples consistently show this: Noah's family is saved with an exception, Lot's household is saved with an exception, and the Israelites cross a sea they must then live faithfully on the far side of. Rescue is not the end of the moral story; it is a transfer into a new set of obligations. The chapter uses the pattern to speak to its own audience, who are being offered the same transfer.

**The Verse Sets Up the Count**

Verse 35 says the believers were brought out; verse 36 says how many there were. The two verses together produce the chapter's starkest statistic: one house. The narration does not comment on the number or moralise about it. It states the extraction and then the count, and leaves the reader to absorb what a city containing one believing household deserves. The following verse then describes what was left behind as a sign, which completes the sequence — removal, destruction, and a remaining trace for those who fear the painful punishment (51:37). Evacuation ahead of the torment is the chapter's clearest statement that the destruction was aimed rather than indiscriminate, and the verse that follows supplies the count it implies: one household. The sequence also answers the question raised by the marked stones — how a punishment assigned by name avoids the people it was not assigned to.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:36

> But We only found one family that had submitted ˹to Allah˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**One House: Lot and His Two Daughters**

The commentators identify the household as Lot together with his two daughters, saved from the punishment while his wife was not. The identification is consistent across the parallel accounts: *“So We saved him and his family except his wife, who was one of the doomed.”* (7:83), *“So We saved him and all of his family, except an old woman, who was one of the doomed.”* (26:170–171), and *“So We delivered him and his family, except his wife. We had destined her to be one of the doomed.”* (27:57). Three separate chapters give the same arithmetic: a household rescued minus one member. The verse in front of us states the result of that arithmetic in the smallest possible unit — one house.

**"We Did Not Find" Describes a Search That Came Up Empty**

The verb implies looking. The statement is not merely that one household believed; it is that a search of the city found no other. The formulation puts the emptiness on the record. A prosperous, populated, architecturally confident settlement is described as containing a single submitted family, and the description is delivered in the language of an inspection. This is the Qur'an's way of stating a majority that does not matter. The number of inhabitants is irrelevant to the finding, and the finding is what determines the outcome. The city was not destroyed because it was large and mostly wicked; it was destroyed because it was entirely wicked except for one house, which was removed.

**The Wife Is Excluded From the House That Was Saved**

Her exclusion is stated as a determination rather than an accident. In al-Ḥijr the angels say: *“As for the family of Lot, we will certainly deliver them all, except his wife. We have determined that she will be one of the doomed.”* (15:59–60). The exception is made inside a promise of total deliverance, which is the sharpest form the statement could take. Kinship with a prophet does not transfer salvation. The same principle is enacted in the accounts of Noah's son and in the description of the day when no soul benefits another (82:19). Verse 36 depends on this: the household counted as one house of submitters does not include every relative of its head.

**Submission After Belief**

Verse 35 described those brought out as believers; this verse describes what was found as a house of those who submitted. The two terms are used with care elsewhere in the Qur'an. When some nomadic Arabs claim belief, they are told: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘You have not believed. But say, ‘We have submitted,’ for faith has not yet entered your hearts.’”* (49:14). Submission can exist without full faith, and faith without visible submission is not the Qur'an's concern. Here the order is reversed and complementary: the people are first named for their belief and then for the act that belief produced — leaving a city at night because they were told to. The commentators read the pairing as describing those who join faith to action.

**The Count Explains the Totality of the Punishment**

If one house is all there is, then everything left in the city belongs to the crime. The marked stones of the previous verse require no exceptions, and the overturning of the settlements requires no partial evacuation. The verse therefore performs an argumentative function: it establishes that the destruction was proportionate. This is the answer to an objection any reader might raise about a whole city being wiped out. The narrative has already shown a messenger sent, a warning given, a rejection made, and an inspection conducted. The result of the inspection is the justification of the sentence.

**A Majority Is Not Evidence of Anything**

The Qur'an states this rule in flat terms and repeats it: *“And most people will not believe—no matter how keen you are—”* (12:103), and *“˹O Prophet!˺ If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah’s Way.”* (6:116). Verse 36 is the narrative form of the same claim. A city's size, wealth, and unanimity carry no weight in the finding. The chapter's audience lived in a commercial centre that prided itself on its consensus and its ancestral practice, and the story places that pride against a count of one household. The comparison is not flattering to the majority, and it is not intended to be.

**A Single Household Changes the Meaning of a City**

The presence of one submitted family is what turns the event from a catastrophe into a judgment. Without them, the destruction would be indistinguishable from a natural disaster that happened to strike a wicked town. With them, it is a measured operation: someone looked, someone was found, someone was removed, and the rest was dealt with. The verse also implies the value of that household in the divine estimate — a city is processed, its population inspected, and the one family that submitted is treated as worth extracting at night under angelic escort. The disproportion between the size of the rescue and the size of the destruction is the chapter's comment on what belief is worth.

**What Remains After the Count**

Two verses have now described the removal and the finding. The next verse describes what was left in the place afterwards: a sign for those who fear the painful punishment (51:37). The sequence is complete — inspection, extraction, destruction, residue — and each stage is stated in a single verse. The chapter then moves on to Moses without pause, because the argument does not need elaboration. One case has been established with enough detail to serve as a precedent, and three more will follow in shorter form. The pattern is what the chapter wants its audience to take away: a warning, a refusal, a sorting, and an execution.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:37

> And We have left a sign there ˹as a lesson˺ for those who fear the painful punishment

**Expanded Commentary**

**Something Was Deliberately Left Behind**

The verb is one of leaving or setting aside, and it is active: *we* left a sign there. The destruction was total, but its remains were not swept away. Something was preserved at the site with an intention attached to it. The commentators describe what was left in two ways — the physical ruins of the town, and the story of the punishment transmitted to later generations — and both readings are supported by the text elsewhere. The two are not alternatives in practice. A ruin that nobody speaks of is a pile of stones; a story with no location is a rumour. What the verse describes is a place and an account of the place, held together so that the second can be verified against the first.

**The Ruins Lie on a Route People Still Use**

The Qur'an insists on the accessibility of the remains. *“Their ruins still lie along a known route. Surely in this is a sign for those who believe.”* (15:76–77). The road is specified as known, which means travelled, and the audience is the one using it. Another chapter puts it even more directly to the Meccans: *“You ˹Meccans˺ certainly pass by their ruins day and night. Will you not then understand?”* (37:137–138). Passing by twice daily and not understanding is the exact failure the verse in front of us addresses. The sign is not hidden in a desert that requires an expedition; it sits beside a commercial route used by the very people disputing the chapter's opening oaths.

**Addressed to a Specific Kind of Person**

Those who fear the painful punishment are named as the beneficiaries. The qualification echoes verse 20, where the signs on earth are for those possessing certainty. Both verses specify the receiver rather than the evidence. The pattern is consistent across the Qur'an's sign-language: the same object functions as a proof for one person and as scenery for another, and the difference lies in a prior disposition. Fear here is not terror but the alertness of someone who takes a warning seriously. A person who does not expect punishment to be possible looks at a ruin and sees geology and history; a person who does expect it looks at the same ruin and sees a precedent.

**Ruined Houses Make the Argument Without Words**

Another chapter describes the same kind of remains in the same terms: *“So their homes are there, ˹but completely˺ ruined because of their wrongdoing. Surely in this is a lesson for people of knowledge.”* (27:52). The causal clause is the point — because of their wrongdoing. The ruin is not presented as a natural decay but as a consequence with a reason attached, and the reason is legible from the site. The verse in adh-Dhāriyāt makes the same claim with the audience changed from people of knowledge to people who fear. Both descriptions assume that a destroyed settlement carries information about why it was destroyed, and that the information is available to anyone who looks with the right question in mind.

**The Sign Confirms That the Oath Was Not Empty**

Verses 5 and 6 swore that the promise is true and that the judgment will certainly come to pass. Verse 37 supplies a completed case. The sign left at the site is evidence that a judgment described in advance was executed on schedule, with the innocent removed and the guilty designated. This is the chapter's method of arguing about the future: it produces a past instance. A promise about the last day can be doubted; a ruin beside a road cannot. The two are connected by the identity of the promiser. What has been done once by a stated instrument, at a stated time, against a stated crime, establishes the credibility of the same authority's announcement about a larger occasion.

**Meccan Caravans Passed Within Sight**

The audience's relation to these ruins is economic as well as geographic. The trade routes that carried Meccan caravans north passed the region of the destroyed cities, and the Qur'an elsewhere ties the people of ʿĀd and Thamūd to visible remains the Meccans could inspect themselves: *“And the people of ’Ȃd and Thamûd ˹met a similar fate˺, which must be clear to you ˹Meccans˺ from their ruins.”* (29:38). The clarity is stated as something already available, not as a claim requiring acceptance. Verse 37 places Lot's city in the same category of public evidence. The sign is left in the open, on a road, in a region the audience visits, which removes the usual excuse of inaccessible proof.

**Fear Is the Capacity That Reads a Sign**

The chapter has now named two capacities required for its evidence: certainty (51:20) and fear (51:37). Neither is an intellectual skill. Both are dispositions that determine what a person does with what he sees. Certainty allows signs in the earth and in the self to function as proofs; fear allows a ruin to function as a warning. A person lacking the second will look at the same stones and conclude that civilizations fall. That conclusion is true and useless. The verse specifies the audience because it knows the material will be misread by everyone else, and because the misreading is not an intellectual error but a failure of seriousness about consequences.

**The Narrative Block Now Shortens**

Twelve verses have been given to Abraham's household and the destruction that followed it. The three cases that come next are compressed: Moses in three verses (51:38–40), ʿĀd in two (51:41–42), Thamūd in three (51:43–45), and Noah in one (51:46). The chapter has established its method in detail and now runs it at speed. Each case will follow the same shape — a messenger sent, a warning refused, a punishment executed by a specific instrument — and the reader is expected to supply the rest from the first telling. Verse 37 closes the long version of the pattern; what follows are its summaries.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:38

> And in ˹the story of˺ Moses ˹was another lesson,˺ when We sent him to Pharaoh with compelling proof

**Expanded Commentary**

**Words Are Deliberately Left Out**

The Arabic reads simply "and in Moses" — a phrase with no verb and no predicate. The translation supplies what the pattern of the chapter requires: was another lesson, or was a sign. The same elliptical construction governs the three cases that follow: and in ʿĀd (51:41), and in Thamūd (51:43), and the people of Noah earlier (51:46). Once the first instance is established, the rest are listed by name alone, and the reader completes each one from the shared structure. The device is economical and it makes a point: these are not four separate stories but four specimens of one thing. Naming them without verbs treats them as items in a series already understood.

**Sulṭān Mubīn Is Authority That Cannot Be Argued With**

The phrase translated as compelling proof is *sulṭān mubīn* — a clear authority, an evident warrant. *Sulṭān* carries the sense of power with a right behind it, not power merely exercised. The commentators read it as the miracles given to Moses, since those provided a clear indication of the truth of his message, and some extend it to the authority God grants His messengers over whom He wills (59:6). Both readings describe the same thing from two sides: the visible signs and the commission behind them. Pharaoh's regime had force; Moses arrived with authority, and the chapter's word choice puts the two in competition before the narrative begins.

**The Proof Is Delivered Before Anything Else Happens**

The verse's order is the argument's order: sent, then given proof, then rejected, then seized. Nothing about the punishment appears before the evidence has been presented. The same sequence is stated as a rule elsewhere: *“And We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺.”* (17:15). In the account of Moses the proof is described in concrete terms — the great sign shown to Pharaoh (79:20) — and Moses' own message states its character: *“And do not be arrogant with Allah. I have certainly come to you with a compelling proof.”* (44:19). The identical phrase appears in both chapters, which fixes its technical sense: a warrant sufficient to oblige the hearer.

**Moses Is the Most Frequently Named Prophet in the Book**

His name appears 136 times in the Qur'an, more than any other prophet — Abraham is named 69 times, and the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ is named four times, though addressed constantly. The count is not trivia. It explains why the Qur'an can compress Moses' story to three verses here and still expect the audience to follow: they already know the case in detail from longer accounts in 20:9–97, 26:10–66, 28:3–46, and 7:103–155. Adh-Dhāriyāt is quoting a story its hearers have heard many times, and selecting from it only the elements the chapter's argument needs — the proof, the refusal, the seizure. Compression of this kind is possible only where the material is already common property.

**In a Meccan Chapter the Story Is an Argument, Not a History**

The commentators note that in the Meccan sūrahs which feature Moses — here, in 40:23–45, 43:46–56, 44:17–31, and 79:15–25 — the account functions as a spiritual example reflecting aspects of the Prophet Muhammad's own challenge rather than as narrative history. The point is confirmed by the chapter's placement. Moses faces a ruler who cannot answer his proofs and so insults him instead; the Prophet ﷺ faces a commercial elite that cannot answer his recitation and so calls him a magician or a madman (51:52). The parallel is not incidental. It is the reason this story is told here, in this order, in this length.

**The Sending Includes an Instruction About Tone**

The mission to Pharaoh is described elsewhere with a detail that belongs to the same verse's logic. Two men are sent to a tyrant, and they are told: *“Go, both of you, to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺. Speak to him gently, so perhaps he may be mindful ˹of Me˺ or fearful ˹of My punishment˺.”* (20:43–44). Gentleness is commanded toward the worst case in the Qur'an's gallery of tyrants, and the purpose is stated as a hope rather than a demand. Verse 38's compelling proof is therefore delivered by a messenger instructed not to provoke. The refusal that follows comes from a hearer who was addressed with both evidence and restraint.

**Pharaoh's Position Is Stated Before His Response**

The transgression is named in the sending itself. He has exceeded all bounds (20:43; 79:17), and elsewhere he is called a tyrant and a transgressor (44:31). The chapter gives the reader the diagnosis before the quotation of his words, so that his response can be evaluated rather than merely recorded. This is a deliberate arrangement. A reader who hears "a magician or a madman" without knowing the speaker's condition might treat it as an argument; hearing it after being told that the speaker has transgressed all bounds and has been shown a clear warrant identifies it correctly as evasion. Verse 38 does that preparatory work in a single clause.

**The Next Verse Records the Refusal**

Everything in this verse is set up for a reply that will not engage with the proof at all. Moses brings authority; Pharaoh brings an accusation. The contrast is the chapter's point about the mockers of verse 12 and the guessers of verse 10: a person who cannot answer an argument will describe the arguer instead. The three verses devoted to Moses are the shortest of the four cases in this block, and the shortest is enough, because the pattern has already been established in detail with Lot's people. What the chapter needs from Moses is one thing: a clear proof, clearly refused, and the consequence that followed.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:39

> but Pharaoh was carried away by his power, saying ˹of Moses˺, “A magician or a madman!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rukn Is the Support a Man Leans On**

The word translated as power is *rukn*, whose literal sense is a corner, a pillar, the part of a structure that bears weight. Applied to a person it means his support: his strength, his tribe, his army, the people and resources he relies on. The commentators give the range explicitly — might and strength, his people, his companions, the hosts around him — and explain it through the verb *yurkinu ʿalayhim*, he leans on them. The image is architectural and it is exact. Pharaoh's position is a structure, and the structure rests on identifiable supports: an army, an administration, a priesthood of magicians, a river-fed economy. The verse says he turned away relying on that structure rather than answering the proof presented to it.

**Tawallā: He Turned His Back**

The verb describes turning away, withdrawing, giving one's back. Another chapter narrates the same movement and adds what followed it: *“Then Moses showed him the great sign, but he denied and disobeyed ˹Allah˺, then turned his back, striving ˹against the truth˺.”* (79:20–22). Three acts in sequence — denial, disobedience, turning — and then active effort against the truth. Verse 39 compresses the whole movement into one word and attaches the support he turned with. The physical metaphor is consistent throughout: a man who has been shown something and rotates away from it, taking his whole apparatus with him. Nothing in the description suggests he examined the proof and found it wanting.

**Two Accusations That Cannot Both Be True**

A magician has skill, training, technique, and intention; a madman has none of these. The two descriptions exclude each other, and the verse offers them joined by "or," which is the mark of a person selecting from available insults rather than stating a finding. This is precisely the condition the chapter diagnosed at its start: *“Surely you are ˹lost˺ in conflicting views ˹regarding the truth˺.”* (51:8). Pharaoh's response is the same contradiction in a single mouth. The Qur'an notes the repetition of the charge across history in the verse that generalises it: *“Similarly, no messenger came to those before them without being told: ‘A magician or a madman!’”* (51:52). The pairing is a formula, and a formula is not an argument.

**The Same Charge Is Recorded Against Moses Everywhere**

The accusation appears at every stage of the confrontation. The chiefs of Pharaoh's people say it first: *“He is indeed a skilled magician”* (7:109). Pharaoh repeats it to his court: *“He is indeed a skilled magician”* (26:34). It is used of the message itself in another encounter: *“But they responded: ‘Magician! Total liar!’”* (40:24). The consistency across accounts shows that the charge was the standard response of an established authority to a messenger it could not refute. The Quraysh used the same vocabulary of the Prophet ﷺ — magician, poet, madman, a man taught by another — and the chapter treats the repetition as evidence about the speakers rather than about the messengers.

**Turning to His Court Implies He Had No Answer**

The commentators draw a sharp inference from the verse: that Pharaoh must turn to his support implies he has no response to Moses except derision, since he cannot argue against the proofs brought to him. The logic is worth stating plainly. A man with an answer gives it. A man without one changes the subject, and the subject change here is the character of the speaker rather than the content of the message. Ad hominem description is the last resort of a position that has run out of material. The verse records the resort, and in recording it, records the exhaustion that preceded it.

**His Boast Is Recorded Elsewhere in the Same Terms**

The content of Pharaoh's confidence is given in another chapter, where he addresses his people from a position of strength: *“O my people! Am I not sovereign over Egypt as well as ˹all˺ these streams flowing at my feet? Can you not see? Am I not better than this nobody who can hardly express himself?”* (43:51–52). Kingdom, rivers, eloquence, and social standing are set against a man with no army and a speech impediment. That is the *rukn* of verse 39 itemised. Note that the boast ends with an appeal to sight — can you not see — which is the same appeal this chapter makes at 51:21, in the opposite direction.

**The Claim of Lordship Follows the Same Movement**

The turning described here ends in an explicit claim. Another account narrates the escalation: he summoned his people and called out, *"I am your lord, the most high!"* and God overtook him, making him an example in this life and the next (79:23–25). The path runs from a proof shown, to a denial, to a turning away with one's support, to a public claim of divinity, to seizure. Verse 39 sits at the third stage, and it is the stage at which the outcome is effectively decided. A man who has turned his back on a clear warrant while leaning on his army has no further move available except to enlarge himself, and enlargement is what the next stage records.

**The Insult Is Answered by the Sea**

The verse ends on a quotation and the following verse ends on an event. Nothing argues with the accusation; the accusation is simply overtaken. The man who called Moses a magician or a madman is seized with his soldiers and thrown into the water (51:40), and the *rukn* he leaned on drowns with him. The structure of the reply is the chapter's method throughout: no refutation of insults, only the arrival of the consequence. A reader who wants to know how the Qur'an answers a man who calls a messenger mad should look at what happens to him in the next verse, and at what is left of his army afterwards.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:40

> So We seized him and his soldiers, casting them into the sea while he was blameworthy

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Verb Covers the Whole Event**

The verb is *akhadhnā*, we seized — a single act with no battle, no campaign, no sequence of engagements. Pharaoh and his hosts are taken together, and the taking is followed immediately by the throwing. The Qur'an's accounts of the drowning preserve this compression: *“So We inspired Moses: ‘Strike the sea with your staff,’ and the sea was split, each part was like a huge mountain. We drew the pursuers to that place, and delivered Moses and those with him all together. Then We drowned the others.”* (26:63–66). Three verbs cover the entire event, and the last one has no object of resistance. Verse 40 states the same thing in even shorter form.

**Nabadhnāhum: Thrown, Not Merely Submerged**

The second verb is one of casting or tossing something away, the way a person discards an object. The army that pursued Israel is described as thrown into the sea, and the choice of verb carries contempt as well as physical description. Something cast is not laid down carefully. The same event is narrated elsewhere with the sea closing over the pursuers: *“And leave the sea parted, for they are certainly an army bound to drown.”* (44:24). The command to leave the sea as it is removes any possibility of rescue, and the description of the army as bound to drown states the outcome before it occurs. Verse 40's two verbs — seized, thrown — match that determination.

**Mulīm: A Man Who Has Earned Blame**

The closing description is *mulīm*, one who is blameworthy, deserving of reproach. The word is used elsewhere in a strikingly different case: *“Then the whale engulfed him while he was blameworthy.”* (37:142), of Jonah in the belly of the fish. The shared term connects two men who both went into water under blame and were treated differently, and it fixes the sense of the word here. Pharaoh's blame is stated as a condition he has brought on himself, not as a charge laid against him by an opponent. He transgressed all bounds (79:17), turned away relying on his power (51:39), and claimed lordship (79:24). The drowning is described with the blame already attached, as its explanation.

**The Sea He Pursued Them Into Became His Grave**

The instrument of the punishment is the route of the pursuit. Pharaoh's army followed the Israelites onto a divided seabed, and the same water closed on the pursuers: *“And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you, and drowned Pharaoh’s people before your very eyes.”* (2:50). The detail that the drowning happened in full view of the rescued is repeated in several accounts and is essential to the scene. The deliverance and the destruction are simultaneous and adjacent, so that neither can be explained as a natural event that happened to favour one group. The witnesses are the people who had just walked through the same water.

**His Profession of Faith Arrived Too Late**

The Qur'an records Pharaoh's final words and their rejection: *“But as Pharaoh was drowning, he cried out, ‘I believe that there is no god except that in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am ˹now˺ one of those who submit.’ ˹He was told,˺ ‘Now ˹you believe˺? But you always disobeyed and were one of the corruptors.’”* (10:90–91). The belief is genuine in content and worthless in timing. The reply names the reason: a lifetime of disobedience and corruption precedes it. This is the same principle the chapter has been illustrating since verse 9, where the turned-away are described as those whose direction was fixed before the evidence arrived. Verse 40's blameworthy is the summary of that fixed direction.

**The Corpse Was Preserved as an Exhibit**

The event did not end with the drowning. *“Today We will preserve your corpse so that you may become an example for those who come after you. And surely most people are heedless of Our examples!”* (10:92). The preservation is stated as an act with a purpose, and the purpose is the same one described in verse 37 for the ruins of Lot's city: a sign left for later people. The closing complaint — most people are heedless of Our examples — connects this verse to the heedlessness of 51:11. Two exhibits are described in this chapter's narrative block, one a ruined city on a road and one a body kept from decay, and both address the same failure to look.

**He Was Made an Example in Two Worlds**

Another chapter states the scope of the outcome: *“So Allah overtook him, making him an example in this life and the next. Surely in this is a lesson for whoever stands in awe of ˹Allah˺.”* (79:25–26). The exemplary function is not limited to the historical event. Pharaoh becomes a permanent reference point for the chapter's audience and for every later one, and the audience is again specified — whoever stands in awe. The qualification matches verse 37's "those who fear the painful punishment." Both verses attach their evidence to a disposition, which is the chapter's consistent method: the material is public, the benefit is conditional.

**The Seizure Is a General Law, Not a Special Case**

The Qur'an generalises the event in a verse that reads as a rule: *“Such is the ˹crushing˺ grip of your Lord when He seizes the societies entrenched in wrongdoing. Indeed, His grip is ˹terribly˺ painful and severe.”* (11:102). Pharaoh's case is presented as an instance of a standing practice, and the practice is described with the same verb used in verse 40. This is what makes the narrative block useful to the chapter's argument. The mockers of verse 12 asked when the day would come; the four cases that follow show a grip that has been applied repeatedly, to societies that believed themselves permanent. Verse 40 closes the second of those cases, and the next verse opens the third with a wind.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:41

> And in ˹the story of˺ ’Âd ˹was another lesson,˺ when We sent against them the devastating wind

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Series Continues With Three Words**

The construction is identical to the Moses verse: "and in ʿĀd" — no verb, no predicate, the reader supplying the rest from the pattern already established. The chapter is now running its four cases at speed: Lot's people in twelve verses, Moses in three, ʿĀd in two, Thamūd in three, Noah in one. The compression is possible because the audience knows these stories. What the chapter needs from each is not the narrative but the precedent — a warning delivered, a warning refused, a punishment executed by a named instrument. Naming ʿĀd without a verb treats the case as a known item in a list rather than as a story requiring introduction.

**ʿĀd Were an Arabian Tribe of Builders**

The people in question were a pre-Islamic Arabian tribe, not a distant nation, and the Qur'an describes them with admiration for their physical achievement: *“Did you not see how your Lord dealt with ’Ȃd— ˹the people˺ of Iram—with ˹their˺ great stature, unmatched in any other land”* (89:6–8). Their own boast is recorded: *“As for ’Ȃd, they acted arrogantly throughout the land with no right, boasting, ‘Who is superior to us in might?’”* (41:15). The verse that follows supplies the answer to the boast — did they not see that the One who created them was far superior in might. Strength, architecture, and stature are the specific qualities the case turns on, and the wind that destroys them is an opponent none of those qualities can resist.

**Rīḥ ʿAqīm Is a Wind That Gives Nothing**

The adjective is *ʿaqīm*, barren. The commentators explain it precisely: a wind carrying no rain and pollinating no trees, unlike the winds this chapter swore by at its opening (51:1). The same word described Sarah's condition nine verses earlier (51:29), where it meant a womb that produces nothing. Applied to weather, it names a force stripped of every benefit winds normally bring — no fertilising, no driving of clouds, no filling of sails. What remains is pure motion with destructive capacity. The contrast with the first oath of the chapter is deliberate and exact: the same element, in the same chapter, functioning as a life-support and then as an executioner.

**Elsewhere the Wind Is Described as Furious and Howling**

The Qur'an gives this wind several descriptions, and they accumulate. It is a furious wind sent for several miserable days to make them taste a humiliating punishment in this life (41:16); a furious wind on a day of unrelenting misery (54:19); and, most emphatically, a furious, bitter wind unleashed on them non-stop (69:6–7). The addition of duration matters: seven nights and eight days. The wind is not a gust or a storm but a sustained operation with a beginning and an end, which fits the chapter's theme of decrees executed on schedule. What began as a boast about might ends as a counting of days.

**Hūd Was Their Messenger**

The warning came from one of their own: *“And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd. He said, ‘O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him. You do nothing but fabricate lies ˹against Allah˺.’”* (11:50). The charge is the same one every messenger in this chapter brings, and the accusation of fabrication is answered by the destruction described in verse 41. The Qur'an summarises the outcome in a single verse that reads like a verdict: *“That was ’Âd. They denied the signs of their Lord, disobeyed His messengers, and followed the command of every stubborn tyrant.”* (11:59). Three items — denial, disobedience, and following tyrants — and then the wind.

**They Mistook the Wind for Rain**

The moment of arrival is narrated elsewhere and it is one of the sharpest scenes in the Qur'an. *“Then when they saw the torment as a ˹dense˺ cloud approaching their valleys, they said ˹happily˺, ‘This is a cloud bringing us rain.’ ˹But Hûd replied,˺ ‘No, it is what you sought to hasten: a ˹fierce˺ wind carrying a painful punishment!’”* (46:24). The people welcomed their destruction as weather, and the reply connects it to the hastening this chapter described in verse 14. What they had asked to be brought forward arrived, and they did not recognise it. That failure of recognition is the practical form of the heedlessness described in verse 11.

**The Duration Is Specified**

Seven nights and eight days, continuous, with the people left lying dead like trunks of uprooted palm trees (69:7). The specification serves the argument. A punishment with a stated length is an administered punishment, not a natural catastrophe. It has a beginning, a middle, and an end, and it stops when it has finished. The image of the bodies compared to hollow palm trunks completes the reversal of their boast about stature: the tallest people in the region, described after the event as fallen timber. The wind took eight days to do what their architecture could not prevent in a single hour.

**The Case Is Addressed to the Audience's Own Landscape**

ʿĀd were Arabs, in Arabia, with remains the Meccans could visit. The Qur'an says so directly: *“And the people of ’Ȃd and Thamûd ˹met a similar fate˺, which must be clear to you ˹Meccans˺ from their ruins.”* (29:38). Verse 41 is therefore not a distant legend but a regional precedent, and its placement immediately after the account of Lot's city — whose ruins lie along a known route (15:76) — makes the geography of the argument explicit. The chapter's audience travels through the evidence. The sign left for those who fear the painful punishment (51:37) is now multiplied: a second case, a second instrument, a second people who boasted and were emptied.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:42

> There was nothing it came upon that it did not reduce to ashes

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Double Negative That Leaves No Exception**

The sentence is constructed to exclude remainder. Nothing that the wind came upon escaped being reduced; the negation covers the thing and the result in one clause. Arabic achieves this with a structure that admits no partial case — not "much of what it touched" and not "most of them," but nothing. The verse refuses to leave room for survivors, for intact buildings, or for a district that was spared by accident. That absoluteness is the point. A punishment described with exceptions would be a disaster; a punishment described without them is an execution. The chapter has been arguing for the second description since the marking of the stones in verse 34.

**Ramīm Is What Old Bone and Chaff Become**

The word translated as ashes is *ramīm*, from a root meaning to decay, to crumble, to become rotten and dry. It is used of bones that have disintegrated and of plant matter reduced to fragments — the same word that appears in the objection about resurrection: *“And they argue with Us— forgetting they were created—saying, ‘Who will give life to decayed bones?’”* (36:78). The choice is loaded. The wind did not merely kill; it reduced its victims to the condition of dry, crumbling matter, which is precisely the state the mockers said could not be reversed. Verse 42 therefore contains an implicit answer to the question posed in verse 12: the same power that reduces a people to *ramīm* is the power that will reassemble them.

**Everything the Wind Passed Over Was Included**

The commentators state the scope plainly: the wind laid waste to the people and to the land they lived on. Nothing is exempted by category. Houses, crops, livestock, and bodies are all inside the phrase "whatever it came upon." Another account describes the result from the outside: *“It destroyed everything by the command of its Lord, leaving nothing visible except their ruins.”* (46:25). The clause about command is important, since it restores agency to an event that otherwise looks like weather. What passes over a valley and leaves nothing visible is not a storm in the ordinary sense; it is a stated operation carried out by a stated authority.

**The Bodies Are Compared to Fallen Palms**

The image given elsewhere is specific and unflattering: the people lay dead like trunks of uprooted palm trees (69:7), and a wind snatched people up, leaving them like trunks of uprooted palms (54:20). Palms are the tallest and most useful plants of that landscape, and the comparison reduces a boasting people to hollow timber lying in rows. The adjective "uprooted" matters, because it describes something removed from the ground that held it. ʿĀd's boast was about might and stature (41:15); the wind answered by treating their bodies as vegetation that had been pulled up. Nothing in the two verses leaves room for the interpretation that this was a tragedy that happened to them.

**The Wind Works as a Broom, Not a Blade**

A sword cuts and leaves a body; this wind leaves nothing visible except ruins (46:25). The mode of destruction is erasure rather than slaughter, and erasure is what makes the case useful to the chapter's argument. A battlefield can be explained by human agency and human error. A valley swept clean over eight days cannot. The Qur'an's question after the event is blunt: *“Do you see any of them left alive?”* (69:8). The expected answer is no, and the question is addressed to a reader standing in a region where the answer can be checked against the ground.

**The Same Element in Two Opposite Roles**

Verse 42 completes a contrast the chapter opened. The winds sworn by in verse 1 scatter dust, drive clouds, fertilise crops, and fill sails; the wind sent on ʿĀd scatters people and leaves nothing standing. The element is identical and the outcome is opposite, which is exactly what an oath by that element is meant to establish — that the wind has no independent character of its own. It does what it is sent to do. The fertile wind and the barren wind (51:41) are one instrument used twice, and the user is the same in both cases. This is the chapter's shortest and strongest argument against reading nature as self-governing.

**Total, and Then Reported**

The verse's finality has a consequence for how the story functions. Because nothing was left, the only remaining evidence is the report and the ruins. The Qur'an relies on both: it narrates the event and it points at the site (29:38). A reader who wants to verify the case cannot examine survivors, since there are none; he can only examine the ground and the account. That is the same position the chapter's audience occupies with respect to the resurrection — an event nobody has witnessed, supported by a report and by evidence in the created order. Verse 42 makes the two situations structurally identical, which is why it appears where it does.

**The Next Case Is Shorter Still**

Two verses have covered ʿĀd: the wind sent, and the fact that nothing survived it. The chapter now moves to Thamūd in three verses, and the compression increases again. The pattern is fixed and the reader is expected to supply the middle — the messenger, the sign, the rejection — from the earlier accounts. What each case contributes is the instrument and the outcome: stones for Lot's city, the sea for Pharaoh, a barren wind for ʿĀd, and next a blast for Thamūd. Four instruments, one administration, and in every case a warning that preceded it. The negation leaves no remainder. Nothing the wind encountered escaped it, and nothing survived in a condition fit for use. A single verse carries the whole of that people's end, and the disproportion between the eight verses spent on the earlier case and the one spent here belongs to the telling rather than to the event.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:43

> And in ˹the story of˺ Thamûd ˹was another lesson,˺ when they were told, “Enjoy yourselves ˹only˺ for a ˹short˺ while.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Series Reaches Its Third Case**

The same elliptical construction appears again: "and in Thamūd." No verb is supplied, and the reader completes it from the two preceding cases. What distinguishes this instance is that the verse quotes a sentence rather than describing an instrument. Lot's people received stones, ʿĀd received wind, and Thamūd received an instruction — enjoy yourselves for a while. The shift is significant. An instrument describes what was done to a people; an instruction describes what was said to them. The chapter includes both because its argument needs both: the punishment and the warning that preceded it, in the same verse.

**Thamūd Were Carvers in Stone**

The people are identified by their construction. Their messenger asks them whether they will be left as they are, secure among what they have been given — gardens and springs, crops and palm trees, and homes carved in the mountains with great skill (26:147–149). Another account adds the succession: *“Remember when He made you successors after ’Âd and established you in the land—˹and˺ you built palaces on its plains and carved homes into mountains.”* (7:74). Their security was architectural. They lived inside rock, in a region whose remains are still standing, which is why the destruction described in the next two verses has to be of a kind that reaches inside a mountain.

**Three Days Were Named**

The "little while" has a stated length elsewhere: *“But they killed her, so he warned ˹them˺, ‘You have ˹only˺ three ˹more˺ days to enjoy life in your homes— this is an unfailing promise!’”* (11:65). A term, a location, and a guarantee that the term will not fail. The precision is the reason this verse can be so short. Nothing needs to be explained about timing, because the timing was published to the people concerned in advance. The chapter has been arguing since verse 12 that the mockers demand dates; here is a case where a date was given, announced by a messenger, and kept to the day.

**"Enjoy" Is Used Ironically Here**

The Qur'an uses the command to enjoy in two registers. In the first it is genuine, describing provision given for use — homes, tents, furnishings made from animal hides and wool for a while (16:80), or enjoyment allowed by mercy for a period (36:44; 37:148). In the second it is ironic, and the irony is marked by the shortness of the period and the guilt of the people: *“Eat and enjoy yourselves for a little while, ˹for˹ you are truly wicked.”* (77:46). Verse 43 belongs to the second register. The commentators note the irony explicitly, and connect it to the Qur'an's standing description of worldly life as nothing but the delusion of enjoyment (3:185; 57:20). A permission to enjoy for three days is not a gift; it is a countdown stated politely.

**The Sign They Were Given Was a Camel**

The case against Thamūd begins with a specific test. Ṣāliḥ presents it: *“O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is Allah’s she-camel as a sign to you. So leave her to graze ˹freely˺ on Allah’s land and do not harm her, or else you will be overcome by a painful punishment.”* (7:73). The instruction is minimal — let her graze, do not harm her — and the warning is attached in the same sentence. Another chapter adds the detail of a shared water supply, with the messenger warning them not to disturb the camel or her turn to drink (91:13). The test was easy to pass and impossible to misunderstand.

**They Killed Her After a Community Decision**

The crime is described as collective and organised. Nine men in the city spread corruption and never did right (27:48); they planned, and God planned, while they were unaware (27:50). Another account compresses it further: *“But they roused a companion of theirs, so he dared to kill ˹her˺.”* (54:29). The rousing matters — this was not one man's impulse but a group's decision, and the man who struck was pushed forward by others. The Qur'an's verdict follows immediately: *“Still they defied him and slaughtered her. So their Lord crushed them for their crime, levelling all to the ground.”* (91:14). All of them, levelled, for a crime most of them did not personally commit but all of them arranged.

**A Respite Given Is Not a Respite Withheld**

The three days are the chapter's clearest instance of a pattern it names elsewhere: delay that looks like generosity. The people had asked for the punishment to be produced, and what they received was a period of enjoyment followed by its arrival. The Qur'an describes the general mechanism bluntly — when people become oblivious to warnings and are then showered with what they desire, the seizure comes by surprise (6:44). Thamūd's three days are that mechanism in miniature, with the difference that the term was announced. They knew exactly how much time they had. That knowledge removes the last possible complaint, which is why the next verse describes their response as insolent defiance rather than as ignorance.

**The Verse States the Warning; the Next States the Defiance**

Verse 43 gives the permission and its limit; verse 44 gives the refusal and its consequence. The two verses are one argument split in half, and the split is deliberate. A reader who takes verse 43 alone has a people offered a short enjoyment; a reader who takes verse 44 alone has a people struck down. Together they produce the chapter's standard case: a warning delivered in words the recipients understood, a period of respite granted, a defiance persisted in, and an execution carried out on time. This is the structure the chapter has been building toward since its opening oath, and it is now shown four times in a row.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:44

> Still they persisted in defying the commands of their Lord, so they were overtaken by a ˹mighty˺ blast while they were looking on

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿAtaw Is Defiance With Attitude**

The verb describes more than disobedience. *ʿataw* carries insolence, arrogance, the refusal of someone who has been addressed and has decided to answer with contempt. The parallel account uses the same register: *“Then they killed the she-camel —defying their Lord’s command—and challenged ˹Ṣâliḥ˺, ‘Bring us what you threaten us with, if you are ˹truly˺ one of the messengers.’”* (7:77). Defiance and challenge are joined there, exactly as they are joined in this chapter's earlier description of mockers who asked when the day would come (51:12). The crime is not merely the killing of the camel. It is the killing plus the demand that the threatened punishment be produced, issued to the man who issued the threat.

**The Instrument Is Called by Several Names**

Thamūd's destruction is described with different words in different chapters, and the commentators reconcile them. A thunderbolt in one place (41:13); a mighty blast in another (11:67); one blast leaving them like the twigs of fence-builders (54:31); an earthquake striking them so they fell lifeless in their homes (7:78); and an overwhelming calamity, *aṭ-ṭāghiya* (69:5). These are not competing accounts but descriptions of one event from different angles — the sound, the force, the ground movement, and the total effect. The variety is itself informative: an event this large is described by whatever aspect the narrator is emphasising. What all the versions agree on is that it arrived at once and left nothing standing.

**They Watched It Arrive**

The clause "while they were looking on" is the verse's most disturbing detail. The punishment was not delivered in sleep or from behind. They saw it coming, and the three days of warning meant they knew what they were seeing. Another chapter describes a similar awareness in a different case: the people who saw the torment approaching as a cloud and called it rain (46:24). Thamūd had no such excuse, since their term had been published with a guarantee that it would not fail (11:65). Looking on is therefore not confusion but recognition. The verse records the moment a warning becomes a sight, and it is the moment the chapter has been describing since verse 13, where the day is defined as one of exposure to the fire.

**The Term Was Kept**

Three days were named, and the punishment came at the end of them. The Qur'an stresses the punctuality: *“But the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook them in the morning”* (15:83). The homes carved in mountains with a feeling of security (15:82) did not delay it by an hour. This precision is the answer to the mockers of verse 12, who demanded a date. Here is a date — given in advance, by a messenger, to the people it concerned, and met exactly. The chapter does not argue that the last day has a published date; it argues that published dates have been kept before, by the same authority, against people who doubted in the same way.

**Their Skill in Stone Did Not Reach the Blast**

Thamūd's identity was construction: palaces on the plains and homes carved into mountains (7:74; 26:149). They believed themselves secure because of it, and the Qur'an records that belief as part of the case (15:82). The blast reached them inside the rock. Their fell lifeless in their homes (11:67) — the location is specified, which is the sharpest form the reversal could take. The shelter they spent generations carving became the place where they were found. No fortification is mentioned as failing; none is needed, because the instrument did not have to enter through a wall.

**The Same Warning Is Turned on the Chapter's Audience**

The Qur'an applies this case forward explicitly: *“If they turn away, then say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘I warn you of a ˹mighty˺ blast, like the one that befell ’Ȃd and Thamûd.’”* (41:13). The two peoples named in verses 41 and 43 of this chapter are combined into a single warning addressed to people still alive. Verse 44 is therefore not only a report about the past. It is the content of a warning currently in force, and the audience hearing it is in the position Thamūd occupied during their three days. The difference is that no term has been published to them, which is the difference between a countdown and an interval.

**Defiance, Not the Camel, Is Named as the Cause**

The verse identifies the reason for the seizure as defiance of the commands of their Lord, in the plural. The camel's killing is the occasion; the defiance is the cause. That distinction matters for how the case functions as precedent. Nobody in the chapter's audience is being asked about a camel. They are being asked whether they are defying commands, and the verse tells them what defiance produced in a people more strongly built than they are. The plural "commands" widens the charge beyond the single prohibition about the animal, and it matches the summary given elsewhere: they denied the signs of their Lord and followed the command of every stubborn tyrant (11:59).

**Two Clauses, One Sequence**

The verse's grammar mirrors its content: they defied, so they were overtaken. The causal particle joins the two halves without elaboration, and no argument intervenes. This is the chapter's method throughout the narrative block — warning, refusal, consequence — stated in the smallest number of words possible. The absence of debate is the point. By this stage the case has been made four times, and each time the refusal is described in the same vocabulary: differing claims (51:8), guessing (51:10), heedlessness (51:11), mockery (51:12), insolence (51:44). The consequence follows the vocabulary rather than the argument, because there is no argument left to answer. Persistence is stated first and the blast second, with no third clause between them. The verse records no repeated warning, no granted reprieve, and no further sign; defiance and consequence are joined directly, which is the shortest narrative distance in any of the five cases.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:45

> Then they were not able to rise up, nor were they helped

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Negations in a Single Line**

The verse contains nothing but two denials. They could not rise, and they were not helped. No verb of punishment appears, no instrument is named, and no time is given — those were supplied in the previous verse. What remains is the condition of the punished, stated in the smallest possible form. The first negation concerns their own capacity: whatever strength the carvers of mountain homes possessed, it did not reach the level of standing up. The second concerns outside assistance: nobody came. Between the two, the verse describes total helplessness from both directions, internal and external, which is the Qur'an's standard description of the punished.


The parallel accounts describe the same posture. An overwhelming earthquake struck them and they fell lifeless in their homes (7:78); the mighty blast overtook the wrongdoers so they fell lifeless in their homes (11:67). The commentators explain the inability as a consequence of the force of the seizure — they could not flee, and they could not stand. Falling lifeless inside one's own house is the specific detail that makes the case concrete. These were people whose identity was upright construction: palaces on plains, homes cut into rock, a boast about might (41:15). The verse describes them as unable to get up from the floor.

**Nor Were They Helped**

The second negation is repeated across the Qur'an as a standing feature of divine punishment. ʿĀd are told they will taste a humiliating punishment in this life and a worse one in the next, and then: *“And they will not be helped.”* (41:16). The phrase closes the possibility of rescue by ally, army, or god. Thamūd had a city, nine prominent men, and a plan; none of it functioned as help. The Qur'an records the outcome of the planning in one verse: *“See then what the consequences of their plan were: We ˹utterly˺ destroyed them and their people all together.”* (27:51). The planners were destroyed with the people they planned for, which is the fullest possible answer to the hope of being helped.

**The Men They Relied On Fell With Them**

The nine who spread corruption and never did right (27:48) are the concrete form of Thamūd's *rukn* — the support a man leans on, the same word used of Pharaoh's power in verse 39. In both cases the support is named, relied upon, and then destroyed alongside the one relying on it. The pattern is consistent across the chapter's four cases: Pharaoh's soldiers thrown into the sea with him (51:40), Lot's city emptied of everyone but one house (51:36), ʿĀd left with nothing standing (51:42), and Thamūd's elite levelled with their people (27:51). The verse's second negation is not an abstraction. It has a body count attached.

**Neither Heaven Nor Earth Mourned Them**

The Qur'an states the emotional register of the event explicitly: *“Neither heaven nor earth wept over them, nor was their fate delayed.”* (44:29). The sentence belongs to Pharaoh's people but describes the general case, and its two halves match verse 45 exactly. No mourning corresponds to "they were not helped"; no delay corresponds to "they were not able to rise." A destruction unmourned and unpostponed is one that has exhausted its moral possibilities. The verse in front of us records the same fact from inside the event rather than from outside it: no capacity and no assistance, at the moment when both were needed.

**Help Did Arrive, for the Others**

The chapter has already shown what help looks like when it is given. The believers in Lot's city were brought out before the punishment (51:35); Ṣāliḥ and those who believed with him were saved by a mercy and spared the disgrace of that day (11:66). The assistance is always extraction, never protection in place. This is the reason verse 45's second negation is not merely descriptive. Help exists in the Qur'an's account of these events, and it is available to a defined group, and it takes a defined form: removal before the arrival. Thamūd's inability to be helped is therefore not a statement about the absence of help in the world. It is a statement about their exclusion from it.

**The Shortest of the Four Cases**

Two verses were given to ʿĀd, three to Thamūd, three to Moses, and twelve to Lot's people. Verse 45 is the shortest unit in the series and does the least narrative work, which is exactly why it is placed last. The case has been made; all that remains is the condition of the condemned. The chapter does not elaborate on suffering, does not describe the scene, and does not moralise. It states two impossibilities and moves on. The economy is a rhetorical choice: a reader is left with the image of people who cannot stand and are not helped, and is expected to do the rest of the thinking without assistance from the text.

**The Series Now Runs Backwards in Time**

Verse 46 will name a people destroyed before all of these. The four cases are not in chronological order, and the reversal at the end is deliberate. Having moved from Lot's city to Moses to ʿĀd to Thamūd, the chapter closes the block by reaching past all of them to Noah, the earliest of the destroyed peoples in the Qur'an's telling. The effect is to place the pattern outside any particular era. It is not a feature of one age or one region; it runs back to the first messenger and the first rejection. What follows then turns away from history entirely, to the sky and the earth themselves. What the four cases share, and what Noah's case adds, is that no generation is exempt and no generation is unprecedented. The audience of this chapter stands inside the same sequence, at a point that has not yet been fixed by an outcome.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:46

> And the people of Noah ˹had also been destroyed˺ earlier. They were truly a rebellious people

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Earlier" Reaches Past Every Other Case**

Two words do the work of this verse's placement: *min qabl*, before, earlier. The chapter has narrated four destructions and now states that a fifth preceded them all. Nothing is recounted — no ark, no flood, no duration — only the priority and the verdict. The compression is possible because the story is the most familiar one in the Qur'an, told at length in 11:25–48, 23:23–30, 26:105–121, and in an entire chapter bearing Noah's name. Verse 46 assumes all of it and uses only what the argument needs: that the pattern of warning, refusal, and destruction begins earlier than any case the audience might think of as ancient.

**Noah Is Named Last Because He Came First**

The ordering is not chronological and the reversal is meaningful. Had the chapter run forward in time it would have ended with Thamūd, a people whose ruins stood within reach of the audience. Instead it ends by reaching behind them, to the first of the destroyed peoples. The effect is to remove any suggestion that these are recent events with local causes. The pattern reaches to the beginning of prophetic history, which makes it a feature of how warnings are handled rather than an accident of one region's politics. The chapter's audience is being told that they are not witnessing a new phenomenon but repeating an old one.

**A Thousand Years Less Fifty**

The duration of Noah's mission is the only figure of its kind in the Qur'an: *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them for a thousand years, less fifty. Then the Flood overtook them, while they persisted in wrongdoing.”* (29:14). The precision of the odd number — not a round thousand, but a thousand minus fifty — marks the account as specific rather than proverbial. The figure matters for the argument of verse 46. Nine and a half centuries of warning preceded the destruction. Whatever complaint can be made about haste or insufficient evidence, it cannot be made about this case. The respite given to Noah's people is the longest in the Qur'an, and it ended the same way the others did.

**Fāsiqūn Names the Verdict**

The word translated as rebellious describes a people who have departed from what is right, transgressing a known limit. It is a moral and religious term rather than a legal one, and the Qur'an uses it as a summary verdict after the fact. Nothing else in the verse is said about them — no crime is itemised, no leader is named, no instrument is described. The single adjective carries the entire case. That economy is only possible because the audience knows what the word refers to in Noah's story: a refusal sustained across generations, an accusation of madness, and a request that the matter be settled (54:9–10).

**The Accusation Against Noah Was the Same One**

The parallel is exact and the chapter depends on it. *“Before them, the people of Noah denied ˹the truth˺ and rejected Our servant, calling ˹him˺ insane. And he was intimidated.”* (54:9). Insane — the same charge levelled at Moses in verse 39, the same charge repeated against every messenger in verse 52, and the same charge made against the Prophet ﷺ by his own contemporaries (68:51). Verse 46 therefore does more than close the historical block. It supplies the earliest instance of the accusation the chapter is about to generalise in the verses that follow. The slogan the Quraysh use is not new; it is inherited from the first people who used it.

**The Flood Came From Both Directions**

When the command arrived, the water did not come from one source. *“So We opened the gates of the sky with pouring rain, and caused the earth to burst with springs, so the waters met for a fate already set.”* (54:11–12). Sky and ground, with the meeting of the two described as a fate already determined. The same event is narrated with the command spoken to both elements: *“O earth! Swallow up your water. And O sky! Withhold ˹your rain˺.”* (11:44). The scale is total, and the coordination is the point — two sources answering one command at one appointed moment. Verse 46's brevity assumes all of this, since an audience that has heard the story needs only the name of the people.

**The Ark Was Left as a Sign**

The survival is as much part of the case as the destruction. *“But We delivered him and those in the Ark, making it a sign for all people.”* (29:15), and *“We certainly left this as a sign. So is there anyone who will be mindful?”* (54:15). The question is put to every generation, and it is the same question verse 37 asked about the ruins of Lot's city. Two exhibits now stand behind the chapter's argument — a ruined settlement on a trade route, and a vessel preserved as a remembered sign — and both are addressed to people capable of paying attention. The Qur'an adds that the ark was carried on planks and nails (54:13), a detail that keeps the sign physical rather than legendary.

**From History Back to Creation**

The narrative block ends here. Verses 24 to 46 have given five cases: Abraham's household, Lot's city, Pharaoh, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and now the people before them all. What follows returns to the sky and the earth — built with might, spread out, and populated with pairs (51:47–49). The movement is from what happened to what is. The chapter's method has been to argue first from nature, then from history, then back to nature, and finally to the duty that follows from both. Verse 46 is the hinge between the historical evidence and the closing instruction.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:47

> We built the universe with ˹great˺ might, and We are certainly expanding ˹it˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Built, Not Merely Made**

The verb is *banā*, to build, and it is the language of construction rather than of origination. A built thing has structure, joints, and a method; it is put together rather than simply caused. The same verb appears in the description of the sky as something raised: *“Which is harder to create: you or the sky? He built it”* (79:27). The addition of *bi-ayd*, with might or with hands, intensifies the construction — the word *ayd* means strength and power, and it is used elsewhere of a servant given strength by God. The verse therefore opens by asserting that the sky is a structure built with force, which is a claim about both its solidity and the effort implied in its making.

**The Sky Is Described as a Raised Structure Throughout**

Building is not a stray metaphor here. The Qur'an repeatedly treats the sky as an edifice: a well-protected canopy that people turn away from the signs of (21:32); something raised high, listed among the things to reflect on (88:18); seven heavens layered one above another with no imperfection visible in them (67:3). This chapter itself opened with an oath by the heaven in its *ḥubuk* — its ruled, woven design (51:7). Verse 47 states the same fact in the language of its making. The sky that was sworn by at the start is now described as built, and the two descriptions belong to one argument: a designed surface is the result of a builder.

**Mūsiʿūn Comes From a Root Meaning Width and Capacity**

The second clause is where the verse's interpretation is contested, and the contest is lexical. The word *mūsiʿūn* derives from *w-s-ʿ*, whose field covers width, spaciousness, abundance, and the capacity or means to do something. In ordinary Arabic *al-mūsiʿ* is a man of means, one able to spend. The Qur'an uses the root in both senses: the wide and the capable. Which sense governs here determines whether the verse speaks of an ongoing expansion or of God's capacity and generosity, and the classical commentators divide accordingly. The dispute is genuine and it is old, and it should not be settled by preference.

**Three Classical Readings, Each With Its Grounds**

Al-Ṭabarī explains *mūsiʿūn* as possessors of vastness and capacity — able to create the heaven and whatever else He wills — and supports it with the usage where *mūsiʿ* means the one with means. Ibn Zayd is reported as reading it simply as "We are capable," from *wusʿ*, capacity. Al-Ḥasan reads it as "expanders of provision by means of the rain," which connects the clause to the sustenance located in heaven eleven verses earlier (51:22). Each reading is linguistically available and each has a coherent fit with the context. Al-Zamakhsharī lists three senses — the one who encompasses, the one who provides, and the one who expands — which shows that the ambiguity was recognised early rather than manufactured later.

**The Modern Cosmological Reading, Weighed**

Since the twentieth century this verse has been widely presented as a reference to the observed expansion of the universe, and the reading is not baseless: the active participle *mūsiʿūn* can describe a continuing action, and expansion is one of the root's meanings. Three considerations weigh against treating that as the verse's meaning. The word for sky here is *as-samāʾ*, which in Qur'anic usage denotes the heaven or sky rather than a cosmos of galaxies. No object pronoun is attached to the participle — the text says "We are *mūsiʿūn*," not "We are expanding *it*" — so the expansion is not explicitly directed at the sky. And the classical commentators, working without modern cosmology, read capacity, provision, and vastness. The honest position is that the verse asserts God's power and generosity in the sky's construction, and that a cosmological reading is a modern inference rather than an established meaning.

**Why This Verse Follows Four Destructions**

The placement is argumentative. Verses 41 to 46 described winds that levelled cities, seas that swallowed armies, and floods that covered the earth — all of them acts within the created order, carried out by elements that also sustain life. Verse 47 responds by describing the order itself: a sky built with might and made vast. The instruments of punishment belong to a structure whose builder is the one who sent them. The sequence answers the unspoken question left by the destructions — whether a world capable of such things is hostile or merely ungoverned. It is neither. It is built, and its builder is the same agent in both operations.

**Vastness Is Also Generosity**

The reading that connects the clause to provision has real support in the chapter. Verse 22 located sustenance in heaven, and verse 58 will describe God as the Supreme Provider, the Possessor of Strength, the Firm. A sky built with might and made vast is a sky large enough to hold the water cycle, the seasons, the light, and the apportioned provision of every creature. On this reading the two halves of verse 47 are one statement: the structure is strong, and the structure is generous. Both are needed for the argument the chapter is making, since a powerful builder who provides nothing would not be the God the verses that follow describe.

**The Verse Pairs With the Next**

Verse 47 concerns the sky and verse 48 the earth, and the two form a matched pair: built with might, then spread out and smoothed. The pairing is the Qur'an's standard summary of the created order, and it appears in the same terms elsewhere — the earth made a place of settlement and the sky a canopy (2:22). Neither half is presented as evidence for a separate claim; together they describe one building project. The chapter then adds a third feature, the creation of all things in pairs (51:49), and moves from description to command. What is built, spread, and paired is not a museum. It is a place with an occupant who has a duty, and the duty is stated in verse 50.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:48

> As for the earth, We spread it out. How superbly did We smooth it out

**Expanded Commentary**

**Spread Out Describes a Surface Made Usable**

The verb is *madaḍa*, to stretch, to extend, to spread out — the same verb used in the description of the earth's preparation: *“As for the earth, He spread it out as well, bringing forth its water and pastures and setting the mountains firmly ˹upon it˺— all as ˹a means of˺ sustenance for you and your animals.”* (79:30–33). The spreading is not a claim about geometry; it is a claim about usability. A surface is spread so that something can be placed on it and moved across it. The Qur'an states the purpose in the same breath elsewhere: the earth spread out, with firm mountains and rivers placed on it, and fruits of every kind created in pairs (13:3). Spreading, stabilising, and producing are three parts of one preparation.

**Māhidūn Comes From the Word for a Cradle**

The commentators note that the word translated as those who smooth it out, *māhidūn*, derives from the root *m-h-d*, the same root as *mahd*, a cradle. The earth is likened to a cradle in two places: *“˹He is the One˺ Who has laid out the earth for you, and set in it pathways for you so that you may find your way.”* (43:10), and the parallel at 20:53. A cradle is prepared for someone who cannot prepare anything for himself. The metaphor is exact and it carries the verse's whole meaning: the earth is a made bed for a dependent creature. The plural form of the noun, in a first-person plural sentence, is the majestic plural — God referring to Himself — and it indicates both the wisdom behind the manner of the earth's making and the generosity of it.

**"How Superbly" Is an Exclamation, Not a Description**

The second sentence is a construction of praise in Arabic: how excellent are those who spread it. It does not add information; it evaluates. After four verses of destruction and one of construction, the chapter pauses to admire the ground. The exclamation is addressed to the listener rather than to God, who does not need to be told how well He worked. Its function is to redirect attention to something the audience walks on every day and has never evaluated. This is the same operation the chapter performed at verse 20, where signs on the earth were named for those with certainty. Here the signs are not enumerated; they are praised in one clause.

**Mountains Are Placed as Weights**

The spreading is accompanied by stabilisation, and the Qur'an describes the mechanism in functional terms: *“He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you, as well as rivers, and pathways so you may find your way.”* (16:15). The same statement appears in two other chapters — mountains placed so the earth does not shake with its inhabitants, and broad pathways made so they may find their way (21:31), and towering firm mountains with fresh water to drink (77:27). The reason given is always the inhabitant's benefit rather than the mountain's grandeur. Verse 48's spreading assumes this stabilisation: a surface spread out for use is a surface that holds still while it is used.

**Pathways Are Part of the Preparation**

Two of the verses quoted above mention roads, and the mention is significant in a chapter addressed to traders. Pathways are not a human addition to a finished earth; they are described as made in it, set there so that people may find their way. The Meccan audience lived by routes — to Syria, to Yemen, past the ruins mentioned in verse 37 and past the remains of Thamūd. The verse tells them that the ground under those routes was prepared with their movement in mind. A world built for travel is a world built for a traveller who has somewhere to go, and the chapter's closing verses supply the destination.

**A Cradle Implies a Child Who Cannot Provide for Itself**

The metaphor chosen for the earth is the one item of furniture made entirely for a creature with no capacity. That fits the verses immediately before and after. Verse 22 located provision in heaven; verse 56 will state that human beings were not created to supply God with anything; verse 57 will say He does not want provision from them or feeding by them. The earth described as a cradle belongs to that sequence. It is a prepared place for a dependent occupant, made comfortable before the occupant could contribute anything to it. The exclamation "how superbly" is therefore praise of an arrangement whose entire benefit runs in one direction.

**Sky and Earth Described as One Project**

Verses 47 and 48 form a single statement in two halves, and the Qur'an presents them that way elsewhere: *“˹He is the One˺ Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you.”* (2:22). Settlement below, canopy above, provision moving between the two. That is also the structure of this chapter's opening oaths — winds scattering, clouds loaded, ships gliding, angels distributing (51:1–4). The same system is described twice, once as a series of agents and once as a building project. Nothing in either description is incidental to the other.

**The Next Verse Adds a Third Feature**

The sky is built, the earth is spread, and now the chapter names a property of everything in both: all things are created in pairs (51:49). The movement from structure to population is the last step before the command. A reader who has followed the three verses has been shown a building, a floor prepared for use, and a contents list — and the list ends with a purpose clause: so that perhaps you would be mindful. What follows is the instruction that the whole sequence has been preparing, in two words: flee to God.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:49

> And We created pairs of all things so perhaps you would be mindful

**Expanded Commentary**

**Pairing Is Stated as a Universal Feature**

The verse makes a claim about everything rather than about a category. *Azwāj* — pairs, kinds, mates — is applied to all things, and the Qur'an states the same claim with its domains listed: *“Glory be to the One Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs —˹be it˺ what the earth produces, their genders, or what they do not know!”* (36:36). Three fields are named there: the produce of the earth, the sexes of living beings, and a third category left undefined. Verse 49 makes the same claim in shorter form and attaches a purpose to it. The pairing is not described as an interesting fact; it is described as a fact with an intended effect on the person noticing it.

**Two Readings of What the Pairs Are**

The commentators offer a literal and a general reading, and both are supported by the text. The literal reading takes pairs as male and female in animals, which is the obvious case and the one 36:36 names explicitly. The general reading takes pairs as opposites throughout creation — night and day, heaven and earth, distress and happiness — so that everything created exists in a relation to something contrasting with it. The second reading is wider and it fits the verse's universal claim better, since not everything created is an animal. The two readings are compatible: sexuality is one instance of a broader principle that nothing in the created order stands alone.

**The Same Statement Is Joined to Human Use**

Another verse pairs the claim with an immediate practical benefit: *“And ˹He is the One˺ Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs, and made for you ships and animals to ride”* (43:12). The joining is instructive. Pairing is stated and then two means of transport are named, both of which depend on paired elements — a hull and a load, a beast and a rider. The same connection appears in the description of human origins: created from dust, then developed from a sperm-drop, then made into pairs, with no female conceiving or delivering except by God's knowledge (35:11). Pairing runs through reproduction, agriculture, and travel, and it is stated in each case as an act rather than as a property of matter.

**Everything Is Paired Except the One Who Made the Pairs**

The claim has an immediate theological consequence, and the Qur'an draws it. If everything created exists in pairs, then whatever is unpaired is not created. The verse that follows the description of God's self-subsistence makes the point absolute: *“Everything is bound to perish except He Himself.”* (28:88). The uniqueness of God is established not by argument from design alone but by the asymmetry between paired creation and an unpaired Maker. This is why the chapter places the statement about pairs immediately before the command to flee to God: the inventory of creation ends with an item that has no counterpart, and the reader is directed to Him.

**"So Perhaps You Would Be Mindful" Names the Intended Effect**

The purpose clause uses *tadhakkur*, recollection or calling to mind — not the acquisition of new information but the recovery of something already known. The Qur'an treats knowledge of its Maker as something human beings possess and then lose sight of, which is why its signs are described as reminders. The effect aimed at here is attention: a person who notices that nothing in creation stands alone notices the one exception, and the noticing is the remembering. The verse does not promise that the reminder will work on everyone. It states the purpose of the arrangement, and the chapter has already explained why the arrangement fails on some people (51:9).

**Pairs Are an Argument Against Rival Gods**

The Qur'an uses the structure of creation to argue against plurality in the divine. *“Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted.”* (21:22). The argument is that a paired, coordinated order — night answering day, rain answering seed, sea answering wind — requires a single ordering will. Competition among governors produces conflict, and conflict in a system this interlocked would produce disorder. A second verse makes the same point about challenge: had there been other gods, they would have sought a way to contest the Lord of the Throne (17:42). Verse 49 supplies the premise both arguments need: everything is paired, and the pairing is coordinated.

**The Third Category Is Left Open**

"What they do not know" in 36:36 is the most striking element of the pairing claim, because it makes the statement unfalsifiable in the useful sense. Whatever is discovered later — in plants, in matter, in the behaviour of the sky — falls inside a category the verse has already reserved. The commentators treat this as an acknowledgment that the inventory of creation exceeds human knowledge, which is precisely why the verse asks for mindfulness rather than for expertise. A reader is not required to catalogue the pairs. He is required to accept that the catalogue is longer than his knowledge and that the one who made it is not paired with anything.

**From Inventory to Instruction**

Three verses have now described the created order: a sky built with might, an earth spread and smoothed, and everything in it made in pairs. The description ends without argument and the next verse is a command — flee to God (51:50). The movement is the chapter's whole method in miniature. Evidence is presented in terms anyone can check, and then a single instruction follows, addressed to the person who has checked it. Nothing bridges the two except the reader's own recognition. The chapter does not say "therefore"; it says "so flee," and leaves the inference where it belongs.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:50

> So ˹proclaim, O Prophet˺: “Flee to Allah! I am truly sent by Him with a clear warning to you

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fleeing Toward Rather Than Away**

The command is *firrū ilā Allāh* — flee to God. Fleeing normally takes a person away from a danger and toward a refuge, and the verse compresses both movements into one preposition. The danger is not named, which is why the commentators supply several: punishment, Satan, ignorance, one's own sins. What is named is the direction. The command is unusual in the Qur'an's vocabulary because it uses the language of panic for an act of approach, and it does so in the plural, addressed to everyone hearing it. A person who has understood verses 41 to 49 — cities levelled, armies drowned, everything created paired and dependent — has grounds for the panic and a stated place to run to.

**Ibn ʿAbbās: Flee From Sins to God Through Repentance**

The most specific reading comes from Ibn ʿAbbās, who takes the verse as an instruction to flee from sins and take shelter with God by repenting. On this reading the danger is the person's own record, and the refuge is the one who keeps it. The reading fits the chapter's structure well. Verses 15 to 19 described people whose nights ended with a request for forgiveness (51:18), and verse 50 generalises that practice into a command for everyone. Repentance is fleeing in the exact sense: a person leaves a position he cannot defend and runs toward the only party who can protect him from its consequences.

**Al-Ṭabarī and Others Give the Wider Range**

Three further readings are preserved. Al-Ṭabarī explains the verse as fleeing from God's punishment to His mercy, through belief in Him, following His command, and working in obedience. Another reading takes it as fleeing from obeying Satan to obeying God. A third takes it as fleeing from ignorance to knowledge. Al-Qushayrī adds the negative form: be cautious of everything other than God, because whoever flees to something other than Him gains nothing from it. The readings are not rivals; they describe the same movement at different levels — punishment and mercy, obedience and its opposite, ignorance and knowledge, and finally the rule that no created refuge functions as a refuge.

**Al-Qushayrī's Account of Why Fleeing Is Necessary**

The same commentator explains the ground of the command. A human being is always in one of two states: desiring something or dreading something, hoping or fearing, seeking benefit or repelling harm. Since the one who benefits and the one who harms is God, the flight must be to God. He then lists the pairs the servant must flee between — from ignorance to knowledge, from caprice to reverence, from doubt to certainty, from Satan to God, from actions that are a trial to actions that are sufficient. The list is a diagnosis rather than an exhortation. It says that every human state is already a movement toward something, and the command is about choosing the destination.

**The Prophet's Own Prayer Performs the Command**

The practice is recorded in the supplication he used in prostration and at the end of the night prayer: "O Allah, I seek refuge in Your pleasure from Your anger, and in Your forgiveness from Your punishment, and I seek refuge in You from You. I cannot count Your praise; You are as You have praised Yourself." The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim and in Sunan an-Nasāʾī 5534. The central phrase — I seek refuge in You from You — is the command of verse 50 in its most concentrated form. There is no refuge from God except God, which is why the flight is toward Him rather than away from Him. The prayer is the practical instruction attached to the verse.

**Why the Command Follows the Verse on Pairs**

The placement is not arbitrary. Verse 49 said everything is created in pairs; verse 50 says flee to God. The connection, noted by the commentators, is that nothing paired can serve as a final refuge, because everything paired is also perishable: *“Everything is bound to perish except He Himself.”* (28:88). An inventory of creation that ends with the exception is followed naturally by an instruction to run to the exception. The reader who has just been told that his own self, his food, his sky, and his ground are all paired items in a created order has been told, in effect, that nothing around him is a candidate for trust. The command supplies the one candidate.

**"A Clear Warner From Him" Fixes the Speaker's Position**

The second half of the verse identifies the one delivering the command: a warner, clear, and from God. The preposition matters. He is not warning on his own account, and the warning is not his opinion about the future. The identical formula appears in Noah's commission: *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people ˹saying to him˺, ‘Warn your people before a painful punishment comes to them.’”*, and Noah's own words to them: *“Indeed, I am sent to you with a clear warning”* (11:25). The chapter thus closes its historical block and its creative block with the same office held by the same kind of man. The warning is clear — *mubīn*, evident, explained — which removes ambiguity as an excuse.

**The Command Is Plural and Urgent**

*Firru* is a plural imperative with no stated object of flight and no condition attached. Everyone is addressed, and no qualification is offered. The verse does not say "flee if you have sinned greatly" or "flee when the punishment approaches." It says flee, in a context where the previous twenty-five verses have described five destroyed peoples and a created order nobody controls. The urgency is the chapter's summary of its own argument. A person who has followed the evidence is in the position of someone standing in a valley where five cities have already fallen, and the instruction given to him is not to examine the ruins further but to move.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:51

> And do not set up another god with Allah. I am truly sent by Him with a clear warning to you.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Sentence Closes Both Verses**

The clause "I am truly sent by Him with a clear warning to you" appears at the end of verse 50 and again at the end of verse 51, word for word. Repetition of this kind in the Qur'an is emphatic rather than redundant, and here it brackets two commands — flee to God, and do not set up another god with Him — inside a single declaration of the messenger's office. The effect is to make the two commands inseparable from the identity of the one giving them. They are not advice, and they are not conclusions drawn from the preceding verses about creation. They are the content of a warning delivered by someone sent, and the repetition states that twice.

**Setting Up Another God Is the Prohibition Everything Else Rests On**

The Qur'an's central command appears in the same terms throughout. *“So do not knowingly set up equals to Allah ˹in worship˺.”* (2:22). *“Worship Allah ˹alone˺ and associate none with Him.”* (4:36). The forbidden list of acts includes associating others with God as a practice He has never authorised (7:33), and the same prohibition heads the catalogue of commands given in another passage: do not associate others with Him, honour parents, do not kill children out of fear of poverty, do not approach indecencies, do not take life except by legal right (6:151). Verse 51 places this prohibition second in a two-command summary, which is where it stands in the Qur'an's own ordering of duties.

**Every Messenger Was Sent With This Command**

The verse is not a local instruction to one audience. *“We never sent a messenger before you ˹O Prophet˺ without revealing to him: ‘There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Me, so worship Me ˹alone˺.’”* (21:25). And again: *“We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, ‘Worship Allah and shun false gods.’”* (16:36). Noah said it (11:26), Hūd said it to ʿĀd (11:50), Ṣāliḥ said it to Thamūd (7:73), and Moses said it to Pharaoh (79:18–19). The five cases narrated in verses 24 to 46 of this chapter are therefore five instances of the same command being delivered and refused. Verse 51 states the command once, after the cases, which is the most economical way to summarise them.

**Its Uniqueness Is Stated in the Strongest Available Terms**

The Qur'an singles out this offence from all others: *“Indeed, Allah does not forgive associating others with Him ˹in worship˺, but forgives anything else of whoever He wills. And whoever associates others with Allah has indeed committed a grave sin.”* (4:48). The verse distinguishes between sins that are forgivable by will and one that is not forgiven while it persists. That asymmetry explains why verse 51 pairs the prohibition with a warning rather than with an explanation. The command to flee to God addresses a person's danger; the command against setting up another god addresses the one thing that would make the flight useless. A person running to God while running to something else has not run anywhere.

**Al-Ghazzālī on the Hidden Form of the Offence**

The classical tradition pushes the prohibition inward. Al-Ghazzālī writes that whoever does not see God in everything sees something other than Him, and that attention given to anything other than God contains an element of hidden association, *al-shirk al-khafī*. Pure monotheism, on his account, consists in seeing only God in everything. This is a stricter reading than the juristic one, which concerns acts of worship directed at other beings, and the two are not in competition. The outward prohibition forbids setting up a rival object of worship; the inward reading warns against a divided attention that functions like one. Verse 51's placement directly after the command to flee supports the inward reading, since fleeing requires a single destination.

**Two Commands That Are One Movement**

Flee to God and do not set up another god with Him are the positive and negative forms of a single instruction. The first says where to go; the second says what would prevent arriving. Neither is complete alone. A person who flees to God while keeping a rival has fled to two places, and a person who rejects rivals without fleeing has rejected something without going anywhere. The chapter's structure makes this clear by giving both commands the same seal — the messenger's declaration of his office — and by placing them immediately after the description of a created order in which everything is paired and nothing is a refuge. The two verses are the practical conclusion of the previous twenty-six.

**"From Him" Locates the Authority Outside the Messenger**

The warner is sent *minhu*, from Him. The preposition removes the messenger from the position of author. He is not proposing a theology, negotiating a compromise, or offering a personal view about the future; he is delivering a warning whose source is elsewhere. That is why the accusation answered in the next verse is so wide of the point. A man who is a magician or a madman (51:52) would be speaking from himself, and his speech could be dismissed by diagnosing him. A man sent from God cannot be dismissed that way, and the only available response is to dispute the sending — which is what every case in verses 24 to 46 records, and what the following verses generalise.

**The Warning Is Clear, Which Removes the Last Excuse**

*Mubīn* means evident, explicit, making the matter plain. The adjective is attached to the warning rather than to the messenger's character, which places the clarity in the message. The chapter has spent fifty verses making the matter plain: four oaths, a diagnosis of contradiction, five historical cases, three verses on the created order, and two commands. Nothing about the case is left obscure, and the warning is described as clear before the audience responds to it. The next verse records that response, and it is the same response given in every previous generation — which is the strongest evidence available that the clarity of the warning was not the obstacle.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:52

> Similarly, no messenger came to those before them without being told: “A magician or a madman!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Similarly" Reaches Back Through the Whole Chapter**

The opening word ties this verse to everything before it. *Kadhālika* — likewise, in the same way — refers back to the five cases just narrated and, more immediately, to Pharaoh's words in verse 39. What happened then happens now. The verse converts a historical report into a law: no messenger came to those before them without receiving this response. The universality is stated without exception, and it is the chapter's summary of the narrative block. Having shown five instances, the chapter now states the pattern they instantiate and applies it to the audience's own situation.


Verse 39 quoted Pharaoh saying "a magician or a madman" about Moses, and verse 52 quotes the same two words as the standing response to every messenger. The repetition across thirteen verses is the point. A charge that appears identically in different centuries, addressed to different men bringing different signs, is not a finding about any of them. It is a formula. The chapter has already described the mental state that produces formulas of this kind — differing claims (51:8), turned-away minds (51:9), professional guessing (51:10) — and verse 52 supplies the empirical confirmation: the same words, produced by people who never met, about men who never met each other.

**The Same Accusations Were Made Against the Prophet ﷺ**

The Qur'an records them as they were made. *“Yet the disbelievers said, ‘Indeed, this ˹man˺ is clearly a magician!’”* (10:2). *“They say, ‘O you to whom the Reminder is revealed! You must be insane! Why do you not bring us the angels, if what you say is true?’”* (15:6–7). *“Or ˹because˺ they say, ‘He is insane?’ In fact, he has come to them with the truth, but most of them are resentful of the truth.”* (23:70). *“Has he fabricated a lie against Allah or is he insane?”* (34:8). *“and say, ‘He is certainly a madman.’”* (68:51). Five separate chapters preserve the charge, which is why verse 52 can state it as a general rule without further evidence.

**Mockery Is the Constant, Not the Specific Charge**

Three verses make the point in almost identical words: no messenger came to a people without being mocked (15:11; 36:30; 43:7). The specific accusation varies — magician, madman, poet, a man taught by another, a fabricator — and the mockery does not. Verse 52 selects one pair of accusations because it is the pair his audience used, but the underlying statement is about the reception itself. Every messenger is met with derision before being met with argument, and in most cases instead of argument. The chapter's own earlier diagnosis of the mockers who asked when the day would come (51:12) belongs to the same finding.

**Two Functions in One Verse**

The commentators read verse 52 as serving two purposes at once, and both are supported by the context. For the Prophet ﷺ it is consolation: his situation is not a personal failure but the standard treatment of his office, shared with every predecessor. For the Quraysh it is a warning: if their response matches the response of Pharaoh's court, the outcome recorded in verse 40 is the outcome available to them. The two readings are not alternatives. A single statement can comfort one listener and alarm another, and the verse is placed immediately after the two commands of verses 50 and 51 precisely so that both listeners hear it in that position.

**A Magician and a Madman Cannot Be the Same Person**

The charge contains its own refutation. Magic presumes training, technique, intention, and a teacher; madness excludes all four. To say "a magician or a madman" is to admit that the speaker has not determined which, and therefore has not examined the case. This is the contradiction the chapter diagnosed at verse 8 and condemned at verse 10 — differing claims held simultaneously, and guessing presented as knowledge. The accusation is not a weak argument; it is the absence of one, wearing the grammatical form of a claim. That is why the verse can state it as a universal rule without ever needing to answer it.

**The Pattern Is Documented, Not Asserted**

The Qur'an's method here is quotation rather than generalisation. Verse 52 asserts a rule, and the rule is supported by the recorded words of previous peoples and by the recorded words of the current one. The chapter has already given the reader Pharaoh's exact phrase, the mockers' exact question, and the accusers' exact adjectives. What verse 52 adds is the observation that the phrases recur. A reader can check the recurrence inside this chapter alone: verses 8, 10, 12, 39, and 52. The evidence is internal, which makes the verse one of the few in the Qur'an that argues from its own text.


If every generation produced the same words independently, one of two things must be true: either the words are correct, or the people producing them are in the same condition. Verse 53 asks the question directly — did they hand this down to one another? — and answers it before the question is finished: no, they are a transgressing people. The arrangement is the chapter's characteristic method: a claim, then the mechanism that explains it. Verse 52 supplies the claim, and the next verse supplies the mechanism, which is not transmission but a shared moral state producing a shared slogan.

**The Insults Are Preserved Inside the Book Itself**

A text intending to flatter its central figure would omit the abuse directed at him. The Qur'an does the opposite: it quotes the insults verbatim and keeps them in circulation. It also records the cost they carried for the man receiving them — *“Do not let their words grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. Surely all honour and power belongs to Allah.”* (10:65), *“Now, perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial, if they ˹continue to˺ disbelieve in this message.”* (18:6), and *“Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their disbelief.”* (26:3). Three separate passages preserve the grief alongside the accusation. For a later reader this has an evidential value that praise would not: the record includes material damaging to its own subject's public standing, which is the mark of a report rather than a monument. Verse 52 generalises that record into a rule, and the rule is checkable against the quotations the same Book preserved.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:53

> Have they passed this ˹cliché˺ down to one another? In fact, they have ˹all˺ been a transgressing people

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question About Transmission**

The verb is *tawāṣaw bihi*, to enjoin something upon one another mutually — the form that describes people passing an instruction or a practice along between them. The question asks whether the accusation quoted in the previous verse was handed down: did Pharaoh's court teach it to the Quraysh, did one generation brief the next? The interrogative is rhetorical and its expected answer is no, since the peoples concerned never met. The question's purpose is to force the alternative explanation into view. If the same words were not transmitted, they were generated — and generation by independent people implies a shared cause inside them rather than a shared source outside them.

**Two Readings of the Question**

Most commentators take the verse as an expression of wonder: is it really the case that they enjoin this on one another, that they pass along the denial of God and of revelation? The astonishment is at the persistence of a false slogan across unrelated peoples. A second reading takes the question as a negation — they do not enjoin one another to it — in which case the object referred to becomes belief rather than the accusation, and the sense is that they never urged each other toward faith. The first reading is stronger, because it follows directly from the quotation in verse 52: the thing being discussed is the accusation, and the question asks how it travelled. The second requires the pronoun to switch its referent without a signal.

**No Council Ever Convened**

The historical impossibility is the verse's engine. ʿĀd and Thamūd were Arabian tribes separated by generations; Pharaoh's court was an Egyptian administration; Noah's people belong to the earliest period the Qur'an narrates. None of them communicated with the others, and none of them had access to a record of what the others said. Yet the charge is identical: magician or madman, insane, a fabricator, a poet. Verse 52 lists the recurrence and verse 53 asks how it happened. The question eliminates the obvious answer — transmission — by making it absurd, which leaves only one remaining explanation, and the second half of the verse supplies it.

**What Is Actually Transmitted Is Ancestral Practice**

The Qur'an identifies the real inheritance, and it is not a slogan but a posture. *“When it is said to them, ‘Follow what Allah has revealed,’ they reply, ‘No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing.’ ˹Would they still do so,˺ even if their forefathers had ˹absolutely˺ no understanding or guidance?”* (2:170). The same statement is generalised across history: *“Similarly, whenever We sent a warner to a society before you ˹O Prophet˺, its ˹spoiled˺ elite would say, ‘We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are walking in their footsteps.’”* (43:23). What is handed down is the refusal to examine, and the slogan is produced fresh by each generation that inherits it.

**Ṭāghūn Names the Shared Condition**

The answer given is that they are a transgressing people — *ṭāghūn*, from the same root as Pharaoh's transgression of all bounds (79:17) and the insolence of Thamūd (51:44). The word describes exceeding a limit, and it is applied here to a collective across time. This is the mechanism the verse proposes. Not a conspiracy, not a school of rhetoric, not a borrowed phrase: a shared moral condition that produces a shared response. Excess in one direction — wealth, power, self-regard — generates the same defence in every generation, and the defence takes the form of discrediting the person who challenges it rather than examining the challenge.

**One Condition Produces One Slogan**

The inference is worth stating plainly, because it is the verse's whole contribution. If unrelated people in unrelated centuries say the same thing about unrelated messengers, the thing said is not a description of the messengers. It is a description of the speakers. The accusation is a symptom with a fixed cause, and the fixed cause is named in the second half of the verse. This reverses the evidential burden: the repetition that the objectors might present as corroboration — everyone has said this about him — becomes the strongest available proof that what is being said has nothing to do with the person it is said about.

**Inheritance Is Not Accepted as an Excuse**

The Qur'an forecloses the defence that a person merely repeated what he was taught. The rhetorical question in the verse about forefathers is explicit: would they follow them even if their forefathers had no understanding or guidance (2:170)? The answer assumed is that following is the follower's act, and the absence of understanding in the one followed does not transfer innocence to the one following. Verse 53 states the same principle in narrative form. The peoples are described as transgressing, not as misled. Whatever they inherited, the transgression is attributed to them, which is consistent with the chapter's earlier account of people being turned away only after they had turned (51:9).

**The Verse Answers the Accusation Without Arguing With It**

No attempt is made to prove that the Prophet ﷺ was neither a magician nor a madman. The charge is not examined; its origin is. This is a deliberate method and it appears throughout the chapter. The mockers' question about the date of the day is answered with a description of the day (51:13); Pharaoh's insult is answered with the sea (51:40); and the recurring slogan is answered here with a diagnosis of the people who produce it. The chapter treats these utterances as data about their speakers rather than as claims requiring refutation, which is the only treatment a repeated falsehood across centuries can receive. A diagnosis of this kind cannot be rebutted by the people it describes, since any reply produced from the same condition confirms it rather than disproving it. The chapter leaves the matter there and turns to the instruction that follows from it.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:54

> So ˹now˺ turn away from them ˹O Prophet˺, for you will not be blamed

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Turn, Not a Retreat**

The verb *tawallā* describes turning away, and it is the same verb used of Pharaoh in verse 39, where he turned his back on a clear proof while relying on his power. The two uses are not the same act. Pharaoh turned away from evidence he could not answer; the Prophet ﷺ is told to turn away from people who will not answer it. The difference lies in what is being left. Nothing in this verse withdraws the message, the warning, or the obligation to deliver it — the following verse commands continued reminding. What is withdrawn is responsibility for the outcome, and the withdrawal is granted by the one who assigned the task.

**"You Will Not Be Blamed" Removes Liability, Not Duty**

The clause *bi-malūm* — one who is blamed — fixes the verse's legal character. Blame attaches to a failure of obligation, and the verse states that no failure exists here. The commentators explain the sense directly: once the message has been delivered, the messenger is not responsible for whether the disbelievers accept it, nor for the punishment that reaches them if they do not. His responsibility is to continue reminding those who benefit. This is a statement about the limits of an office. A warner who has warned has discharged the warning; the response belongs to the one warned, and the consequence belongs to the one who sent him.

**Guidance Was Never Assigned to Him**

The Qur'an states the division of labour without ambiguity: *“You are not responsible for people’s guidance ˹O Prophet˺—it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills.”* (2:272). The verse belongs to a context about charity toward those who refuse the message, and its principle is general. Guidance is not a deliverable of prophethood. What is delivered is the warning, the evidence, and the reminder; the effect on the heart is not in the messenger's hands. Verse 54 applies that principle to the emotional weight the Prophet ﷺ was carrying, since several passages record the toll the refusals took on him (18:6; 26:3).


The command is not an isolated concession. *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺.”* (88:21–22). *“We know best what they say. And you ˹O Prophet˺ are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺. So remind with the Quran ˹only˺ those who fear My warning.”* (50:45). Both passages pair the removal of compulsion with the continuation of reminding, which is exactly the pairing of verses 54 and 55 here. The consistency across chapters shows that this is the settled definition of the office rather than a temporary adjustment to a difficult period.

**Compulsion Is Excluded From the Job Description**

The verses quoted above say something stronger than "you are not to blame." They say the messenger is not there to compel. Belief produced by force is not belief, and a mission that depended on compulsion would produce compliance rather than conviction. This connects to the chapter's earlier account of the promised son and the freedom required for worship: a creature made contingent and free cannot be forced into the worship he was created for, or the worship would not be his (51:56). Verse 54's removal of blame is the pastoral form of the same principle. The messenger is not measured by results, because results depend on a freedom he does not control.

**The Turn Is Directed at a Specific Group**

"Turn away from them" refers to the people just described: those who inherited a slogan from a shared condition of transgression (51:53). The instruction is not general withdrawal from the world or from argument. It is a cessation of effort toward people who have demonstrated that further effort produces only the same formula. The chapter has already given the diagnosis, and the instruction follows the diagnosis rather than preceding it. A reader who takes verse 54 alone could mistake it for despair; taken after verse 53 it is a judgement about a specific case, made on evidence the chapter has just laid out.

**The Next Verse Immediately Qualifies This One**

No pause separates the two instructions. Turn away, and remind. The conjunction is not adversarial in effect; it prevents the first command from being read as silence. The reminder continues because there is an audience for it, and the verse that follows identifies that audience by the benefit it receives. The pairing is the chapter's answer to a question any messenger faces: when to stop pressing a case and when to keep speaking. The answer given is that the speaking never stops, while the pressing stops where it produces nothing. What ends is the expectation, not the announcement.

**Comfort Addressed to a Man Under Strain**

Read in the light of the passages that record the Prophet's grief over their disbelief (18:6; 26:3; 35:8), verse 54 functions as relief. It tells a man carrying the refusals of a city as personal failure that the failure is not his to carry. The verse does not minimise the refusals or explain them away; verse 53 has already explained them in terms that leave no room for the messenger's inadequacy. What verse 54 adds is the release. The message has been delivered clearly (51:50–51), the accusation against the deliverer is a formula inherited from transgression (51:52–53), and the responsibility for what happens next sits elsewhere.

**What the Verse Does Not Permit**

Turning away is not permission to stop warning. The standing instruction on how the invitation is to be made is unchanged: *“Invite ˹all˺ to the Way of your Lord with wisdom and kind advice, and only debate with them in the best manner. Surely your Lord ˹alone˺ knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided.”* (16:125). That verse also assigns the knowledge of who is guided to God, which is precisely the knowledge the messenger is released from here. The two statements belong together: the method is wisdom and good debate, the outcome is not the messenger's, and the release from blame does not alter the method. Verse 54 removes an expectation, not an obligation, and the verse after it restores the obligation in the same breath.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:55

> But ˹continue to˺ remind. For certainly reminders benefit the believers

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Imperative Placed Immediately After a Turn**

Verse 54 said turn away; verse 55 says remind. The two are joined without transition, and the second is unqualified — no audience specified, no condition attached, no duration set. The imperative *dhakkir* stands alone at the head of the verse, which gives it maximum force. The arrangement answers a misreading of the previous verse before the misreading can form: turning away from a particular group is not withdrawal from the work. The work continues, and the reason for its continuation is supplied in the second half of the verse rather than in a command from above it.

**The Reason Given Is Benefit, Not Duty Alone**

Most commands in the Qur'an are followed by a rationale, and this one is no exception. Reminding is to continue because the reminder benefits the believers. The clause converts an instruction into a statement of fact about how revelation operates. It produces an effect in a specific category of people, and the effect is real rather than hoped for. This is why the command survives the refusal described in verses 52 and 53. A reminder that benefits someone cannot be discontinued because others reject it; its value does not depend on universal acceptance. The verse supplies the messenger with a reason to continue that does not rely on the response of the people he has just been told to turn away from.

**Another Chapter States the Same Principle**

The formulation is close to a passage in a neighbouring Meccan chapter: *“So ˹always˺ remind ˹with the Quran˺—˹even˺ if the reminder is beneficial ˹only to some˺. Those in awe ˹of Allah˺ will be mindful ˹of it˺.”* (87:9–10). The two verses agree on three points: the reminding is to continue, its benefit is partial rather than total, and the people who benefit are identified by a quality they already possess — awe, or belief. The partiality is stated without regret. A reminder that reaches some people has done what reminders do, and the number reached is not the measure of its value.

**Adh-Dhikrā Is the Qur'an Itself**

The commentators take the reminder here as a reference to the Qur'an, and the verse as an injunction to preach it. The word *dhikr* and its cognates are used throughout the Book as a name for the revelation: it is called a reminder sent down, and the Prophet ﷺ is told to remind with it specifically (50:45). On this reading verse 55 is not a general instruction to give advice. It is an instruction to keep reciting and expounding a particular text, because that text has a defined effect on a defined audience. The specificity matters: the benefit promised is attached to the reminder, not to exhortation in general.


The classical commentary defines the benefit precisely. The reminder increases the believer's spiritual insight — *baṣīrah*, the seeing that distinguishes one thing from another — and gives strength in certainty and knowledge. This is a description of growth rather than of conversion. The person being reminded already believes; what the reminder adds is clarity and firmness. That explains why the command continues after the refusal of others. Its primary function is not to win the unwilling but to build the willing, and a work that builds its audience cannot be abandoned because part of the audience has left.

**Addressed to the Believers, or to Both**

Two readings are preserved. One takes the reminder as aimed at the believers to the exclusion of the disbelievers, since the verse says it benefits them. The other takes it as addressed to both, with the benefit realised in one group. The second reading is stronger in context. Verse 54 has just distinguished the messenger's relation to the rejecters from his duty to remind, and verse 52 has established that messengers are sent to people who reject them. A reminder confined to those already convinced would make the sending pointless. The first reading captures the verse's emphasis — the benefit is realised in the believers — while the second preserves the scope of the work.

**Reminding Continues Because the Audience Is Not Only the Rejecters**

The practical consequence is immediate. A preacher who measures his work by the responses of the loudest objectors will stop, since those responses are fixed, as verse 52 has just shown. A preacher who measures it by the benefit realised in those who receive it will continue indefinitely, since that benefit accrues every time the reminder is given. Verse 55 supplies the second measure. It also explains the chapter's own existence: this text is a reminder, and it was revealed to people most of whom rejected it, and it is still read by people who benefit from it. The verse describes the mechanism of its own transmission.

**Two Verses That Define the Office**

Taken together, verses 54 and 55 give the most compact statement in this chapter of what a messenger is for. He is not responsible for guidance (2:272), not there to compel (88:22), not to be blamed for the refusal of those he warned (51:54), and not permitted to stop speaking (51:55). The office consists of delivery and continuation, with the effect left to the one who sent him and to the condition of the one hearing. The chapter now moves from the office to its ground — why human beings exist to worship at all, and what the Creator requires of them, which is nothing.

**The Effect Is Described Elsewhere in Physical Terms**

Two passages state what the reminder does to the people it benefits, and both describe a bodily reaction. *“˹It is˹ Allah ˹Who˺ has sent down the best message—a Book of perfect consistency and repeated lessons—which causes the skin ˹and hearts˺ of those who fear their Lord to tremble, then their skin and hearts soften at the mention of ˹the mercy of˺ Allah.”* (39:23). The second is shorter and sharper: *“We send down the Quran as a healing and mercy for the believers, but it only increases the wrongdoers in loss.”* (17:82). Trembling, softening, healing, mercy for one group; increase in loss for the other. Verse 55's claim that the reminder benefits the believers is not a hopeful generalisation but a report of an effect documented in these terms, and the same effect explains why the reminder is not discontinued when others refuse it.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:56

> I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Statement of Purpose, Not a Prediction**

The verse uses the construction of exception — I did not create them except for this — which states an aim rather than an outcome. Al-Zamakhsharī makes the distinction explicit: what the verse states is the end at which the creature's making was aimed, and that end stands whether or not the creature reaches it, as indeed most do not. That reading is required by the facts. Most people do not worship, and the verse cannot be asserting that they do. What it asserts is that worship is the reason the creature exists in the form it exists, and a purpose unfulfilled remains a purpose. The construction is legal rather than descriptive, like the statement of what a thing was made for, and it leaves the failure of the maker's aim entirely on the side of the one made.

**ʿIbādah Covers the Whole of a Directed Life**

The word translated as worship denotes servitude and devotion together: the posture of a servant and the acts that express it. In Qur'anic usage it includes prayer, obedience, gratitude, and the direction of one's aims. Its range is wider than ritual, which is why the verse can serve as a summary of human purpose rather than as a rule about prayer times. The same word appears in the commission given to every messenger: worship God alone (21:25; 16:36). Verse 56 states from the side of the Creator what those verses state from the side of the messengers — one purpose, delivered repeatedly, to every community.

**Ibn ʿAbbās's Reading: Except to Know Me**

An interpretation carried in the tradition from Ibn ʿAbbās renders the exception as "except to know Me." The reading is preserved by al-Baghawī and treated seriously in the tradition, and it fits the immediate context well. Verse 49 said all things were created in pairs so that people might be mindful, and verse 21 directed attention to the signs within the self; a creation arranged for the purpose of being recognised supports the gloss. The two readings are not in competition. Knowledge of God is the content of worship, and worship is the form knowledge takes in a creature. A person who knows and does not worship has not, on this verse's terms, known what the verse means by knowing.

**Worship Must Be Free, or It Is Not Worship**

Al-Zamakhsharī draws a further inference. If worship were produced by coercion — *qasr* or *iljāʾ*, constraint and compulsion — it would be found in every human being, since nothing could resist it. It is not found in everyone, which shows that the creature was made contingent and free, able to fulfil the purpose or to miss it. The argument supports the chapter's earlier statements about compulsion being outside the messenger's office (51:54; 88:22). A purpose that can only be reached freely is not served by force, and the freedom is not a defect in the arrangement. It is the condition of the arrangement working at all.

**Jinn Are Included in the Statement**

The verse names two kinds of creature: jinn and human beings. The inclusion matters, since jinn are addressed elsewhere in the Qur'an as morally responsible beings with their own communities, their own hearing of the revelation, and their own outcome. The purpose clause covers both, which widens the statement from a claim about human history to a claim about the created order's intelligent inhabitants. It also connects to the chapter's opening oaths, where the administration of affairs is carried by angels (51:4) — an unseen order already described as real and active. A world containing angels, jinn, and human beings has more than one kind of creature with a purpose, and this verse states the purpose shared by two of them.

**A Report Cited in the Classical Commentary**

A ḥadīth qudsī preserved in the exegetical tradition on this verse puts the demand in direct speech: God tells the son of Adam to devote himself to worship, promising to fill his heart with sufficiency and dispel his poverty, and warning that otherwise his heart will be filled with distractions and his poverty will not be dispelled. It is reported in this connection by Ibn Kathīr. **The "Hidden Treasure" Report and Its Transmission**

A second saying is often quoted beside this verse, to the effect that God was a treasure concealed, wished to be recognised, and therefore brought creatures into existence so that recognition might occur. **The Verse Explains the Command to Flee**

Verse 50 said flee to God, and this verse supplies the reason. Everything other than God distracts a creature from the purpose it was made for, which is why flight must be toward Him rather than toward any created refuge. Al-Qushayrī's catalogue of flights — the passage out of ignorance into knowledge, out of caprice into reverence, out of doubt into certainty — describes the same movement in detail. The chapter closes its argument by identifying the destination and the reason for it. A creature made for one thing, in a world full of competing objects, needs a stated direction, and the direction is given twice: run to God, and worship nothing else.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:57

> I seek no provision from them, nor do I need them to feed Me

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Negations Covering One Idea**

The verse denies twice, and the two denials are close enough to look redundant. God does not want provision from them, and He does not want to be fed by them. The first addresses the general category of supply; the second addresses the specific act of a servant providing food for a master. The pairing is emphatic rather than repetitive, and it targets a practice rather than an idea. Ancient worship across Arabia and beyond was organised around feeding the gods — offerings, sacrifices, first fruits set out at a shrine. The verse denies both the abstract claim and the concrete ritual, which leaves no version of the arrangement intact.

**Rizq Moves in One Direction**

Provision in the Qur'an has a fixed direction of travel: from God to the creature. The verse states it as a guarantee that covers every living thing: *“There is no moving creature on earth whose provision is not guaranteed by Allah. And He knows where it lives and where it is laid to rest. All is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record.”* (11:6). This chapter has already placed the supply above the recipient — in heaven is your sustenance (51:22). Verse 57 completes the picture by denying the reverse flow. A creature cannot add to a stock that is already written, guaranteed, and located in heaven. The denial is therefore not a statement about God's modesty but about the arithmetic of the arrangement.

**The Verse Answers a Real Practice**

The Qur'an depicts the practice it is denying with unusual clarity. Standing before the images his people set out with food, Abraham asks them: *“Will you not eat ˹your offerings˺? What is wrong with you that you cannot speak?”* (37:91–92). Meals were placed before gods, and the gods did not eat them. Verse 57 generalises Abraham's irony into doctrine: no provision is sought from the creature, and no feeding is required. The scene in aṣ-Ṣāffāt shows what the practice looks like from outside — a meal going cold in front of an object — and this verse states why the practice cannot be what its practitioners think it is.

**They Are the Ones Being Fed**

The reversal is stated in one sentence elsewhere and it is the verse's positive content: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Will I take any guardian other than Allah, the Originator of the heavens and the earth, Who provides for all and is not in need of provision?’”* (6:14). The two halves are joined — provider, and not provided for — in the same clause. Verse 57 says the same thing in negative form, and verse 58 will say it in positive form with three divine names. Between them the three verses settle the direction of every flow in the relationship: the creature receives, the Creator supplies, and nothing moves the other way.

**Worship Is Not a Service Contract**

The verse removes an entire class of misunderstanding about the purpose stated in verse 56. If human beings were created to worship, worship could be mistaken for labour performed for a master who needs it done — a contract in which the servant supplies something and the master benefits. The denial forecloses that reading. Worship is not production. It is the orientation of a creature toward the one it already depends on, and the dependence runs one way. A person who thinks his worship enriches God has misunderstood both terms, and a person who thinks his disobedience impoverishes God has made the same mistake in the opposite direction.

**Al-Muṭʿim: The One Who Feeds**

The second half of the verse uses a participle meaning one who feeds, and the Qur'an applies the same description to God as an argument against taking anything else as a guardian (6:14). Feeding is the most basic form of provision and the most continuous; it happens daily and cannot be stored against by the recipient. Choosing it as the image is deliberate, because it is the one act of supply no creature can perform for itself over time. The verse therefore denies the possibility of reciprocation at the most elementary level. A creature who cannot feed himself for a day without the arrangement continuing has nothing to offer the one who maintains it.

**Why This Follows the Verse on Purpose**

Verse 56 stated why the creature exists; verse 57 states what the Creator does not get from it. The sequence is necessary. A purpose can be read as a requirement, and a requirement implies a beneficiary. By denying the benefit immediately after naming the purpose, the verse establishes that the purpose is for the creature's sake rather than for God's. Worship is what a dependent being does when it correctly understands its dependence, and the understanding is the benefit. Nothing accrues upward. This is the same asymmetry the chapter described in the reward of the reverent, who receive what their Lord gave them (51:16) — receiving, not supplying.

**The Denial Protects the Command From Being Bargained With**

A person who believes his worship supplies something can negotiate its quantity, delay it, or withhold it as leverage. The verse removes the leverage. There is nothing to withhold, because nothing was ever being given. What remains is the creature's own condition — improved by worship and damaged without it — and that condition is the only thing at stake in the command. The chapter's closing verses then state the alternative outcome in the same terms of self-interest: a share of torment like the share of their predecessors (51:59), and woe on the day they were warned about (51:60). No part of that account involves a loss to God. Nothing in the arrangement can be renegotiated, because there was never an exchange. Both the worship and the refusal leave the Provider unchanged, and the three names in the verse that follows state why that must be so.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:58

> Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ is the Supreme Provider—Lord of all Power, Ever Mighty

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ar-Razzāq Is the Intensive Form of Provider**

The first name is not the plain noun for a provider but its intensive pattern: one who provides abundantly, repeatedly, and to many. Arabic builds this form to indicate frequency and scale, and its application here answers the denial in the previous verse. God does not want provision from the creature because He is the one whose providing is continuous and comprehensive. The scale is stated elsewhere in general terms: every moving creature's provision is guaranteed (11:6), and He gives abundantly or in measure to whomever He wills (34:39). The name gathers those statements into a single description and places it directly after the verse that removed the flow in the other direction.

**Dhū al-Quwwa: Possession of Strength, Not Its Display**

The second title describes God as the possessor of strength. The construction is significant: not "the strong one who acts" but "the one who has strength." Possession implies a reserve, capacity held rather than exercised. The Qur'an states the same idea elsewhere in terms of what power belongs where: all honour and power belong to God (10:65), and He is All-Powerful, Almighty (22:74). The title answers the cases narrated earlier in this chapter. ʿĀd boasted about might and asked who was superior to them in strength (41:15); Pharaoh leaned on his support (51:39). Both were answered by a possession of strength they did not have, and the verse states that possession as a standing fact rather than as an episode.

**Al-Matīn: Firmness That Does Not Fatigue**

The third name denotes firmness, solidity, the quality of something that cannot be shaken or worn down. The Qur'an makes the point about weariness explicit in a verse concerning creation itself: *“Indeed, We created the heavens and the earth and everything in between in six Days, and We were not ˹even˺ touched with fatigue.”* (50:38). Firmness without fatigue is the specific combination the name asserts. A provider whose supply could be exhausted, or a power that could be worn down by the demands on it, would require the creature's contribution. Verse 57 denied that contribution, and verse 58 supplies the reason: the one providing is inexhaustible.

**Three Names Answering Three Verses**

The sequence is deliberate. Verse 56 named the creature's purpose; verse 57 denied that anything flows back to God; verse 58 gives the three attributes that make the denial coherent. Provision answers the dependency of the creature; power answers the possibility of the arrangement being maintained; firmness answers the possibility of its being maintained indefinitely. Nothing is asserted that the previous verses did not require. The chapter's argument about worship, provision, and judgment rests on exactly these three claims, and the verse states them in a single sentence with no elaboration.


The Qur'an states the counterpart plainly: *“O humanity! It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.”* (35:15). Two descriptions, one for each party, and the second includes praise — self-sufficiency that is also praiseworthy rather than merely indifferent. Verse 58's three names are the detailed form of that self-sufficiency, and verse 57's denial is the detailed form of the creature's need. The pairing removes the possibility of a relationship understood as mutual benefit. One side has everything and needs nothing; the other has nothing and needs everything.

**Provision and Power Are Joined Because Both Are Required**

A provider without power could be prevented from providing; a powerful being without generosity might not provide. The two titles are joined because the arrangement described in this chapter requires both — a supply that is willed and a will that cannot be obstructed. The chapter has illustrated the second half repeatedly: winds sent for eight days, seas closing on an army, stones marked for named individuals, a blast reaching inside carved rock. None of those events was resisted successfully. The first half is illustrated by the loaded clouds, the ships gliding, and the sustenance located in heaven (51:2, 51:3, 51:22). The verse combines the two illustrations in three words.

**The Verse Closes the Argument About Need**

Nothing further is said about provision, worship, or dependence after this point. The chapter's theological block ends here, and the two remaining verses turn to the outcome for those who refused. The closing is structural as well as thematic: the argument has moved from oaths about the natural order, through the diagnosis of denial, through five historical cases, through the description of creation, to the two commands and the reason for them, and finally to the three names that make the reason hold. Verse 58 is the last piece of the case, and it is stated as a declaration rather than as a proof.

**The Two Final Verses Return to the Mockers**

With the argument complete, the chapter returns to the people it began by answering. Those who asked when the day would come (51:12) and who sought to hasten the punishment (51:14) are addressed once more, in two verses that give their portion and their day. The return is the chapter's frame closing: the oath that the judgment will certainly come to pass (51:6) is restated at the end as a woe pronounced on the day they were warned of. Between the two statements the whole case has been made — from nature, from history, from the self, and from the character of the one who promised.

**Firmness Is Named Alongside Power for a Reason**

Power on its own describes capacity at a moment; firmness describes capacity sustained. A being who is strong could act once and be spent, and a single act of provision would not answer the condition of a creature who must eat every day for a whole life. The third name closes that gap. Provision is not an event but a continuing arrangement, and the arrangement holds across generations without the provider tiring — the point made in the verse about creating the heavens and the earth without being touched by fatigue (50:38). This chapter illustrates sustained rather than momentary power throughout: a wind held over a people for eight nights and seven days (69:7), a supply written and guaranteed for every moving creature (11:6), a day counted down moment by moment while the counted one is unaware (19:84). The names answer the scale of what they govern. Firmness is the attribute on which daily life depends most, and it is the one the verse names last.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:59

> The wrongdoers will certainly have a share ˹of the torment˺ like that of their predecessors. So do not let them ask Me to hasten ˹it˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dhanūb Is a Measured Portion**

The word translated as share is *dhanūb*, from a root connected with the tail and, in common usage, with the portion drawn by a bucket at a watering place. It denotes an allotted quantity — what comes to someone's turn. The image is of a distributed supply, and its use for punishment is exact: the torment is apportioned, and the apportionment has a precedent. The same root family produces the word for a bucket's turn at water, which is why the term carries the sense of an amount fixed by an arrangement rather than by chance. The wrongdoers are not threatened with an unspecified catastrophe. They are assigned a portion.

**Like the Portion of Their Companions**

The comparison is with *ashāb*, companions or fellows — understood by the commentators as the wrongdoers of previous generations, the peoples whose destructions this chapter has just narrated. The reference gathers all five cases: Lot's city, Pharaoh, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the people of Noah. Each received a portion, and the portion is described as similar. The similarity is not in the instrument — stones, water, wind, and blast were all different — but in the fact of a measured allotment arriving after a warning was refused. The verse generalises the narrative block into a rule about portions.


The logic connecting the two halves of the comparison is stated earlier in the chapter. Every generation said the same thing about its messenger (51:52), not because the words were transmitted but because the speakers shared a condition (51:53). Verse 59 draws the consequence: people in the same condition receive the same portion. The parallel is exact on both sides — one slogan, one outcome. Nothing in the arrangement depends on the era, the region, or the specific form the transgression takes. This is what makes the historical cases useful as precedent rather than as mere history.

**Following a Precedent Does Not Reduce the Portion**

The commentators note a second sense of the comparison: those who follow the people or customs of their own time blindly will not find that following accepted as an excuse on the Day of Judgment. The point is consistent with the chapter's earlier treatment of inherited practice — would they follow their forefathers even if those forefathers had no understanding or guidance (2:170)? Verse 59 states the outcome of that following in the language of shares. Imitation does not split the portion or transfer it. Each generation's transgressors receive a portion like the portion of the generation they imitated, which means the imitation is counted against them rather than in their favour.

**The Second Half Returns to the Mockers**

"Do not let them ask Me to hasten it" resumes the thread dropped at verse 14, where the mockers were told to taste what they had sought to hurry. The demand for acceleration is documented throughout the Qur'an. They prayed for stones from the sky if the message was true (8:32); they challenged the Prophet ﷺ to hasten the torment, and were told that a day with God is like a thousand of their years (22:47); and a people who saw their punishment approaching as a cloud welcomed it as rain and were told it was the very thing they had hurried (46:24). Verse 59 addresses the same posture in the same audience.

**Hastening Is Not Within Their Power or His**

The reply to the demand has already been given in this chapter's terms: the torment they seek to hasten is not within the messenger's power, and the timing belongs to God alone (6:57). Verse 59 restates it as a prohibition — let them not ask Me — which locates the request in the wrong place. They are asking a warner to alter an allotment. The allotment is fixed, as the word *dhanūb* implies, and a fixed portion cannot be accelerated by requesting it. The only effect of the request is to record the requester's posture, which is what verse 14 did.

**A Fixed Portion Makes Haste Meaningless**

The two halves of the verse answer each other. If the portion is measured and matches the portion of predecessors, then it arrives when it is allotted to arrive, and asking for it early changes nothing. The mocker's strategy — force the promise into my timeframe or admit it is false — fails against an allotment. This is the same answer the chapter gave to the question "when is this day" (51:12–13): a description instead of a date. Verse 59 gives the description in commercial terms the audience understood: a share, a portion, a turn at the water, already assigned and identical to the turn taken by those before them.

**The Final Verse Names the Day**

What remains is the day on which the portion is collected, and verse 60 pronounces woe on those who will face it — their day, the one they were warned of. The two verses close the chapter by returning to its opening claim that the promise is true and the judgment will fall (51:5–6). Between those verses and these, the chapter has produced its evidence: four oaths, a diagnosis of denial, five destroyed peoples, three descriptions of creation, two commands, and three divine names. The woe is the last word, and it is addressed to people still living, which is the only condition under which a woe can function as a warning.

**The Portion Reaches Named Individuals**

The chapter's cases show that the allotment is not a blanket catastrophe but a distribution reaching particular people. The stones were sent marked one by one (51:34), so that each falling stone had a named destination. Pharaoh and those with him were drowned together (51:40), and the wording gathers his supporters into his portion. Lot's family was saved by command, with the exception of his wife who remained behind (51:36) — one person held out of a delivered household. Thamūd's blast reached inside homes carved from rock (51:45), which no natural disaster explains by scale alone. Each case shows the same structure: a portion assigned, a household or an army included in it, and individuals specified within the group. The wrongdoers of verse 59 receive a share on these terms, which is why the verse says a share rather than a punishment. The measurement is personal, and it is the measurement the chapter has been demonstrating since the stones of verse 34.

## Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt 51:60

> Woe then to the disbelievers when they face their Day which they are warned of

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wayl Is an Invocation of Ruin**

The word is an exclamation of destruction — woe, ruin, perishment — used in Arabic to announce that someone has fallen into a state from which there is no recovery. The Qur'an employs it repeatedly and always with a specified cause: woe to those who pray and are unmindful of their prayer (107:4–5), woe to every slanderer and gossiper, woe to those who measure unfairly. In each case the exclamation is attached to a described behaviour rather than to a person's identity. Verse 60 follows the pattern: woe is pronounced on the disbelievers, and the ground is the day they are about to face. The form is an invocation, but its content is a verdict already reached.

**"Their Day" Is a Possessive**

The day is described as belonging to them — *yawmuhum*. The possessive is the verse's sharpest feature. A day that belongs to a person is a day defined by what happens to him, and the Qur'an uses the same construction for a people's appointed destruction. The day they mocked in verse 12, asked the date of, and tried to hasten in verse 14 is now identified as theirs. It is not a general event they will happen to be present at; it is their event, assigned to them, in the same way that the portion in verse 59 was assigned. The two verses describe one allotment from two sides — the amount and the date.

**The Day They Were Warned Of**

The relative clause ties the ending to the chapter's two commands: flee to God, I am sent with a clear warning (51:50), and do not set up another god with Allah, I am sent with a clear warning (51:51). The warning was delivered twice, in the same words, and the day is now described by reference to it. Nothing about the day is new. It is not an unforeseen development or a change in the arrangement; it is the item the warner named. The phrase removes the last defence available to a person facing a consequence — that he was not told. He was told, twice, in the same sentence that gave him the alternative.

**The Chapter's Frame Closes**

The chapter began with four oaths and two assertions: what you are promised is true, and the judgment will certainly come to pass (51:5–6). It ends with the day named as belonging to the people who denied those two assertions. The frame is exact. Between the opening and the closing, the chapter produced its evidence in four registers — the natural order sworn by, the diagnosis of contradictory denial, five historical cases of warning refused, and three descriptions of creation ending in the creature's purpose. Verse 60 does not add a new claim. It applies the opening claim to the people who have just been described across fifty-five verses.

**The Mockers' Question Is Answered at Last**

Verse 12 asked when the day of judgment would be, and the chapter answered with a description rather than a date: the day they will be tormented over the fire (51:13). Verse 60 completes the answer by describing the arrival. The mockers wanted a calendar entry; they are given a day that belongs to them, warned of in advance, carrying a portion like the portions of their predecessors. The answer is more specific than the one they asked for and less useful to them, because a date they could calculate would have allowed preparation by arithmetic. What they receive instead is certainty about arrival combined with silence about timing — the arrangement described at verse 6.

**Return and Reckoning Are Located in One Place**

The Qur'an states the destination of everyone in terms that match this verse: *“Surely to Us is their return, then surely with Us is their reckoning.”* (88:25–26). Both clauses are emphatic and both locate the transaction in one party. Verse 60's "their day" is that day of reckoning, and the woe pronounced on it is a statement about what the reckoning will find. Another verse describes the countdown running in the background of every life: *“So do not be in haste against them, for indeed We are ˹closely˺ counting down their days.”* (19:84). The counting is divine, continuous, and invisible to the one being counted, which is why the haste described in verse 59 accomplishes nothing.

**Woe Pronounced on the Living Is a Warning**

The people addressed by this verse were alive when it was recited. A woe pronounced on the dead is an epitaph; pronounced on the living it is an offer. The chapter's whole structure depends on that fact — it argues, diagnoses, narrates, commands, and warns, all of which are activities directed at people who can still change their position. Verse 60 is therefore not the last word of a closed case. It is the last word of an open one, and the openness is what makes the preceding fifty-nine verses worth reciting. The alternative to the woe has been stated in the same chapter: gardens and springs, receiving what their Lord gave them (51:15–16).

**The Final Word Matches the First**

The chapter opens with the wind scattering dust and closes with a day belonging to the disbelievers. Between them it has argued that a scattered thing is gathered, that provision is written, that promises are kept, and that the same administration which sends rain sends stones. Its last sentence is a warning and its first sentence is an oath, and both are about the same event. The chapter ends where it began, which is the shape of a completed argument: the promise sworn to in verse 5 arrives in verse 60 as a day, a portion, and a woe. Nothing has been added at the end that was not asserted at the start.
