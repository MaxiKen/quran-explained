# Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn (Chapter 83) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Defrauders of the Scale and the Rust Upon the Heart**

Al-Muṭaffifīn, "The Defrauders" or "Those Who Give Short Measure," is the eighty-third chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 36 verses. In classical chronology, it occupies a famous transitional status: Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Nasā'ī record that it was the very last chapter revealed in Makkah or the very first chapter revealed as the Prophet entered Madinah. It opens with an explosive, legal and moral thunderbolt: *“Woe to those who give short measure (*Waylun li-l-muṭaffifīn*)! Those who, when they take a measure from people, take in full; but when they measure for them or weigh for them, they cause a loss!”* (83:1–3). Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn is celebrated for its revolutionary fusion of commercial ethics and the cosmic teaching about the Last Day: demonstrating that cheating an ordinary buyer of a few ounces of grain in the marketplace is not a minor commercial misdemeanor, but a catastrophic symptom of disbelief in the Day of Resurrection (*“Do they not think that they will be resurrected, for a Tremendous Day?”*, 83:4–5 — *“for a tremendous Day”*). The sūrah contains the famous psychological diagnosis of verse 14: *“Nay! But upon their hearts is the rust (*rān*) of that which they used to earn!”*

**The Structural Anatomy of Sijjīn and ʿIlliyyīn**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn moves across three distinct movements:

1. The Commercial Indictment and the Standing before the Lord (1–6): Condemns those who take their rights in full while shortchanging others, asking the devastating existential question: *“Do those not think that they will be resurrected, for a Tremendous Day—the Day when mankind will stand before the Lord of the worlds?”*

2. The Prison-Record of Sijjīn and the Rusting of Hearts (7–17): Unveils the destiny of the wicked (*al-fujjār*): their record is inscribed in *Sijjīn* (a dark, subterranean, narrow prison register), decrying the deniers who dismiss the warnings of the Qur'an as *Asāṭīr al-Awwalīn* (fables of the ancients). Verse 14 delivers the profound spiritual law of the heart: *“No! But upon their hearts is the rust/stain (*Rān*) of what they used to earn!”* The Prophet ﷺ explained in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī (3334) that when a servant commits a sin, a black dot is etched upon his heart; if he repents, it is polished clean; but if he continues sinning, the rust spreads until it encases the entire heart! On that Day, they will be utterly veiled from their Lord (*kallā innahum ʿan Rabbihim yawma'idhin la-maḥjūbūn*), cast into the Blaze, and told: *“This is what you used to deny!”*

3. The Exalted Registry of ʿIlliyyīn and the Divine Wine (18–36): Unveils the destiny of the righteous (*al-abrār*): their record is in *ʿIlliyyīn* (the highest, loftiest celestial registers), witnessed by the angels brought near. They recline on couches gazing upon splendor, recognized by the radiant freshness of delight on their faces, given to drink of a sealed nectar whose seal is pure musk (*khitāmuhū misk*)—*“and for this let the competitors compete (*wa-fī dhālika fa-l-yatanafas al-mutanāfisūn*)!”*—mixed with the water of *Tasnīm*, a spring from which those brought nearest drink. The chapter concludes by inverting the laughter of the world: on earth, the arrogant criminals laughed at the humble believers, winked at them in mockery, and branded them astray; but on the Day of Judgment, *“the believers will laugh at the disbelievers, on couches gazing: Have the disbelievers not been rewarded for what they used to do?”*

The historical occasion of al-Muṭaffifīn is preserved in Sunan Ibn Mājah (2223) by Ibn ʿAbbās: *“When the Prophet ﷺ arrived in Madinah, its people were the worst of mankind in weighing and measuring. Then Allah revealed: 'Woe to the defrauders!' Whereupon they adjusted their scales and became the most scrupulous of men in measuring after that.”* In ancient Yathrib, the powerful commercial cartels operated two sets of scales and weights: a heavy, generous weight used when buying goods from peasants to cheat them of their produce, and a light, shaved weight used when selling goods to the public.

## Verse 83:1

> Woe to the defrauders

**Wayl**

*Wayl* is a noun of calamity — woe, ruin, destruction — and the Qur'an uses it as the opening or refrain of a rebuke more than twenty times. It is not a verb and it does not invoke; it declares. The construction *waylun li-* states that woe belongs to a specified group, and the belonging is presented as a fact rather than as a wish. Nothing in the word indicates who utters the woe or whether it is a curse pronounced by someone else. Its force comes from its declarative form: ruin is the condition of these people, and the verse records the condition. The chapters that use it as an opening — al-Humazah, al-Mursalāt, al-Muṭaffifīn — are all short Makkan or early Madinan passages in which the rebuke is the whole of the first movement.

**Why Measure Is the Test Case**

The Qur'an's legislation on weights and measures is extensive and it appears in the mouths of prophets. Shuʿayb is sent to Midian with the command to give full measure and weigh with justice, and not to defraud people of their property (11:85 — *“O my people! Give full measure and weigh with justice. Do not defraud people of their property, nor go about spreading corruption in the land”*). The same command is given to the believers generally: give in full when you measure, and weigh with an even balance (17:35 — *“Give in full when you measure, and weigh with an even balance. That is fairest and best in the end”*). Sūrat al-Raḥmān places the balance inside the cosmic order — God raised the sky and set the balance so that people would not exceed it (55:7–9 — *“As for the sky, He raised it ˹high˺, and set the balance ˹of justice˺”*). The measure is therefore not a technical detail of trade but the visible instance of a principle that governs the whole of creation. A chapter that opens with the defrauders opens with the point at which that principle is most easily violated and least easily detected.

**A Verdict Delivered in Three Words**

The verse is a complete sentence: woe, to, the defrauders. It contains no verb of accusation, no evidence, and no procedure, and the absence is what distinguishes a verdict from a case. The chapters that open with *wayl* all share this feature — they begin at the point where a trial has already concluded, and the reader arrives after the finding. Sūrat al-Humazah opens the same way, with woe to every backbiter and slanderer who amasses wealth and counts it repeatedly (104:1–2 — *“Woe to every backbiter, slanderer”*), and the description that follows there is as specific as the one in verses 2 and 3 of this chapter. Both chapters state the ruin first and the conduct second, and both leave the conduct without an explicit prohibition. Nothing says do not defraud; the verse says ruin belongs to those who do. The form is declarative rather than imperative, and the declaration is what makes the opening irreversible.

## Verse 83:2

> Those who take full measure ˹when they buy˺ from people

**A Description Begun and Not Finished**

The verse opens with *alladhīna*, those who, and it does not close. The relative clause started here is completed only in the following verse, where the second half of the behaviour is given. The Arabic therefore holds the reader inside a single sentence across a verse boundary, and the sentence describes a contrast. The technique is the same one used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār, where the watchers of verse 10 are named as noble recording angels only in verse 11. Splitting a description across verses forces each half to be heard separately, and here the separation is the whole point: the first half, taken alone, describes an upright buyer. It is only when the second half arrives that the behaviour is revealed as fraud.

**Taking Measure Upon People**

*Idhā iktālū ʿalā al-nās* — when they take measure upon people. The verb is the eighth form of *k-y-l*, to measure, and the eighth form carries the sense of taking for oneself: to have something measured out and received. The preposition *ʿalā*, upon, is what gives the phrase its direction. They measure upon people, that is, they take from them, and the taking is expressed as an imposition rather than a receipt. The commentators note that the preposition indicates the advantage sought: the measure is taken upon the other party, at the other party's expense. The translation renders the direction with a bracketed gloss, "when they buy", which supplies the commercial situation the Arabic implies through the verb form and the preposition together.

## Verse 83:3

> but give less when they measure or weigh for buyers

**Two Instruments**

The verse names both *kāla*, to measure by volume, and *wazana*, to weigh. Grain, dates, and liquids are measured; gold, silver, and spices are weighed. The two instruments cover the whole of the market's transactions, and the naming of both removes any suggestion that the prohibition applies to one kind of trade. The pair recurs throughout the Qur'an's legislation on the subject: give full measure and weigh with an even balance (17:35 — *“Give in full when you measure, and weigh with an even balance. That is fairest and best in the end”*); give full measure and weigh with justice (26:182 — *“Weigh with an even balance”*); weigh with justice and do not give short measure (55:9 — *“Weigh with justice, and do not give short measure”*). The consistency of the pairing across Makkan and Medinan passages indicates a settled rule, and this verse states its violation rather than the rule itself.

**The Commanded Standard**

The positive form of the same standard appears in several places, and the wording is nearly identical each time. *“Give in full when you measure, and weigh with an even balance. That is fairest and best in the end”* (17:35). *“Give full measure, and cause no loss ˹to others˺. Weigh with an even balance, and do not defraud people of their property. Nor go about spreading corruption in the land”* (26:181–183). The second passage joins the fraud to corruption in the land, which is the Qur'an's widest term for social disorder. Sūrat al-Anʿām places the command inside a list of fundamental obligations alongside the treatment of orphans, just speech, and the keeping of covenants (6:152 — *“Give full measure and weigh with justice.”*). The measure is therefore not a minor regulation but one of the things the revelation groups with its largest commands.

**Shuʿayb and Midian**

The offence has a prophetic history. *“And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shuʿayb. He said, ‘O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him. And do not give short measure and weight. I do see you in prosperity now, but I truly fear for you the torment of an overwhelming Day’”* (11:84). The command to full measure follows immediately on the command to worship, and the two are joined in one speech. The verse continues with the same pairing found in this sūrah: give full measure and weigh with justice, do not defraud people of their property (11:85 — *“O my people! Give full measure and weigh with justice. Do not defraud people of their property, nor go about spreading corruption in the land”*). A people destroyed for this practice are named in the Qur'an, and the naming gives this chapter's opening its precedent — the defrauders are not a new category but a recurring one with a known outcome.

**The Balance Set in the Sky**

Sūrat al-Raḥmān places the instrument of weighing inside the created order: *“As for the sky, He raised it ˹high˺, and set the balance ˹of justice˺ so that you do not defraud the scales. Weigh with justice, and do not give short measure”* (55:7–9). The balance is set by God alongside the raising of the sky, and the sequence makes the market scale a participation in a cosmic arrangement rather than a human convention. To defraud the scale is therefore to act against something established in the structure of the world. This verse describes the act without that framing, but the framing is available to a reader who has heard Sūrat al-Raḥmān, and the availability raises the small commercial offence to the level the chapter's later verses require.

**The Shortfall as Corruption in the Land**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ joins the two in a single command: do not defraud people of their property, nor go about spreading corruption in the land (26:183 — *“and do not defraud people of their property. Nor go about spreading corruption in the land”*). The joining is not a comparison but an identification — the fraud is the corruption, stated at two scales. Shuʿayb's warning to Midian makes the same connection when he tells a prosperous people that he fears for them the torment of an overwhelming Day (11:84 — *“I do see you in prosperity now, but I truly fear for you the torment of an overwhelming Day”*), and the fear is for the society rather than for the individual merchant.

## Verse 83:4

> Do such people not think that they will be resurrected

**Resurrected**

*Mabʿūthūn* is the passive participle of *b-ʿ-th*, to send, raise, and bring forth. The same root is used of the graves being emptied and their contents brought out, and it is the Qur'an's standard verb for the raising of the dead. The passive form is important: they will be raised, not raise themselves, and the raising is done to them by an agent who is not named here. The agent is named in the verse after next, as the Lord of the worlds. The participle's plurality matches the plural subject of the question, and the state it means is one of having been brought forth — a completed condition rather than an event in progress. The verse asks whether these people reckon on having been raised.

**The Same Diagnosis Elsewhere**

Sūrat al-Nabaʾ gives the same diagnosis in a single line about the disbelievers: they never expected any reckoning (78:27 — *“For they never expected any reckoning”*). The absence of expectation is presented there as the whole of their error, and the verse that follows it states that everything has been recorded in a book. The correspondence with this chapter is close: an absent expectation, a written record, and a Day on which the record is produced. Sūrat al-Muddaththir describes the same class of people as those who do not fear the hereafter, and Sūrat al-Qiyāmah asks whether man supposes that he will be left neglected. The question in this verse belongs to a settled line of argument in the Makkan preaching, and the argument's target is always the expectation rather than the information.

## Verse 83:5

> for a tremendous Day—

**The Preposition of Appointment**

The verse consists of three words: *li-yawmin ʿaẓīm* — for a tremendous Day. The preposition *li-* carries the sense of appointment, purpose, and destination: the resurrecting is for a Day, oriented toward it, ending at it. The resurrection is therefore not described as an event in itself but as a movement with a terminus. What the person is raised for is the Day, and the Day is what gives the raising its meaning. The preposition also connects this verse grammatically to the previous one, since the verb of resurrection governs it. Read together: do they not think they will be resurrected for a tremendous Day. The whole weight of the statement falls on the adjective, and the adjective is the verse's only content.

**An Overwhelming Day in the Mouth of a Prophet**

The same adjective appears in Shuʿayb's warning to Midian, in the passage that this sūrah's opening recalls: *“I do see you in prosperity now, but I truly fear for you the torment of an overwhelming Day”* (11:84). The correspondence is exact in substance — a people prospering through short measure are warned of a tremendous day — and the warning is given by a prophet who has already told them to weigh with justice. Nothing in this verse names Midian or Shuʿayb, and the naming is unnecessary for a reader who knows the story. The recurrence of the adjective binds the two passages, and the binding means that the defrauders addressed here are being placed in a category with a known precedent and a known outcome.

## Verse 83:6

> the Day ˹all˺ people will stand before the Lord of all worlds

**Standing**

*Yaqūmu* is from *q-w-m*, to stand, rise, and be established. The standing described here is the Qur'an's standard posture for the Day: people standing, rising from their graves, standing for the reckoning, standing before their Lord. The verb means a posture held rather than a movement completed. The people have arrived and they remain upright, and the remaining is what the verse describes. Nothing is said about how long they stand, what they are standing on, or what happens while they stand. The posture alone carries the meaning: a person standing before a lord is a person awaiting a decision he cannot make. The Qur'an's other descriptions of the Day fill in the posture — eyes downcast, voices hushed, no speaking except by permission.

**The Name and the Prayer**

A Muslim who prays recites *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh rabbi al-ʿālamīn* in every unit of his prayer, and the phrase is the second of the Qur'an (1:2 — *“All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds”*). The recurrence of that phrase at the end of a sentence about short measure places the market inside the prayer. The person who takes a full measure and gives a short one has said the Lord of the worlds many times, and the saying has not altered his practice. The verse does not reproach him for the prayer or suggest that the prayer is invalid; it simply uses the name as the term of the standing, and the use makes the name a fact about the Day rather than a formula of worship. The connection between the two is left for the reader, and the leaving is more effective than any statement would be.

## Verse 83:7

> But no! The wicked are certainly bound for Sijjîn ˹in the depths of Hell˺—

**The Particle of Refusal**

*Kallā* opens the second movement of the chapter, and its function is the same as in Sūrat al-Infiṭār, where it refused the denial of the Judgement after three verses describing the gifts of creation. Here it follows six verses about the defrauders and the Day on which all people stand before the Lord of the worlds. What it refuses is not stated in a proposition, and the refusal is directed at the condition just described: the expectation that a shortfall of measure can coexist with standing before the Lord of the worlds cannot stand. The particle marks a break in the argument and a turn to consequence, and the turn is immediate — the very next words name a destination. Nothing in the particle's use here suggests a mild correction; it is the strongest form of negation available in the chapter's language.

**The Wicked Named**

*Al-fujjār* is the same word used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār for the class that will burn in Hell (82:14 — *“and the wicked will be in Hell”*), and its root is the root of breaking open. A *fājir* is one who has broken through a limit, and the intensive plural means a people characterized by the breaking. The word's appearance in this chapter connects the defrauders of verses 1 to 3 to a moral category rather than a commercial one. What began as a description of a market practice ends as a description of a kind of person, and the shift is carried by a single noun. The defrauder is not a merchant who made an error; he is one of the *fujjār*, and the classification places him with everyone else who has exceeded the bounds set for his conduct.

## Verse 83:8

> and what will make you realize what Sijjîn is?—

**The Idiom of Realization**

*Mā adrāka* — what will make you realize, what has informed you, what has caused you to know. The construction is the one used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār, where the Day of Judgement is asked about twice in successive verses (82:17–18 — *“What will make you realize what Judgment Day is”*), and in the short sūrahs that ask about the Striking Disaster, the Inevitable Hour, Hell, the nightly star, and the Crusher. The phrase is not a request for information but a statement that the information exceeds the addressee's reach. Something would have to be done to him for him to know, and the doing is not his. The verse therefore interrupts the description of Sijjīn with a question that the description cannot answer, and the interruption is what marks the thing described as beyond ordinary comprehension.

**Asked Once Here, Twice There**

Sūrat al-Infiṭār doubles the question; this chapter asks it once. The variation is not accidental. In al-Infiṭār the object of the question is the Day itself, which the chapter's audience has been denying; the doubling answers the denial by insisting twice. Here the object is a named place that the audience has just heard for the first time, and the single question introduces it rather than refutes a claim about it. The economy of the chapter requires the question to be short, since three separate movements — the wicked, the virtuous, and the mockers — follow within the remaining verses. A doubled question would have consumed the space the movements need. The single question does the work of marking gravity and then yields the sentence to the answer.

## Verse 83:9

> a fate ˹already˺ sealed

**Markūm**

The participle is from *r-k-m*, a root meaning to heap, pile up, stack, and press together. Applied to writing it means a document whose contents have been accumulated and then fixed — inscribed, numbered, folded, or sealed. The image is of marks layered upon marks until the surface is complete, and of a completion that cannot be added to. The commentators render the word variously as written, inscribed, numbered, and sealed, and the renderings agree in one respect: the writing is finished. Nothing further will be entered in this book. The finished state is what the translation captures with "already sealed", and the adverb is a fair addition, since the Arabic participle means a condition already holding rather than an action about to occur.

**The Record in the Qur'an's Account of the Day**

The document produced on the Day is described at length elsewhere. Every person's destiny is bound to his neck, and on the Day of Judgement a record is brought forth and found laid open, with the command to read it (17:13–14 — *“We have bound every human’s destiny to their neck. And on the Day of Judgment We will bring forth to each ˹person˺ a record which they will find laid open”*). The record in Sūrat al-Kahf leaves nothing unlisted, small or great, and the wicked see it and cry out at its completeness (18:49 — *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it.”*). Sūrat al-Infiṭār describes the keepers of the record as noble angels who write and know whatever people do (82:10–12 — *“They know whatever you do”*). The four passages agree that the document is individual, exhaustive, produced, and read by the person it concerns. This verse adds the fact of sealing, and the sealing is what makes the production on the Day a disclosure rather than a compilation. The book was finished long before it is opened.

## Verse 83:10

> Woe on that Day to the deniers—

**The Return of Woe**

*Wayl* appeared in the first verse of the chapter, attached to the defrauders, and it returns here attached to the deniers. The recurrence ties two descriptions together that the audience might have kept apart. The first movement spoke of market conduct — full measure taken, short measure given — and said nothing about belief. This verse speaks of denial and says nothing about commerce. The shared word of ruin is what joins them, and the joining is completed by the verses that follow, which define the deniers and state that none denies the Day except an evildoing transgressor. The structure of the chapter therefore identifies the defrauder and the denier as the same person described from two sides, and the identification is made by a repeated noun rather than by an argument.

**A Refrain Used Elsewhere**

Sūrat al-Mursalāt uses this exact sentence as a refrain, repeating it ten times through the chapter — *Woe on that Day to the deniers* (77:15, 77:19, and eight further occurrences — *“Woe on that Day to the deniers”*). Each repetition follows a description of a sign, a favour, or a warning, and the refrain closes each movement with the same verdict. The use of the identical sentence in this chapter, once rather than ten times, connects the two sūrahs and marks the phrase as a recognized formula of the Makkan preaching. A reader who has heard al-Mursalāt hears the phrase here as a quotation of a settled refrain, and the hearing supplies the repetition that this chapter does not perform. The phrase is therefore weightier in its single occurrence than a newly composed sentence would be.

## Verse 83:11

> those who deny Judgment Day

**Yawm al-Dīn**

The phrase combines *yawm*, day, with *dīn*, which carries the twin senses of judgement and religion. The combination is the Qur'an's standard name for the Day, and it appears in the opening sūrah as a description of God's dominion: Master of the Day of Judgement (1:4 — *“Master of the Day of Judgment”*). It appears in Sūrat al-Infiṭār four times in nineteen verses, as the thing denied, the time of the burning, and the object of the doubled question about realization (82:9 — *“But no! In fact, you deny the ˹final˺ Judgment”*, 82:15 — *“burning in it on Judgment Day”*, 82:17–18 — *“What will make you realize what Judgment Day is Again, what will make you realize what Judgment Day is”*). It appears in the mouths of prophets as the day they hope for forgiveness on (26:82 — *“And He is ˹the One˺ Who, I hope, will forgive my flaws on Judgment Day.”*) and in the mouths of their opponents as the day they mockingly ask the date of (51:12 — *“They ask ˹mockingly˺, “When is this Day of Judgment?”*). The phrase is therefore one of the most heavily used in the Qur'an, and its appearance here places this chapter's deniers inside a category named throughout the revelation.

**The Verb of Denial**

*Yukadhdhibūna* is the intensive form of the verb of calling something false, and it is in the imperfect, meaning a continuing activity. The denial is not a single act of disbelief but an ongoing practice of rejection. The intensive form adds effort and repetition: they keep calling it false, and they call it false emphatically. The same verb appears in the next verse in its singular non-intensive form — none denies it except every evildoing transgressor — where the shift to the singular describes the disposition of an individual rather than the practice of a class.

**A Relative Clause Doing the Work of a Definition**

Grammatically this verse is an appositive: those who deny the Day of Judgement, standing in for the deniers of the previous verse. The construction means that the two verses form a single utterance, and the utterance moves from a label to a description. The label *al-mukadhdhibūn* is general — anyone who calls anything false is inside it. The description restricts it to those whose falsification is directed at the Day. The restriction is what makes the woe of the previous verse precise, and the precision is necessary because the chapter has already described a different group — the defrauders — whose relation to the Day is one of failing to reckon on it rather than of denying it.

**The Exclamation**

The printed text ends the verse with an exclamation mark, and the mark reflects the Arabic construction, in which the relative clause carries an emphatic force. The exclamation is not a cry of surprise but a specification delivered with weight. The deniers are those who deny the Day of Judgement — and the naming of the object is what makes the class intelligible. A reader who has heard the phrase *yawm al-dīn* in prayer and in the mouths of prophets receives the specification as a recognition, and the recognition is what the exclamation marks. The chapter is not introducing a new idea but identifying which idea has been rejected, and the identification is emphatic because the idea is one the audience already holds.

## Verse 83:12

> None would deny it except every evildoing transgressor

**Muʿtadī**

The first adjective is from *ʿ-d-w*, a root whose field is crossing, exceeding, transgressing, and acting with hostility. The same root gives *ʿudwān*, aggression, and *iʿtidāʾ*, transgression of a limit. A *muʿtad* is one who goes beyond what is set for him, and the going beyond is not a single act but a settled pattern, which is why the form means a character rather than an event. The word is used in the Qur'an of those who exceed the bounds set in law, of aggressors in war, and of those who transgress against others' rights. Its application here places the denier in the category of the person who does not accept limits, and the refusal of limits is what makes the refusal of a Day of Judgement coherent. A limit-setter implies a limit-keeper, and a person who will not keep limits will not accept a keeper.

**The Denial as a Consequence**

The verse's logic is causal and its direction is important. Denial does not produce transgression; transgression produces denial. A person who has exceeded the bounds and accumulated sin has a stake in the absence of a reckoning, and the stake shapes his judgement about whether a reckoning exists. The Qur'an describes this mechanism elsewhere in the heart: a covering placed over it, a seal set on it, locks upon it (2:7 — *“Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment”*, 47:24 — *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Or are there locks upon their hearts”*). This chapter supplies the mechanism directly in the verse that follows, where the heart is said to be stained by what its owner used to earn. The two verses together give a complete account — the conduct is of a certain kind, and conduct of that kind produces a condition in which the Day cannot be acknowledged. Nothing in the account excuses the denier, because the conduct that produced the condition was his.

## Verse 83:13
> Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they say, “Ancient fables!”

**The Occasion of the Response**

The verse describes a situation: revelations are recited, and a response is given. The recitation is the Qur'an's standard term for its own delivery — the verses are recited, read out, proclaimed — and the term indicates an oral event witnessed by the person responding. The response is therefore not to a text encountered privately but to a performance attended in person. The situation is a public one, and the saying is a public saying. Nothing in the verse describes the content of what was recited on the occasion, and the omission generalizes the situation: whatever is recited, this is what is said. The adverb *whenever* carries the generality, and the generality means the response is a disposition rather than a reaction to a particular passage.

**Asāṭīr al-Awwalīn**

The phrase is the response in full: *asāṭīr al-awwalīn* — the fables, legends, or written tales of the ancients. The noun *asāṭīr* is a plural whose singular is disputed; the commentators connect it either to *saṭr*, a line of writing, so that it means things written down in rows, or to a root meaning to spread or compose tales. Either derivation produces the same sense: stories set down by earlier people. The addition of *al-awwalīn*, the ancients or the former peoples, dates them and dismisses them in one move. What is old and written by predecessors is not revelation; it is inherited literature. The phrase is a genre claim rather than a refutation, and its force lies in the confidence with which the genre is assigned.

**A Phrase Repeated Across the Qur'an**

The same words appear in at least eight other passages, and the repetition marks the phrase as the standard dismissal of the early opponents. The disbelievers argue with the Prophet saying this Qur'an is nothing but ancient fables (6:25 — *“The disbelievers would ˹even˺ come to argue with you, saying, “This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but ancient fables!”*). They claim they have already heard it and could produce something similar (8:31 — *“This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but ancient fables!”*). Asked what their Lord has revealed, they answer ancient fables (16:24 — *“And when it is said to them, “What has your Lord revealed?” They say, “Ancient fables!”*). The phrase is put in the mouths of those who were promised it before, along with their forefathers (23:83 — *“We have already been promised this, as well as our forefathers earlier. This is nothing but ancient fables!”*, 27:68 — *“We have already been promised this, as well as our forefathers earlier. This is nothing but ancient fables!”*).

It is described as something written down and rehearsed morning and evening (25:5 — *“And they say, “˹These revelations are only˺ ancient fables which he has had written down, and they are rehearsed to him morning and evening.”*); an unbelieving son scolds his parents with it (46:17 — *“But some scold their parents, “Enough with you!”*); and a man whose wealth and children are described says it whenever the revelations are recited to him (68:15 — *“whenever Our revelations are recited to him, he says, “Ancient fables!”*). The recurrence shows that the Qur'an records the dismissal verbatim and repeatedly rather than paraphrasing it once.

**The Shift to the Singular**

The Arabic of this verse is singular: whenever Our revelations are recited to *him*, *he says*. The translation renders it in the plural to match the deniers of the previous verses, and the rendering is defensible because the singular functions generically. But the Arabic singular does specific work. It describes one person responding, and the person is the type named in verse 12 — every evildoing transgressor. The singular also matches Sūrat al-Qalam's description of the man who says ancient fables whenever the revelations are recited to him (68:15 — *“whenever Our revelations are recited to him, he says, “Ancient fables!”*), where the singular is clearly individual. The movement from the plural class to the singular instance is characteristic of this chapter, which repeatedly converts a collective accusation into a description of one person so that the reader can recognize himself.

---
## Verse 83:14

> But no! In fact, their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit

**Two Particles in Succession**

The verse opens with *kallā bal* — no, but rather. The two particles perform different work and their combination is emphatic. *Kallā* refuses what has just been said or implied; *bal* replaces it with something else. Together they deny the preceding claim and substitute a diagnosis. What is refused is the account given in verse 13: that the revelations are ancient fables. What is substituted is a statement about the condition of the person who says so. The construction is the Qur'an's standard form of correction, and its appearance here means that the dismissal of the previous verse is not being answered on its own terms. No argument about the age or origin of the recitation is offered. The verse changes the subject from the text to the reader of the text, and the change is the refutation.

**The Same Condition Described Elsewhere**

The Qur'an describes the obstructed heart in several images, and the images agree on the causality. God has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered (2:7 — *“Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment”*). The hearts of some were sealed for their disbelief, in connection with covenant-breaking, rejection of signs, and killing the prophets (4:155 — *“˹They were condemned˺ for breaking their covenant, rejecting Allah’s signs, killing the prophets unjustly, and for saying, “Our hearts are unreceptive!”—it is Allah Who has sealed their hearts for their disbelief, so they do not believe except for a few”*). The question is asked whether people do not reflect on the Qur'an, or whether there are locks upon their hearts (47:24 — *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Or are there locks upon their hearts”*). Veils are cast over hearts, leaving them unable to understand, with deafness in the ears (6:25 — *“There are some of them who ˹pretend to˺ listen to your recitation ˹of the Quran˺, but We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears.”*). The four passages differ in image — sealing, locking, veiling, covering — and agree in attaching the condition to prior conduct. This verse contributes the image of accumulation, which is the one that most clearly explains how the condition forms over time.

---

## Verse 83:15

> Undoubtedly, they will be sealed off from their Lord on that Day

**On That Day**

*Yawmaʾidhin* — on that Day. The same temporal specification used in verse 10 for the woe of the deniers returns here, and the recurrence ties the veiling to the Day of Judgement rather than to the present life. Nothing in the verse says that they are veiled from their Lord now, and the limitation is important. In this life the access is available; the covering on the heart obstructs recognition but does not close the door, and the door is closed on the Day. The specification of the date therefore preserves the possibility of repentance in the present while removing it in the future. The chapter's argument throughout has been that the deniers have time now and will not have it then, and this verse states the second half of that claim with a temporal marker rather than an urging.

**The Gates Not Opened**

Sūrat al-Aʿrāf describes the same exclusion in a different image: *“Surely those who receive our revelations with denial and arrogance, the gates of heaven will not be opened for them, nor will they enter Paradise until a camel passes through the eye of a needle”* (7:40). The closed gate and the veil are two pictures of one condition, and both are attached to the same causes — denial and arrogance. The impossibility stated in the second half of that verse, a camel passing through a needle's eye, is the measure of the exclusion's finality. This verse makes no comparison and states the condition plainly, and the plainness is more severe than the image. A person told that a gate will not open can hope for it to be opened later; a person told that he will be veiled from his Lord on that Day has been given a date.

**The Contrast With Those Brought Near**

The chapter's other class is described in verses 21 and 28 as witnessed by, and drinking with, those nearest to God — *al-muqarrabūn*, the ones brought near. Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah names the same group and places them at the head of the people of Paradise: the foremost in faith will be the foremost, and they are the ones nearest to God (56:10–11 — *“and the foremost ˹in faith˺ will be the foremost ˹in Paradise˺”*).

---

## Verse 83:16

> Moreover, they will surely burn in Hell

**Ṣāluhā**

The verb is from *ṣ-l-y*, the root used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār for the wicked burning in the Fire on the Day of Judgement (82:15 — *“burning in it on Judgment Day”*). The same root means exposure to fire, entering it, and undergoing its heat. Its active form here attributes the burning to the subjects rather than to an agent who applies it: they burn in it. The attribution is consistent with the chapter's treatment of responsibility throughout — the record is what they earned, the covering on the heart is what they accumulated, and the burning is what they undergo in a voice that makes it their own.

**The Third Consequence**

Verses 15 and 16 state two consequences of the denial, and the second is the one that concerns the body. The first was exclusion — veiled off from their Lord. The second is burning. The order is significant: access is closed before pain is inflicted, and the closure is described in the verse that precedes rather than the one that follows. A reader who encounters the burning first would receive it as the whole of the punishment; encountering the veiling first, he receives the burning as an addition to a loss that is already complete. The chapter's arrangement therefore treats separation from God as the primary fact and the Fire as what accompanies it, which is the reverse of the ordinary expectation.

**Jaḥīm**

The name of the Fire here is *jaḥīm*, the intensely blazing fire, the same name used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār for the enclosure of the wicked (82:14 — *“and the wicked will be in Hell”*). The word specifies heat and glare rather than depth or duration, and its recurrence across the two neighbouring chapters marks it as the preferred name in this strand of the Makkan preaching. The Qur'an has other names for the Fire — *saʿīr*, *laẓā*, *saqar*, *ḥuṭamah* — and the choice among them is not random. *Jaḥīm* belongs to the vocabulary of these two chapters, which are concerned with the Day's reckoning rather than with the Fire's architecture. The name appears here without description, and the absence of description is possible because the name has already been used in the chapter that precedes this one.

---

## Verse 83:17

> and then be told, “This is what you used to deny.”

**What You Used to Deny**

The verb is imperfect with *kānū*, meaning a continuous past activity: what you were in the habit of denying, what you kept denying. The construction recalls the intensive present of verse 11, where the deniers are those who keep calling the Day false. The two tenses join: the habitual denial in this life is met by the announcement in the next, and the announcement uses the same verb. Nothing in the verse says that the denial was mistaken or that the deniers have been proven wrong; it says only that this is what they denied. The proof is the presence of the thing rather than an argument about it, and the presence is what makes the announcement unnecessary as information and devastating as recognition.

**The Same Announcement Elsewhere**

The Qur'an uses this form of address to the people of the Fire in several places. In Sūrat al-Anʿām the deniers are asked whether this is not the truth, and they answer that it is, by their Lord, and are then told to taste the punishment for what they used to deny (6:30 — *“He will ask ˹them˺, “Is this ˹Hereafter˺ not the truth?”*). In Sūrat Yūnus the same structure appears: the announcement that the promise of God has come true, followed by the settling of the account. The recurrence establishes the announcement as a fixed element of the Day's procedure — a moment in which the people concerned are made to state the identity between their denial and their condition. This verse gives the announcement in its shortest form, and the shortness is consistent with the chapter's method of stating the fact and leaving the elaboration to other passages.

---

## Verse 83:18

> But no! The virtuous are certainly bound for ’Illiyûn ˹in elevated Gardens˺—

**al-Abrār**

The class named is the same one described in Sūrat al-Infiṭār as being in bliss (82:13 — *“Indeed, the virtuous will be in bliss”*), and the word is the plural of *barr*, one who is good, upright, and generous in his dealings. The root *b-r-r* means the widest kind of goodness — truthfulness, kindness, piety, and the fulfilment of obligations. Its use here, immediately after a movement about the wicked, sets the two classes in the terms the chapter has been using: *fujjār* and *abrār*, those who break bounds and those who keep them. The pairing is not only oppositional; it is exhaustive within the chapter's framework, and every person described in it belongs to one of the two. The defrauders of the opening verses are being classified here, by implication, as *fujjār*.

**ʿIlliyūn**

The name occurs only in this chapter, twice — here and in the question of the following verse. Its form is a plural pattern meaning elevation, and the commentators connect it to *ʿuluww*, height and loftiness, so that it means the highest places. The bracketed gloss in the translation, "in elevated Gardens", supplies an identification the Arabic leaves open. The name's uniqueness is matched by Sijjīn's uniqueness, and the two unique names form a pair: one derived from descent and confinement, the other from ascent and height. The chapter therefore gives each class a proper name that exists nowhere else in the Qur'an, and the naming is what makes the two destinations concrete rather than general. A place with a name is a place, and the naming is the chapter's way of insisting on it.

---

## Verse 83:19

> and what will make you realize what ’Illiyûn is?—

**The Idiom Used Twice in One Chapter**

The question is the same one asked about Sijjīn ten verses earlier, and its repetition establishes a pattern within the chapter. Each unique name — the one given nowhere else in the Qur'an for the destination of the wicked, the one given nowhere else for the destination of the virtuous — is followed by the phrase that marks incomprehensibility. The pattern tells the reader that neither name can be understood from its sound, its derivation, or its associations. Both require disclosure, and both receive the same partial disclosure in the form of a sealed record. The doubling of the phrase within a single chapter is unusual, and the unusualness is what signals that the chapter is introducing two terms rather than recalling one.

**Applied to Elevation as to Descent**

The same phrase was applied to Sijjīn, a name of confinement and depth, and is now applied to ʿIlliyūn, a name of height. The application to both directions is significant. The phrase is ordinarily used in the Qur'an for matters of terror and magnitude — the Hour, the Fire, the Striking Disaster, the Crusher — and its use here for a destination of reward extends its range. What the extension indicates is that the incomprehensibility belongs to the unseen as such, not to its unpleasant parts. The elevated place exceeds the listener's realization in the same way and to the same degree as the low one. Nothing in the verse distinguishes the two cases, and the undifferentiated application is what gives the reward its weight.

---

## Verse 83:20

> a fate ˹already˺ sealed

**A Record Rather Than a Place**

The substitution of a document for a location was discussed in connection with Sijjīn, and it applies with equal force here. ʿIlliyūn is a name of elevation, and the answer to the question about it is a book. The elevation is therefore not mainly spatial; it is the elevation of a record and of the people associated with it. A reader expecting a description of heights, gardens, and light receives the name of a document, and the receipt reorients the whole movement. What follows in verses 22 to 28 is indeed a description of gardens, couches, faces, wine, and a spring — but the description is preceded by the document, and the precedence means that the description is what the document entitles its holders to.

**Between the Question and the Witness**

The verse occupies a position between two others that add what it does not contain. Verse 19 asked the question, and verse 21 supplies the witness. This verse gives the document, and the document is what the question and the witness both concern. Without it the question would have no answer and the witness would have nothing to witness. The verse is therefore the load-bearing element of the movement, and its three words carry the whole of it. The economy is characteristic of the chapter's second half, which consistently states its most important facts in its shortest verses. The two longest statements of the movement — the description of the couches and the description of the wine — come after, and they elaborate what this verse has already fixed.

---

## Verse 83:21

> witnessed by those nearest ˹to Allah˺

**The Single Difference**

Verses 7 to 9 and verses 18 to 21 are parallel in almost every element: the particle of refusal, the class named, an unique destination, the phrase of realization, and a sealed record. This verse is the only element present in the second sequence and absent from the first. Nothing further is said of the sealed book belonging to the wicked. That of the virtuous is sealed and it is also witnessed. The addition is what distinguishes the two movements, and its position as the last word of the parallel sequence makes it impossible to miss. A reader who has followed both movements arrives at this verse expecting the same three words he received before, and receives an extra clause instead. The extra clause is the chapter's statement of what the virtuous have that the wicked do not.

**Al-Muqarrabūn**

The witnesses are *al-muqarrabūn*, the ones brought near. The participle is passive and intensive: those who have been caused to draw near, the people of proximity. The rank is named elsewhere in the Qur'an, and its naming there confirms its status. Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah divides humanity into three groups and places the foremost at the head: the foremost in faith will be the foremost in Paradise, and they are the ones nearest to God (56:10–11 — *“and the foremost ˹in faith˺ will be the foremost ˹in Paradise˺”*). The same word describes the soul at the moment of death when the deceased is one of those brought near (56:88 — *“So, if the deceased is one of those brought near ˹to Us˺”*). The rank is therefore recognized and exalted, and its members are the witnesses named in this verse. Nothing here describes them further, and the description is unnecessary for a reader who knows the term.

**Nearness Against Veiling**

The chapter's other movement ended with the wicked veiled off from their Lord (83:15 — *“Undoubtedly, they will be sealed off from their Lord on that Day”*). This movement contains the people brought near. The two participles are exact opposites in the same semantic field of proximity and access: *maḥjūbūn* and *muqarrabūn*. The opposition is the chapter's central contrast, and it is stated in two words eleven verses apart.

**Witnesses of Rank**

The witnessing is performed by the highest order of beings, and the choice of witnesses is what confers honour on the document. A record witnessed by nobodies is a private paper; a record witnessed by those brought near is a public attestation before the court of the Day. The Qur'an describes the same rank as inheriting Gardens of Delight, being a multitude from earlier generations and a few from later (56:12–14 — *“and a few from later generations”*), and the scarcity of the later generations makes the witnessing more distinguished rather than less. Those who attend the record of the virtuous are few, and their fewness is a measure of the value of what they attend. Nothing in this verse describes their appearance or their number, and the withholding keeps the attention on the fact of the witnessing rather than on the witnesses.

---

## Verse 83:22

> Surely the virtuous will be in bliss

**The Resumption After the Document**

Verses 18 to 21 named the destination, asked about it, and answered with a sealed record witnessed by those brought near. This verse resumes the description of the class itself, and the resumption is marked by a return to the emphatic construction: *inna al-abrār la-fī naʿīm*. The particle *inna* and the *lām* of the predicate produce the doubly emphasized statement used throughout the chapter for its central claims. The emphasis is not new information but a settling of what has been established. The record has been named; the state of its holders is now declared. The declaration is the point from which the following six verses unfold, and each of them adds one feature of the state.

**Naʿīm**

The word means delight, ease, softness of life, and the condition of one whose needs are met and exceeded. Its root is used of a life free of hardship and of the enjoyment that follows sufficiency. In the Qur'an it names both the pleasures of this world, which are temporary and will be asked about (102:8 — *“Then, on that Day, you will definitely be questioned about ˹your worldly˺ pleasures”*), and the condition of Paradise, which is what this verse describes. The same word in the two contexts is what makes the verse's claim intelligible: the delight known in this life is a name borrowed from the delight that is proper. Nothing in the verse specifies the content of the bliss, and the unspecified noun allows the following verses to fill it with couches, faces, wine, musk, and a spring.

---

## Verse 83:23

> ˹seated˺ on ˹canopied˺ couches, gazing around

**The First Particular**

Verse 22 declared the bliss; this verse gives its first feature. The movement from declaration to description is immediate, and the description begins with a posture. The virtuous are on couches, and they are looking. Nothing is said yet about what they see, what they eat, or what they drink; the verse establishes where they are and what they are doing. The choice of a posture as the opening particular is significant, because a posture indicates a state of the body over time. Sitting on a couch is not an action but a condition sustained, and the condition is rest. The chapter's description of reward therefore begins with the absence of exertion, which is the first thing a person who has laboured in the market would recognize as different.

**Arāʾik**

The word is the plural of *arīkah*, a couch or bed furnished with a canopy or curtains — a piece of furniture associated in Arabic usage with brides, with royalty, and with the ease of a person who does not need to rise. The translation supplies the canopy in brackets because the word's sense includes it. The Qur'an uses the same word in several descriptions of Paradise: the believers and their spouses reclining in shade on canopied couches (36:56 — *“They and their spouses will be in ˹cool˺ shade, reclining on ˹canopied˺ couches”*); the people of the Gardens of Eternity reclining on them (18:31 — *“It is they who will have the Gardens of Eternity, with rivers flowing under their feet.”*); the virtuous reclining on them, never seeing scorching heat or bitter cold (76:13 — *“There they will be reclining on ˹canopied˺ couches, never seeing scorching heat or bitter cold”*). The recurrence marks the couch as a fixed element of the Qur'an's depiction, and its recurrence across Makkan and Medinan passages indicates a settled image rather than an incidental detail.

**The Couch in the Qur'an's Depiction**

The image belongs to a small set of furnishings that recur in the descriptions of Paradise: couches, cushions, carpets, vessels, cups, and thrones raised high. Sūrat al-Ghāshiyah lists several of them in sequence — thrones raised high, cups set at hand, cushions lined up, carpets spread out (88:13–16 — *“and cups set at hand”*). Sūrat al-Raḥmān describes the believers reclining on furnishings lined with rich brocade, with the fruit of both Gardens hanging within reach (55:54 — *“Those ˹believers˺ will recline on furnishings lined with rich brocade. And the fruit of both Gardens will hang within reach”*). The set is consistent and its consistency means that each item carries the others. This verse contributes the couch and the gaze, and the gaze is its distinctive addition — the furnishings are described elsewhere, but the looking of the people on them is this chapter's contribution to the image.

---

## Verse 83:24

> You will recognize on their faces the glow of delight

**Naḍrah**

The word means brightness, freshness, radiance, and the bloom of a face that is well-provided for. Its root is used of vegetation that is lush and of a complexion that is healthy and luminous. The translation renders it as glow, which carries both the light and the health. The word is attached here to *naʿīm*, the bliss of the previous verse, so that the phrase is the glow or bloom of delight. The construction makes the inner state visible by naming its effect on the face, and the naming is precise: not a smile, not an expression, but a quality of the skin and the countenance. The Qur'an uses the same root elsewhere for the faces of that Day — faces glowing with bliss, fully pleased with their striving (88:8–9 — *“˹fully˺ pleased with their striving”*).

**The Same Image in al-Ghāshiyah**

Sūrat al-Ghāshiyah opens by dividing the faces of that Day into two groups: faces humiliated, labouring, exhausted, entering a scorching fire; and faces on that Day glowing with bliss, fully pleased with their striving (88:2–9 — *“˹fully˺ pleased with their striving”*). The division by faces is the same technique used here, and its recurrence shows that the Qur'an's account of the Day habitually reads states from countenances. The addition in al-Ghāshiyah is the pleasure with striving, which supplies the cause of the glow as effort well spent. This verse supplies the cause as bliss itself. The two accounts agree that the face is a document, and the agreement is what makes the sealed record of verse 20 intelligible as a description of persons rather than of paper.

---

## Verse 83:25

> They will be given a drink of sealed, pure wine

**A Passive of Giving**

The verb is *yusqawna* — they will be given drink, they will be watered. The passive form is important: the virtuous do not get the drink, acquire it, or pour it. It is given to them. The passivity is consistent with the posture of the previous two verses — reclining on couches, gazing — and it establishes the character of the whole scene. Nothing in the description requires an act from its inhabitants. They are seated, they are recognized, and they are given.

**Raḥīq**

The word means a pure, choice wine — specifically, wine that is unmixed and free of dregs, the best of what is drunk. Its use distinguishes the drink of Paradise from the wine of this world in the one respect the Qur'an consistently stresses: purity. The wine of this world is described elsewhere as causing the loss of reason and as the work of Satan, and the wine of Paradise is described as delicious to drink and as not containing anything that harms. Sūrat Muḥammad lists it among the rivers of Paradise, alongside water that does not stagnate, milk whose taste does not change, and clarified honey (47:15 — *“The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that in it are rivers of fresh water, rivers of milk that never changes in taste, rivers of wine delicious to drink, and rivers of pure honey.”*). The inclusion of wine in that list, and the specification of purity here, together establish that the drink named in this verse shares only its name with the prohibited one.

**The Drink in the Qur'an's Depiction of Paradise**

The image belongs to a set that recurs: rivers of wine, cups of crystal filled precisely as desired, silver vessels, a drink flavoured with camphor, a spring from which God's servants drink flowing at their will (76:5–6 — *“˹from˺ a spring where Allah’s servants will drink, flowing at their will”*, 76:15–16 — *“crystalline silver, filled precisely as desired”*). Sūrat al-Ghāshiyah lists cups set at hand among the furnishings of the elevated Garden (88:14 — *“and cups set at hand”*). The set is consistent and its items are shared across chapters, which means that each occurrence carries the others. This verse contributes the sealed pure wine and the musk that follows it, and the contribution is distinctive in one respect: the sealing. No other description of the drink of Paradise specifies that the vessel is closed, and the specification belongs to a chapter whose whole vocabulary is about documents that have been finished and closed.

---

## Verse 83:26

> whose last sip will smell like musk. So let whoever aspires to this strive ˹diligently˺

**Khitāmuhū Misk**

The phrase is *khitāmuhu misk* — its seal is musk, its closing is musk. The commentators explain it in two related ways: the vessel is sealed with musk rather than with clay, and the last of the drink, the final sip or the dregs that remain at the bottom of the cup, has the fragrance of musk. The translation adopts the second, and both readings agree that the musk is at the end. The placement of the finest element last is a deliberate reversal of ordinary drinking, in which the best is at the beginning and the dregs at the bottom. A drink whose seal and whose remainder are musk improves toward its end, and the improvement is what the following urging appeals to. Nothing in this life behaves that way, and the difference is stated in three words.

**Competition as a Commanded Attitude**

The Qur'an commands competition in several places and always attaches it to a spiritual object. Compete with one another in good works, with the assurance that God will bring everyone together (2:148 — *“So compete with one another in doing good.”*). Compete for forgiveness from your Lord and a Garden as vast as the heavens and the earth (57:21 — *“˹So˺ compete with one another for forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those who believe in Allah and His messengers.”*). Hasten toward forgiveness and the same Garden (3:133 — *“And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those mindful ˹of Allah˺”*). The commands are addressed to people who already compete — for wealth, for status, for measure and weight — and the redirection is the whole of the instruction. This verse performs the same redirection in the shortest form: in that, let the aspirants aspire. The object is the sealed wine and its musk, and the object's desirability has been established by two verses of description before the command is given.

---

## Verse 83:27

> And this drink’s flavour will come from Tasnîm—

The name is derived by the commentators from *sanam*, height or elevation, so that Tasnīm means something that rises or flows from above, and the form of the word means an elevation conferred. The sense of a drink that comes down from a height is what the following verse develops, since a spring from which the nearest drink is a source above them.

**Mizāj**

The word translated as flavour is *mizāj*, from *m-z-j*, to mix, blend, and adulterate. It means the admixture that gives a drink its character — what is mixed into it to make it what it is. The Qur'an uses the same word in Sūrat al-Insān for a cup whose mixture is camphor (76:5 — *“Indeed, the virtuous will have a drink ˹of pure wine˺—flavoured with camphor”*) and for a drink whose mixture is ginger (76:17 — *“And they will be given a drink ˹of pure wine˺ flavoured with ginger”*). The recurrence establishes *mizāj* as the technical term for the composition of Paradise's drinks, and its use here identifies Tasnīm as the mixing element of the sealed wine. The wine of verse 25 is pure; its mixture is from Tasnīm; and the mixture is what the next verse describes as a spring drunk by those brought near. The composition is therefore specified in two verses without being fully disclosed.

**The Hierarchy Inside the Reward**

The verse introduces a distinction that the following one specifies: the drink of Tasnīm is associated with those brought near. The sealed wine of verse 25 is given to the virtuous generally; the spring of verse 28 is drunk by the nearest. The distinction implies ranks within Paradise, and the ranks are named elsewhere in the Qur'an — the foremost being the ones nearest to God, in Gardens of Delight, a throng from the earlier peoples and a few from the later (56:10–14 — *“and a few from later generations”*). This chapter does not elaborate the hierarchy; it marks it with two verses and moves on. The marking is significant because it means the reward described in verses 22 to 26 is not the whole of Paradise, and that something further belongs to a rank above the one being described.

---

## Verse 83:28

> a spring from which those nearest ˹to Allah˺ will drink

**The Description of Tasnīm**

Verse 27 named Tasnīm as the source of the drink's mixture and left it on a dash. This verse supplies what it is: a spring. The disclosure is a category rather than a description — the reader learns that the name means a flowing source of water, and nothing further. No location, no volume, no appearance, and no account of how a spring produces the mixture of a wine. The economy matches the treatment of the chapter's other two unique names, each of which was answered with a sealed book and no elaboration.

**A Spring and an Eye**

The word *ʿayn* means both an eye and a spring, and the double sense belongs to the same root. A spring is called an eye because it is the point at which water becomes visible, the source from which it looks out at the world. The Qur'an uses the word for the springs of Paradise repeatedly and for the springs of the earth as signs of provision. Its application here places Tasnīm in the category of the sources described elsewhere — a spring whose water the servants of God drink, flowing at their will (76:6 — *“˹from˺ a spring where Allah’s servants will drink, flowing at their will”*), and a spring called Salsabīl in the same sūrah (76:18 — *“from a spring there, called Salsabîl”*). The chapter's named source joins a small group of named springs in the Qur'an's depiction of Paradise, and the group is one of the few sets of proper names the depiction contains.

**The Same Rank Elsewhere**

Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah names the group and gives its inheritance: the foremost in faith will be the foremost in Paradise, and they are the ones nearest to God, in Gardens of Delight, a multitude from earlier generations and a few from later (56:10–14 — *“and a few from later generations”*). The same word describes the soul at death when the deceased is one of those brought near, for whom there is serenity, fragrance, and a Garden of Bliss (56:88–89 — *“So, if the deceased is one of those brought near ˹to Us˺”*). The two passages agree that nearness is a rank above the general company of the people of the right hand, and that it carries a distinct provision. This chapter's use of the term is therefore not an innovation but a reference to an established category, and the reference allows the verse to say very little and mean a great deal. This verse closes the movement on the virtuous, which ran from the particle of refusal in verse 18 through the unique name, the question, the sealed record, the witnesses, the bliss, the couches, the faces, the wine, the musk, the urging, the mixture, and the spring.

## Verse 83:29

> Indeed, the wicked used to laugh at the believers

**A Habit in the Past**

The construction combines the emphatic particle *inna* with the auxiliary *kāna* and an imperfect verb: they were, emphatically, in the habit of laughing. The combination means a repeated past activity, not a single event. Nothing in the verse describes one occasion of mockery; it describes a practice. The practice has a duration, a frequency, and a settled character, and all three are carried by the grammatical form. The same form appears in the next three verses, each of which adds one element of the practice — the wink, the return home, the words spoken on seeing the faithful. Four verses describe one habit from four angles, and the habit is what the chapter's final movement presents as the content of the sealed record.

**Laughter as an Attack**

The laughter described here is not amusement but derision, and the Qur'an treats it as a form of harm. Sūrat al-Baqarah states that the life of this world has been made appealing to the disbelievers and that they mock the believers, adding that those who are mindful of God will rank above them on the Day of Judgement (2:212 — *“The life of this world has been made appealing to the disbelievers, and they mock the believers.”*). Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ describes the disbelievers making fun of the Prophet when they see him, asking whether this is the one who speaks ill of their gods (21:36 — *“When the disbelievers see you ˹O Prophet˺, they only make fun of you, ˹saying,˺ “Is this the one who speaks ˹ill˺ of your gods?” while they disbelieve at the mention of the Most Compassionate”*). The mockery in both cases is directed at faith itself and at the person who embodies it, and it functions as a social weapon rather than an expression of surprise. This verse places the weapon in the hands of the wicked as a habitual practice.

---

## Verse 83:30

> wink to one another whenever they passed by

**A Reciprocal Form**

The verb is in a reciprocal pattern, meaning an action performed mutually: they winked at one another, they exchanged glances. The reciprocity is essential to the scene. The mockery is not a solitary opinion but a shared performance, and the sharing is what makes it socially effective. A man who winks alone has expressed nothing; a man who winks and is winked back at has established a consensus. The verse therefore describes the formation of a group opinion in the smallest possible unit — two looks. The group opinion is what verse 32 will report in words, and the words are the public version of a signal that has already been exchanged in private.

**Public Mockery and Its Audience**

A wink requires a third party as much as a target. The gesture is performed for whoever is present, and its effect on that person is to recruit him into the consensus. The mockery described in this verse is therefore not only an attack on the believers but an act of self-constitution by the mockers: they become a group by agreeing, wordlessly, about someone else. The Qur'an describes the same mechanism in Sūrat al-Baqarah, where the hypocrites return to their evil associates and assure them that they were only mocking, and God throws their mockery back at them (2:14–15 — *“When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.”*). The recruitment of an audience is what makes public derision a social fact rather than a personal one, and the social fact is what the sealed record contains.

---

## Verse 83:31

> and muse ˹over these exploits˺ upon returning to their own people

**Two Scenes, One Practice**

Verses 29 to 32 describe the mockery in four settings: among the believers, in passing, at home, and on seeing the faithful again. The multiplication of settings is the chapter's way of establishing that the practice is not occasional. It occupies the street and the house, the encounter and the recollection. Nothing in the four verses suggests a single quarrel, a specific provocation, or a particular occasion. The description is of a standing relation between two groups, and the relation is expressed in laughter, gestures, talk, and words. The accumulation of settings is what allows verse 33 to make its general reply and verse 34 to reverse the whole arrangement rather than one incident in it.

**The Word's Irony**

A root meaning fruit and delight is used for the talk of people deriding others. The irony is present in the Arabic and is not accidental. What the mockers experience as pleasure is described in the vocabulary of pleasure, and the description does not correct their experience or deny it. The chapter's method is to report the scene as the participants live it and then to place the scene under the Day. The ironic use of a pleasant root for an unpleasant practice is part of that method: the verse grants that the mockery was enjoyed, and the granting is what makes the reversal of verse 34 a reversal of enjoyment rather than a denial that any occurred.

## Verse 83:32

> And when they saw the faithful, they would say, “These ˹people˺ are truly astray,”

**Astray**

*Ḍāllūn* is the plural of one who has gone astray, from *ḍ-l-l*, the root of losing the way, wandering, and erring. The word is used throughout the Qur'an for those who have missed guidance, and it is the standard accusation of the disbelievers against the believers. Its application here is a complete inversion of the chapter's own framework. The people being called astray are described in verse 29 as believers and in this verse as the faithful; the people calling them astray are described as wicked, as deniers of the Day, as transgressors steeped in sin, and as people whose hearts are covered. The verse reports the inversion without correcting it in its own terms, and the correction arrives in the reversal of verse 34.

**The Same Accusation Elsewhere**

The charge of being astray or foolish is the standard response to faith in the Qur'an's accounts. The hypocrites, told to believe as others believe, reply: will we believe as the fools believe? And the verse answers that it is they who are the fools, but they do not know (2:13 — *“And when they are told, “Believe as others believe,” they reply, “Will we believe as the fools believe?” Indeed, it is they who are fools, but they do not know”*). The disbelieving chiefs of Noah's people tell him that they see him as a fool and think him a liar (7:66 — *“The disbelieving chiefs of his people responded, “We surely see you as a fool, and we certainly think you are a liar.”*). When the clear revelations are recited, the disbelievers ask the believers mockingly which of the two parties is better in status and superior in assembly (19:73 — *“When Our clear revelations are recited to them, the disbelievers ask the believers ˹mockingly˺, “Which of the two of us is better in status and superior in assembly?”*). The recurrence shows that the accusation in this verse belongs to a settled pattern of response, and the pattern is one in which the accuser's own condition is the thing described.

Four verses have described the mockery — the laughter, the wink, the talk at home, and now the spoken verdict. This verse is the climax of the description, because it is the only one that gives words. The gesture was deniable, the recollection was private, and the laughter was ambiguous; the sentence is explicit. The chapter therefore saves the clearest evidence for last, and the clarity is what verse 33 can answer directly.

---

## Verse 83:33

> even though they were not sent as keepers over the believers

**The Reply**

This verse is the chapter's only direct answer to the mockers, and it is one clause long. The accusation of verse 32 was that the faithful are astray; the reply is that the accusers were not sent as keepers over them. The answer does not defend the believers, describe their guidance, or refute the charge of straying. It denies the accusers' standing. The technique is precise: rather than contesting a claim, the verse removes the position from which the claim could be made. A person who has no mandate to supervise others cannot render a verdict on their condition, and the absence of mandate is stated as a fact rather than argued as a principle.

**Keepers**

*Ḥāfiẓīn* is the same word used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār for the watchers set over every soul: you are certainly observed by vigilant ones — noble angels who record (82:10–11 — *“while you are certainly observed by vigilant”*). The root *ḥ-f-ẓ* covers guarding, preserving, and keeping, and its use in both chapters describes a real function performed by real agents. The recurrence makes this verse's negation sharper. Keepers over human beings do exist, and they are angels commissioned by God. The mockers are not among them. The verse therefore does not deny that the believers are kept; it denies that these people are the keepers. The distinction is between a function that exists and a claim to perform it without appointment.

---

## Verse 83:34

> But on that Day the believers will be laughing at the disbelievers

**So Today**

The verse opens with *fa-l-yawma* — so today, now, on this day. The particle *fāʾ* marks a consequence, and the definite noun with the implied temporal sense fixes the moment. The transition from the street scene of verses 29 to 32 to this verse is accomplished in two words, and the words do all the work of the reversal. Nothing intervenes: no description of death, of the resurrection, of the reckoning, or of the arrival. The chapter cuts from the mockery to its inversion, and the cut is the argument. A reader who has seen the wink and heard the verdict is placed immediately in the moment when the same parties are arranged differently, and the arrangement is what the verse states.

**The Reversal as a Principle**

The Qur'an states the principle explicitly elsewhere. When the hypocrites say to their associates that they were only mocking, the answer is that God will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance (2:15 — *“Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance”*). The throwing back is the same operation performed here: the act returns to its originator, and the originator becomes its object. Sūrat al-Baqarah adds the ranking that this chapter's reversal implies — those who are mindful of God will rank above them on the Day of Judgement (2:212 — *“Those who are mindful ˹of Allah˺ will rank above them on the Day of Judgment.”*). The principle is therefore not local to this sūrah but a settled feature of the Qur'an's account of the Day, and this verse is its most compressed statement.

**What the Laughter Is At**

The verse does not specify the object of the laughter beyond the persons laughed at, and the following verse supplies the posture from which it is performed: on couches, looking on. The laughter is therefore at a sight, and the sight is the condition of the disbelievers. The Qur'an describes the same looking elsewhere, in Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt, where a man of Paradise looks and spots his former companion in the midst of the Hellfire, and says that he nearly ruined him (37:55–56 — *“Then he ˹and the others˺ will look and spot him in the midst of the Hellfire”*). The looking there is followed by speech and by gratitude; here it is followed by laughter and by a question. The chapter's version is the shorter one, and the shortness leaves the laughter unexplained beyond its direction.

## Verse 83:35

> as they sit on ˹canopied˺ couches, looking on

**Couches Returning**

The couches of verse 23 return in the final movement of the chapter, and their return is the link between the description of Paradise and the scene of reversal. In verse 23 the virtuous were on canopied couches, gazing; here the believers are on the same couches, looking on. The same noun, the same posture, and a verb of sight in both cases. The recurrence means that the reversal of verse 34 is not a separate event but the same scene described from the side of the disbelievers. The believers have been on the couches since verse 23, and what verse 35 adds is what they are looking at. Nothing in verse 23 specified the object of the gaze, and the specification arrives twelve verses later.

**Looking On**

The verb is intransitive here as it was in verse 23: they look, they observe, they take in a scene. The object is understood from the previous verse — the disbelievers — and the understanding is what makes the two verses a single statement. The Qur'an describes the same looking in Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt, where a man of Paradise looks down and spots his former companion in the midst of the Hellfire (37:55 — *“Then he ˹and the others˺ will look and spot him in the midst of the Hellfire”*). The looking there is followed by a speech of gratitude and by a question about whether they will die again; the looking here is followed by a question about requital. The two passages agree on the posture — a person in safety observing a person he knew in the place of punishment — and both make the observation an occasion of speech rather than of action.

**Seat as a Position of Judgement**

A couch raised and furnished is not only a place of rest but a place of eminence, and eminence is what the mockers claimed in this world. When the clear revelations were recited to them, the disbelievers asked the believers mockingly which of the two parties was better in status and superior in assembly (19:73 — *“When Our clear revelations are recited to them, the disbelievers ask the believers ˹mockingly˺, “Which of the two of us is better in status and superior in assembly?”*). The question was about position, and this verse answers it without referring to it. The believers are on couches; the disbelievers are what is looked at.

---

## Verse 83:36

> ˹The believers will be asked,˺ “Have the disbelievers ˹not˺ been paid back for what they used to do?”

**The Final Sentence**

The chapter ends on a question, and the question is not addressed to the reader. It is spoken within the scene — asked of the believers on their couches, or by them as they look on, or by those around them. The ambiguity of the speaker is preserved in the Arabic, which gives the question without attribution, and the translation marks the uncertainty with a bracketed gloss. What is certain is the setting: the question belongs to the scene of verse 35, and it is asked by people who can see its answer. A chapter that opened with a declarative verdict — woe to the defrauders — closes with an interrogative whose answer is already visible, and the closing form is the chapter's last structural statement.

**What They Used to Do**

The object is *mā kānū yafʿalūn* — what they were in the habit of doing. The construction is the same one used in verse 14 for what the wicked used to earn, and the same continuous-past form used in verse 29 for what they used to laugh at. The recurrence of the construction across the chapter ties its three movements together: the covering on the heart was formed by habitual earning, the mockery was a habitual practice, and the requital is for habitual doing. Nothing in the verse limits the object to the mockery. The requital covers the whole of the conduct — the short measure, the denial, the dismissal of the revelations, the wink, the talk at home, and the verdict spoken on sight.
