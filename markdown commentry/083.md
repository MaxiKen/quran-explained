# Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn (Chapter 83) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:1

> Woe to the defrauders!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wayl**

*Wayl* is a noun of calamity — woe, ruin, destruction — and the Qur'an uses it as the opening or refrain of a rebuke more than twenty times. It is not a verb and it does not invoke; it declares. The construction *waylun li-* states that woe belongs to a specified group, and the belonging is presented as a fact rather than as a wish. Nothing in the word indicates who utters the woe or whether it is a curse pronounced by someone else. Its force comes from its declarative form: ruin is the condition of these people, and the verse records the condition. The chapters that use it as an opening — al-Humazah, al-Mursalāt, al-Muṭaffifīn — are all short Makkan or early Madinan passages in which the rebuke is the whole of the first movement.

**al-Muṭaffifīn**

The word is the active participle of a form derived from *ṭ-f-f*, a root connected with the small amount by which a measure falls short of its rim, the last thin increment that would complete a vessel. A *muṭaffif* is therefore not a thief in the ordinary sense but someone who takes a fraction, and the fraction is precisely what makes the offence possible. A large theft is visible and provable; a shortfall of a handful is not. The term names a category of wrongdoing defined by its minuteness and its repetition, and the naming is what gives the sūrah its title. No other chapter of the Qur'an is named after a commercial practice, and the singularity of the naming marks the practice as one the revelation considered worth a whole book.

**An Opening Without Preparation**

The verse begins the chapter with the rebuke. There is no oath, no vocative, no description of the sky or the Day, and no address to the Prophet — all of which are standard openings in the short sūrahs around it. Sūrat al-Infiṭār opens with a cosmic event; Sūrat al-Inshiqāq with the sky splitting; Sūrat al-Burūj with an oath by the heaven. This one opens with a sentence about market conduct. The abruptness is itself a feature of the message: the matter requires no framing, because it is a matter the audience already knows and has been practising. The absence of preparation signals that what follows is not new information but an assessment of behaviour already in place, and the assessment is delivered in the first two words.

**A Statement, Not a Description**

The verse does not describe the defrauders. It does not say what they do, when they do it, or to whom. The description arrives in the next two verses, and the arrival is delayed by one verse so that the verdict comes first. The order is significant: the reader hears that ruin belongs to a group before he learns who the group is. The technique produces a question that the following verses answer, and the question is answered with a specificity — buying in full, giving short — that could not have been guessed from the title alone. A chapter that began with the description would have read as legislation; a chapter that begins with the verdict reads as judgement, and the judgement is what the rest of the sūrah develops.

**The Same Word Later in the Chapter**

*Wayl* returns in verse 10, where it is attached not to the defrauders but to the deniers: woe on that Day to those who deny. The recurrence ties two things together that the audience may have kept apart — cheating in the market and denying the Day of Judgement. The tie is made explicit in verse 12, which states that none denies the Day except every evildoing transgressor. The verse under discussion therefore opens a chain that runs through the whole chapter: the fraud, the denial behind it, the record prepared for the deniers, and the ruin that belongs to both. Nothing in verse 1 indicates the range of the word's later use, and the indication is unnecessary, because the recurrence does the work.

**Why Measure Is the Test Case**

The Qur'an's legislation on weights and measures is extensive and it appears in the mouths of prophets. Shuʿayb is sent to Midian with the command to give full measure and weigh with justice, and not to defraud people of their property (11:85). The same command is given to the believers generally: give in full when you measure, and weigh with an even balance (17:35). Sūrat al-Raḥmān places the balance inside the cosmic order — God raised the sky and set the balance so that people would not exceed it (55:7–9). The measure is therefore not a technical detail of trade but the visible instance of a principle that governs the whole of creation. A chapter that opens with the defrauders opens with the point at which that principle is most easily violated and least easily detected.

**A Verdict Delivered in Three Words**

The verse is a complete sentence: woe, to, the defrauders. It contains no verb of accusation, no evidence, and no procedure, and the absence is what distinguishes a verdict from a case. The chapters that open with *wayl* all share this feature — they begin at the point where a trial has already concluded, and the reader arrives after the finding. Sūrat al-Humazah opens the same way, with woe to every backbiter and slanderer who amasses wealth and counts it repeatedly (104:1–2), and the description that follows there is as specific as the one in verses 2 and 3 of this chapter. Both chapters state the ruin first and the conduct second, and both leave the conduct without an explicit prohibition. Nothing says do not defraud; the verse says ruin belongs to those who do. The form is declarative rather than imperative, and the declaration is what makes the opening irreversible.

**The Sin of the Small Amount**

What makes the *muṭaffif* a figure worth a chapter is that his offence is inside the permitted form. He measures, he weighs, he trades, he completes a transaction that looks identical to an honest one. The shortfall exists only in the quantity, and the quantity is known to him alone. The Qur'an's interest in this case is an interest in the interior of an act that has a lawful exterior, and the interest anticipates the chapter's later movement, which is entirely about what is recorded of acts and what is hidden in hearts. A religion that requires the measure to be full is a religion that requires the hidden increment to be given up, and the requirement is the first thing this sūrah states.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:2

> Those who take full measure ˹when they buy˺ from people,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Description Begun and Not Finished**

The verse opens with *alladhīna*, those who, and it does not close. The relative clause started here is completed only in the following verse, where the second half of the behaviour is given. The Arabic therefore holds the reader inside a single sentence across a verse boundary, and the sentence describes a contrast. The technique is the same one used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār, where the watchers of verse 10 are named as noble recording angels only in verse 11. Splitting a description across verses forces each half to be heard separately, and here the separation is the whole point: the first half, taken alone, describes an upright buyer. It is only when the second half arrives that the behaviour is revealed as fraud.

**Taking Measure Upon People**

*Idhā iktālū ʿalā al-nās* — when they take measure upon people. The verb is the eighth form of *k-y-l*, to measure, and the eighth form carries the sense of taking for oneself: to have something measured out and received. The preposition *ʿalā*, upon, is what gives the phrase its direction. They measure upon people, that is, they take from them, and the taking is expressed as an imposition rather than a receipt. The commentators note that the preposition indicates the advantage sought: the measure is taken upon the other party, at the other party's expense. The translation renders the direction with a bracketed gloss, "when they buy", which supplies the commercial situation the Arabic implies through the verb form and the preposition together.

**They Take in Full**

*Yastawfūn* is the tenth form of *w-f-y*, a root whose field is completion, fulfilment, and the paying of what is owed. The tenth form is intensive and reflexive at once: they seek the full amount and they obtain it. Nothing in the word suggests reluctance or negotiation; it describes a person who insists on his due and receives it. The insistence is legitimate in itself. A buyer who requires the full measure he has paid for is acting justly, and the Qur'an commands the same standard on the other side of the transaction. The verse therefore begins its description of the defrauder with an act that is not wrong, and the withholding of judgement for one verse is what makes the following verse's contrast effective.

**The Asymmetry of Self-Interest**

What the two verses together describe is a single disposition operating in two directions. When this person receives, he insists on completeness. When he gives, he does not. The asymmetry is not a difference of opinion about a standard but a difference of application: the standard is acknowledged in the case that benefits him and abandoned in the case that costs him. The Qur'an's diagnosis of the *muṭaffif* is therefore not that he lacks a sense of measure but that he applies it selectively. The verse under discussion establishes the first half of the diagnosis, and it establishes it in the strongest available terms — the verb of seeking completeness, the preposition of advantage, and the insistence on the full amount.

**A Root of Falling Short**

The title of the sūrah and the description in these two verses turn on *ṭ-f-f*, the root of the thin margin by which a vessel falls short of full. The margin is small, and its smallness is what allows the practice to continue. A merchant who takes in full and gives in full is honest; a merchant who takes in full and gives nine-tenths is committing a fraud that no buyer will notice on a single transaction. The verse describes the taking side with a verb of completeness and leaves the giving side for the next verse, where the verb will be one of causing loss. The two verbs are not symmetrical in weight, and the asymmetry mirrors the practice: completeness demanded for oneself, loss inflicted on another.

**The Verse Stops Mid-Sentence**

The effect of the break is worth stating. A listener hearing this verse alone hears a description of a person who requires his full measure when he buys, and there is nothing to condemn in it. The condemnation arrives with the next verse, and the arrival is what makes the listener supply the contrast himself. A description given whole would have been received as a prohibition; a description given in halves is received as a recognition. The listener identifies the person described, and the identification is the rhetorical aim. The Qur'an's short chapters frequently use suspended syntax for exactly this purpose, and the suspension here is short enough to be felt without being long enough to be resolved by guessing.

**The Other Party Is Not Described**

The verse mentions people only as the source from which the measure is taken. Nothing is said about who they are, whether they consented, whether they noticed, or whether they were harmed in a way they could prove. The omission is deliberate: the case being made is about the taker rather than the taken-from, and the taker's disposition is the whole subject. A description of the victims would have turned the passage into an appeal to sympathy, and the chapter is not making one. It is describing a person who requires completeness for himself, and the description is complete without reference to anyone else. The same economy appears in the second half, where the buyers are named only in the translation's gloss and the act is given as causing loss. The loss has an owner, and the owner is never described.

**Measure as a Test of the Interior**

The act described is external and quantifiable, which is why it can serve as a test. A measure is either full or it is not, and the difference is knowable. Most of the conduct the Qur'an legislates is not measurable in this way: intention, gratitude, patience, and sincerity have no instrument. The measure has one, and the existence of an instrument means the fraud can be committed with certainty of detection by God even where detection by people is impossible. The verse therefore opens the chapter with the clearest possible case of a hidden wrong, and the clarity of the case is what makes the following verses' turn to the record of deeds and the state of the heart intelligible. What is measured in the market is a rehearsal for what is weighed on the Day.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:3

> but give less when they measure or weigh for buyers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Description Completed**

This verse finishes the relative clause begun in the previous one, and the finishing supplies the contrast that makes the description a condemnation. Read together, the two verses describe a single person who insists on completeness when receiving and causes loss when giving. The completion is short — six words in Arabic — and its brevity relative to the first half mirrors the practice it describes: the shortfall is small, and so is the statement of it. Nothing is added about the manner, the frequency, or the amount. The verse names the act and stops, and the naming is sufficient because the act is one the audience performs and recognizes.

**Causing Loss**

The verb is *yukhsirūn*, from *kh-s-r*, a root whose field is loss, diminishment, and ruin. The fourth form is causative: they cause to lose, they make short. The choice of a causative verb rather than a simple one is precise. The defrauder does not merely receive less than he gives; he produces a loss in another person. The loss belongs to the buyer, and the buyer did not agree to it. The root is also used in the Qur'an for the ruin of a soul on the Day — those who lose themselves, those who have lost — and the wider use of the same root connects the market loss to the final one. A person who causes loss to others in small amounts is practising the thing that will be done to him in the largest possible amount.

**Two Instruments**

The verse names both *kāla*, to measure by volume, and *wazana*, to weigh. Grain, dates, and liquids are measured; gold, silver, and spices are weighed. The two instruments cover the whole of the market's transactions, and the naming of both removes any suggestion that the prohibition applies to one kind of trade. The pair recurs throughout the Qur'an's legislation on the subject: give full measure and weigh with an even balance (17:35); give full measure and weigh with justice (26:182); weigh with justice and do not give short measure (55:9). The consistency of the pairing across Makkan and Medinan passages indicates a settled rule, and this verse states its violation rather than the rule itself.

**The Commanded Standard**

The positive form of the same standard appears in several places, and the wording is nearly identical each time. *“Give in full when you measure, and weigh with an even balance. That is fairest and best in the end”* (17:35). *“Give full measure, and cause no loss ˹to others˺. Weigh with an even balance, and do not defraud people of their property. Nor go about spreading corruption in the land”* (26:181–183). The second passage joins the fraud to corruption in the land, which is the Qur'an's widest term for social disorder. Sūrat al-Anʿām places the command inside a list of fundamental obligations alongside the treatment of orphans, just speech, and the keeping of covenants (6:152). The measure is therefore not a minor regulation but one of the things the revelation groups with its largest commands.

**Shuʿayb and Midian**

The offence has a prophetic history. *“And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shuʿayb. He said, ‘O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him. And do not give short measure and weight. I do see you in prosperity now, but I truly fear for you the torment of an overwhelming Day’”* (11:84). The command to full measure follows immediately on the command to worship, and the two are joined in one speech. The verse continues with the same pairing found in this sūrah: give full measure and weigh with justice, do not defraud people of their property (11:85). A people destroyed for this practice are named in the Qur'an, and the naming gives this chapter's opening its precedent — the defrauders are not a new category but a recurring one with a known outcome.

**The Balance Set in the Sky**

Sūrat al-Raḥmān places the instrument of weighing inside the created order: *“As for the sky, He raised it ˹high˺, and set the balance ˹of justice˺ so that you do not defraud the scales. Weigh with justice, and do not give short measure”* (55:7–9). The balance is set by God alongside the raising of the sky, and the sequence makes the market scale a participation in a cosmic arrangement rather than a human convention. To defraud the scale is therefore to act against something established in the structure of the world. This verse describes the act without that framing, but the framing is available to a reader who has heard Sūrat al-Raḥmān, and the availability raises the small commercial offence to the level the chapter's later verses require.

**The Shortfall as Corruption in the Land**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ joins the two in a single command: do not defraud people of their property, nor go about spreading corruption in the land (26:183). The joining is not a comparison but an identification — the fraud is the corruption, stated at two scales. What is corrupting about a shortfall of a handful is that it makes trust in transactions impossible, and a market without trust requires every party to verify every quantity, which is a cost borne by everyone including the honest. The corruption spreads from the single transaction to the whole of commercial life, and from commercial life to the general expectation that a word means what it says. Shuʿayb's warning to Midian makes the same connection when he tells a prosperous people that he fears for them the torment of an overwhelming Day (11:84), and the fear is for the society rather than for the individual merchant.

**Why This Sin Is Named First**

The chapter has thirty-six verses and moves from the market to the record of deeds, the state of the heart, the two destinations, the mockery of the believers, and the reversal on the Day. All of that is opened by a sentence about short measure. The choice of entry point is a statement about what the revelation considers basic. A person who cannot be trusted with a handful of grain cannot be trusted with a claim about the unseen, and the sūrah proceeds as though the two were continuous. Verse 4 makes the continuity explicit by asking whether such people do not reckon that they will be resurrected. The fraud is not a separate matter from belief; it is belief's evidence, and the evidence is what the chapter begins with.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:4

> Do such people not think that they will be resurrected

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question About an Expectation**

The verse asks whether these people do not think — *a-lā yaẓunnūna*. The verb *ẓanna* covers thinking, supposing, and reckoning, and in the Qur'an it frequently denotes a settled expectation rather than a passing opinion. The interrogative prefix with the negative produces the standard form of rebuke: do they not? The question is not a request for information about their beliefs. It is a statement that the belief they lack is the one their conduct requires. The construction implies that the answer is known and that the asking is for the benefit of the person being asked, who is expected to recognize the omission in himself. Nothing in the verse accuses them of knowing and refusing; the accusation is that they do not reckon on it.

**The Faculty at Fault**

What is missing is not knowledge but calculation. The defrauder takes a full measure and gives a short one, and the calculation that makes this rational is a calculation within a limited horizon: the gain now, the loss to the other party, the smallness of the amount, the unlikelihood of detection. The verse asks whether a further term has been omitted from that calculation. The omission is not intellectual; it is practical. A person can hold a belief about the hereafter and still leave it out of his arithmetic, and the leaving out is what the verb *yaẓunnūna* describes. The Qur'an's diagnosis of this class of people is consistently that they act without an expectation, and the absence of expectation is what makes the act possible.

**Resurrected**

*Mabʿūthūn* is the passive participle of *b-ʿ-th*, to send, raise, and bring forth. The same root is used of the graves being emptied and their contents brought out, and it is the Qur'an's standard verb for the raising of the dead. The passive form is important: they will be raised, not raise themselves, and the raising is done to them by an agent who is not named here. The agent is named in the verse after next, as the Lord of the worlds. The participle's plurality matches the plural subject of the question, and the state it denotes is one of having been brought forth — a completed condition rather than an event in progress. The verse asks whether these people reckon on having been raised.

**The Link Between Fraud and Denial**

The connection between the two verses is causal and it is stated nowhere explicitly. The defrauders are described in verses 2 and 3; the question about their expectation of resurrection follows in verse 4; and the denial is named in verses 10 to 12. The sequence implies that the fraud is possible because of the denial, and that the denial is the thing to be corrected rather than the fraud. A person who expected to stand before the Lord of the worlds would not take a full measure and give a short one, and the impossibility is not a moral claim but an arithmetic one. The verse therefore treats the commercial offence as a symptom and directs its question at the underlying expectation. Nothing in the chapter proposes a regulation of the market; the whole of its argument is about what a person reckons will happen.

**The Same Diagnosis Elsewhere**

Sūrat al-Nabaʾ gives the same diagnosis in a single line about the disbelievers: they never expected any reckoning (78:27). The absence of expectation is presented there as the whole of their error, and the verse that follows it states that everything has been recorded in a book. The correspondence with this chapter is close: an absent expectation, a written record, and a Day on which the record is produced. Sūrat al-Muddaththir describes the same class of people as those who do not fear the hereafter, and Sūrat al-Qiyāmah asks whether man supposes that he will be left neglected. The question in this verse belongs to a settled line of argument in the Makkan preaching, and the argument's target is always the expectation rather than the information.

**A Sentence That Continues**

The verse does not end with a complete thought. The resurrecting is followed by a preposition in the next verse — for a tremendous Day — and the Day is then specified in the verse after that. Three verses form one sentence, and the sentence's structure is a question containing a description. The suspension has the same effect as the one in verses 2 and 3, where a single description was split across a boundary. Here the splitting spreads the question over three verses, and the spreading means that the reader cannot dispose of it quickly. He is held inside an interrogative while the Day is described, and the description arrives as part of the question rather than as an answer to it.

**The Missing Term in the Calculation**

Every commercial act involves a reckoning: what is given, what is received, what remains. The defrauder performs this reckoning accurately and completely within its limits, and the accuracy is what makes him dangerous to himself. A person who cannot calculate does not defraud; he merely errs. The verse's question therefore addresses a competent calculator and asks whether one term has been left out of his arithmetic. The term is not small and it is not uncertain — it is the Day on which all people stand before the Lord of the worlds, and it is the only term whose inclusion would change the result. The chapter treats the omission as the whole of the fault, and the treatment explains why the rebuke is directed at thinking rather than at trading. The trader is competent; the thinker has left something out.

**Why the Question Comes After the Description**

The order matters. The defrauders are described first, in concrete commercial terms, and the question about their belief comes second. A chapter that began with the question would have read as theology; a chapter that begins with the practice reads as an inspection of a habit. The inspection then produces the question, and the question is unavoidable because the habit has been specified precisely enough that no one can claim not to recognize it. The technique places the reader inside the case before asking him about his belief, and the placement is what gives the question its force. The defrauders are asked whether they do not think that they will be resurrected, and the asking follows two verses in which the reader has already seen himself taking the full measure.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:5

> for a tremendous Day —

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Preposition of Appointment**

The verse consists of three words: *li-yawmin ʿaẓīm* — for a tremendous Day. The preposition *li-* carries the sense of appointment, purpose, and destination: the resurrecting is for a Day, oriented toward it, ending at it. The resurrection is therefore not described as an event in itself but as a movement with a terminus. What the person is raised for is the Day, and the Day is what gives the raising its meaning. The preposition also connects this verse grammatically to the previous one, since the verb of resurrection governs it. Read together: do they not think they will be resurrected for a tremendous Day. The whole weight of the statement falls on the adjective, and the adjective is the verse's only content.

**ʿAẓīm**

The word denotes greatness in magnitude, weight, and consequence. It is used in the Qur'an of God's signs, of the Qur'an itself, of the Day of Judgement, and of punishment. Applied to a day it specifies that the day exceeds other days in every dimension the word can carry. Nothing in the verse says in what respect the Day is tremendous, and the unspecified adjective is stronger than a specification would be. A day described as long, hot, dark, or crowded can be imagined and therefore contained; a day described as tremendous cannot be. The Qur'an's descriptions of the Day supply particular features elsewhere — fifty thousand years, eyes downcast, the sky rolled up — and this verse declines to supply any of them, preferring the general term.

**An Overwhelming Day in the Mouth of a Prophet**

The same adjective appears in Shuʿayb's warning to Midian, in the passage that this sūrah's opening recalls: *“I do see you in prosperity now, but I truly fear for you the torment of an overwhelming Day”* (11:84). The correspondence is exact in substance — a people prospering through short measure are warned of a tremendous day — and the warning is given by a prophet who has already told them to weigh with justice. Nothing in this verse names Midian or Shuʿayb, and the naming is unnecessary for a reader who knows the story. The recurrence of the adjective binds the two passages, and the binding means that the defrauders addressed here are being placed in a category with a known precedent and a known outcome.

**The Verse Ends on a Dash**

The chapter's printed text marks this verse as incomplete, and the incompletion is real: the Day is described further in the following verse, where it becomes the Day on which all people will stand before the Lord of the worlds. The dash indicates that the reader must continue. The effect of the break is to leave the adjective hanging for the length of a verse. A tremendous Day — and then nothing, until the next verse supplies what the Day consists in. The suspension is the same technique used in verses 2 and 3 and again in verses 4 to 6, and its cumulative effect across the chapter's opening is a style of continuous unfolding rather than of separate statements. Nothing here is finished; everything continues.

**Greatness Rather Than Terror**

The adjective chosen is not one of fear. The Qur'an has words for a day of terror, a day of anguish, a painful day, and it uses them. This verse uses greatness, and the choice is deliberate. Terror describes an affect produced in the person; greatness describes a property of the thing. A verse that named the terror would have been about the defrauders' experience; a verse that names the greatness is about the Day itself. The distinction matters because the chapter's argument is that these people have failed to reckon on a fact rather than that they have failed to feel a fear. The fact is the Day's magnitude, and the magnitude is stated in the verse's only adjective.

**The Day as the Chapter's Horizon**

Every movement of this sūrah is oriented toward this Day. The record of the wicked is a record sealed for it; the record of the virtuous is a record witnessed by those nearest to God on it; the standing before the Lord of the worlds occurs on it; the mockery of the believers is reversed on it; the deniers are told what they denied on it. The verse under discussion is the point at which the horizon is set, and it is set in five words. A chapter that opens with a market offence and reaches its horizon in the fifth verse has moved from the smallest transaction to the largest appointment, and the movement is carried by a preposition and an adjective.

**A Day Described by Its Scale**

The Qur'an describes the Day in many terms — a day of gathering, a day of account, a day when the trumpet is blown, a day that will make children grey. This verse uses none of them and settles on magnitude. The choice is appropriate to the chapter's argument, which is about quantities. The defrauder's whole practice is a practice of quantity: how much is measured, how much is given, how much is lost. A Day described as tremendous is a Day whose quantity exceeds every quantity he has handled, and the excess is not a matter of degree but of kind. Nothing in the verse quantifies the greatness, and the refusal to quantify is what makes the adjective effective in this context. A specified number of years could be compared with a specified handful of grain; a magnitude beyond specification cannot be compared with anything.

**The Adjective and the Measure**

There is a proportion between the two that the verse makes available without stating it. The defrauder's gain is a fraction of a measure — the thin margin by which a vessel falls short. The Day he is being raised for is tremendous. The two quantities are incommensurate, and the incommensurability is the argument. A person who has weighed a handful against a handful and found the difference negligible is being told that the difference is recorded and that the day of its settlement exceeds every quantity he has handled. Nothing in the verse draws the comparison, and none needs to be drawn: the adjective does the work by being applied to a day that follows, in the same sentence, a description of a shortfall too small to see.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:6

> the Day ˹all˺ people will stand before the Lord of all worlds?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Completed With a Question Mark**

Verses 4, 5, and 6 form one interrogative sentence, and the question mark falls only here. The reader has therefore been held inside a question for three verses: do such people not think that they will be resurrected for a tremendous Day — the Day all people will stand before the Lord of the worlds? The completion supplies what the Day consists in, and the supplying is what makes the question answerable in principle. A tremendous Day is an adjective without content; a Day on which everyone stands before the Lord of the worlds is a description with a subject, a posture, and a person stood before. The three verses move from accusation to appointment to audience, and the audience is the last thing named.

**Standing**

*Yaqūmu* is from *q-w-m*, to stand, rise, and be established. The standing described here is the Qur'an's standard posture for the Day: people standing, rising from their graves, standing for the reckoning, standing before their Lord. The verb denotes a posture held rather than a movement completed. The people have arrived and they remain upright, and the remaining is what the verse describes. Nothing is said about how long they stand, what they are standing on, or what happens while they stand. The posture alone carries the meaning: a person standing before a lord is a person awaiting a decision he cannot make. The Qur'an's other descriptions of the Day fill in the posture — eyes downcast, voices hushed, no speaking except by permission.

**Before**

The preposition is *li-*, the same preposition used in the previous verse for the Day of appointment, and here it governs the Lord. Its sense is presence and orientation: standing for the Lord, before the Lord, in relation to the Lord. The commentators note that the preposition indicates the standing is for His judgement and His reckoning rather than merely in His vicinity. The double use of *li-* in two successive verses — resurrected for a Day, standing for the Lord — creates a chain of orientation in which everything points toward the one before whom the standing occurs. Nothing in the verse describes a place, and the absence of place is consistent with the preposition's sense: what matters is not where the people stand but toward whom.

**The Lord of the Worlds**

*Rabb al-ʿālamīn* is the name by which the Qur'an opens: all praise belongs to God, the Lord of the worlds (1:2). The name combines lordship — sustaining, disposing, owning — with the widest possible object: the worlds, everything that exists in ordered plurality. Its use here is the verse's decisive move. The defrauders took a full measure from people and gave a short one to people; the verse places them standing before the Lord of all that exists. The disproportion between the transaction and the audience is the whole of the argument, and the argument is carried by a name rather than by a statement. A reader who has prayed the opening sūrah has said this name many times, and its appearance at the end of a sentence about short measure reinterprets the prayer he has been saying.

**All People**

*Al-nās* is general and it is definite: the people, humanity, everyone. Nothing restricts the standing to the defrauders, and the restriction is unnecessary because the universality includes them. The verse does not say that the defrauders will stand before the Lord of the worlds; it says that all people will, and the defrauders are inside the statement. The technique is characteristic of the Qur'an's rebukes: rather than addressing the offender directly, it describes a situation that contains him. The offender can hear the description as a general truth about humanity and recognize himself inside it, and the recognition is more effective than an accusation because it is not evadable by claiming to be someone else.

**The Standing as the Answer to the Fraud**

The connection between the three verses of description and the three verses of question is a single contrast. The defrauder stood before a buyer and gave less than was due; on the Day, all people stand before the Lord of the worlds and nothing can be given less than is due. The posture is the same and the counterparty is different, and the difference is what makes the Day tremendous. A transaction with a person can be manipulated because a person's knowledge is limited; a standing before the Lord of the worlds cannot be, because His knowledge is not. The verse supplies the counterparty and leaves the reader to complete the contrast, and the completion is the point of the chapter's opening movement.

**The Name and the Prayer**

A Muslim who prays recites *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh rabbi al-ʿālamīn* in every unit of his prayer, and the phrase is the second of the Qur'an (1:2). The recurrence of that phrase at the end of a sentence about short measure places the market inside the prayer. The person who takes a full measure and gives a short one has said the Lord of the worlds many times, and the saying has not altered his practice. The verse does not reproach him for the prayer or suggest that the prayer is invalid; it simply uses the name as the term of the standing, and the use makes the name a fact about the Day rather than a formula of worship. The connection between the two is left for the reader, and the leaving is more effective than any statement would be.

**Why the Chapter Ends Its First Movement Here**

Six verses, and the movement closes on a name of God. Nothing follows in the form of a conclusion, an exhortation, or a summary. The next verse begins with the particle of refusal and turns to the record of the wicked. The abruptness of the turn is possible only because the first movement has ended at its highest point. A movement that ended on the description of the fraud would have required an argument to reach the record; a movement that ends on the Lord of the worlds requires none, since the standing before Him already implies the reckoning. The verse is therefore both the completion of a sentence and the ground of everything that follows, and its position at the hinge of the chapter is what gives the hinge its strength.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:7

> But no! The wicked are certainly bound for Sijjîn ˹in the depths of Hell˺—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Particle of Refusal**

*Kallā* opens the second movement of the chapter, and its function is the same as in Sūrat al-Infiṭār, where it refused the denial of the Judgement after three verses describing the gifts of creation. Here it follows six verses about the defrauders and the Day on which all people stand before the Lord of the worlds. What it refuses is not stated in a proposition, and the refusal is directed at the condition just described: the expectation that a shortfall of measure can coexist with standing before the Lord of the worlds cannot stand. The particle marks a break in the argument and a turn to consequence, and the turn is immediate — the very next words name a destination. Nothing in the particle's use here suggests a mild correction; it is the strongest form of negation available in the chapter's language.

**The Wicked Named**

*Al-fujjār* is the same word used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār for the class that will burn in Hell (82:14), and its root is the root of breaking open. A *fājir* is one who has broken through a limit, and the intensive plural denotes a people characterized by the breaking. The word's appearance in this chapter connects the defrauders of verses 1 to 3 to a moral category rather than a commercial one. What began as a description of a market practice ends as a description of a kind of person, and the shift is carried by a single noun. The defrauder is not a merchant who made an error; he is one of the *fujjār*, and the classification places him with everyone else who has exceeded the bounds set for his conduct.

**Sijjīn**

The name is unique to this chapter, where it occurs twice — here and in the question of the following verse. The commentators derive it from *sijn*, prison, in an intensive pattern that denotes a place of severe confinement, or from a root meaning the lowest and most abject, so that it signifies the bottom of the earth or the lowest of the low. Both derivations agree on a place of confinement at the extreme of descent. The bracketed gloss in the translation, "in the depths of Hell", supplies the identification the Arabic leaves implicit. Nothing else in the Qur'an uses the word, and the uniqueness means that the chapter is introducing a term rather than recalling one. The introduction is followed immediately by a question about it, which is how the Qur'an handles names whose content exceeds what can be stated.

**A Sentence That Continues**

The verse ends on a dash, and the dash is not a pause but a link. The following verse asks what will make the addressee realize what Sijjīn is, and the verse after that answers with three words. Three verses form one statement — the wicked are bound for Sijjīn, what will make you realize what Sijjīn is, a record inscribed — and the statement is delivered in pieces. The technique is the chapter's method throughout its second half: name a thing, ask about it, give a short answer, and move on. The naming and the asking are what produce attention, and the short answer is what withholds the content. A reader who wanted a description of Sijjīn will not receive one, and the withholding is the point of the idiom used in the following verse.

**Bound For**

The construction is *la-fī sijjīn* — they are certainly in Sijjīn, with the emphatic particle *inna* and the preposition of containment. The present tense of the nominal sentence describes a state that already holds, not one that will begin on the Day. The wicked are in Sijjīn now, in the sense that their destination is fixed and their record is written. The preposition of containment is the same one used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār of the virtuous in bliss and the wicked in Hell, and its recurrence marks this as a settled formula. What the formula denotes is enclosure: the class is inside its outcome, and the outcome is not an event awaiting them but a place they are already assigned to. The emphatic particle removes any suggestion that the assignment is provisional.

**Descent as the Direction of the Wicked**

The chapter's structure is spatial, and the space is vertical. The wicked are in Sijjīn, which the derivations place at the lowest point; the virtuous are in ʿIlliyūn, whose name denotes elevation and whose gloss places it in elevated Gardens. The two destinations are named in a pair of passages that mirror each other almost exactly — the same particle of refusal, the same emphatic construction, the same idiom of realization, the same three-word answer about a sealed record. The symmetry is the chapter's architecture. A defrauder who took a small amount for himself and gave less to another is described as belonging to the lowest place, and the description is given in a name whose derivation is descent.

The movement from conduct to documentation is the point at which the chapter stops being about a market and becomes about an archive, and the archive is the form in which the market's transactions are kept. Every shortfall is already written; every full measure taken is already written; and the writing is what the name of the place refers to. The verse's dash leads directly into that fact.
**From the Market to the Record**

The six verses of the first movement described conduct and its appointment. This verse introduces the document. What follows in verse 9 is a sealed or inscribed record, and the record is what connects the conduct to the destination. A person is in Sijjīn because his book is there, and his book is there because of what was written in it. The turn from behaviour to documentation is the chapter's central move, and this verse is where it occurs. The defrauders were described in the language of the market — measure, weight, loss — and they are now described in the language of the archive, with names of places, records, and witnesses. The two vocabularies are not alternatives; the second is the settlement of the first.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:8

> and what will make you realize what Sijjîn is?—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Idiom of Realization**

*Mā adrāka* — what will make you realize, what has informed you, what has caused you to know. The construction is the one used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār, where the Day of Judgement is asked about twice in successive verses (82:17–18), and in the short sūrahs that ask about the Striking Disaster, the Inevitable Hour, Hell, the nightly star, and the Crusher. The idiom is not a request for information but a statement that the information exceeds the addressee's reach. Something would have to be done to him for him to know, and the doing is not his. The verse therefore interrupts the description of Sijjīn with a question that the description cannot answer, and the interruption is what marks the thing described as beyond ordinary comprehension.

**Asked Once Here, Twice There**

Sūrat al-Infiṭār doubles the question; this chapter asks it once. The variation is not accidental. In al-Infiṭār the object of the question is the Day itself, which the chapter's audience has been denying; the doubling answers the denial by insisting twice. Here the object is a named place that the audience has just heard for the first time, and the single question introduces it rather than refutes a claim about it. The economy of the chapter requires the question to be short, since three separate movements — the wicked, the virtuous, and the mockers — follow within the remaining verses. A doubled question would have consumed the space the movements need. The single question does the work of marking gravity and then yields the sentence to the answer.

**Applied to a Named Place**

Elsewhere the idiom is applied to events and to the Fire in general. Here it is applied to a proper name, and the application is unusual. A name introduced for the first time and immediately asked about is a name whose content the audience cannot supply. The question functions as a declaration that Sijjīn is not a word the listener already knows the meaning of, and the declaration prepares the three-word answer that follows. The technique prevents the reader from assimilating the new name to a familiar idea. He cannot think of Sijjīn as Hell by another title, because the verse has told him that what Sijjīn is exceeds what he can realize. The answer given in the next verse is therefore received as a partial disclosure rather than a definition.

**Dashes on Both Sides**

The printed verse is enclosed by dashes, and the enclosure indicates that the question is parenthetical. The statement begun in verse 7 — the wicked are certainly bound for Sijjīn — resumes in verse 9 with the description of what Sijjīn is. The question sits inside the statement rather than interrupting it, and the placement is what allows the answer to be so short. A question posed as a sentence of its own would require a full answer; a question posed as a parenthesis can be answered by the resumption of the sentence. The dashes are a typographical representation of an Arabic structure that uses word order and the idiom itself to achieve the same effect. The reader experiences the question as an aside within a declaration, and the aside is what makes the declaration emphatic.

**What the Question Withholds**

No description of Sijjīn follows. The answer given is *kitābun markūm* — a record inscribed, a sealed book, a numbered writing. The answer describes a document rather than a place, and the substitution is the verse's most significant effect. A listener asking what Sijjīn is expects to be told about a pit, a fire, a depth, or a prison. He is told about a book. The withholding of the place and the supplying of the document converts the question from geography to record-keeping, and the conversion is consistent with the chapter's argument: what determines a person's destination is what has been written of him. Nothing in the verse announces the conversion, and the announcement is unnecessary, because the answer performs it.

**The Function of the Question in the Chapter's Architecture**

The same idiom returns in verse 19, applied to ʿIlliyūn, and the return produces the chapter's central symmetry. The wicked are named, their place is asked about, and a sealed record is given; the virtuous are named, their place is asked about, and a sealed record witnessed by those nearest to God is given. The two movements are identical in form and opposite in direction, and the shared form is what makes the opposition exact. The verse under discussion establishes the form on its first occurrence, and the establishment means that when the reader reaches verse 19 he knows what is coming and can attend to the single difference — the witness. The question is therefore a structural device as much as a rhetorical one.

**The Listener Placed Inside the Question**

The second person of the idiom is singular, and the singular address does the same work here that it does in Sūrat al-Infiṭār: it converts a general statement into a personal one. The wicked are bound for Sijjīn is a claim about other people. What will make you realize what Sijjīn is a claim about the listener's own incapacity, and the incapacity is the thing he must acknowledge. The chapter's first movement addressed the defrauders in the third person and then asked a question of them in the second; this verse completes the turn. From this point the reader is no longer overhearing a case against a class of merchants. He is being told that there is something he cannot know, and the something is the destination of people whose conduct he has just been shown in detail.

**A Mark of Gravity**

The Qur'an reserves this idiom for a small number of subjects, and all of them are matters of the unseen in its largest dimensions: the Hour, the Fire, the celestial sign sworn by, the disaster that strikes, the crusher. The addition of Sijjīn to the list places the destination of the defrauders in that company. A person who takes a short measure is being described in the vocabulary reserved for the greatest matters of the revelation, and the elevation of his case is not an exaggeration but an indication of scale. The verse tells the reader that the subject he is about to hear about is of the same order as the Hour and the Fire, and it tells him in the only form the Qur'an uses for that purpose.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:9

> a fate ˹already˺ sealed.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer in Three Words**

The question of the previous verse asked what Sijjīn is, and the answer given is *kitābun markūm* — a written book, an inscribed record, a sealed document. Three words answer a question introduced by the Qur'an's gravest idiom. The disproportion is the point. A listener expecting a description of a pit, a depth, or a fire receives the name of a document, and the receipt is what reorients the whole passage. Sijjīn is not primarily a place in the sense of a location to be pictured; it is a register, and the register is what determines the place. The brevity of the answer also respects the question. The idiom of realization declared that the thing exceeds the addressee's capacity, and an answer of three words is consistent with that declaration — it discloses without explaining.

**Markūm**

The participle is from *r-k-m*, a root meaning to heap, pile up, stack, and press together. Applied to writing it denotes a document whose contents have been accumulated and then fixed — inscribed, numbered, folded, or sealed. The image is of marks layered upon marks until the surface is complete, and of a completion that cannot be added to. The commentators render the word variously as written, inscribed, numbered, and sealed, and the renderings agree in one respect: the writing is finished. Nothing further will be entered in this book. The finished state is what the translation captures with "already sealed", and the adverb is a fair addition, since the Arabic participle denotes a condition already holding rather than an action about to occur.

**A Fate Rather Than a Record**

The translation renders *kitāb* as fate, which shifts the emphasis from the document to what the document settles. Both emphases are in the Arabic. A *kitāb* in the Qur'an is a book, a scripture, a decree, and a record of deeds, and the range is not accidental: what is written is what is fixed. The record of a person's deeds is his fate in the sense that it determines what will happen to him, and the determination is expressed in the form of a document. The verse's answer to the question about Sijjīn therefore identifies the destination with a writing, and the identification means that a person's end is not a place he travels to but a document that has been completed about him.

**The Same Answer for the Opposite Class**

Verse 20 gives the identical three words as the answer to the question about ʿIlliyūn. The record of the virtuous is also a *kitāb markūm*, written and sealed, and the only addition made there is that it is witnessed by those nearest to God. The shared answer is remarkable, and it means that the two destinations are not distinguished by the nature of their documents. Both are completed records; both are fixed. What differs is the place, the direction, and the witnesses. The symmetry removes any suggestion that the record of the wicked is somehow less formal or less final than the record of the virtuous. Both are sealed, and the sealing is the same act in both cases. The difference lies entirely in what has been written, and what has been written is a person's conduct.

**The Record in the Qur'an's Account of the Day**

The document produced on the Day is described at length elsewhere. Every person's destiny is bound to his neck, and on the Day of Judgement a record is brought forth and found laid open, with the command to read it (17:13–14). The record in Sūrat al-Kahf leaves nothing unlisted, small or great, and the wicked see it and cry out at its completeness (18:49). Sūrat al-Infiṭār describes the keepers of the record as noble angels who write and know whatever people do (82:10–12). The four passages agree that the document is individual, exhaustive, produced, and read by the person it concerns. This verse adds the fact of sealing, and the sealing is what makes the production on the Day a disclosure rather than a compilation. The book was finished long before it is opened.

**Sealed, and Therefore Unalterable**

A document that is sealed cannot be amended. The sealing excludes the possibility that the record will be revised in the person's favour, that a deed will be removed, or that a shortfall will be overlooked. The exclusion is what makes the verse a statement about finality rather than about documentation. The defrauders of verses 1 to 3 operated on the assumption that a small quantity taken or withheld would not be accounted; the sealed record denies the assumption at its root. The quantity is in the book, the book is complete, and the completion is fixed. Nothing in the verse says who sealed it or when, and the unstating leaves the fixing as a bare fact — the record is sealed, and the sealing is what Sijjīn is.

**The Book and the Person**

The record described here is individual, and its individuality is what makes the sealing consequential. A general register of a people's conduct could be corrected or supplemented by the conduct of others; a personal book cannot be. The Qur'an's descriptions of the Day insist on the same point — every person's destiny bound to his own neck, a record brought forth to each, a command addressed to each to read his own book (17:13–14). The sealing in Sijjīn is the closing of that personal document, and the closing means that the last entry has been made. Whatever the defrauder does after the sealing cannot alter it, and the chapter's urgency comes from the fact that the sealing has not yet occurred for anyone still reading it.

**From Place to Document**

The movement of verses 7 to 9 is a substitution, and the substitution is the chapter's method of teaching. A name is given that sounds like a place; a question is asked about it in the idiom that marks incomprehensibility; and the answer is a document. The reader who follows the movement has learned that the destinations of the hereafter are constituted by records rather than by geography. The geography follows from the record, not the other way round. Sijjīn is where the sealed book of the wicked is, and ʿIlliyūn is where the sealed book of the virtuous is, and a person's belonging to one or the other is settled by what has been written of him. The chapter's opening movement described the writing in advance, in the market, one handful at a time.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:10

> Woe on that Day to the deniers—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Return of Woe**

*Wayl* appeared in the first verse of the chapter, attached to the defrauders, and it returns here attached to the deniers. The recurrence ties two descriptions together that the audience might have kept apart. The first movement spoke of market conduct — full measure taken, short measure given — and said nothing about belief. This verse speaks of denial and says nothing about commerce. The shared word of ruin is what joins them, and the joining is completed by the verses that follow, which define the deniers and state that none denies the Day except an evildoing transgressor. The structure of the chapter therefore identifies the defrauder and the denier as the same person described from two sides, and the identification is made by a repeated noun rather than by an argument.

**On That Day**

The addition of *yawmaʾidhin* — on that Day — is what distinguishes this woe from the first. The woe of verse 1 was undated: ruin belongs to the defrauders, without specification of when. The woe of this verse is dated, and the date is the Day described in verses 5 and 6, the tremendous Day on which all people stand before the Lord of the worlds. The dating converts a general condition into an appointment. A person can live inside an undated ruin without noticing it; a person told that ruin belongs to him on a specified day has been given something to reckon with. The verse also implies that the ruin is not only future — the undated woe of verse 1 covers the present — but that it has a moment of fullest realization, and the moment is the Day.

**To the Deniers**

*Al-mukadhdhibūn* is the intensive plural of the one who calls something false, from *k-dh-b*, the root of lying and of treating as a lie. The intensive form denotes repeated and effortful denial, and the plural denotes a class. The class is not defined here; the definition follows in the next verse, which identifies them as those who deny the Day of Judgement. The verse therefore names a group by its activity and leaves the object of the activity for the following verse to supply. The technique produces a suspension of one verse's length, and the suspension means that the reader hears "the deniers" before he hears what is denied. The order matters: the denial is the settled feature of the group, and its object is what the chapter specifies.

**A Refrain Used Elsewhere**

Sūrat al-Mursalāt uses this exact sentence as a refrain, repeating it ten times through the chapter — *Woe on that Day to the deniers* (77:15, 77:19, and eight further occurrences). Each repetition follows a description of a sign, a favour, or a warning, and the refrain closes each movement with the same verdict. The use of the identical sentence in this chapter, once rather than ten times, connects the two sūrahs and marks the phrase as a recognized formula of the Makkan preaching. A reader who has heard al-Mursalāt hears the phrase here as a quotation of a settled refrain, and the hearing supplies the repetition that this chapter does not perform. The phrase is therefore weightier in its single occurrence than a newly composed sentence would be.

**The Dash and the Continuation**

The verse ends on a dash, and the dash indicates that the sentence continues into the next verse, where the deniers are defined. The construction is a single statement spread across two verses: woe on that Day to the deniers — those who deny the Day of Judgement. The suspension is the same technique used in verses 2 and 3, 4 to 6, and 7 to 9, and its cumulative presence makes the chapter's second half a sequence of unfolding sentences rather than of discrete statements. The effect on the reader is that nothing is closed until it has been fully specified, and the full specification of the deniers requires the next two verses. The chapter is unwilling to leave a term undefined, and the dashes are the marks of that unwillingness.

**Woe as a Verdict on Belief**

The first woe was a verdict on conduct. This one is a verdict on belief, and the shift is what allows the chapter to move from the market to the heart. The intervening verses — the record of the wicked, the sealed book — have already made the move possible by introducing documentation, and documentation is a matter of what was done and what was claimed. A person who denies the Day has made a claim, and the claim is the object of the woe. The verse does not say that the denial is punished; it says that ruin belongs to the denier on the Day. The belonging is stated as a fact about the Day rather than as a consequence of a decision, and the fact is what the following verses develop into an account of the heart's condition.

**Why the Denier Rather Than the Fraudster Is Named Here**

The chapter could have returned to the defrauders and dated their ruin. It names the deniers instead, and the substitution indicates that the denial is the deeper fact. The fraud is possible because of the denial; the denial is not caused by the fraud. A person who expected to stand before the Lord of the worlds would give the full measure, and the absence of that expectation is the thing to be addressed. The verse therefore moves the argument from the symptom to the condition, and the movement is permanent — from this point the chapter speaks of denial, of the heart's stain, of dismissal of the revelations as ancient fables, and of being veiled from the Lord. The market returns only in the final movement, as mockery of the believers, and it returns as a consequence of the denial rather than as a subject in its own right.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:11

> those who deny Judgment Day!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Definition Supplied**

The previous verse named the deniers and left them undefined; this verse supplies the definition in five words. *Alladhīna yukadhdhibūna bi-yawm al-dīn* — those who call the Day of Judgement false. The definition is narrow and specific. It does not describe the deniers as rejecting God, the prophets, or the Qur'an in general; it identifies one object of denial. The narrowing is deliberate, because the object named is the one that governs conduct. A person who denies that God exists has made a metaphysical claim; a person who denies that there is a Day of Judgement has made a claim about whether he will be held to account, and the second claim is the one that determines how he behaves in a market.

**Yawm al-Dīn**

The phrase combines *yawm*, day, with *dīn*, which carries the twin senses of judgement and religion. The combination is the Qur'an's standard name for the Day, and it appears in the opening sūrah as a description of God's dominion: Master of the Day of Judgement (1:4). It appears in Sūrat al-Infiṭār four times in nineteen verses, as the thing denied, the time of the burning, and the object of the doubled question about realization (82:9, 82:15, 82:17–18). It appears in the mouths of prophets as the day they hope for forgiveness on (26:82) and in the mouths of their opponents as the day they mockingly ask the date of (51:12). The phrase is therefore one of the most heavily used in the Qur'an, and its appearance here places this chapter's deniers inside a category named throughout the revelation.

**The Verb of Denial**

*Yukadhdhibūna* is the intensive form of the verb of calling something false, and it is in the imperfect, denoting a continuing activity. The denial is not a single act of disbelief but an ongoing practice of rejection. The intensive form adds effort and repetition: they keep calling it false, and they call it false emphatically. The same verb appears in the next verse in its singular non-intensive form — none denies it except every evildoing transgressor — where the shift to the singular describes the disposition of an individual rather than the practice of a class. The two forms together cover both the collective activity and the personal condition behind it, and the pairing across two verses is one of the chapter's characteristic moves.

**A Relative Clause Doing the Work of a Definition**

Grammatically this verse is an appositive: those who deny the Day of Judgement, standing in for the deniers of the previous verse. The construction means that the two verses form a single utterance, and the utterance moves from a label to a description. The label *al-mukadhdhibūn* is general — anyone who calls anything false is inside it. The description restricts it to those whose falsification is directed at the Day. The restriction is what makes the woe of the previous verse precise, and the precision is necessary because the chapter has already described a different group — the defrauders — whose relation to the Day is one of failing to reckon on it rather than of denying it. The two groups are being distinguished even as they are being identified with one another.

**Denial of the Day as the Ground of the Fraud**

The connection between this verse and the chapter's opening is the chapter's central argument, and the argument is made by ordering rather than by stating. The defrauders are described; they are asked whether they do not think they will be resurrected; the Day is described as tremendous and as the occasion of standing before the Lord of the worlds; the wicked are assigned their sealed record; and the deniers of the Day are pronounced ruined. The sequence presents the fraud as downstream of the denial. Nothing says that the defrauder is a denier, and none needs to say it, because verse 12 will state the general principle that none denies the Day except an evildoing transgressor. The principle closes the loop: the transgression and the denial are mutually implicating, and each confirms the other.

**The Exclamation**

The printed text ends the verse with an exclamation mark, and the mark reflects the Arabic construction, in which the relative clause carries an emphatic force. The exclamation is not a cry of surprise but a specification delivered with weight. The deniers are those who deny the Day of Judgement — and the naming of the object is what makes the class intelligible. A reader who has heard the phrase *yawm al-dīn* in prayer and in the mouths of prophets receives the specification as a recognition, and the recognition is what the exclamation marks. The chapter is not introducing a new idea but identifying which idea has been rejected, and the identification is emphatic because the idea is one the audience already holds.

**What Denial Removes**

To deny the Day is to remove from one's account of the world the only term that gives conduct its weight. Without it, a shortfall is a gain and a full measure is a loss; with it, the two are reversed. The verse names the object of the denial precisely because the object is what determines everything else. Nothing in the chapter proposes that the deniers are mistaken about a detail of doctrine or about the identity of a prophet. They are mistaken about whether there is a settlement, and the mistake is total in its consequences. That is why the woe of the previous verse is dated to the Day rather than left general, and why this verse supplies the date's name as the definition of the class.

**The Day and the Standing**

Verse 6 described the Day as the day on which all people stand before the Lord of the worlds. This verse names the same Day as the object of denial, and the naming brings the two descriptions together. What is denied is not an abstract judgement but a standing, an audience, a posture held before a named Lord. The specificity of verse 6 is what gives the denial its content here. A person who denies a reckoning in the abstract can maintain that he denies nothing in particular; a person who denies the Day on which all people stand before the Lord of the worlds is denying a scene, and the scene has been described. The chapter's method is to describe before it accuses, and the description is what makes the accusation unanswerable.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:12

> None would deny it except every evildoing transgressor.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Exception That Functions as a Diagnosis**

The verse is constructed as a negative with an exception: nothing denies it except every such-and-such person. The construction excludes every other possibility, and the exclusion is the verse's claim. Denial of the Day of Judgement is not presented as an intellectual position that a person might arrive at through reflection, nor as an inherited custom, nor as a reasonable doubt. It is presented as the property of a specific kind of person, and the kind is described by two adjectives. The diagnosis reverses the ordinary assumption about unbelief — that a person denies because he has not been convinced, and would believe if the evidence were sufficient. This verse states that the denial follows from the person's conduct, and that no one whose conduct is otherwise commits it.

**Muʿtadī**

The first adjective is from *ʿ-d-w*, a root whose field is crossing, exceeding, transgressing, and acting with hostility. The same root gives *ʿudwān*, aggression, and *iʿtidāʾ*, transgression of a limit. A *muʿtad* is one who goes beyond what is set for him, and the going beyond is not a single act but a settled pattern, which is why the form denotes a character rather than an event. The word is used in the Qur'an of those who exceed the bounds set in law, of aggressors in war, and of those who transgress against others' rights. Its application here places the denier in the category of the person who does not accept limits, and the refusal of limits is what makes the refusal of a Day of Judgement coherent. A limit-setter implies a limit-keeper, and a person who will not keep limits will not accept a keeper.

**Athīm**

The second adjective is from *ʾ-th-m*, the root of sin, fault, and guilt. The pattern *faʿīl* is intensive: not merely one who sins but one steeped in sin, habituated to it, characterized by it. The pairing of the two adjectives is precise and non-redundant. *Muʿtad* describes the direction of the conduct — outward, across a boundary, against another. *Athīm* describes its accumulation — inward, repeated, forming a character. The denier of the Day is therefore both an aggressor and a habitual sinner, and the two features explain the denial from two sides. A person who takes what is not his has an interest in there being no settlement; a person whose taking has become his character has that interest permanently. The verse supplies both and requires neither to be argued.

**The Singular After the Plural**

The previous verses spoke of the deniers in the plural. This verse uses the singular throughout: none denies it except every evildoing transgressor. The singular generalizes — the statement is about a type, and every instance of the type is included. The effect is to remove the class as a collective entity and replace it with an individual description that applies to whoever fits. Nothing in the verse restricts the type to the defrauders of the chapter's opening, to the Quraysh, or to any historical group. The description is portable, and the portability is what makes the verse a diagnosis rather than an accusation of a particular people. A reader who recognizes the two adjectives in himself is inside the statement, and the recognition is available without any external evidence.

**The Denial as a Consequence**

The verse's logic is causal and its direction is important. Denial does not produce transgression; transgression produces denial. A person who has exceeded the bounds and accumulated sin has a stake in the absence of a reckoning, and the stake shapes his judgement about whether a reckoning exists. The Qur'an describes this mechanism elsewhere in terms of the heart: a covering placed over it, a seal set on it, locks upon it (2:7, 47:24). This chapter supplies the mechanism directly in the verse that follows, where the heart is said to be stained by what its owner used to earn. The two verses together give a complete account — the conduct is of a certain kind, and conduct of that kind produces a condition in which the Day cannot be acknowledged. Nothing in the account excuses the denier, because the conduct that produced the condition was his.

**Every**

The word *kullu*, every, is what makes the exception exhaustive. It is not some evildoing transgressors, or most of them, or those among them who are worst. Every instance of the type denies the Day. The universality removes the possibility of a counter-example, and the removal is what gives the verse its force as a diagnosis. A statement that some transgressors deny the Day would be an observation; a statement that every one of them does is a claim about the relation between conduct and belief. The claim is falsifiable in principle — one honest denier would refute it — and the verse does not admit the possibility. The confidence is consistent with the chapter's treatment of the subject throughout, which is to state relations between inner condition and outer conduct without qualifying them.

The two adjectives are therefore not a general moral sketch but a description of the man the chapter began with, and the description is what makes the opening verses about measure into a statement about the soul.
**The Connection to the Defrauder**

The two adjectives describe the person of verses 2 and 3 exactly. He exceeds a limit when he gives less than he takes, and the exceeding is his practice rather than his mistake. The application of the diagnosis to him is what the chapter's structure has been preparing since the first verse. A reader who arrives here has already seen the defrauder described in commercial terms, questioned about his expectation of resurrection, told of the tremendous Day, shown the sealed record of the wicked, and warned of ruin on the Day. The verse now names him as a transgressor steeped in sin and states that his denial of the Day is the necessary consequence of what he is. The naming is the chapter's judgement on the practice it opened with, and the judgement is delivered in a general sentence about a type.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:13

> Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they say, “Ancient fables!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Occasion of the Response**

The verse describes a situation: revelations are recited, and a response is given. The recitation is the Qur'an's standard term for its own delivery — the verses are recited, read out, proclaimed — and the term indicates an oral event witnessed by the person responding. The response is therefore not to a text encountered privately but to a performance attended in person. The situation is a public one, and the saying is a public saying. Nothing in the verse describes the content of what was recited on the occasion, and the omission generalizes the situation: whatever is recited, this is what is said. The adverb *whenever* carries the generality, and the generality means the response is a disposition rather than a reaction to a particular passage.

**Asāṭīr al-Awwalīn**

The phrase is the response in full: *asāṭīr al-awwalīn* — the fables, legends, or written tales of the ancients. The noun *asāṭīr* is a plural whose singular is disputed; the commentators connect it either to *saṭr*, a line of writing, so that it denotes things written down in rows, or to a root meaning to spread or compose tales. Either derivation produces the same sense: stories set down by earlier people. The addition of *al-awwalīn*, the ancients or the former peoples, dates them and dismisses them in one move. What is old and written by predecessors is not revelation; it is inherited literature. The phrase is a genre claim rather than a refutation, and its force lies in the confidence with which the genre is assigned.

**A Phrase Repeated Across the Qur'an**

The same words appear in at least eight other passages, and the repetition marks the phrase as the standard dismissal of the early opponents. The disbelievers argue with the Prophet saying this Qur'an is nothing but ancient fables (6:25); they claim they have already heard it and could produce something similar (8:31); asked what their Lord has revealed, they answer ancient fables (16:24); the phrase is put in the mouths of those who were promised it before, along with their forefathers (23:83, 27:68); it is described as something written down and rehearsed morning and evening (25:5); an unbelieving son scolds his parents with it (46:17); and a man whose wealth and children are described says it whenever the revelations are recited to him (68:15). The recurrence shows that the Qur'an records the dismissal verbatim and repeatedly rather than paraphrasing it once.

**The Shift to the Singular**

The Arabic of this verse is singular: whenever Our revelations are recited to *him*, *he says*. The translation renders it in the plural to match the deniers of the previous verses, and the rendering is defensible because the singular functions generically. But the Arabic singular does specific work. It describes one person responding, and the person is the type named in verse 12 — every evildoing transgressor. The singular also matches Sūrat al-Qalam's description of the man who says ancient fables whenever the revelations are recited to him (68:15), where the singular is clearly individual. The movement from the plural class to the singular instance is characteristic of this chapter, which repeatedly converts a collective accusation into a description of one person so that the reader can recognize himself.

**A Dismissal Rather Than an Argument**

Nothing in the response engages with the content of what was recited. No verse is answered, no claim is examined, no evidence is offered against the revelation. The response assigns the recitation to a category and stops. The technique of dismissal is rhetorically effective because it refuses the terms of the discussion: a person who says that a text is old literature has not entered the text's argument and cannot be refuted by it. The Qur'an's rebuttals elsewhere address the same refusal directly, asking whether the listeners have not reflected, whether there are locks on their hearts, whether they have not travelled in the land and seen the end of those before them. The rebuttals are addressed to the refusal rather than to the claim, because the claim has no content to rebut.

**What the Dismissal Asserts**

Three things are asserted by the phrase, and none of them is argued. That the recitation is old — it belongs to the ancients. That it is written rather than revealed — it is *asāṭīr*, things set down in lines. That it is narrative rather than truth — it is tales. The three assertions together produce a complete alternative account of the Qur'an's origin: it is a body of inherited literature composed by earlier peoples. The account is falsifiable in principle, since a person who could identify the source would have to name it, and the Qur'an's challenge elsewhere is precisely a demand for the source — bring a book of guidance from God older than these two, if you are truthful. The dismissal does not attempt the identification, and the absence of an identified source is what the following verses treat as evidence of a different kind of obstruction.

**Recording the Words of the Opponents**

The Qur'an preserves the exact phrases used against it, and the preservation is a feature of its method rather than an incidental report. A text that answered its opponents in general terms could be read as avoiding them; a text that quotes them verbatim and repeatedly has taken their words into itself. The phrase in this verse is short enough to be a slogan, and its recurrence across at least nine passages suggests that it was one. The chapter's decision to quote the slogan rather than describe it means that the reader hears the dismissal in the voice of the person making it, and the hearing is what prepares the diagnosis that follows.

**The Heart Behind the Words**

The verse describes speech and the next verse describes its cause. The connection is causal and it is not stated in the verse itself: the person says ancient fables because something has happened to his heart. The following verse will state that his heart has been covered by what he used to earn, and the covering will explain why the recitation produces this response rather than another. The order is important. A reader who hears the dismissal first and the explanation second understands the dismissal as a symptom, and the understanding is what prevents him from treating it as a position to be debated. The chapter does not argue with the phrase. It diagnoses the person who says it, and the diagnosis is delivered in the verse that follows.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:14

> But no! In fact, their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Particles in Succession**

The verse opens with *kallā bal* — no, but rather. The two particles perform different work and their combination is emphatic. *Kallā* refuses what has just been said or implied; *bal* replaces it with something else. Together they deny the preceding claim and substitute a diagnosis. What is refused is the account given in verse 13: that the revelations are ancient fables. What is substituted is a statement about the condition of the person who says so. The construction is the Qur'an's standard form of correction, and its appearance here means that the dismissal of the previous verse is not being answered on its own terms. No argument about the age or origin of the recitation is offered. The verse changes the subject from the text to the reader of the text, and the change is the refutation.

**Rāna**

The verb is from *r-y-n*, and the commentators explain it as a covering that accumulates until it seals what it covers. The classical image is of rust on a metal surface: a layer forms, thickens, and eventually prevents the metal from being seen or used. Some derive it from a root meaning to be overcome or prevailed upon, so that the heart is said to have been overwhelmed by what covered it. Both senses are present in the word's use here, and the translation captures them with "stained". The verb is perfective and it is followed by the preposition *ʿalā*, upon, which gives the covering a position: it is on the hearts, over them, pressing down. The heart is described as a surface that has been acted upon, and the action has produced a condition rather than an event.

**The Organ of Perception**

The heart in the Qur'an is not primarily the seat of emotion but the organ of understanding, perception, and recognition. It is what comprehends a sign, what receives a revelation, what knows a truth. The Qur'an speaks of hearts that understand, hearts that are sound, hearts that are diseased, hearts that are sealed, and hearts that have locks upon them. A covering on the heart is therefore a covering on the faculty by which revelation is recognized, and the covering explains the response of the previous verse more precisely than any argument could. The person who says ancient fables is not reasoning badly; he is not reasoning at all, because the organ of reasoning has been obstructed. The obstruction is what makes his dismissal immune to evidence.

**By What They Used to Earn**

The covering is attributed to *mā kānū yaksibūn* — what they used to earn, acquire, or commit. The verb *kasaba* is the Qur'an's general term for what a person does and thereby acquires, including its consequences. The construction *kānū yaksibūn* combines the auxiliary of continuity with the imperfect, denoting a repeated activity over time: what they were in the habit of earning. The attribution is the verse's decisive statement. The covering on the heart is not imposed from outside as an arbitrary punishment; it is produced by the person's own accumulated conduct. Each act of earning left a residue, and the residues formed the layer. Nothing in the verse says that God caused the covering independently of the earning, and the absence is what preserves the responsibility of the person described.

**The Same Condition Described Elsewhere**

The Qur'an describes the obstructed heart in several images, and the images agree on the causality. God has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered (2:7). The hearts of some were sealed for their disbelief, in connection with covenant-breaking, rejection of signs, and killing the prophets (4:155). The question is asked whether people do not reflect on the Qur'an, or whether there are locks upon their hearts (47:24). Veils are cast over hearts, leaving them unable to comprehend, with deafness in the ears (6:25). The four passages differ in image — sealing, locking, veiling, covering — and agree in attaching the condition to prior conduct. This verse contributes the image of accumulation, which is the one that most clearly explains how the condition forms over time.

**Why the Diagnosis Follows the Dismissal**

The order of verses 13 and 14 is instructive. The dismissal is quoted first, in the words of the person who makes it, and the diagnosis follows. A reader who encountered the diagnosis first would receive it as a claim about other people's hearts, unverifiable and possibly unfair. Encountering the dismissal first, he recognizes the phenomenon — the confident assignment of a text to a dismissive category without engaging it — and the recognition prepares the diagnosis. The chapter's method throughout is to show before it explains: the defrauder's conduct is shown before the question about his expectation, the sealed record is shown before the woe, and the dismissal is shown before the covering. The showing is what makes the explaining credible, and the credibility is the verse's rhetorical achievement.

**The Stain and the Measure**

The connection between this verse and the chapter's opening is not verbal but structural. The defrauder takes a little more than is due and gives a little less, repeatedly, in transactions too small to notice. The covering on the heart forms in the same way — by accumulation, in increments too small to be detected at the time, until the surface is sealed. The two processes are described in the same terms of habit and repetition, and the parallel is what makes the chapter a single argument rather than a collection of rebukes. A person who has practised the small shortfall in the market has practised the formation of a layer, and the layer forms on the heart as it forms on the measure. The verse names the result without drawing the parallel, and the parallel is available to anyone who has read the chapter from its first line.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:15

> Undoubtedly, they will be sealed off from their Lord on that Day.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Maḥjūbūn**

The word is the passive participle of *ḥ-j-b*, the root of the veil, the curtain, the screen, and the covering that separates one thing from another while leaving both in existence. A *maḥjūb* is one who has been veiled off, excluded from access, prevented from reaching. The root is used in the Qur'an of the veil between the people of Paradise and the people of the Fire, of the screen behind which a messenger speaks, and of the barrier that prevents a thing from being perceived. Its application here is to a relation between persons: they are veiled from their Lord. The image is not of distance but of obstruction — the two are not far apart, and something stands between them. The standing of the something is the punishment, and the verse names it in a single participle.

**The Preposition of Separation**

*ʿAn rabbihim* — from their Lord. The preposition *ʿan* denotes separation and turning away, and its use with a passive of veiling produces a specific sense: they are veiled off from Him, cut off from access to Him. The construction does not describe God as veiling Himself in a spatial sense but as being inaccessible to them. The accessibility is what has been lost, and the loss is stated as their condition rather than as His action. The verse is careful in this respect. It does not say that God hid Himself from them; it says that they are veiled from Him. The passive leaves the agent unnamed, and the unnamed agent allows the covering of the previous verse to be understood as the veil — the stain on the heart is what prevents the access.

**Their Lord**

The possessive is significant. The one they are veiled from is described as *their* Lord, not as the Lord of the worlds in the general form used in verse 6. The personal possessive indicates a relation that exists and has been blocked rather than a relation that never existed. The same people stand before the Lord of the worlds on the Day, as verse 6 stated; nothing removes them from that standing. What is denied them is access. The distinction is precise: a person can be present before a lord and be excluded from him, and the exclusion within presence is a more exact description of the Day than absence would be. The verse therefore does not contradict verse 6 but specifies what the standing will be for this class.

**On That Day**

*Yawmaʾidhin* — on that Day. The same temporal specification used in verse 10 for the woe of the deniers returns here, and the recurrence ties the veiling to the Day of Judgement rather than to the present life. Nothing in the verse says that they are veiled from their Lord now, and the limitation is important. In this life the access is available; the covering on the heart obstructs recognition but does not close the door, and the door is closed on the Day. The specification of the date therefore preserves the possibility of repentance in the present while removing it in the future. The chapter's argument throughout has been that the deniers have time now and will not have it then, and this verse states the second half of that claim with a temporal marker rather than an exhortation.

**The Gates Not Opened**

Sūrat al-Aʿrāf describes the same exclusion in a different image: *“Surely those who receive our revelations with denial and arrogance, the gates of heaven will not be opened for them, nor will they enter Paradise until a camel passes through the eye of a needle”* (7:40). The closed gate and the veil are two pictures of one condition, and both are attached to the same causes — denial and arrogance. The impossibility stated in the second half of that verse, a camel passing through a needle's eye, is the measure of the exclusion's finality. This verse makes no comparison and states the condition plainly, and the plainness is more severe than the image. A person told that a gate will not open can hope for it to be opened later; a person told that he will be veiled from his Lord on that Day has been given a date.

**The Contrast With Those Brought Near**

The chapter's other class is described in verses 21 and 28 as witnessed by, and drinking with, those nearest to God — *al-muqarrabūn*, the ones brought near. The two participles are exact opposites: brought near and veiled off. The nearness of the one class and the exclusion of the other are described with words from the same semantic field of proximity and access, and the pairing is the chapter's central contrast. Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah names the same group and places them at the head of the people of Paradise: the foremost in faith will be the foremost, and they are the ones nearest to God (56:10–11). The nearness is therefore a recognized rank, and the veiling is its absence. Nothing in this verse names the opposite class, and the naming arrives six verses later, where the elevation of ʿIlliyūn and the witness of those brought near are given.

**The Sequence of the Three Verses**

Verses 13, 14, and 15 form a single progression: a dismissal spoken, a covering formed, an access closed. Each verse is the consequence of the one before it, and the consequences are cumulative rather than alternative. The person says ancient fables because his heart is stained; his heart is stained because of what he earned; and because the stain is there, he will be veiled from his Lord on the Day. The chain contains no arbitrary link — nothing is imposed that was not produced by the person's own conduct — and the absence of arbitrariness is what makes the progression a diagnosis. The chapter presents the outcome of denial as the working out of the denial itself, and the working out requires three verses to state, each of them short and each of them dependent on the one before.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:16

> Moreover, they will surely burn in Hell,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Consequence**

Verses 15 and 16 state two consequences of the denial, and the second is the one that concerns the body. The first was exclusion — veiled off from their Lord. The second is burning. The order is significant: access is closed before pain is inflicted, and the closure is described in the verse that precedes rather than the one that follows. A reader who encounters the burning first would receive it as the whole of the punishment; encountering the veiling first, he receives the burning as an addition to a loss that is already complete. The chapter's arrangement therefore treats separation from God as the primary fact and the Fire as what accompanies it, which is the reverse of the ordinary expectation.

**The Emphatic Construction**

The verse contains two markers of emphasis: *inna* at the head and the *lām* prefixed to the predicate, both of which the translation renders with "surely". A doubly emphasized nominal sentence in Arabic is as strong an assertion as the language provides. The assertion is about a future event, but its grammatical form is present and nominal, which describes a settled state rather than an occurrence. They are, emphatically, burners in Hell. The construction removes contingency from the statement in the same way that the sealing of the record removed contingency from the document. What has been written is fixed, and what is fixed will be undergone. Nothing in the verse introduces a condition, an exception, or a possibility of avoidance.

**Ṣāluhā**

The verb is from *ṣ-l-y*, the root used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār for the wicked burning in the Fire on the Day of Judgement (82:15). The same root denotes exposure to fire, entering it, and undergoing its heat. Its active form here attributes the burning to the subjects rather than to an agent who applies it: they burn in it. The attribution is consistent with the chapter's treatment of responsibility throughout — the record is what they earned, the covering on the heart is what they accumulated, and the burning is what they undergo in a voice that makes it their own. Nothing in the verse describes someone casting them in, and the omission leaves the Fire as the destination of a path they walked.

**Jaḥīm**

The name of the Fire here is *jaḥīm*, the intensely blazing fire, the same name used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār for the enclosure of the wicked (82:14). The word specifies heat and glare rather than depth or duration, and its recurrence across the two neighbouring chapters marks it as the preferred name in this strand of the Makkan preaching. The Qur'an has other names for the Fire — *saʿīr*, *laẓā*, *saqar*, *ḥuṭamah* — and the choice among them is not random. *Jaḥīm* belongs to the vocabulary of these two chapters, which are concerned with the Day's reckoning rather than with the Fire's architecture. The name appears here without description, and the absence of description is possible because the name has already been used in the chapter that precedes this one.

**The Sequence of Sijjīn and Jaḥīm**

The chapter names two things for the wicked: Sijjīn in verse 7 and Hell in this verse. The relation between them is not explained. Sijjīn is identified with a sealed record, and Hell is identified with burning. One reading takes them as the same destination described from two sides — the register and the fire. Another takes Sijjīn as the lowest place, in which the record of the wicked is kept, and Hell as what they enter on the Day. Nothing in the chapter decides between them, and the undecidability is consistent with its method of naming without elaborating. What both readings agree on is that the wicked have a fixed destination and that the destination is described twice, once as a document and once as a fire.

**The Verse in the Chapter's Movement**

This verse closes the section on the wicked, which began with the particle of refusal in verse 7 and ran through the question about Sijjīn, the sealed record, the woe to the deniers, the definition of the deniers, the diagnosis of their character, the quotation of their dismissal, the covering of their hearts, and the veiling from their Lord. Eleven verses, and the movement ends on a single statement of burning. The brevity of the ending is proportionate to the length of the preparation. Nothing needs to be said about the Fire after eleven verses of diagnosis, because the diagnosis has already established who will be there and why. The verse states the outcome and yields to the next movement, which begins with the same particle of refusal and turns to the virtuous.

**Burning as the Body's Share**

The chapter's account of the wicked has moved through several registers — a sealed document, an exclusion from access, an announcement — and this verse is the one that concerns the body. The registers are not alternatives and they are not ranked, and the chapter gives no indication that one is worse than another. What it gives is a complete description: the person's record is fixed, his access is closed, his body burns, and his denial is named to him while he burns. Nothing in the account is left to be inferred, and nothing in it is elaborated beyond a single clause. The economy is what makes the description severe. A longer account would have allowed the reader to dwell on one element; four elements stated in four clauses allow no dwelling at all, and the movement past each of them is the chapter's way of saying that all of them are true.

**Burning and Being Told**

The verse that follows supplies an addition that is not physical. After the burning comes an announcement: this is what you used to deny. The two verses together describe a punishment with two components — the Fire and the naming of the denial. The naming is what makes the punishment complete, since it connects the suffering to the conduct that produced it. A person who burns without being told why experiences pain; a person who burns and is told that this is the thing he called false experiences the settlement of an account. The chapter's whole argument has been about accounts — measures, weights, records, sealed books — and the announcement in the next verse is the final entry. This verse provides the condition in which the announcement will be heard.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:17

> and then be told, “This is what you used to deny.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Announcement Inside the Punishment**

The verse describes speech addressed to people who are burning. The speech is short, and its content is not new information: they are told that this is the thing they used to deny. Nothing is added about the nature of the Fire, the length of the stay, or the reason for the sentence. The announcement is purely identificatory — it names the relation between what is being undergone and what was previously rejected. The naming is the punishment's completion, and its placement after the burning rather than before it means that the identification is made to someone already experiencing the thing identified. The order is what gives the sentence its force: the announcement arrives when it can no longer be argued with.

**The Demonstrative**

*Hādhā* — this. The word points at something present, and what is present is the burning described in the previous verse. The demonstrative performs an identification between the Fire and the Day of Judgement: the thing you denied is this thing you are in. The denial was of an event, a day, a reckoning; the demonstration is of a condition. The verse therefore converts an abstract object of disbelief into a concrete experience and states their identity. Nothing in the denial could have prepared the denier for the identification, because the denial consisted precisely in refusing to treat the event as real. The demonstrative removes the refusal by making the event palpable, and the removal is performed in one word.

**What You Used to Deny**

The verb is imperfect with *kānū*, denoting a continuous past activity: what you were in the habit of denying, what you kept denying. The construction recalls the intensive present of verse 11, where the deniers are those who keep calling the Day false. The two tenses join: the habitual denial in this life is met by the announcement in the next, and the announcement uses the same verb. Nothing in the verse says that the denial was mistaken or that the deniers have been proven wrong; it says only that this is what they denied. The proof is the presence of the thing rather than an argument about it, and the presence is what makes the announcement unnecessary as information and devastating as recognition.

**The Same Announcement Elsewhere**

The Qur'an uses this form of address to the people of the Fire in several places. In Sūrat al-Anʿām the deniers are asked whether this is not the truth, and they answer that it is, by their Lord, and are then told to taste the punishment for what they used to deny (6:30). In Sūrat Yūnus the same structure appears: the announcement that the promise of God has come true, followed by the settling of the account. The recurrence establishes the announcement as a fixed element of the Day's procedure — a moment in which the people concerned are made to state the identity between their denial and their condition. This verse gives the announcement in its shortest form, and the shortness is consistent with the chapter's method of stating the fact and leaving the elaboration to other passages.

**The Denial Named by Others**

Throughout the chapter the deniers have been described in the third person: they say ancient fables, their hearts are stained, they are veiled, they burn. This verse is the first moment at which they are addressed directly. The shift from description to address is significant, because address presupposes that the person addressed can hear and understand. The veiling of verse 15 excluded them from access to their Lord; this verse does not exclude them from being spoken to. The distinction is exact: they are cut off from proximity, not from communication, and the communication they receive is the naming of their own denial. Nothing in the verse suggests that the naming produces repentance or relief, and the absence of any such effect is what makes it a punishment rather than an instruction.

**The Connection to the Record**

The sealed record of verse 9 contains what was written of these people, and the announcement of this verse is its oral counterpart. A document that is sealed cannot be amended; a statement that is spoken to a person cannot be un-heard. The two forms of fixation — written and spoken — complete the chapter's account of how the denial is settled. The document fixes what was done; the announcement fixes what was claimed. Nothing in the verse mentions the record, and the omission allows the announcement to stand alone as the final word of the movement. After it, the chapter turns with the particle of refusal to the other class, and the turn is possible because this movement has been closed by a sentence that admits no reply.

**The Voice of the Day**

Nothing in the verse identifies the speaker, and the absence of identification is a feature of the Qur'an's descriptions of the Day, in which announcements are frequently given in the passive or without attribution. What matters is that the announcement comes from the Day itself rather than from the person receiving it. The denier is not confessing and not being persuaded; he is being informed by a voice that is not his own and that speaks about him in the second person plural. The grammar places him inside a sentence he does not control, and the placement is the completion of a chapter that began with a man controlling a quantity. The scale, the weight, the margin, the claim that the revelations are old tales — all of these were his to manage. This sentence is not.

**Why the Denial Is the Thing Named**

The announcement could have identified the Fire as the punishment for fraud, for transgression, or for accumulated sin — all of which the chapter has described. It identifies the Fire as the thing that was denied. The choice tells the reader what the chapter considers primary. The defrauding, the transgressing, and the staining of the heart are all real and all described, and none of them is what the announcement names. What is named is the denial of the Day, and the naming means that the denial is the fault from which the others follow. The chapter has argued this from verse 4 onward, and the argument reaches its conclusion in a sentence spoken over people who are burning. The sentence is the chapter's verdict, and it is delivered in the voice of the Day rather than in the voice of the revelation.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:18

> But no! The virtuous are certainly bound for ’Illiyûn ˹in elevated Gardens˺—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Particle, the Opposite Class**

Verse 7 opened the movement on the wicked with *kallā*, and this verse opens the movement on the virtuous with the same particle. The repetition creates a hinge: the refusal that introduced Sijjīn introduces ʿIlliyūn. What the particle refuses in each case is the same thing — any alternative to the destination being named. The symmetry of the two openings is the chapter's architectural principle, and it means that the description of Paradise is not a consolation appended to a warning but a parallel statement of equal grammatical and structural weight. The two movements are built from the same materials in the same order, and the sameness is what makes the difference between them legible.

**al-Abrār**

The class named is the same one described in Sūrat al-Infiṭār as being in bliss (82:13), and the word is the plural of *barr*, one who is good, upright, and generous in his dealings. The root *b-r-r* denotes the widest kind of goodness — truthfulness, kindness, piety, and the fulfilment of obligations. Its use here, immediately after a movement about the wicked, sets the two classes in the terms the chapter has been using: *fujjār* and *abrār*, those who break bounds and those who keep them. The pairing is not merely oppositional; it is exhaustive within the chapter's framework, and every person described in it belongs to one of the two. The defrauders of the opening verses are being classified here, by implication, as *fujjār*.

**ʿIlliyūn**

The name occurs only in this chapter, twice — here and in the question of the following verse. Its form is a plural pattern denoting elevation, and the commentators connect it to *ʿuluww*, height and loftiness, so that it signifies the highest places. The bracketed gloss in the translation, "in elevated Gardens", supplies an identification the Arabic leaves open. The name's uniqueness is matched by Sijjīn's uniqueness, and the two unique names form a pair: one derived from descent and confinement, the other from ascent and height. The chapter therefore gives each class a proper name that exists nowhere else in the Qur'an, and the naming is what makes the two destinations concrete rather than general. A place with a name is a place, and the naming is the chapter's way of insisting on it.

**Vertical Structure**

The two movements are arranged as a descent and an ascent. The wicked are in Sijjīn, at the lowest point, with a sealed record; the virtuous are in ʿIlliyūn, at the highest, with a sealed record witnessed by those brought near. The vertical axis is the chapter's spatial imagination, and it is consistent with the Qur'an's wider use of height and lowness as images of rank. Nothing in this verse describes the ascent or the means of it; the destination is named and the preposition of containment places the class inside it. The absence of a described journey means that the position is a state rather than a destination reached by travel, and the state has been determined by the record.

**Same Emphatic Construction**

*Innahum la-fī* — they are certainly in, with the double emphasis used in verse 16 for the burning. The identical construction is applied to both classes, and the identity removes any difference of certainty between the two statements. The virtuous are as certainly in ʿIlliyūn as the wicked are certainly burning in Hell, and the certainty is marked by the same two particles in both cases. Nothing in the chapter suggests that one destination is more assured than the other, or that the reward is conditional in a way the punishment is not. The symmetry of emphasis is a theological statement made grammatically, and it means that the chapter's promises are to be read with the same confidence as its warnings.

**Dash and What Follows**

The verse ends on a dash, and the dash leads into the question of verse 19 and the answer of verses 20 and 21. The structure repeats the one used for Sijjīn exactly: a statement of destination, a question in the idiom of realization, and a short answer about a sealed record. The only difference is the addition in verse 21, where the record of the virtuous is described as witnessed by those nearest to God. The addition is the single point of asymmetry between the two movements, and its placement at the end of the parallel sequence makes it unmistakable. Everything else — the particle, the class, the unique name, the question, the sealed book — is the same. The witness is what is not.

**A Name Given Without a Map**

The chapter introduces ʿIlliyūn and refuses to locate it. No direction, no distance, no relation to the heavens or the earth, and no comparison with any place the reader has heard of. The refusal is the same one made for Sijjīn, and its effect on both names is to make them irreducible. A place that can be located can be imagined, and a place that can be imagined can be discounted as an invention. These names cannot be. They arrive as terms in a statement about a sealed document, and the statement is what the reader is required to accept. The chapter therefore asks for assent to a geography it will not describe, and the asking is possible only because the assent has already been secured by the six verses about the market, the resurrection, and the standing before the Lord of the worlds.

**Turn From the Fire**

The chapter has spent eleven verses on the wicked and their outcome, ending with an announcement spoken over people burning. This verse turns away from that scene without comment, without transition, and without resolution. The turn is made by a particle rather than by a conjunction, and the particle's refusal is directed at whatever the preceding movement might have seemed to establish as final. The virtuous are named, and the naming changes the subject completely. Nothing in the verse acknowledges the Fire, and the non-acknowledgment is itself a statement: the Fire is not the whole of what the chapter has to say, and the reader who has followed the description of the wicked is now required to attend to something else. The requirement is the chapter's summons, and it arrives as a change of subject.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:19

> and what will make you realize what ’Illiyûn is?—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Idiom Used Twice in One Chapter**

The question is the same one asked about Sijjīn ten verses earlier, and its repetition establishes a pattern within the chapter. Each unique name — the one given nowhere else in the Qur'an for the destination of the wicked, the one given nowhere else for the destination of the virtuous — is followed by the idiom that marks incomprehensibility. The pattern tells the reader that neither name can be understood from its sound, its derivation, or its associations. Both require disclosure, and both receive the same partial disclosure in the form of a sealed record. The doubling of the idiom within a single chapter is unusual, and the unusualness is what signals that the chapter is introducing two terms rather than recalling one.

**What the Question Does to a Name**

A proper name normally functions by pointing: the listener either knows the thing named or does not. This question suspends both possibilities. It does not assume that the listener knows ʿIlliyūn, and it does not offer to inform him in the ordinary way. It asks what would make him realize, which is a question about the conditions of knowledge rather than about the object. The effect is to place the name beyond the reach of learning. A reader cannot resolve the question by asking someone, consulting a source, or reasoning from the derivation of the word. The chapter has foreclosed all three routes, and the foreclosure is what makes the answer that follows authoritative — it is not information the listener could have obtained, and it is given to him anyway.

**Applied to Elevation as to Descent**

The same idiom was applied to Sijjīn, a name of confinement and depth, and is now applied to ʿIlliyūn, a name of height. The application to both directions is significant. The idiom is ordinarily used in the Qur'an for matters of terror and magnitude — the Hour, the Fire, the Striking Disaster, the Crusher — and its use here for a destination of reward extends its range. What the extension indicates is that the incomprehensibility belongs to the unseen as such, not to its unpleasant parts. The elevated place exceeds the listener's realization in the same way and to the same degree as the low one. Nothing in the verse distinguishes the two cases, and the undifferentiated application is what gives the reward its weight.

**The Parenthetical Position**

The verse is enclosed by dashes, and the enclosure marks it as parenthetical to the statement begun in verse 18. The statement — the virtuous are certainly in ʿIlliyūn — resumes in verse 20 with the description of what ʿIlliyūn is. The parenthesis is the same structural device used in verses 8 and 9, and its repetition means that the reader encounters the same shape twice within eleven verses. The familiarity of the shape is what allows the answer in verse 20 to be so short. A new structure would require elaboration; a repeated one requires only the difference. The difference arrives in verse 21, and the arrival is the payoff of the repetition. The chapter trains its reader in its own form and then uses the form to make a single new point.

**The Question and the Listener**

The second person is singular, as it was in verse 8. The question is addressed to one listener, and the listener is whoever hears the chapter. The singular address converts a description of a class into a statement about the hearer's own knowledge. The virtuous are in ʿIlliyūn is a claim about other people; what will make you realize what ʿIlliyūn is a claim about the listener's incapacity. The two claims together produce a specific effect: the listener is told that a place exists, that a class of people is in it, and that he cannot comprehend it. The combination is an invitation rather than a description, and the invitation is what the chapter's final movement develops into an exhortation to compete for what has been described.

**What Is Not Asked**

The question is not "where is ʿIlliyūn", "who is in it", or "what is it like". It is what will make you realize what it is — a question about the realization itself. The formulation shifts attention from the object to the knowing of the object, and the shift is characteristic of the idiom wherever it appears. The Qur'an's use of this construction is consistently epistemic rather than descriptive. It marks a boundary in the listener's knowledge and then gives him something on the far side of it. What is given here is a document and a witness, and neither answers the question in the form it was asked. The mismatch between the question and the answer is the point: the question was not asked to be answered but to prepare the listener for what he is about to be given.

**What the Question Concedes**

To ask what will make you realize is to concede that the listener cannot realize it on his own, and the concession is generous rather than dismissive. It does not accuse the listener of stupidity or of bad faith; it locates the limit in the object rather than in the subject. ʿIlliyūn is the kind of thing that exceeds realization, and the exceeding is a property of it. The same concession was made for Sijjīn and for the Day of Judgement in Sūrat al-Infiṭār, and the recurrence establishes a consistent epistemology: the matters of the unseen are not difficult, they are inaccessible, and access to them is given rather than achieved. The verse states the inaccessibility in the form of a question, and the question is addressed to a single listener so that he will acknowledge it as his own.

**The Second Hinge of the Chapter**

Verse 8 was the hinge between the naming of Sijjīn and its identification with a sealed record. This verse is the hinge between the naming of ʿIlliyūn and its identification with a sealed record witnessed by those brought near. The two hinges are identical in form and differ in their second element. The chapter's whole argument about the two classes therefore passes through two questions of the same shape, and the shape is what makes the argument memorable. A reader who remembers the questions will remember that both destinations are records, and that one record is witnessed. The witnessing is the only asymmetry the chapter permits itself, and it is placed at the exact point where the two parallel structures meet.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:20

> a fate ˹already˺ sealed,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Identical Answer**

The answer to the question about ʿIlliyūn is the same three words given for Sijjīn: *kitābun markūm*, a written book, an inscribed and sealed record. The identity of the two answers is the chapter's most striking structural feature. The destination of the wicked and the destination of the virtuous are each identified with a sealed document, and the documents are described in exactly the same terms. Nothing distinguishes them at this point. The distinction arrives in the following verse, and its arrival is what makes the identity meaningful — the reader is told twice that both places are books, and then told once that one of the books has witnesses. The single addition stands out against a background of complete sameness.

**A Record Rather Than a Place**

The substitution of a document for a location was discussed in connection with Sijjīn, and it applies with equal force here. ʿIlliyūn is a name of elevation, and the answer to the question about it is a book. The elevation is therefore not primarily spatial; it is the elevation of a record and of the people associated with it. A reader expecting a description of heights, gardens, and light receives the name of a document, and the receipt reorients the whole movement. What follows in verses 22 to 28 is indeed a description of gardens, couches, faces, wine, and a spring — but the description is preceded by the document, and the precedence means that the description is what the document entitles its holders to.

**Markūm Again**

The participle denotes a writing that has been accumulated, inscribed, numbered, and sealed. Its application to the record of the virtuous carries the same sense of completion it carried for the record of the wicked. The book is finished; nothing will be added to it. For the wicked this completion was severe, since it excluded any later amendment in their favour. For the virtuous it is equally severe in the opposite direction: nothing will be removed from what has been written of their goodness, and no deed they performed will be lost. The sealing guarantees both outcomes with the same mechanism, and the mechanism's neutrality is what makes it credible. A record that could be amended for one class could be amended for the other.

**The Fixing of the Good**

The chapter has described the wicked as earning a covering on their hearts by what they accumulated, and the accumulation was gradual and unnoticed. The record of the virtuous is accumulated in the same gradual way, and the accumulation is fixed by the same sealing. What the virtuous did — the full measure given, the weight made even, the property not defrauded — is in the book, and the book is closed. The closing is what makes the reward certain rather than probable. Nothing in the verse describes the reward; it describes the document that determines it. The reward arrives in the verses that follow, and its arrival is the reading of a book that was sealed long before.

**The Same Word in Both Cases**

The Qur'an uses *kitāb* for the scripture, for the decree, for the record of a community, and for the record of an individual. The word's range is what allows it to function here as the answer to two different questions. A book is a fixed thing, and fixity is the property both destinations require. Sijjīn is fixed as a place of confinement; ʿIlliyūn is fixed as a place of elevation; and the fixity of each is expressed by the same noun and the same participle. The chapter therefore makes its central claim — that both outcomes are settled — in a phrase that is repeated verbatim. The repetition is the claim, and no further statement of it is made anywhere in the sūrah.

**Between the Question and the Witness**

The verse occupies a position between two others that add what it does not contain. Verse 19 asked the question, and verse 21 supplies the witness. This verse gives the document, and the document is what the question and the witness both concern. Without it the question would have no answer and the witness would have nothing to witness. The verse is therefore the load-bearing element of the movement, and its three words carry the whole of it. The economy is characteristic of the chapter's second half, which consistently states its most important facts in its shortest verses. The two longest statements of the movement — the description of the couches and the description of the wine — come after, and they elaborate what this verse has already fixed.

**The Document as the Person's Shape**

A sealed book is not an external object in the Qur'an's account of the Day; it is what a person has become. The destiny bound to the neck, the record laid open, the command to read your own book — each formulation identifies the document with the person rather than placing it beside him. The verse's answer to the question about ʿIlliyūn therefore says something stronger than that the virtuous have a record. It says that ʿIlliyūn is a book, and that the book is what the place consists in. The elevation named in the destination is the elevation of a document and of those named in it, and the elevation is fixed by the sealing. What has been written of a person is his height, and his height has already been determined by the time he arrives.

**The Sealed Book and the Market**

The chapter opened with a man taking a full measure and giving a short one, in transactions too small for anyone to notice. It arrives here at a book in which every such transaction has been entered and which has been sealed. The distance between the two points is the distance the chapter has travelled, and the travel is what makes its opening verses into something other than commercial regulation. The sealed book is the reason the shortfall matters, and the book is sealed for both classes. Nothing in the verse distinguishes between great deeds and small ones, and the absence of a distinction is what makes the market's small quantities significant. The measure is entered, the weight is entered, and the entry is final.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:21

> witnessed by those nearest ˹to Allah˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Single Difference**

Verses 7 to 9 and verses 18 to 21 are parallel in almost every element: the particle of refusal, the class named, a unique destination, the idiom of realization, and a sealed record. This verse is the only element present in the second sequence and absent from the first. Nothing further is said of the sealed book belonging to the wicked. That of the virtuous is sealed and it is also witnessed. The addition is what distinguishes the two movements, and its position as the last word of the parallel sequence makes it impossible to miss. A reader who has followed both movements arrives at this verse expecting the same three words he received before, and receives an extra clause instead. The extra clause is the chapter's statement of what the virtuous have that the wicked do not.

**Yashhaduhu**

The verb is from *sh-h-d*, the root of witnessing, testifying, being present at, and observing. The form here is active with the record as its object: they witness it, they attend it, they testify to it. Witnessing in the Qur'an carries a legal sense — a witness establishes a fact in a proceeding — and a perceptual sense, being present at something. Both are present in this verse. The record of the virtuous is attended by beings who are present at it and who testify to it, and the testimony is what gives the document its standing. A sealed book witnessed by persons of rank is a document whose contents cannot be disputed, and the undisputability is the reward's first feature, preceding every description of gardens and wine.

**Al-Muqarrabūn**

The witnesses are *al-muqarrabūn*, the ones brought near. The participle is passive and intensive: those who have been caused to draw near, the people of proximity. The rank is named elsewhere in the Qur'an, and its naming there confirms its status. Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah divides humanity into three groups and places the foremost at the head: the foremost in faith will be the foremost in Paradise, and they are the ones nearest to God (56:10–11). The same word describes the soul at the moment of death when the deceased is one of those brought near (56:88). The rank is therefore recognized and exalted, and its members are the witnesses named in this verse. Nothing here describes them further, and the description is unnecessary for a reader who knows the term.

**Nearness Against Veiling**

The chapter's other movement ended with the wicked veiled off from their Lord (83:15). This movement contains the people brought near. The two participles are exact opposites in the same semantic field of proximity and access: *maḥjūbūn* and *muqarrabūn*. The opposition is the chapter's central contrast, and it is stated in two words eleven verses apart. A person excluded from access and a person brought into proximity describe the same axis from its two ends, and the axis is the one the chapter has been constructing since verse 6, where all people stand before the Lord of the worlds. Standing before Him is common to everyone; being brought near to Him is not; being veiled from Him is the alternative. This verse names the second of the three positions.

**Why the Record Needs Witnesses**

A sealed record requires no witness for its own validity — what is written is written, and the sealing fixes it. The witness is therefore for someone else, and the someone else is the person whose record it is and whoever might contest it. The Qur'an's account of the Day includes moments of dispute, denial, and self-justification, and the presence of witnesses closes them. Attending that document are beings of the highest rank, and their attendance means that no claim against it can be sustained. Nothing in the verse says that a contest occurs, and the silence leaves the witnessing as an honour rather than a defence. The record is witnessed because it is worth witnessing, and the worth is what the following verses describe.

**The Witness and the Assembly**

Witnessing is a public act, and the public character of this verse contrasts with the privacy of the chapter's opening. The defrauder acted in transactions that no one observed; the shortfall was known only to him and to the buyer who did not notice. The record of his conduct is sealed in Sijjīn without witness. Their document is sealed and attended. The contrast converts the private act into a public document in one case and leaves it private in the other, and the conversion is what the chapter offers as the ground of its summons. A person who gives the full measure when no one is watching has his giving witnessed by those brought near, and the witnessing is the reward's first and least describable component.

**Witnesses of Rank**

The witnessing is performed by the highest order of beings, and the choice of witnesses is what confers honour on the document. A record witnessed by nobodies is a private paper; a record witnessed by those brought near is a public attestation before the court of the Day. The Qur'an describes the same rank as inheriting Gardens of Delight, being a multitude from earlier generations and a few from later (56:12–14), and the scarcity of the later generations makes the witnessing more distinguished rather than less. Those who attend the record of the virtuous are few, and their fewness is a measure of the value of what they attend. Nothing in this verse describes their appearance or their number, and the withholding keeps the attention on the fact of the witnessing rather than on the witnesses.

**The End of the Parallel Sequence**

After this verse the chapter abandons the parallel structure and moves into description. The wicked were described through their denial, their hearts, their veiling, and their burning; the virtuous are now described through their bliss, their couches, their faces, their wine, and their spring. The descriptions are not parallel — the movement on the virtuous is seven verses long and the movement on the wicked, after its diagnosis, was two. The asymmetry begins here, and this verse is its hinge. The witnessing of the record is the last formal element shared with the other movement, and everything after it belongs to the depiction of a reward that the chapter is willing to elaborate at length.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:22

> Surely the virtuous will be in bliss,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Resumption After the Document**

Verses 18 to 21 named the destination, asked about it, and answered with a sealed record witnessed by those brought near. This verse resumes the description of the class itself, and the resumption is marked by a return to the emphatic construction: *inna al-abrār la-fī naʿīm*. The particle *inna* and the *lām* of the predicate produce the doubly emphasized statement used throughout the chapter for its central claims. The emphasis is not new information but a settling of what has been established. The record has been named; the state of its holders is now declared. The declaration is the point from which the following six verses unfold, and each of them adds one feature of the state.

**Bliss as an Enclosure**

The preposition is *fī*, in, and it denotes containment. The virtuous are not given bliss as a possession to be held; they are inside it as an environment. The construction is the one used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār for both classes — the virtuous in bliss, the wicked in Hell — and its use here continues that formula. Containment has a specific implication: what encloses cannot be put down, set aside, or lost. A possession can be taken; a condition one is inside cannot be. The verse therefore describes the reward in terms that exclude its cessation, and the exclusion is made grammatically rather than by the addition of a word meaning forever. The chapter does not say that the bliss is eternal; it says that they are in it, and the being-in carries the permanence.

**Naʿīm**

The word denotes delight, ease, softness of life, and the condition of one whose needs are met and exceeded. Its root is used of a life free of hardship and of the enjoyment that follows sufficiency. In the Qur'an it names both the pleasures of this world, which are temporary and will be asked about (102:8), and the condition of Paradise, which is what this verse describes. The same word in the two contexts is what makes the verse's claim intelligible: the delight known in this life is a name borrowed from the delight that is proper. Nothing in the verse specifies the content of the bliss, and the unspecified noun allows the following verses to fill it with couches, faces, wine, musk, and a spring.

**A Class Named, Not a Person**

The subject is *al-abrār*, the virtuous, a plural denoting a class. The chapter has moved from the singular transgressor of verse 12 to a collective designation here, and the movement is significant. The diagnosis of denial was made about an individual type — every evildoing transgressor — while the reward is described for a group. The difference reflects the nature of the two states: transgression is committed by persons one at a time, while bliss is inhabited in company. The verses that follow describe a social scene — couches, faces recognized, a drink shared — and the social character of the reward is prepared by the plural subject of this verse. Nothing in the chapter describes the virtuous as alone, and the absence of solitude is one of the differences between the two movements.

**The Verse as a Heading**

Functionally this verse is the title of the section that follows. Verses 23 to 28 are its contents: the couches and the gazing, the glow on the faces, the sealed wine, the musk, the exhortation to compete, the flavour from Tasnīm, and the spring from which those brought near drink. Each verse adds one item to a list whose head is this declaration. The technique is common in the Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise, where a general statement of reward is followed by particulars. What distinguishes this instance is the brevity of the heading — five words — and the specificity of the particulars that follow. The chapter gives almost nothing in the general statement and almost everything in the details.

**The Contrast With the Two Preceding Verses**

Verses 16 and 17 described burning and an announcement. This verse describes bliss, eleven words later. The proximity is deliberate and severe. A reader who has just heard the deniers told that the Fire is what they denied is told, without transition, that the virtuous are in delight. The absence of transition means that the two statements are held together rather than separated by an argument, and the holding together is the chapter's presentation of the two outcomes. Nothing softens the juxtaposition. No verse explains why one class receives one state and the other receives the other; the explanation has already been given in verses 12 and 14, and it is not repeated here. The reward arrives as a fact adjacent to the punishment.

**The Shortest Heading and the Longest List**

Five words introduce seven verses of description, and the proportion is the chapter's method in miniature. The general statement is kept as brief as possible so that the particulars can carry the weight, and the particulars are concrete enough to be pictured — a couch, a gaze, a face, a cup, a fragrance, a spring. Nothing abstract is offered as the reward. The chapter that began with the most concrete of sins, a handful withheld from a scale, ends with the most concrete of rewards, and the concreteness on both sides is what makes the argument continuous. A person who understands a measure can understand a cup, and a person who understands a cup can be told to compete for it. The general heading exists to organize the list, not to replace it.

**Why the Bliss Is Stated Before It Is Described**

The order — declaration, then particulars — has an effect on how the particulars are received. A reader who encounters couches, wine, and musk without a preceding statement of bliss might treat them as a list of comforts. Encountering the statement first, he receives them as the contents of a condition already declared. The declaration therefore governs the description, and the governance means that no single item in the following verses can be taken as the reward itself. The reward is the being-in-bliss; the couches, the faces, the wine, and the spring are its features. The verse establishes the whole before the parts, and the establishment is what prevents the parts from being read as an inventory.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:23

> ˹seated˺ on ˹canopied˺ couches, gazing around.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Particular**

Verse 22 declared the bliss; this verse gives its first feature. The movement from declaration to description is immediate, and the description begins with a posture. The virtuous are on couches, and they are looking. Nothing is said yet about what they see, what they eat, or what they drink; the verse establishes where they are and what they are doing. The choice of a posture as the opening particular is significant, because a posture indicates a state of the body over time. Sitting on a couch is not an action but a condition sustained, and the condition is rest. The chapter's description of reward therefore begins with the absence of exertion, which is the first thing a person who has laboured in the market would recognize as different.

**Arāʾik**

The word is the plural of *arīkah*, a couch or bed furnished with a canopy or curtains — a piece of furniture associated in Arabic usage with brides, with royalty, and with the ease of a person who does not need to rise. The translation supplies the canopy in brackets because the word's sense includes it. The Qur'an uses the same word in several descriptions of Paradise: the believers and their spouses reclining in shade on canopied couches (36:56); the people of the Gardens of Eternity reclining on them (18:31); the virtuous reclining on them, never seeing scorching heat or bitter cold (76:13). The recurrence marks the couch as a fixed element of the Qur'an's depiction, and its recurrence across Makkan and Medinan passages indicates a settled image rather than an incidental detail.

**Reclining as a Posture of Arrival**

The related verb in the parallel passages denotes reclining rather than sitting upright, and the reclining is the posture of one who has arrived and intends to remain. A person who reclines is not preparing to leave, to work, or to defend himself. The posture excludes all three. In the context of this chapter the exclusion is pointed: the defrauder's life was one of standing at a scale, watching a quantity, and calculating a margin. The virtuous are described as reclining on furnished couches. The contrast is between two bodily states, and the chapter presents them without comment. Nothing in the verse says that the reclining is a reward for labour, and the absence of the statement leaves the reader to make the comparison with the chapter's opening himself.

**Yanẓurūn**

The verb is from *n-ẓ-r*, to look, gaze, and consider. The form here is intransitive and continuous: they are gazing, they are looking about. The object is not stated, and the unstated object opens several readings. Some take it as gazing at the blessings around them; others as looking at what God has given them; others as looking at their Lord. The generality of the verb allows all of these, and the allowance is a feature rather than a defect. A gaze with a specified object would be a single act; a gaze without one is a sustained condition of attention. The virtuous are people who are looking, and the looking is unhurried, unthreatened, and uninterrupted — the opposite of the watchfulness of a merchant guarding a margin.

**Gazing and the Face**

The verse that follows describes what the gazing produces or accompanies: a glow of delight recognizable on their faces. The two verses therefore work together, one describing the direction of attention and the other its visible result. The sequence is physiologically exact — sustained ease shows in the face — and the showing is what makes the state perceivable to others. Nothing in this verse describes company, but the recognizability of the faces in the next verse implies that there are others present to recognize them. The couches of Paradise are described elsewhere as set in rows and as occupied by people facing one another, and the implication here is the same: the gazing is not solitary.

**The Couch in the Qur'an's Depiction**

The image belongs to a small set of furnishings that recur in the descriptions of Paradise: couches, cushions, carpets, vessels, cups, and thrones raised high. Sūrat al-Ghāshiyah lists several of them in sequence — thrones raised high, cups set at hand, cushions lined up, carpets spread out (88:13–16). Sūrat al-Raḥmān describes the believers reclining on furnishings lined with rich brocade, with the fruit of both Gardens hanging within reach (55:54). The set is consistent and its consistency means that each item carries the others. This verse contributes the couch and the gaze, and the gaze is its distinctive addition — the furnishings are described elsewhere, but the looking of the people on them is this chapter's contribution to the image.

**Gazing Without a Named Object**

The verb of looking is left without an object, and the leaving is what allows the following verses to supply the contents of the gaze. The faces glow; the drink is given; the seal is musk; the mixture is from a spring. Each of these can be what the virtuous are looking at, and none is specified. The unspecified gaze is therefore a form of attention that the description fills without ever closing. A reader who wanted to know what Paradise looks like is given a series of items and a posture, and the posture tells him that the items are being looked at rather than merely present. The gazing is what converts an inventory into a scene, and the scene is what makes the exhortation of verse 26 possible.

**Rest as the First Feature of the Reward**

The order of the particulars tells the reader what the chapter considers most characteristic of the reward. The first is not food, drink, companionship, or vision but a posture of rest with an unhurried gaze. The placement is appropriate to a chapter whose opening described a man engaged in the smallest possible calculation. The rest is the negation of that calculation, and the gaze is the negation of that watchfulness. Nothing in the verse names the negation, and the naming is unnecessary, because the posture carries it. A reader who has followed the chapter from the defrauders to the couches receives the second as the answer to the first, and the answer is given in a bodily state rather than in a proposition.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:24

> You will recognize on their faces the glow of delight.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Statement Addressed to an Observer**

The verse shifts person. The previous verse described the virtuous in the third person; this verse addresses a second person who will recognize something on their faces. The address is not specified — it may be the Prophet, any hearer, or the people of Paradise observing one another — and the unspecified addressee makes the statement available to whoever reads it. The effect is to place the reader inside the scene as a witness. He is not being told what the virtuous will experience; he is being told what he will see when he looks at them. The shift from experience to appearance is what allows the chapter to describe an inner state, since inner states can only be reported through their visible signs.

**Taʿrifu**

The verb is from *ʿ-r-f*, to know, recognize, and be acquainted with. Its use here is specifically recognition — knowing a thing by its marks, distinguishing it from others. The verse does not say that the observer will learn something new; it says that he will recognize. Recognition presupposes prior acquaintance, and the acquaintance in this case is with the human face as an indicator of condition. A person who has seen grief, fear, exhaustion, or joy on faces knows what these look like. The verse relies on that knowledge and applies it to a state whose cause is named: delight. The recognition is therefore an act of ordinary perception applied to an extraordinary object, and the application is what makes the description credible.

**Naḍrah**

The word denotes brightness, freshness, radiance, and the bloom of a face that is well-provided for. Its root is used of vegetation that is lush and of a complexion that is healthy and luminous. The translation renders it as glow, which carries both the light and the health. The word is attached here to *naʿīm*, the bliss of the previous verse, so that the phrase is the glow or bloom of delight. The construction makes the inner state visible by naming its effect on the face, and the naming is precise: not a smile, not an expression, but a quality of the skin and the countenance. The Qur'an uses the same root elsewhere for the faces of that Day — faces glowing with bliss, fully pleased with their striving (88:8–9).

**The Face as the Chapter's Indicator**

This chapter is concerned throughout with what is hidden and what is disclosed. The shortfall in the measure is hidden; the record is sealed; the covering on the heart is invisible; the dismissal is spoken. This verse is the point at which a hidden state becomes visible without being spoken. The bliss of the virtuous is not announced by them or described by a narrator; it is read on their faces by whoever looks. The visibility is the reward's social dimension, and it contrasts with the wicked, whose condition in verse 15 is described as exclusion and whose announcement in verse 17 is made to them rather than by them. One class is recognized; the other is addressed. The difference between being recognized and being addressed is the difference the chapter draws between the two outcomes.

**The Same Image in al-Ghāshiyah**

Sūrat al-Ghāshiyah opens by dividing the faces of that Day into two groups: faces humiliated, labouring, exhausted, entering a scorching fire; and faces on that Day glowing with bliss, fully pleased with their striving (88:2–9). The division by faces is the same technique used here, and its recurrence shows that the Qur'an's account of the Day habitually reads states from countenances. The addition in al-Ghāshiyah is the pleasure with striving, which supplies the cause of the glow as effort well spent. This verse supplies the cause as bliss itself. The two accounts agree that the face is a document, and the agreement is what makes the sealed record of verse 20 intelligible as a description of persons rather than of paper.

**Delight Recognized by Others**

The recognition is mutual or at least shared, and the sharing is what makes the scene social. A couch occupied by one person gazing alone would not produce a verse addressed to an observer. The presence of the observer in the grammar implies the presence of company, and the company is what the following verses develop — a drink given, a seal of musk, an exhortation to compete, a spring from which those brought near drink. The scene is populated, and the population is populated by people whose faces disclose their state to one another. Nothing in this verse describes the relations among them, and the absence of description leaves the recognition as the sole stated form of contact. They see each other's delight, and the seeing is the first feature of the company.

**Faces Instead of Testimony**

The wicked are described in this chapter by what they say — ancient fables — and by what is said to them. The virtuous are described by their faces. The difference is between speech and appearance, and appearance is more reliable than speech in the Qur'an's account of the Day, where the seals are set on mouths and the hands and feet testify. A face that glows cannot be arranged or faked, and its glow is the visible counterpart of the sealed record. The chapter therefore gives the virtuous a testimony that requires no words, and the wordless testimony is what stands against the words of verse 13. Those words were a dismissal; this appearance is a disclosure, and the disclosure is made by the body rather than by the tongue.

**The Glow as the Answer to the Stain**

Verse 14 described a covering on the hearts of the wicked, formed by what they accumulated. This verse describes a brightness on the faces of the virtuous, produced by what they have been given. The two images are internal states made manifest, and the manifest forms are opposite: a layer that obscures and a glow that discloses. The chapter's vocabulary of surfaces — the rust on the heart, the veil from the Lord, the brightness of the face — is consistent, and the consistency means that the reader is being shown the same principle twice. What a person accumulates or is given becomes visible, and the visibility is final. The stain could be hidden in this life; the glow cannot be hidden in the next, and neither can the stain.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:25

> They will be given a drink of sealed, pure wine,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Passive of Giving**

The verb is *yusqawna* — they will be given drink, they will be watered. The passive form is important: the virtuous do not obtain the drink, acquire it, or pour it. It is given to them. The passivity is consistent with the posture of the previous two verses — reclining on couches, gazing — and it establishes the character of the whole scene. Nothing in the description requires an act from its inhabitants. They are seated, they are recognized, and they are given. The absence of acquisition is the negation of the chapter's opening scene, in which a man takes a full measure for himself and gives a short one to another. Here nothing is taken, and the giving is done to them by an agent who is not named.

**Raḥīq**

The word denotes a pure, choice wine — specifically, wine that is unmixed and free of dregs, the best of what is drunk. Its use distinguishes the drink of Paradise from the wine of this world in the one respect the Qur'an consistently emphasizes: purity. The wine of this world is described elsewhere as causing the loss of reason and as the work of Satan, and the wine of Paradise is described as delicious to drink and as not containing anything that harms. Sūrat Muḥammad lists it among the rivers of Paradise, alongside water that does not stagnate, milk whose taste does not change, and clarified honey (47:15). The inclusion of wine in that list, and the specification of purity here, together establish that the drink named in this verse shares only its name with the prohibited one.

**Makhtūm**

The participle is from *kh-t-m*, to seal, stamp, and close — the same root used for the seal of the prophets and for the closing of a document. Applied to the drink it denotes a vessel that has been sealed, and the sealing carries two implications. The first is that the contents are protected and untouched; the second is that they are choice, since only a valuable drink is sealed. The following verse describes the seal itself as musk, which the translation renders as the last sip smelling like musk, so that the seal is not wax but the fragrance that closes the drinking. The word's recurrence in the chapter is significant: the record of both classes was described as *markūm*, sealed and inscribed, and the drink of the virtuous is *makhtūm*, sealed. The chapter's vocabulary of sealing extends from documents to cups.

**The Second Sealed Thing**

Verses 9 and 20 described a sealed book for each class. This verse describes a sealed drink for one of them. The parallel is not incidental. Sealing in this chapter marks finality and integrity: a book that cannot be amended and a cup that has not been opened. The wicked have the first and not the second; the virtuous have both. The distribution is the chapter's argument about the two classes stated in the imagery of closure. Nothing is said about the record of the virtuous being opened for them, and nothing is said about the drink of the wicked being sealed. The asymmetry of the two sealings is what the reader is meant to notice, and the noticing is left to him without comment.

**Given Rather Than Earned**

The verse's grammar removes the drink from the economy of exchange. A person who buys wine pays for it; a person who is given wine does not. The chapter has been about exchange — measure for measure, weight for weight, a shortfall taken and a loss given — and its description of Paradise suspends exchange entirely. The drink is given, the couches are furnished, the faces are brightened, and nothing is paid. The suspension is not a denial that the reward corresponds to conduct; the sealed record establishes the correspondence. What is suspended is the mechanism of payment. The reward is delivered as a gift to people whose deeds have been recorded, and the delivery as gift is what distinguishes it from a wage.

**The Drink in the Qur'an's Depiction of Paradise**

The image belongs to a set that recurs: rivers of wine, cups of crystal filled precisely as desired, silver vessels, a drink flavoured with camphor, a spring from which God's servants drink flowing at their will (76:5–6, 76:15–16). Sūrat al-Ghāshiyah lists cups set at hand among the furnishings of the elevated Garden (88:14). The set is consistent and its items are shared across chapters, which means that each occurrence carries the others. This verse contributes the sealed pure wine and the musk that follows it, and the contribution is distinctive in one respect: the sealing. No other description of the drink of Paradise specifies that the vessel is closed, and the specification belongs to a chapter whose whole vocabulary is about documents that have been finished and closed.

**A Cup That Cannot Be Bought**

The chapter's opening described a transaction, and its description of Paradise describes a gift. Between the two lies the whole of the chapter's argument about measure, record, and destination. The sealed cup is the final negation of the market: it is pure, it is closed, it is given, and its last sip is the best part of it. Nothing in the economy of exchange can produce such a cup, because exchange requires that something be withheld in order to be paid for. The giving here withholds nothing, and the non-withholding is the definition of the reward. A reader who has understood the defrauder understands what has been reversed, and the reversal is described in the grammar of a passive verb rather than in a statement about generosity.

**The Verse Ends on a Comma**

The description continues into the following verse, which describes the seal as musk and then turns, without transition, to an exhortation: let whoever aspires to this compete. The continuation means that the drink is described in two verses and that the second of them changes register mid-sentence. The reader is given the wine, given its fragrance, and then addressed. The address is the chapter's only direct exhortation in its second half, and it arrives inside a description of a drink. The placement is deliberate: the summons is attached to the object of desire rather than separated from it, and the attachment is what makes the summons persuasive rather than imperative.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:26

> whose last sip will smell like musk. So let whoever aspires to this strive ˹diligently˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Khitāmuhū Misk**

The phrase is *khitāmuhu misk* — its seal is musk, its closing is musk. The commentators explain it in two related ways: the vessel is sealed with musk rather than with clay, and the last of the drink, the final sip or the dregs that remain at the bottom of the cup, has the fragrance of musk. The translation adopts the second, and both readings agree that the musk is at the end. The placement of the finest element last is a deliberate reversal of ordinary drinking, in which the best is at the beginning and the dregs at the bottom. A drink whose seal and whose remainder are musk improves toward its end, and the improvement is what the following exhortation appeals to. Nothing in this life behaves that way, and the difference is stated in three words.

**Musk**

Musk is the most valued fragrance in the Arabic vocabulary of the Qur'an's audience, and its mention is a marker of the highest quality rather than a specific detail of the drink. The Qur'an uses the same word for the fragrance associated with the reward of the virtuous elsewhere, and its recurrence indicates a settled image of Paradise as a place of scent. The scent is not incidental to the description: the previous verse described the drink, this verse describes its fragrance, and the fragrance is what remains after the drinking is finished. The chapter therefore gives the reward a feature that persists past its consumption, and the persistence is the point of naming the last sip rather than the first.

**A Sentence That Turns**

The verse is divided into two halves of entirely different kinds. The first describes the drink; the second addresses the reader. The turn is unmarked — no particle of transition, no new verse — and the absence of marking makes it abrupt. The reader is inside a description of Paradise and is suddenly spoken to. The technique is one of the Qur'an's most characteristic moves, and its effect here is to convert the description into an offer. A description of a reward can be received as information about other people; the same description with an exhortation attached must be received as an address. The chapter's second half has been describing the virtuous in the third person throughout, and this verse breaks the pattern for the only time.

**Falyatanāfas al-Mutanāfisūn**

The phrase is *wa-fī dhālika falyatanāfas al-mutanāfisūn* — and in that let the competitors compete, let those who aspire strive. The root *n-f-s* is connected with the self, with what is precious, and with the reluctance to let a thing pass to another; the form used here denotes competing for something valued, striving to be the one who obtains it. The verb and its subject come from the same root, which produces an intensive construction: let the aspirants aspire, let those given to competition compete. The repetition inside the sentence is emphatic, and the emphasis is directed at the appropriate use of a disposition the audience already possesses. The urge to outdo others is not condemned here; it is redirected.

**Competition as a Commanded Attitude**

The Qur'an commands competition in several places and always attaches it to a spiritual object. Compete with one another in good works, with the assurance that God will bring everyone together (2:148). Compete for forgiveness from your Lord and a Garden as vast as the heavens and the earth (57:21). Hasten toward forgiveness and the same Garden (3:133). The commands are addressed to people who already compete — for wealth, for status, for measure and weight — and the redirection is the whole of the instruction. This verse performs the same redirection in the shortest form: in that, let the aspirants aspire. The object is the sealed wine and its musk, and the object's desirability has been established by two verses of description before the command is given.

**The Only Exhortation in the Second Half**

The chapter's second half contains eleven verses of description — the record, the witness, the bliss, the couches, the faces, the wine, the musk, the spring — and one exhortation. The single exhortation is placed inside the description of the drink rather than at the end of the section, and the placement means that the reader is addressed at the moment of greatest desirability. An exhortation at the end of the section would have come after the description had concluded and the desire had begun to fade. An exhortation inside the description arrives while the wine is still being described, and the arrival is what gives it its force. The chapter's method of address is therefore timed rather than merely placed, and the timing is the reason this verse is the pivot of the movement.

**Musk as the Last Thing**

The placement of the finest element at the end of the drinking is the verse's central claim about Paradise, and it is a claim about time. In this world the best of anything comes first and the remainder deteriorates; the fragrance of a drink is at its opening and the dregs are at its bottom. A reward whose last sip is musk reverses the order, and the reversal means that nothing in it is lost by being continued. The exhortation to compete is therefore addressed to people whose experience has taught them that everything declines, and the teaching is what makes the exhortation necessary. To aspire to a thing that ends better than it begins requires a redirection of the disposition that has been trained on things which end worse.

**Aspiration Against Defrauding**

The contrast between this verse and the chapter's opening is exact. The defrauder aspires to a small quantity and competes for a margin; the aspirant addressed here competes for a sealed drink whose last sip is musk. Both are engaged in the same activity — seeking an advantage over what they currently have — and the verse does not condemn the activity but reassigns its object. The reassignment is the chapter's practical conclusion. Nothing in the verse says to stop competing; it says to compete for this. The command is therefore addressed to the same disposition that produced the fraud, and the disposition is treated not as a fault to be removed but as an energy to be directed toward an object worthy of it.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:27

> And this drink’s flavour will come from Tasnîm—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Third Unique Name**

The chapter has introduced three proper names found nowhere else in the Qur'an: Sijjīn for the destination of the wicked, ʿIlliyūn for the destination of the virtuous, and Tasnīm for the source of their drink. The three names are introduced without etymology, without location, and without parallel passages, and each is followed by a partial clarification rather than a definition. Sijjīn is identified with a sealed record; ʿIlliyūn with a sealed record witnessed by those brought near; Tasnīm with a spring from which those brought near drink. The pattern of naming is the chapter's distinctive contribution to the Qur'an's vocabulary of the unseen, and the pattern is consistent: a new word, then a statement of what it is connected to, then no further explanation.

**Tasnīm**

The name is derived by the commentators from *sanam*, height or elevation, so that Tasnīm signifies something that rises or flows from above, and the form of the word denotes an elevation conferred. The sense of a drink that comes down from a height is what the following verse develops, since a spring from which the nearest drink is a source above them. The name is not explained in the chapter, and the unexplained name is followed immediately by a verse that describes it as a spring. The description supplies the category without defining the thing, which is the same procedure used for the other two names. The reader learns what kind of entity Tasnīm is and nothing further about it.

**Mizāj**

The word translated as flavour is *mizāj*, from *m-z-j*, to mix, blend, and adulterate. It denotes the admixture that gives a drink its character — what is mixed into it to make it what it is. The Qur'an uses the same word in Sūrat al-Insān for a cup whose mixture is camphor (76:5) and for a drink whose mixture is ginger (76:17). The recurrence establishes *mizāj* as the technical term for the composition of Paradise's drinks, and its use here identifies Tasnīm as the mixing element of the sealed wine. The wine of verse 25 is pure; its mixture is from Tasnīm; and the mixture is what the next verse describes as a spring drunk by those brought near. The composition is therefore specified in two verses without being fully disclosed.

**The Verse Ends on a Dash**

The dash indicates continuation into the following verse, where Tasnīm is described as a spring. The suspension is the same technique used in verses 2, 5, 7, 10, and 18, and its recurrence makes the chapter a sequence of statements that complete one another across boundaries. Nothing in this verse is a complete sentence in the ordinary sense; it announces a source and leaves the source undescribed. The effect is to hold the reader's attention on the name for the length of a verse boundary, and the holding is what makes the description in the next verse arrive as a disclosure. A name introduced and immediately explained would be a footnote; a name introduced, held, and then explained is a revelation.

**The Hierarchy Inside the Reward**

The verse introduces a distinction that the following one specifies: the drink of Tasnīm is associated with those brought near. The sealed wine of verse 25 is given to the virtuous generally; the spring of verse 28 is drunk by the nearest. The distinction implies ranks within Paradise, and the ranks are named elsewhere in the Qur'an — the foremost being the ones nearest to God, in Gardens of Delight, a throng from the earlier peoples and a few from the later (56:10–14). This chapter does not elaborate the hierarchy; it marks it with two verses and moves on. The marking is significant because it means the reward described in verses 22 to 26 is not the whole of Paradise, and that something further belongs to a rank above the one being described.

**From the Cup to the Spring**

The movement of these two verses is from a vessel to a source. The reader has been given a sealed cup of pure wine whose last sip is musk; he is now told that its mixture comes from a spring. The shift from the particular drink to its origin is the same shift the chapter made from the destination to its record, and the method is consistent: name a thing, then name what it comes from. The origin is what confers the quality, and the quality is what the exhortation of verse 26 asked the reader to compete for. The chapter therefore grounds the desire it has just aroused in a source, and the source is named rather than described, which is the strongest form of grounding available to a text that has declared the matter beyond realization.

**Three Names and One Method**

Sijjīn, ʿIlliyūn, and Tasnīm are the chapter's three unique names, and each is handled the same way: named, then connected to something the reader can hold — a sealed book, a sealed book with witnesses, a spring drunk by those brought near. The method gives the unseen a structure without giving it a description, and the structure is what the reader is asked to accept. Nothing in the chapter claims that the names are metaphors or that they are literal, and the refusal to claim either is consistent with the idiom of realization used for the first two. The reader is told that these things exist, that they exceed his capacity to realize, and that what can be said about them is their relation to records and to ranks. The relation is the disclosure, and it is complete.

**Water and Wine in the Same Description**

The drink is wine and its mixture is from a spring. The two categories are joined without difficulty, and the joining is characteristic of the Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise, in which rivers of water, milk, wine, and honey run together and cups are filled from springs. The categories of this world's beverages do not organize the next world's, and the chapter's use of both terms in one sentence is a small instance of the general principle. Nothing in the verse reconciles them or explains how a wine is mixed from a spring, and the absence of explanation is consistent with the idiom of realization used twice earlier. The reader is told what the drink is mixed from and is not told how, and the withholding is the same withholding that has governed the chapter's naming throughout.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:28

> a spring from which those nearest ˹to Allah˺ will drink.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Description of Tasnīm**

Verse 27 named Tasnīm as the source of the drink's mixture and left it on a dash. This verse supplies what it is: a spring. The disclosure is a category rather than a description — the reader learns that the name denotes a flowing source of water, and nothing further. No location, no volume, no appearance, and no account of how a spring produces the mixture of a wine. The economy matches the treatment of the chapter's other two unique names, each of which was answered with a sealed book and no elaboration. Tasnīm is answered with a spring and no elaboration. The three names therefore share a method of disclosure: identify the kind of thing, state its relation to the people concerned, and stop.

**A Spring and an Eye**

The word *ʿayn* means both an eye and a spring, and the double sense belongs to the same root. A spring is called an eye because it is the point at which water becomes visible, the source from which it looks out at the world. The Qur'an uses the word for the springs of Paradise repeatedly and for the springs of the earth as signs of provision. Its application here places Tasnīm in the category of the sources described elsewhere — a spring whose water the servants of God drink, flowing at their will (76:6), and a spring called Salsabīl in the same sūrah (76:18). The chapter's named source joins a small group of named springs in the Qur'an's depiction of Paradise, and the group is one of the few sets of proper names the depiction contains.

**Drinking By It**

The construction is *yashrabu bihā* — they drink by it, with it, from it. The preposition *bāʾ* here has been read in two ways: as instrumental, so that they drink by means of the spring, and as a preposition of accompaniment or location, so that they drink at it or with it. Both readings produce the same picture — the spring is not a distant source piped to a cup but a place where drinking happens. The commentators note that the preposition indicates that those brought near drink from the spring directly, while the wine mixed from it is given to the virtuous generally. The distinction is one of access, and access is the chapter's recurring theme.

**Those Brought Near Again**

*Al-muqarrabūn* appeared in verse 21 as the witnesses of the sealed record, and they return here as the drinkers at the spring. The repetition of the same term within seven verses establishes the rank as a distinct group inside the virtuous. The chapter therefore contains three tiers: the wicked in Sijjīn, the virtuous in bliss on couches given a sealed wine, and those brought near who witness the record and drink at the spring. Nothing in the chapter ranks them explicitly or explains how a person comes to belong to the highest tier, and the withholding is consistent with the idiom of realization. The tiers are named, their privileges are stated, and the way from one to another is not described.

**The Same Rank Elsewhere**

Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah names the group and gives its inheritance: the foremost in faith will be the foremost in Paradise, and they are the ones nearest to God, in Gardens of Delight, a multitude from earlier generations and a few from later (56:10–14). The same word describes the soul at death when the deceased is one of those brought near, for whom there is serenity, fragrance, and a Garden of Bliss (56:88–89). The two passages agree that nearness is a rank above the general company of the people of the right hand, and that it carries a distinct provision. This chapter's use of the term is therefore not an innovation but a reference to an established category, and the reference allows the verse to say very little and mean a great deal.

**The End of the Description**

This verse closes the movement on the virtuous, which ran from the particle of refusal in verse 18 through the unique name, the question, the sealed record, the witnesses, the bliss, the couches, the faces, the wine, the musk, the exhortation, the mixture, and the spring. Fourteen verses of description, and the last is the shortest and the highest. The movement ends not on the general company of the blessed but on the rank above them, and the ending is what leaves the reader with the summit rather than the middle. Nothing follows in the form of a summary or a conclusion. The chapter turns immediately, in the next verse, back to the wicked and to a scene of mockery in the street.

**The Turn Back to the Wicked**

The juxtaposition is the sharpest in the chapter. A spring drunk by those brought near is followed without transition by the statement that the wicked used to laugh at the believers. The absence of transition means the two scenes are held together, and the holding is what makes the reversal of verse 34 intelligible. The reader has seen the destination of the mockers' victims and the destination of the mockers, and he has seen them in that order within a few verses. The chapter's final movement is therefore prepared by adjacency rather than by argument, and the adjacency is the reason the movement needs only eight verses to complete itself.

**Water as the Highest Provision**

The Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise consistently place water at the centre — rivers flowing beneath the Gardens, a spring whose water does not stagnate, springs named and drunk from. Water is the provision without which nothing lives, and its abundance in the depiction is what makes the depiction comprehensible to a people for whom water was scarce. This verse gives the highest rank its provision in the same terms, and the terms are the most basic available. Those nearest to God drink at a spring. The simplicity is not a diminishment of the rank but its expression: the greatest nearness is met with the most necessary gift, and the gift is a source rather than a quantity.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:29

> Indeed, the wicked used to laugh at the believers,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Movement**

The chapter divides into three movements: the defrauders and the Day, the two records and the two destinations, and the scene of mockery with its reversal. This verse opens the third, and the opening returns to the vocabulary of the second — *al-fujjār*, the wicked, named in verse 7 and named again here. The return signals that the movement to come is about the same people described in the record of Sijjīn, now shown in the conduct that filled it. The chapter has described their document, their exclusion, their burning, and the announcement made to them; it now describes what they did among people. The ordering is significant, because it places the destination before the behaviour and lets the behaviour be read as the content of a book already sealed.

**A Habit in the Past**

The construction combines the emphatic particle *inna* with the auxiliary *kāna* and an imperfect verb: they were, emphatically, in the habit of laughing. The combination denotes a repeated past activity, not a single event. Nothing in the verse describes one occasion of mockery; it describes a practice. The practice has a duration, a frequency, and a settled character, and all three are carried by the grammatical form. The same form appears in the next three verses, each of which adds one element of the practice — the wink, the return home, the words spoken on seeing the faithful. Four verses describe one habit from four angles, and the habit is what the chapter's final movement presents as the content of the sealed record.

**Laughter as an Attack**

The laughter described here is not amusement but derision, and the Qur'an treats it as a form of harm. Sūrat al-Baqarah states that the life of this world has been made appealing to the disbelievers and that they mock the believers, adding that those who are mindful of God will rank above them on the Day of Judgement (2:212). Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ describes the disbelievers making fun of the Prophet when they see him, asking whether this is the one who speaks ill of their gods (21:36). The mockery in both cases is directed at faith itself and at the person who embodies it, and it functions as a social weapon rather than an expression of surprise. This verse places the weapon in the hands of the wicked as a habitual practice.

**The Asymmetry of the Scene**

The laughter presupposes a position of strength. A person laughs at another when he considers himself secure and the other exposed — secure in wealth, in numbers, in standing, and exposed in poverty, in fewness, in contempt. The chapter's opening described the same asymmetry in commercial terms: the man who takes a full measure from people and gives them less. The mocker occupies the same position in the street that the defrauder occupies at the scale. Nothing in the verse describes the believers' condition, and the absence of description allows the reader to supply it from the rest of the revelation: a small group, poor, unprotected, and holding a claim that their society considers absurd.

**The Verb That Returns**

The verb of laughing appears again in verse 34, where the believers are described as laughing at the disbelievers on that Day. The return of the same verb is the chapter's central structural device in this movement, and the return inverts its direction. Nothing else changes — not the act, not the parties, not the social grammar of the scene. What changes is the day on which it occurs. The verse under discussion therefore establishes a term that the chapter will use again in the opposite sense, and the establishment is what makes the later verse a reversal rather than a new statement. A reader who does not hear the first occurrence will not hear the second as its answer.

**Why the Chapter Ends With This Scene**

A chapter that has described sealed records, unique destinations, couches, faces, wine, musk, and a spring could have ended on the spring. It ends instead on a scene of street mockery and its reversal. The choice indicates what the chapter considers its practical matter. The defrauders and the mockers are the same people, and their offence is social — it is committed against other people in public, in transactions and in looks. A description of Paradise does not address that offence; a description of the offence and its reversal does. The chapter therefore ends where its audience lives, in the market and the street, and it ends by placing both locations under the Day described in its opening verses.

**The Wicked Named for the Last Time**

*Al-fujjār* appears twice in this chapter, in verse 7 and here, and the two occurrences frame the whole of its treatment of this class. The first names them as the occupants of Sijjīn, holders of a sealed record, veiled from their Lord, and burning. The second names them as people laughing in a street. Nothing in the chapter explains the connection between the two namings, and the absence of explanation is the chapter's method: the same word is used for the destination and for the conduct, and the reader is left to understand that the conduct is what produced the destination. The interval between the two verses is twenty-two, and the interval is filled with the description of the other class, so that the second naming of the wicked arrives after a long account of what the virtuous receive. The placement means that the laughter is heard against the sound of the reward.

**Laughter as a Form of Denial**

The movement on the wicked that ran from verse 7 to verse 17 described denial — of the Day, of the revelations, by hearts covered over. This movement describes the same denial performed socially. The man who laughs at a believer is not arguing against belief; he is exhibiting his disbelief in a form that requires no words. The exhibit is more effective than an argument because it does not have to be answered, and it is more damaging because it is witnessed by others. The chapter's decision to end with the exhibit rather than with the argument is consistent with its treatment of verse 13, where the dismissal was quoted rather than refuted. What people do with their faces and their gestures is recorded alongside what they say, and both are in the sealed book.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:30

> wink to one another whenever they passed by,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gesture**

The verb is *yataḡāmazūn*, from *gh-m-z*, to gesture with the eye, the brow, or the hand — to signal, nudge, or point at something without naming it. The gesture is a wink or a meaningful look, and its function is to communicate an assessment in a form that the person assessed cannot hear. The verse specifies the gesture rather than describing it generally, and the specification is what makes the scene concrete. A reader is given a bodily movement — the closing of one eye, the raising of a brow, the tilt of a head toward a passing person — and the movement is recognizable. The concreteness is the chapter's method throughout its third movement: not an account of disbelief but a record of what people did with their faces.

**A Reciprocal Form**

The verb is in a reciprocal pattern, denoting an action performed mutually: they winked at one another, they exchanged glances. The reciprocity is essential to the scene. The mockery is not a solitary opinion but a shared performance, and the sharing is what makes it socially effective. A man who winks alone has expressed nothing; a man who winks and is winked back at has established a consensus. The verse therefore describes the formation of a group opinion in the smallest possible unit — two looks. The group opinion is what verse 32 will report in words, and the words are the public version of a signal that has already been exchanged in private.

**Whenever They Passed By**

*Idhā marrū bihim* — when they passed by them. The pronouns admit two readings, and the commentators record both. Either the wicked passed by the believers, so that the mockery was directed at people they encountered in the street; or the believers passed by the wicked, so that the mockery was directed at people who came into their vicinity. The two readings differ in who is moving and who is stationary, and both produce the same scene: an encounter in public, a gesture exchanged, and a target who does not see the gesture or cannot respond to it. The ambiguity is not resolved by the chapter, and the unresolved pronoun makes the description apply to either arrangement. What is fixed is the encounter and the gesture.

**A Language Without Words**

The verse describes communication that has no verbal content. A wink does not state a proposition; it indicates one and relies on a prior understanding to supply it. The understanding in this case is supplied by verse 32, where the words are given: these people are truly astray. The gesture is therefore the compressed form of the sentence, and the sentence is the expanded form of the gesture. The chapter gives both, in that order, and the order means that the reader first sees the signal and then hears what it means. Nothing in the gesture itself is false or true, and its moral character comes entirely from the proposition it carries and from the fact that it is delivered behind the target's back.

**The Believers as the Object of a Look**

The scene makes the believers objects rather than participants. They are looked at, gestured about, and discussed; they do not speak, respond, or appear as agents anywhere in the four verses of the description. Their silence is not presented as weakness but as a fact of the arrangement, and the arrangement is what the reversal of verse 34 undoes. A reader who follows the movement sees a group of people who are talked about without being talked to, and the seeing prepares the later scene in which the same people are on couches looking on. The chapter's account of the mockery is therefore also an account of a social position — being the object of other people's gestures — and the position is what the Day reverses.

**Public Mockery and Its Audience**

A wink requires a third party as much as a target. The gesture is performed for whoever is present, and its effect on that person is to recruit him into the consensus. The mockery described in this verse is therefore not only an attack on the believers but an act of self-constitution by the mockers: they become a group by agreeing, wordlessly, about someone else. The Qur'an describes the same mechanism in Sūrat al-Baqarah, where the hypocrites return to their evil associates and assure them that they were only mocking, and God throws their mockery back at them (2:14–15). The recruitment of an audience is what makes public derision a social fact rather than a personal one, and the social fact is what the sealed record contains.

**The Gesture and the Record**

A wink leaves no trace. It occupies a fraction of a second, it is deniable by the person who made it, and it is invisible to the person it concerns. The chapter's insistence on recording it is therefore a statement about the scope of the sealed book described in verse 9. Nothing in that description limited the record to deeds of consequence, to transactions, or to speech. A gesture exchanged between two people in a street is in the document, and the document is finished and closed. The verse's inclusion of the smallest possible social act is what gives the chapter's account of the record its force, and the force is what makes the opening verses about a handful of grain intelligible. The scale and the eyebrow are recorded by the same keepers, in the same book, with the same finality.

**The Verse Continues Into the Next**

The description is a list of four habitual actions joined across verse boundaries: they used to laugh, wink, muse on returning home, and say on seeing the faithful. The list is a single sentence in effect, and each verse adds one item. The technique means that no item is complete on its own and that the reader accumulates the picture across four verses. The accumulation is what produces the weight of the description. A single act of mockery could be dismissed as an incident; four coordinated habits, performed in the street, at home, and on every encounter, describe a way of life. The chapter gives the way of life in four short verses and then reverses it in three.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:31

> and muse ˹over these exploits˺ upon returning to their own people.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Return**

*Idhā anqalabū* — when they turned back, when they returned. The verb is from *q-l-b*, a root meaning to turn over, to invert, to reverse, and to change direction. Its eighth form denotes the completion of a turning, and it is used in the Qur'an for returning home, for turning back from an expedition, and for the reversal of a state. The word's field of turning and inversion is not lost in this chapter, whose final movement is itself an inversion. The mockers return home after their encounter in the street, and the return is described with a verb whose root means to turn over. Nothing in the verse draws the connection, and none needs to be drawn; the reversal arrives three verses later and the root has already prepared it.

**To Their Own People**

*Ilā ahlihim* — to their family, their household, their own people. The phrase marks a change of audience. The wink of the previous verse was exchanged among the mockers in public; the musing of this verse happens at home, among people who are already theirs. The two audiences require different performances. In the street the gesture is brief and deniable; at home the account can be extended, elaborated, and enjoyed. The verse therefore describes the second stage of the mockery, in which what was signaled is recounted. The household is the place where the consensus established by the wink is consolidated into a story, and the story is what makes the mockery durable beyond the moment of the encounter.

**Tafakkahūn**

The verb is from *f-k-h*, a root connected with fruit, delight, and pleasant conversation. The form used here has been explained in two directions that converge: to amuse oneself, to entertain oneself with talk, and to speak about something with relish and irony. The commentators describe it as the talk of people who recount an amusing incident to one another for enjoyment. The word therefore denotes not merely speaking but enjoying the speaking, and the enjoyment is the verse's addition to the scene. What began as a gesture becomes a pleasure, and the pleasure is repeated at home. The chapter records not only the act of mockery but the satisfaction taken in it, and the satisfaction is what makes the habit a settled feature of these people's lives.

**Two Scenes, One Practice**

Verses 29 to 32 describe the mockery in four settings: among the believers, in passing, at home, and on seeing the faithful again. The multiplication of settings is the chapter's way of establishing that the practice is not occasional. It occupies the street and the house, the encounter and the recollection. Nothing in the four verses suggests a single quarrel, a specific provocation, or a particular occasion. The description is of a standing relation between two groups, and the relation is expressed in laughter, gestures, talk, and words. The accumulation of settings is what allows verse 33 to make its general reply and verse 34 to reverse the whole arrangement rather than one incident in it.

**Rehearsal as a Feature of Mockery**

The recollection at home is a rehearsal, and rehearsal is what converts an incident into a tradition. A group that recounts its mockery to itself is teaching its members how to mock and confirming who belongs. The verse describes this process in a single word, and the word's root of enjoyment indicates that the teaching is pleasurable. The chapter's interest in the rehearsal is consistent with its interest in accumulation elsewhere — the covering on the heart formed by what people used to earn, the sealed record filled by what people did. The mockery accumulates in the same way, by repetition and by recollection, and the accumulation is what the record contains.

**The Word's Irony**

A root meaning fruit and delight is used for the talk of people deriding others. The irony is present in the Arabic and is not accidental. What the mockers experience as pleasure is described in the vocabulary of pleasure, and the description does not correct their experience or deny it. The chapter's method is to report the scene as the participants live it and then to place the scene under the Day. The ironic use of a pleasant root for an unpleasant practice is part of that method: the verse grants that the mockery was enjoyed, and the granting is what makes the reversal of verse 34 a reversal of enjoyment rather than a denial that any occurred.

**A Practice With No Interior Limit**

The four verses of this movement describe the mockery in public and in private, in gesture and in words, on encounter and in recollection. Nothing remains outside the practice. There is no setting in which these people refrain, no company before which they are careful, and no moment of reflection recorded between the act and its repetition. The absence of an interior limit is the chapter's diagnosis, and it corresponds to the covering described in verse 14 — a heart on which a layer has formed until nothing reaches it. A person whose heart is sealed cannot stop himself by reflection, because reflection requires an organ that has been obstructed. The verse's description of the talk at home is therefore not an additional detail but the evidence that the practice has no brake, and the evidence is what makes the reversal of verse 34 necessary rather than merely fitting.

**What the Verse Adds to the Scene**

Without this verse the description would consist of a public act and a public statement. The verse adds the private dimension — the household, the recollection, the satisfaction — and the addition completes the picture of a practice that has no interior limit. What a person does in public can be constrained by the presence of those he is acting against; what he recounts at home cannot. The chapter therefore shows the mockery at its freest, in the place where it is most itself, and the showing is what makes the sealed record of verse 9 comprehensible as a document. Nothing was private. The wink in the street and the talk at home are both in the book, and the book is sealed.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:32

> And when they saw the faithful, they would say, “These ˹people˺ are truly astray,”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sight as the Trigger**

*Idhā raʾawhum* — when they saw them. The verse locates the speech in an encounter: the seeing precedes the saying, and the saying is habitual. Nothing provokes the statement except the presence of the believers. No question is asked of them, no argument is offered by them, no offence is committed by them; they are seen, and the seeing produces a verdict. The verse therefore describes a judgment triggered by appearance rather than by conduct, and the appearance in question is the mere fact of being faithful. The trigger's simplicity is what makes the scene indictable. A person who condemns another on sight has no grounds, and the absence of grounds is the chapter's implicit statement about the whole practice.

**A Doubly Emphatic Sentence**

The words attributed to the mockers are *inna hāʾulāʾ la-ḍāllūn* — indeed these are truly astray, with the emphatic particle and the *lām* of the predicate. The construction is the same one the Qur'an uses for its own central assertions: they are certainly bound for Sijjīn, they are certainly in bliss, they will surely burn. The mockers are given the strongest form of declaration the language provides, and the giving is ironic. Their confidence is expressed in the grammar of certainty, and the certainty is unfounded. Nothing in the verse notes the irony, and the noting is unnecessary; the following verse answers the declaration with a single clause, and the answer is more devastating for being short.

**Astray**

*Ḍāllūn* is the plural of one who has gone astray, from *ḍ-l-l*, the root of losing the way, wandering, and erring. The word is used throughout the Qur'an for those who have missed guidance, and it is the standard accusation of the disbelievers against the believers. Its application here is a complete inversion of the chapter's own framework. The people being called astray are described in verse 29 as believers and in this verse as the faithful; the people calling them astray are described as wicked, as deniers of the Day, as transgressors steeped in sin, and as people whose hearts are covered. The verse reports the inversion without correcting it in its own terms, and the correction arrives in the reversal of verse 34.

**The Demonstrative of Contempt**

*Hāʾulāʾ* — these. The demonstrative is used of people who are present and near, and its use without a noun is a form of pointing. The mockers do not say that the believers are astray; they say that *these* are astray, and the pointing is part of the insult. A pronoun of proximity reduces the persons indicated to objects in the visual field, and the reduction is what the gesture of verse 30 performed without words. The two verses therefore describe the same act in two media: the wink points, the sentence points. Nothing in the reported speech names the believers, describes them, or acknowledges them as speakers. They are indicated, and the indication is the whole content of the verdict.

**The Same Accusation Elsewhere**

The charge of being astray or foolish is the standard response to faith in the Qur'an's accounts. The hypocrites, told to believe as others believe, reply: will we believe as the fools believe? And the verse answers that it is they who are the fools, but they do not know (2:13). The disbelieving chiefs of Noah's people tell him that they see him as a fool and think him a liar (7:66). When the clear revelations are recited, the disbelievers ask the believers mockingly which of the two parties is better in status and superior in assembly (19:73). The recurrence shows that the accusation in this verse belongs to a settled pattern of response, and the pattern is one in which the accuser's own condition is the thing described.

**Projection as the Mechanism**

The mockers attribute to the believers the condition the chapter has attributed to the mockers. The believers are called astray by people who deny the Day of Judgement, whose hearts are covered by what they earned, who are veiled from their Lord, and who are bound for Sijjīn. The attribution is therefore a projection, and the Qur'an names the mechanism explicitly in the case of the fools of Sūrat al-Baqarah: it is they who are the fools, but they do not know (2:13). The not-knowing is the covering described in verse 14 of this chapter. A heart that has been sealed by its own accumulation cannot recognize its own condition, and the unrecognized condition is exported onto whoever is nearest.

**The Verdict and Its Author**

A verdict requires a judge, and the verse's reply in the following line is that these people have not been appointed as judges. The verdict itself is nevertheless recorded in full, in the strongest grammatical form available, and the recording is significant. The chapter does not omit the accusation or soften it into a report that they considered the believers mistaken. It quotes the sentence with its emphatic particles intact, so that the reader hears the confidence of the speakers. The confidence is what makes the reply effective, and the reply is what makes the confidence unfounded. Between the two lies the whole of the chapter's earlier argument — the denial of the Day, the covering of the heart, the veiling from the Lord — which explains how a person covered and veiled can be certain about the guidance of others.

**The Verse's Place in the Sequence**

Four verses have described the mockery — the laughter, the wink, the talk at home, and now the spoken verdict. This verse is the climax of the description, because it is the only one that gives words. The gesture was deniable, the recollection was private, and the laughter was ambiguous; the sentence is explicit. The chapter therefore saves the clearest evidence for last, and the clarity is what verse 33 can answer directly. The sequence is a legal one in effect: the conduct is described, the statement is recorded, and the reply is made. Nothing in the reply engages with the content of the statement, because the content is a claim about guidance made by people whose own guidance has been described in eleven preceding verses.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:33

> even though they were not sent as keepers over the believers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reply**

This verse is the chapter's only direct answer to the mockers, and it is one clause long. The accusation of verse 32 was that the faithful are astray; the reply is that the accusers were not sent as keepers over them. The answer does not defend the believers, describe their guidance, or refute the charge of straying. It denies the accusers' standing. The technique is precise: rather than contesting a claim, the verse removes the position from which the claim could be made. A person who has no mandate to supervise others cannot render a verdict on their condition, and the absence of mandate is stated as a fact rather than argued as a principle.

**Not Sent**

*Wa-mā ursilū* — and they were not sent. The verb is from *r-s-l*, the root of sending, of message, and of messenger. Its passive form is the Qur'an's standard way of describing the commissioning of a prophet: messengers were sent, a messenger was sent to a people, We sent so-and-so. The verse's use of it for the mockers is therefore charged with irony. Sending is a function of God, and it confers authority on the one sent. The mockers have not been sent, and the negation is absolute — not that they were sent badly, or sent to someone else, or sent without instruction, but that they were not sent at all. Their supervision of the believers has no origin, and the absence of origin is the whole of the reply.

**Keepers**

*Ḥāfiẓīn* is the same word used in Sūrat al-Infiṭār for the watchers set over every soul: you are certainly observed by vigilant ones — noble angels who record (82:10–11). The root *ḥ-f-ẓ* covers guarding, preserving, and keeping, and its use in both chapters describes a real function performed by real agents. The recurrence makes this verse's negation sharper. Keepers over human beings do exist, and they are angels commissioned by God. The mockers are not among them. The verse therefore does not deny that the believers are kept; it denies that these people are the keepers. The distinction is between a function that exists and a claim to perform it without appointment.

**The Preposition of Authority**

*ʿAlayhim* — over them. The preposition *ʿalā* denotes position above, and in this construction it carries the sense of authority and supervision. The same preposition was used in verse 2 of this chapter for the defrauders taking measure *upon* people, and its use there also indicated an advantage taken over another. The recurrence ties the two movements together: the man at the scale takes upon people what is not his, and the mocker in the street claims over believers an authority he does not hold. Both are acts of uncommissioned supervision, and both are recorded. Nothing in the verse draws the parallel, and the parallel is available to anyone who reads the chapter from its first line.

**The Guardianship That Does Exist**

The Qur'an assigns the keeping of the believers to God and to those He appoints. God is the guardian of those who believe, and He brings them out of darkness into light. Angels are set over every soul to record what it does. The prophets are sent as bearers of good news and warnings, not as overseers of individual souls' standing. The verse's negation is therefore not a denial of all guardianship but a reallocation of it. The believers are kept, and their keeping belongs to God; the mockers are not keepers, and their verdicts belong to no one. The reallocation is what makes the reply complete in one clause — it does not need to describe the true guardians in order to exclude the false ones.

**Mockery Without Mandate**

The verse identifies the fault in the mockery more exactly than a moral condemnation would. The fault is not that the mockers were cruel, though they were; it is that they acted without authority. A cruel person with a mandate would be a different case; a person without a mandate who renders verdicts on the guidance of others is performing an office he does not hold. The Qur'an's treatment of the offence is consistent with its treatment of the defrauder: both take something that is not theirs, and both are described in terms of what has been taken rather than what has been felt. The chapter's moral vocabulary is a vocabulary of limits and appointments, and this verse is its clearest statement.

**Standing Rather Than Truth**

The reply denies an appointment and says nothing about the truth of the accusation. The silence is not an evasion but a method. A person who claims that others are astray has made a claim that could be examined on its merits, and the chapter declines to examine it. What it examines is the claimant. The examination produces a single finding — that he was not sent — and the finding disposes of the claim without touching it. The method is consistent with the Qur'an's treatment of similar accusations elsewhere, where the response to a charge of folly or straying is frequently a statement about the accuser rather than a defence of the accused. The technique places the burden where it belongs: on the person who has taken an office he does not hold, rather than on the person whose conduct he has undertaken to assess.

**The End of the Description**

After this verse the chapter returns to the Day. Verses 29 to 32 described the mockery in four settings; this verse replies; and the verses that follow describe the reversal. The reply is therefore the hinge of the movement, and its brevity is proportionate to its function. Nothing further is said about the mockers' claim, their reasons, their social position, or their fate in this world. The clause excludes them from an office and the chapter moves on. The moving on is itself a statement: the accusation of verse 32 does not merit a refutation, only a denial of standing, and after the denial the only thing left to describe is what will happen on the Day when the same parties meet again.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:34

> But on that Day the believers will be laughing at the disbelievers,

**Expanded Commentary**

**So Today**

The verse opens with *fa-l-yawma* — so today, now, on this day. The particle *fāʾ* marks a consequence, and the definite noun with the implied temporal sense fixes the moment. The transition from the street scene of verses 29 to 32 to this verse is accomplished in two words, and the words do all the work of the reversal. Nothing intervenes: no description of death, of the resurrection, of the reckoning, or of the arrival. The chapter cuts from the mockery to its inversion, and the cut is the argument. A reader who has seen the wink and heard the verdict is placed immediately in the moment when the same parties are arranged differently, and the arrangement is what the verse states.

**The Same Verb, Reversed**

*Yaḍḥakūn* — they laugh. The verb is the one used in verse 29 of the wicked laughing at the believers. Its return here with the parties exchanged is the chapter's structural centre. Nothing else in the reversal is new: no new punishment is invented, no new reward is introduced, no new image is supplied. The act of laughter is taken from one group and given to the other. The economy of the device is what makes it effective. A description of the mockers burning would have been a different scene; a description of the believers laughing at them is the same scene turned over. The chapter has therefore reversed its own material rather than adding to it, and the reversal is complete in a single verse.

**At the Disbelievers**

The preposition is *min*, from, which with the verb of laughing produces the sense of laughing at. The object is *al-kuffār*, the disbelievers — a new term in this chapter, which has used *al-fujjār*, *al-mukadhdhibūn*, and *kullu muʿtadin athīm*. The substitution is significant: the people who called the faithful astray are now named by the Qur'an's own term for those who cover over the truth. The naming is the chapter's verdict on the accusation of verse 32, and it is delivered by a change of noun rather than by a refutation. The believers laugh at the disbelievers, and the word disbelievers is what the mockers have been called by the One who was not asked.

**The Reversal as a Principle**

The Qur'an states the principle explicitly elsewhere. When the hypocrites say to their associates that they were only mocking, the answer is that God will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance (2:15). The throwing back is the same operation performed here: the act returns to its originator, and the originator becomes its object. Sūrat al-Baqarah adds the ranking that this chapter's reversal implies — those who are mindful of God will rank above them on the Day of Judgement (2:212). The principle is therefore not local to this sūrah but a settled feature of the Qur'an's account of the Day, and this verse is its most compressed statement.

**What the Laughter Is At**

The verse does not specify the object of the laughter beyond the persons laughed at, and the following verse supplies the posture from which it is performed: on couches, looking on. The laughter is therefore at a sight, and the sight is the condition of the disbelievers. The Qur'an describes the same looking elsewhere, in Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt, where a man of Paradise looks and spots his former companion in the midst of the Hellfire, and says that he nearly ruined him (37:55–56). The looking there is followed by speech and by gratitude; here it is followed by laughter and by a question. The chapter's version is the shorter one, and the shortness leaves the laughter unexplained beyond its direction.

**Laughter Without Cruelty**

The reversal raises a question the chapter does not address: what is the moral character of the believers' laughter? The answer is in the arrangement of the scene. The laughter is not inflicted — nothing is done to the disbelievers by the believers — and it is not gloating over suffering one has caused. It is the response of people who were mocked, called astray, and denied any standing, and who are now seated in safety watching the verdict reverse itself. The Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise exclude every form of harm from its inhabitants, including harmful speech, and the laughter described here belongs to that exclusion. It is the cessation of an injustice, experienced by those who bore it.

**The Day Named as the Place of Reversal**

*Yawmaʾidhin*, on that Day — the same temporal marker used for the woe of the deniers in verse 10 and for the veiling from the Lord in verse 15. The three occurrences of the phrase in this chapter place the chapter's outcomes on a single date, and the date is the Day described in verses 5 and 6 as tremendous and as the occasion of standing before the Lord of the worlds. The reversal of the laughter is therefore not a general principle of justice but an event with a time. Nothing in the verse says that the believers are compensated in this life for the mockery, and the omission is consistent with the chapter's whole framework, in which the settlement is deferred to a named Day. The deferral is what makes the defrauders' calculation rational in the short term and catastrophic in the long one.

**The Pair Completed**

Verses 29 and 34 form a pair, and the pair is the chapter's final argument. In the first, the wicked laugh at the believers; in the second, the believers laugh at the disbelievers. Between them lie four verses of description and one of reply, and the whole sequence is a single movement from an act to its inversion. Nothing in the pair refers to the records, the destinations, the couches, or the wine described earlier; the pair stands on its own as an account of two groups and one gesture. The chapter ends its argument about conduct where conduct began, in a public act performed by one group upon another, and the ending is what makes the whole sūrah a single statement about what happens to acts.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:35

> as they sit on ˹canopied˺ couches, looking on.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Couches Returning**

The couches of verse 23 return in the final movement of the chapter, and their return is the link between the description of Paradise and the scene of reversal. In verse 23 the virtuous were on canopied couches, gazing; here the believers are on the same couches, looking on. The same noun, the same posture, and a verb of sight in both cases. The recurrence means that the reversal of verse 34 is not a separate event but the same scene described from the side of the disbelievers. The believers have been on the couches since verse 23, and what verse 35 adds is what they are looking at. Nothing in verse 23 specified the object of the gaze, and the specification arrives twelve verses later.

**Two Gazes**

Verse 23 used *yanẓurūn* for the gazing of the virtuous, and this verse uses the same verb for the looking of the believers. The identity of the verb raises the question of whether the two gazes are one, and the commentators take them as related: the gazing of verse 23 is the general condition of ease and delight, and the looking of this verse is its particular direction on the Day. The two verses therefore describe the same posture at two moments or in two aspects. What unites them is that the believers are seated, at ease, and seeing — and that their seeing is not a search but a resting of the eyes on something already disclosed. The chapter gives no other description of the activity of Paradise.

**Looking On**

The verb is intransitive here as it was in verse 23: they look, they observe, they take in a scene. The object is understood from the previous verse — the disbelievers — and the understanding is what makes the two verses a single statement. The Qur'an describes the same looking in Sūrat al-Ṣāffāt, where a man of Paradise looks down and spots his former companion in the midst of the Hellfire (37:55). The looking there is followed by a speech of gratitude and by a question about whether they will die again; the looking here is followed by a question about requital. The two passages agree on the posture — a person in safety observing a person he knew in the place of punishment — and both make the observation an occasion of speech rather than of action.

**Posture of Safety**

Sitting on a furnished couch and looking is the posture of a person who is not required to do anything. Nothing in the verse describes the believers as judging, punishing, deciding, or intervening. They are seated and they are watching. The passivity is significant, because it removes any suggestion that the reversal is an act of vengeance performed by the mocked. What they experienced in the street — being laughed at, winked about, discussed at home, and called astray — is answered by a scene in which they do nothing at all. The answering is done by the Day, and their part in it is to be present, comfortable, and able to see. The chapter's reversal is therefore complete without requiring anything of the believers.

**Seat as a Position of Judgement**

A couch raised and furnished is not only a place of rest but a place of eminence, and eminence is what the mockers claimed in this world. When the clear revelations were recited to them, the disbelievers asked the believers mockingly which of the two parties was better in status and superior in assembly (19:73). The question was about position, and this verse answers it without referring to it. The believers are on couches; the disbelievers are what is looked at. The chapter's answer to the claim of superiority is therefore given in furniture and posture rather than in argument, and the giving is the same technique used throughout the sūrah: state the arrangement, and let the arrangement be the reply.

**Continuation of the Sentence**

Verse 34 stated the reversal and this verse continues it, and the two form a single sentence across the boundary: on that Day the believers will be laughing at the disbelievers, on couches, looking on. The continuation is the chapter's familiar technique of completing a statement across verses, used in the description of the defrauders, of the Day, of Sijjīn, of ʿIlliyūn, and of Tasnīm. The technique means that the reversal is not announced and then explained but announced and then situated. The situation is what gives the announcement its reality: the laughter happens somewhere, from a posture, with a view. Nothing in the verse describes the view beyond its object, and the object is enough.

**What the Looking Establishes**

The looking of this verse establishes three things at once: that the believers are safe, that they can see, and that what they see is disclosed to them rather than hidden. The safety is given by the couch, the seeing by the verb, and the disclosure by the object understood from the previous verse. Nothing in the Qur'an's account of Paradise makes seeing a source of distress to its inhabitants, and the absence of distress here is what distinguishes this looking from the looking of the wicked, who will see the Fire and be told that it is what they denied. Both classes see on that Day; one sees from a couch and the other from inside the thing seen. The chapter gives both scenes and does not compare them, and the comparison is left to a reader who has followed the movement from the street to the couches.

**Last Image Before the Question**

The chapter's final image is the believers seated and looking. The verse that follows is a question, and the question is asked of or by people in this position. The image is therefore the setting of the chapter's last sentence, and the setting determines how the sentence is heard. A question asked from a couch by people who are looking on is not a query but a confirmation — they can see the answer. The chapter ends with the believers in possession of a view and of a question whose answer is visible from where they sit. The arrangement is the completion of the reversal begun in verse 34, and it is described in seven words.

## Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn 83:36

> ˹The believers will be asked,˺ “Have the disbelievers ˹not˺ been paid back for what they used to do?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Sentence**

The chapter ends on a question, and the question is not addressed to the reader. It is spoken within the scene — asked of the believers on their couches, or by them as they look on, or by those around them. The ambiguity of the speaker is preserved in the Arabic, which gives the question without attribution, and the translation marks the uncertainty with a bracketed gloss. What is certain is the setting: the question belongs to the scene of verse 35, and it is asked by people who can see its answer. A chapter that opened with a declarative verdict — woe to the defrauders — closes with an interrogative whose answer is already visible, and the closing form is the chapter's last structural statement.

**Thuwwiba**

The verb is from *th-w-b*, a root meaning to return, to come back, to revert to a place or a state. Its second form denotes causing a return, and in the Qur'an's usage it means to requite or reward — to give back what corresponds to what was done. The word is used for both reward and punishment, and its neutrality is important here: the question asks whether the disbelievers have been paid back, and the payment is the return of their own conduct to them. Nothing in the verb specifies a quantity or a quality. The requital is described as a return, and the return is what the whole chapter has been preparing — the sealed record returned to its owner, the denial named over the burning, the mockery thrown back at the mocker.

**What They Used to Do**

The object is *mā kānū yafʿalūn* — what they were in the habit of doing. The construction is the same one used in verse 14 for what the wicked used to earn, and the same continuous-past form used in verse 29 for what they used to laugh at. The recurrence of the construction across the chapter ties its three movements together: the covering on the heart was formed by habitual earning, the mockery was a habitual practice, and the requital is for habitual doing. Nothing in the verse limits the object to the mockery. The requital covers the whole of the conduct — the short measure, the denial, the dismissal of the revelations, the wink, the talk at home, and the verdict spoken on sight.

**A Question That Confirms**

The interrogative form with the negative particle produces a question expecting affirmation: have they not been paid back? The asker already knows the answer, and the asking is for the benefit of whoever hears. In the scene of verse 35 the hearers are the believers on the couches, and the question confirms to them what they are looking at. The confirmation is the completion of the reversal. A person who was mocked and called astray has been told, by a question asked in his presence, that the account has been settled. Nothing in the verse describes his response, and the absence of a response is appropriate: the chapter's last act of speech belongs to the Day, and the believers' part is the laughter and the looking of the two preceding verses.

**The Balance Restored**

The chapter opened with a man who took a full measure and gave a short one. It closes with a question about payment. The two are the same subject seen from opposite ends: the defrauder's act was an imbalance in requital, taking more than he gave, and the Day's act is the restoration of the balance. Nothing in the closing verse mentions measure or weight, and the absence of mention is what makes the connection structural rather than verbal. The reader who has followed the chapter arrives at the final question holding the first verse in mind, and the holding produces the recognition that the requital asked about is the settlement of the shortfall described at the beginning.

**The Frame of the Chapter**

Thirty-six verses, and the frame is exact. Verse 1 declares ruin to those who take more than they give; verse 36 asks whether they have been given what corresponds to what they did. Between them lie the Day and its standing before the Lord of the worlds, the two sealed records, the two destinations, the covering on the heart, the veiling from the Lord, the burning and the announcement, the couches and the faces and the wine and the spring, the mockery in the street and at home, the reply about the keepers, and the reversal of the laughter. The frame closes on the question of payment, and the question is what the whole chapter has been an elaboration of.

**Why the Chapter Ends on a Question**

A declaration would have closed the argument; a question opens it onto the reader. The chapter's final sentence is spoken inside a scene the reader has been shown but does not inhabit, and the showing has been detailed enough that he knows where he would have to be to hear it. The question is therefore addressed to him by its exclusion of him. He is not on the couch and he is not among those looked at, and the two positions have both been described. Nothing in the verse invites him, warns him, or exhorts him, and the absence of an address leaves him with the only thing the chapter has left to give — the choice of which scene he is preparing to be in. The exhortation of verse 26 was the chapter's single direct summons, and it precedes this question by ten verses.

**The Requite and the Record**

The last word of the chapter's argument is the requital, and the requital depends on the record. What the disbelievers used to do is in a sealed book, and the book is in Sijjīn, and the settlement is the reading of it. The chapter's three unique names, its two sealed documents, its witnesses, and its scenes of mockery and reversal all exist for the sake of the question asked in this verse. The question is whether the payment has been made, and the answer is visible from the couches. A reader who has followed the chapter from the defrauders to this sentence has been shown the whole of the procedure — the conduct, the writing, the sealing, the arrival, the announcement, and the requital — and the showing is what makes the final question answerable without being answered.
