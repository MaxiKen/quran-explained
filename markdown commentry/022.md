# Sūrah al-Ḥajj (Chapter 22) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Cosmic Tremor and the Universal Gathering**

Al-Ḥajj, "The Pilgrimage," is the twenty-second chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 78 verses. It occupies an unique, hybrid status in Qur'anic chronology: classical authorities, including al-Suyūṭī and Ibn ʿAbbās, note that it contains verses revealed in Makkah, verses revealed in Madinah, verses revealed during travel, verses revealed during war, and verses revealed in peace. It takes its name from verses 26–37, which present the immortal Abrahamic proclamation of the annual pilgrimage to the Ancient House (*al-Bayt al-ʿAtīq*): *“And proclaim to the people the Ḥajj; they will come to you on foot and on every lean camel; they will come from every distant pass”* (22:27). Sūrah al-Ḥajj is also distinguished liturgically as containing two distinct verses of prostration (*sajdat al-tilāwah*, verses 18 and 77), highlighting its intense devotional demand. Bridging the twilight of Makkan persecution with the dawn of Medinan political independence, the sūrah conjoins the physical pilgrimage of the body with the ultimate Last-Day migration of all human souls to the Divine Court.

**The Structural Architecture of the Pilgrimage**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Ḥajj moves from apocalyptic terror to biological embryology, and from Abrahamic ritual to the defense of sacred spaces. The opening movement (1–25) strikes with apocalyptic force, warning humanity of the terrifying earthquake of the Final Hour (*Zalzalat al-Sāʿah*), which will cause nursing mothers to forget their infants and pregnant women to miscarry from sheer terror. It establishes the reality of resurrection through two observable proofs: the intricate stages of human embryological development in the womb (22:5 — *“˹Then˺ We settle whatever ˹embryo˺ We will in the womb for an appointed term, then bring you forth as infants, so that you may reach your prime.”*), and the barren, dead earth that stirs, swells, and brings forth pairs of beautiful growth when touched by rain. The second movement (26–38) transports the reader to Makkah: Abraham purifying the House from idolatry and summoning mankind to the pilgrimage. It details the sacred rites: the slaughter of sacrificial livestock, feeding the impoverished and destitute, fulfilling vows, circumambulating the Ancient House, and honoring the symbols of God (*shaʿā'ir Allāh*), concluding with the famous spiritual maxim: *“Their meat will not reach Allah, nor will their blood, but what reaches Him is piety (*taqwā*) from you”* (22:37). The third movement (39–57) expresses the charter of defensive warfare, reviews the ruined dwellings of past deniers, and consoles the Prophet against the whisperings of Satan, affirming that God abolishes satanic interpolations and confirms His clear signs. The fourth movement (58–78) contrasts the majesty of divine creation—the ships gliding upon the oceans, the sky held from falling upon the earth except by divine leave—against the pathetic impotence of false gods in the famous parable of the fly: *“O people, an example is presented, so listen to it! Indeed, those you invoke beside Allah will never create a fly, even if they gathered together for that purpose. And if the fly should steal away from them a thing, they could not recover it from him. Weak are the seeker and the sought!”* (22:73). The chapter concludes (77–78) with the grand summons to the believers: to bow, prostrate, worship their Lord, and strive for Allah with the striving due to Him, reminding them: *“He has chosen you and has not placed upon you in the religion any difficulty—the religion of your father Abraham. He named you 'Muslims' before and in this [scripture].”*

## Verse 22:1

> O humanity! Fear your Lord, for the ˹violent˺ quaking at the Hour is surely a dreadful thing

**A Pilgrimage Sūrah That Opens With an Earthquake**

The sūrah carries the name of the pilgrimage because the rite is proclaimed in it (22:27 — *“Call ˹all˺ people to the pilgrimage. They will come to you on foot and on every lean camel from every distant path”*), yet its first sentence is not about a rite at all. Before anyone is told how to walk around a House, the ground itself refuses to hold still. That ordering makes a claim about worship. A ritual performed against a backdrop of permanent, indifferent nature is one thing; a ritual performed inside a world with an expiry date is another. Whoever reaches the pilgrimage verses of this sūrah has already been told, in its opening line, that the stage on which the rites are performed will be shaken apart.

The address is *yā ayyuhā al-nāsu* — O humanity — the widest form of address in the Qur'an's vocabulary. It is not *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū*, the formula that opens most Medinan passages and that appears near the end of this sūrah (22:77 — *“O believers! Bow down, prostrate yourselves, worship your Lord, and do ˹what is˺ good so that you may be successful”*). The terrors of the Hour are not announced to a community that already accepts them; they are announced to everyone, and the announcement is built to produce the fear it calls for.

**The Hour Arrives in the Text Already Familiar**

Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ opens on the nearness the definite article assumes: *“˹The time of˺ people's judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away”* (21:1). The definiteness of *al-sāʿah* is not a compliment to the audience's knowledge; it is the mark of a thing so often announced that heedlessness toward it has become the normal posture.

**"A Dreadful Thing" Refuses to Specify**

The adjective is the Qur'an's standing word for the Day, and Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn attaches it to the resurrection of the very people who shortchange: *“Do such people not think that they will be resurrected for a tremendous Day”* (83:4–5). *Yawmin ʿaẓīm* — the same *ʿaẓīm* as verse 1's *shayʾun ʿaẓīm*. The vagueness of *shayʾ* is therefore not empty; it is the Day with its contents still sealed, tremendous before anything in it has been named. Verse 2 will begin the naming — the nursing mother, the pregnant woman, the reeling crowd — and the naming never catches up with the adjective.

**Why the Sūrah Starts Here Instead of at the Kaʿbah**

That is also why the sūrah ends where it does, with a summons to strive for God as He deserves (22:78 — *“Strive for ˹the cause of˺ Allah in the way He deserves, for ˹it is˺ He ˹Who˺ has chosen you, and laid upon you no hardship in the religion—the way of your forefather Abraham.”*) and a description of a community chosen and witnessed over. Between the earthquake of verse 1 and that summons, the sūrah builds a people who know what time they are living in.

Sūrah al-Raḥmān states the frame in its barest form: *“Every being on earth is bound to perish”* (55:26). Six words in the Arabic, no exception offered. A pilgrimage performed inside that sentence is a different act from the same motions performed inside permanence — every circuit a rehearsal of departure, every standing a preview of the standing. Verse 1 puts the perishability first so that nothing commanded later can be mistaken for furniture of a lasting world.

Sūrah Luqmān pairs the command with the Day in the same breath: *“O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord, and beware of a Day when no parent will be of any benefit to their child, nor will a child be of any benefit to their parent”* (31:33).

Sūrah al-Zalzalah takes the same word as its title and its opening: *“When the earth is shaken ˹in˺ its ultimate quaking”* (99:1).

The same word is used elsewhere in the Qur'an for a shaking that is not geological at all — the shaking of people under pressure: *“Then and there the believers were put to the test, and were violently shaken”* (33:11).

## Verse 22:2

> The Day you see it, every nursing mother will abandon what she is nursing, and every pregnant woman will deliver her burden ˹prematurely˺. And you will see people ˹as if they were˺ drunk, though they will not be drunk; but the torment of Allah is ˹terribly˺ severe

**The Scale of the Terror Is a Human Scale**

Verse 1 called the quaking a dreadful thing and left the dread unspecified. Verse 2 supplies the measurement, and the measurement is not geological. Nothing here describes faults opening or buildings falling. What the verse describes is a mother letting go of her child.

Sūrah ʿAbasa describes the same dissolution from the inside, and its list runs outward from the closest bond: *“on that Day every person will flee from their own siblings, and ˹even˺ their mother and father, and ˹even˺ their spouse and children”* (80:34–36). What is being measured in both places is the same thing — not how hard the ground shakes but how far the terror reaches into the arrangements that hold people together.

**Which Quaking? The Commentators Genuinely Differ**

The stronger reading, in my judgment, is the second. The details point to a scene still inside time: pregnant women miscarrying and infants being abandoned belong to a moment at which bodies are still bodies and consequences still fall. Once the Resurrection is under way, miscarriage has no purchase — the dead are being raised, not born. The verse pictures the world ending while people are still doing ordinary things in it, which is what makes it frightful. The parallel passage in Sūrah al-Muzzammil describes the same moment with the mountains in motion: *“on the Day the earth and mountains will shake ˹violently˺, and mountains will be ˹reduced to˺ dunes of shifting sand”* (73:14).

**"The Day You See It": When Seeing Arrives, Belief Is Too Late**

The principle is stated plainly elsewhere: *“On the Day your Lord’s signs arrive, belief will not benefit those who did not believe earlier or those who did no good through their faith”* (6:158). Faith is a response to what has not been seen, which is why the Qur'an calls the unseen its first article. Once the object is in front of the eyes, the act that was required becomes impossible — not forbidden, but impossible, because believing what you can see is not belief.

**The Last Clause Turns the Description Into an Address**

The sūrah will use the same technique at its close, where a description of the disbelievers' fate is followed by a command addressed to the believers (22:77 — *“O believers! Bow down, prostrate yourselves, worship your Lord, and do ˹what is˺ good so that you may be successful”*). The pattern is consistent: the Qur'an does not leave its audience in the position of spectators at somebody else's catastrophe.

## Verse 22:3

> ˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, and follow every rebellious devil

**Why the Argument Appears Next to the Earthquake**

The sūrah has just described the world coming apart, mothers dropping children and crowds reeling. Verse 3 turns, without transition, to a man holding an opinion: *wa-mina al-nāsi man yujādilu fī Allāhi bighayri ʿilmin* — and among the people is he who disputes about God without knowledge.

Sūrah al-Isrāʾ records the standard form the rejection takes — never a plain refusal, always an objection: *“And nothing has prevented people from believing when guidance comes to them except their protest: ‘Has Allah sent a human as a messenger?’”* (17:94). Nothing prevented them *except* the protest: the objection is named as the whole obstacle.

**Who Is Meant: Two Candidates, and the Text Covers Both**

The commentators divide over the reference. One reading takes the verse as aimed at those who denied the Resurrection specifically — the people whose objection is preserved elsewhere in the Qur'an: *“When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?”* (37:16). On this reading the disputant here is the man of verses 1–2, the one who has just been told that the world ends and answers that bones do not come back.

The other reading, preferred by several later commentators, takes it as aimed at those who dismissed the revelation wholesale, of the sort reported saying: *“We have already heard ˹the recitation˺. If we wanted, we could have easily produced something similar. This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but ancient fables!”* (8:31).

**"Every Rebellious Devil": Shayṭān Is a Category, Not an Individual**

The same phrase guards the heavens, and the guarding shows what the rebels are barred from: *“and ˹for˺ protection from every rebellious devil”* (37:7). *Kulli shayṭānin mārid* — identical words, identical distributive *every*. Above, the rebels are kept out by the guarded heaven; below, the disputant enrolls under them by choice. The symmetry is exact and it is the verse's quiet verdict: what the stars are protected from, the man without knowledge volunteers for.

**The Hidden Positive: Disputation With Knowledge Is Something Else**

The lawful form of the same verb is commanded in Sūrah al-Naḥl, with its manner stated: *“Invite ˹all˺ to the Way of your Lord with wisdom and kind advice, and only debate with them in the best manner”* (16:125). *Wa-jādilhum bi-llatī hiya aḥsan* — debate, the same root verse 3 condemns, made licit by knowledge and bounded by excellence. The condemnation was never of argument as such; it was of argument from nothing, conducted anyhow. Wisdom first, then the debate — and the debate in the best manner, or not at all.

## Verse 22:4

> It has been decreed for such devils that whoever takes them as a guide will be misguided and led by them into the torment of the Blaze

**Two Antecedents for One Pronoun**

The pronoun in "it has been decreed for him" is grammatically available to two referents, and the commentators split accordingly.

The second reading takes it as the follower: it is decreed for the person who follows a devil that he will be misguided. This yields a harder, more predestinarian sense, and it is recorded by al-Rāzī among others.

The stronger reading is the first. The nearest antecedent in verse 3 is *shayṭānin marīdin*, the rebellious devil, and the pronoun in "takes him (*hu*) as a guide" has to be the devil for the sentence to make sense at all. Readings that make the follower the subject of the decree must supply that reference across a clause boundary, and they sit awkwardly beside verses that place the choice squarely with the human being: *“˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve”* (18:29). The coherence of the Qur'an's moral language is a real argument here, and it favours the reading that keeps the decree a law rather than a list.

**Patronage: a Word Borrowed From the Marketplace**

The verb translated "takes as a guide" is *tawallāhu*, from the same root as *walī*. In pre-Islamic Arabia a *walī* was a protector — the man or clan to whom you attached yourself for safety, who answered for your blood and whose standing covered you. Clientage was a real institution with real consequences, and everyone hearing this verse understood the transaction.

Using that vocabulary for a spiritual relationship is a deliberate transfer.

**What the Devil Himself Will Say**

The Qur'an lets the other party describe it later, after judgment has been passed, in words that undo the contract completely: *“I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves”* (14:22). The patron of verse 4 turns out to have had no power to deliver anything, and his own testimony is that the follower walked in on his own feet.

Read together, the two verses settle the question of coercion from both ends. The decree of verse 4 guarantees that the path leads where it leads; the confession of 14:22 — *“And Satan will say ˹to his followers˺ after the judgment has been passed, “Indeed, Allah has made you a true promise. I too made you a promise, but I failed you. I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves. I cannot save you, nor can you save me. Indeed, I denounce your previous association of me with Allah ˹in loyalty˺. Surely the wrongdoers will suffer a painful punishment.””* establishes that nobody was dragged along it.

## Verse 22:5

> O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh—fully formed or unformed—in order to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you. ˹Then˺ We settle whatever ˹embryo˺ We will in the womb for an appointed term, then bring you forth as infants, so that you may reach your prime. Some of you ˹may˺ die ˹young˺, while others are left to reach the most feeble stage of life so that they may know nothing after having known much. And you see the earth lifeless, but as soon as We send down rain upon it, it begins to stir ˹to life˺ and swell, producing every type of pleasant plant

**Four Stages, and the Sequence Is the Argument**

The stages are given in order: dust, then a sperm-drop, then a clinging clot, then a lump of flesh. The parallel passage in Sūrah al-Muʾminūn lays out the same sequence with the bones made explicit: *“then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation. So Blessed is Allah, the Best of Creators”* (23:14).

That the first stage is dust rather than nothing is also part of the argument. The Qur'an frequently pairs the two: *“He created him from dust, then said to him, ‘Be!’ And he was!”* (3:59). Creation from a substance is presented as the harder and stranger case, since something is being shaped; the Resurrection is described in this sūrah as the revival of what has already been shaped once.

**Two Endings for One Life: Death, or the Feeble Stage**

From birth the verse moves to maturity and then splits the road. Some die; others are returned to "the most feeble stage of life so that they may know nothing after having known much.".

The second ending is the one the Qur'an dwells on, and it is a harder thing to contemplate than death. Sūrah al-Naḥl states it with the same words: *“And some of you are left to reach the most feeble stage of life so that they may know nothing after having known much. Indeed, Allah is All-Knowing, Most Capable”* (16:70). Sūrah Yā Sīn names the mechanism: *“And whoever We grant a long life, We reverse them in development. Will they not then understand?”* (36:68).

The choice of this as the second proof of the Resurrection is deliberate. A person who has watched a mind undo itself has watched a Creator take back what He gave, in the same body, over the same lifetime. If knowledge can be withdrawn from a living brain, it can be restored to a decayed one. The arc of weakness to strength and back again is drawn explicitly elsewhere: *“It is Allah Who created you in a state of weakness, then developed ˹your˺ weakness into strength, then developed ˹your˺ strength into weakness and old age”* (30:54).

**From the Womb to the Field**

The argument then leaves the body and takes up the ground: you see the earth lifeless, and when We send down water it stirs, swells, and puts out every kind of pleasant growth.

This is the Qur'an's single most repeated analogy for the Resurrection, and the repetition is not decorative. Sūrah al-Rūm states the conclusion in the same breath as the observation: *“See then the impact of Allah’s mercy: how He gives life to the earth after its death! Surely That ˹same Creator˺ can raise the dead. For He is Most Capable of everything”* (30:50). Sūrah al-Aʿrāf draws the line explicitly: *“Similarly, We will bring the dead to life, so perhaps you will be mindful”* (7:57).

The analogy works because both halves are the same kind of event: something dry, dead, and dispersed receives what it cannot produce for itself and becomes a living, ordered thing. The earth does not rain on itself. Neither does a grave.

It is recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (3208) and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2643).

**The Stages and the Hadith Behind Them**

The verse sets out the making of a person in stages — dust, then a drop, then a clot, then a lump of flesh — and the Sūrah of the Believers completes the list with bones and flesh and the second creation (23:12-14 — *“then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation.”*). The Prophet gave the timetable in the well-known report in Bukhārī and Muslim: the creation of a person is gathered in the mother's womb for forty days as a drop, then forty days as a clot, then forty days as a lump, and then an angel is sent to breathe the spirit into him and to write the four things: his provision, his term, his deeds and his misery or happiness. The scholars made this report the basis of the ruling they agreed on, that the life protected by law begins with the breathing of the spirit at four months, and the rules on inheritance, naming and mourning were built on it.
**The Second Half of the Verse**

The verse then turns to the ground and uses the same argument: you see the earth barren, and when the water comes down it stirs and swells and brings out every kind of beautiful plant. The Book uses the picture to answer the doubters of the raising of the dead, and the next two verses state the conclusion directly, that the One who gives the earth life will raise the dead, and that the Hour is coming with no doubt about it (22:7 — *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves”*). The same argument appears in the Sūrah of the Angels with the seed and the grain (36:33 — *“There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat”*) and in the Sūrah of the News in the rain (78:14-16 — *“producing by it grain and ˹various˺ plants”*).
**Where the Passage Stands**

The sūrah has just told its audience that God does not wrong people and that they wrong themselves (22:10 — *“˹They will be told,˺ “This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation.””*), and it begins this verse with an address to all people, which the commentators read as a call to everyone in the town of Makkah. The verse is followed by the promise of the resurrection and the naming of God as the Knower of what is in the breasts. Both halves of it, the womb and the field, are put in front of the same audience: the change in a body being made and the change in a dead land, which are the two things the Qur'an keeps pointing at when it argues about the Day.

## Verse 22:6

> That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth, He ˹alone˺ gives life to the dead, and He ˹alone˺ is Most Capable of everything

**The First Premise: God Is the Truth**

*Al-Ḥaqq* is one of the divine names, and its semantic range is wider than "truthful." It means the Real, that which truly is, that which does not fail or dissolve. Applied to God, it sets up the contrast that verse 62 of this same sūrah makes explicit: God is the Truth and what is invoked besides Him is falsehood.

**The Second Premise: He Gives Life to the Dead**

That present tense is the argument. The objector grants that God gives life in the ordinary course — every pregnancy, every spring. What he denies is the exceptional case. The verse collapses the distinction: the One who gives life gives life, and the dead He revives include both the field in autumn and the person in the grave. Sūrah al-Rūm states the inference in so many words: *“See then the impact of Allah’s mercy: how He gives life to the earth after its death! Surely That ˹same Creator˺ can raise the dead”* (30:50).

Sūrah al-Anʿām states the life-giving as a standing activity with the same present-tense verbs: *“Indeed, Allah is the One Who causes seeds and fruit stones to sprout. He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living”* (6:95). Seeds split open in the soil — the same field verse 5 described — and the bringing-forth runs in both directions, living from dead and dead from living, as an ongoing operation rather than a scheduled event. The Resurrection is that operation applied once more, to the dead in the graves rather than the dead in the ground.

**The Third Premise: Power Over Everything**

Sūrah al-Mulk opens on the same clause, attached to dominion rather than to proof: *“Blessed is the One in Whose Hands rests all authority. And He is Most Capable of everything”* (67:1). Power over everything is the Qur'an's most repeated closing attribute, and its function is always the same — to remove the last qualification. Whatever the doubter grants about God's reality and God's life-giving, the third premise forbids him from stopping there: capability admits no exception, and the Resurrection is not one.

**The Logic of the Greater and the Smaller**

Underlying the whole passage is an inferential form the Qur'an deploys repeatedly in this context and always in the same direction: the harder creation is the one already granted, so the easier one cannot reasonably be denied. *“Can the One Who created the heavens and the earth not ˹easily˺ resurrect these ˹deniers˺?” Yes ˹He can˺! For He is the Master Creator, All-Knowing”* (36:81). The same form closes Sūrah al-Qiyāmah: *“Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life?”* (75:40).

Sūrah Ghāfir states the comparison in its most direct form, with the Resurrection as the explicit subject: *“The creation of the heavens and the earth is certainly greater than the recreation of humankind, but most people do not know”* (40:57). Greater and smaller named as such — the heavens against the remaking of a human being — and the failure to draw the inference diagnosed as ignorance rather than as reasoning. Verse 6's third premise is that comparison compressed into an attribute: power over everything includes power over the smaller thing.

The sequence is the Qur'an's stated rule for punishment itself: *“And We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺”* (17:15).

## Verse 22:7

> And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves

**"No Doubt in It" Removes the Doubt, Not the Difficulty**

*Lā rayba fīhi* — no doubt in it. The phrase is used in the Qur'an's opening for the Book itself, and its transfer to the Hour is a deliberate equivalence. The Book and the Hour are the two things the Qur'an describes this way, and the shared formula ties them together: the text that cannot be doubted announces the event that cannot be doubted.

The negation also tells us where the doubt actually sits. It is not said that the Hour is easy to conceive, or that the mind finds it natural. What is denied is that the doubt has an object. A person who doubts the Hour is not responding to a genuine uncertainty in the evidence; he is responding to something else completely, which the sūrah named two verses earlier as disputing without knowledge (22:3 — *“˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, and follow every rebellious devil”*).

The Book's opening states the formula verse 7 borrows: *“This is the Book! There is no doubt about it — a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺”* (2:2). *Lā rayba fīh* — identical words, identical position at the start of an argument. The equivalence the borrowing creates runs both ways: the Book vouches for the Hour, and the Hour's certainty reflects back on the Book that announced it. To accept the formula in one place and dispute it in the other is to split what the shared wording joins.

**"Those in the Graves"**

That plainness is the point. The Qur'an's claim is not about a general immortality of the soul or a survival of influence; it is about the contents of specific holes in the ground. Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt puts the same vision in a single line: *“Do they not know that when the contents of the graves will be spilled out”* (100:9). Sūrah Fāṭir uses the same phrase to mark the limit of the Prophet's own mission: *“Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ makes whoever He wills hear, but you ˹O Prophet˺ can never make those in the graves hear ˹your call˺”* (35:22).

**Doubt Is Traced as a Condition Throughout This Sūrah**

The word *rayb* does not appear here by accident. It is the same noun the sūrah used in verse 5, when it addressed people *in doubt* about the Resurrection, and it will return at verse 55, where the disbelievers remain in doubt *“until the Hour takes them by surprise, or the torment of a terminating Day comes to them”* (22:55).

**Resurrection Precedes Judgment in the Sūrah's Order**

Sūrah Yā-Sīn stages the sequence the sūrah's order assumes: *“The Trumpet will be blown ˹a second time˺, then — behold! — they will rush from the graves to their Lord”* (36:51). Blowing, then rushing — the raising first, the standing second, with the *fa-idhā* marking the immediacy. Verse 7's *yabʿathu man fī al-qubūr* is that rushing stated as doctrine before it is staged as drama: the graves opened here, the courtroom convened later, and the verses between them observed in that order.

The assertion rests on a promise, and Sūrah Yūnus states the promise with the reasoning attached: *“To Him is your return all together. Allah's promise is ˹always˺ true. Indeed, He originates the creation then resurrects it”* (10:4).

Sūrah Ghāfir joins the coming to the denial of doubt in a single line: *“The Hour is certainly coming, there is no doubt about it. But most people do not believe”* (40:59).

## Verse 22:8

> ˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, guidance, or an enlightening scripture

**A Second Portrait, and Two Words Heavier**

Verse 3 described a man who disputes about God without knowledge and follows every rebellious devil. Verse 8 describes him again — and the second portrait is heavier than the first by two nouns. What was *bighayri ʿilmin* has become *bighayri ʿilmin wa-lā hudan wa-lā kitābin munīr*: without knowledge, and without guidance, and without an illuminating scripture.

The requirement is stated as a direct prohibition in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ, with the audit attached: *“Do not follow what you have no ˹sure˺ knowledge of. Indeed, all will be called to account for ˹their˺ hearing, sight, and intellect”* (17:36). Hearing, sight, heart — the three channels questioned, matching the three absences of verse 8. The escalation from verse 3 is therefore not rhetorical piling but the full inventory of what the disputant cannot produce when the accounting comes.

**Three Negations Cover the Three Ways of Knowing**

The three terms are not synonyms, and the care with which they are chosen is the substance of the verse.

*ʿIlm* is knowledge arrived at — what a person works out, observes, or is taught and verifies. *Hudā* is guidance received — direction given from outside oneself, the thing a person is granted rather than achieves. *Kitāb munīr* is an illuminating scripture: a written, public, checkable source that gives light and can be consulted by anyone.

Sūrah al-Zumar puts the inequality as a question that answers itself: *“Are those who know equal to those who do not know?”* (39:9). The night worshipper set against the disputant — prostrating and standing on one side, arguing from nothing on the other — and the verse declines to treat them as two opinions. Verse 8's triple negation is that inequality worked out as a criterion: the knower has sources to be checked against, and the man with none of the three has nothing to be weighed.

**"Enlightening Scripture": One Word Doing Two Jobs**

*Munīr* is the active participle of *anāra*, to shine or give light. It describes something that is itself luminous and, at the same time, something that illuminates what is around it. Both senses are in play.

The adjective is rare in the Qur'an and appears in a narrow set of contexts, two of which concern earlier revelation. Messengers before Muhammad ﷺ came *“with clear proofs, divine Books, and enlightening Scriptures”* (3:184), and the same formula is repeated for the peoples who denied them: *“Their messengers came to them with clear proofs, divine Books, and enlightening Scriptures”* (35:25).

**Why the Sūrah Returns to This Man**

The partitive construction opens the Qur'an's portrait of the hypocrites in the same terms: *“And there are some who say, ‘We believe in Allah and the Last Day,’ yet they are not ˹true˺ believers”* (2:8).

**Luqmān's Version of the Same Line**

The same indictment appears in Sūrah Luqmān, addressed to a man who cannot name what he is relying on: *“Have you not seen that Allah has subjected for you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, and has lavished His favours upon you, both seen and unseen? ˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, or guidance, or an enlightening scripture”* (31:20). There the dispute is set against the backdrop of visible provision, which makes the ignorance harder to excuse: the evidence is in front of the man, and he argues anyway.

## Verse 22:9

> turning away ˹in pride˺ to lead ˹others˺ astray from Allah’s Way. They will suffer disgrace in this world, and on the Day of Judgment We will make them taste the torment of burning

**Disgrace in This World, Then Burning in the Next**

*Khizyun fī al-dunyā* — disgrace in the present life — followed by the torment of burning on the Day of Resurrection. The two are not alternatives and not a sequence of coincidence. The Qur'an pairs them as a standard form wherever it describes organized opposition to the revelation.

Those who prevent God's name from being mentioned in His places of worship are told: *“For them is disgrace in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter”* (2:114). The same pairing is given to those who wage war against God and His messenger (5:33 — *“This ˹penalty˺ is a disgrace for them in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter”*), to those whose tongues believe while their hearts do not (5:41 — *“For them is disgrace in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter”*). In the plain statement of Sūrah al-Zumar: *“So Allah made them taste humiliation in this worldly life, but far worse is the punishment of the Hereafter, if only they knew”* (39:26).

**Disgrace Here Can Be Lifted; the Other Cannot**

That said, disgrace in this life is not fixed in the way the punishment of the next is fixed, and the Qur'an says so. Sūrah Yūnus singles out the one community in history whose belated belief changed their earthly sentence: *“If only there had been a society which believed ˹before seeing the torment˺ and, therefore, benefited from its belief, like the people of Jonah. When they believed, We lifted from them the torment of disgrace in this world and allowed them enjoyment for a while”* (10:98).

The exception proves the rule and explains the logic. This is the same principle the sūrah established four verses earlier with the Hour that is *coming* (22:7 — *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves”*): there is an interval, and the interval is the point.

Pharaoh supplies the counterexample that bounds the exception, refused in a single word: *“˹He was told,˺ ‘Now ˹you believe˺? But you always disobeyed and were one of the corruptors’”* (10:91). *Now* — *āl-āna* — belief arriving after the interval closed, at the sight of drowning. Jonah's people believed before the torment and were lifted; Pharaoh believed inside it and was not. Between the two verses the rule is complete: disgrace in this world lifts while warnings still work, and the work stops where the seeing starts.

**"We Will Make Them Taste"**

Sūrah Fuṣṣilat uses the same verb for the same double sentence: *“So We sent against them a furious wind, for ˹several˺ miserable days, to make them taste a humiliating punishment in this worldly life. But far more humiliating will be the punishment of the Hereafter”* (41:16). The early taste is a sample, and the sample is meant to be read as a sample.

Sūrah al-Sajdah names the tasting's purpose outright: *“We will certainly make them taste some of the minor torment ˹in this life˺ before the major torment ˹of the Hereafter˺, so perhaps they will return”* (32:21). Minor before major, tasting before receiving — and the purpose clause *so perhaps they will return* stating what the sample is for. Verse 9's *nudhīquhu* belongs to that pedagogy: the disgrace here is the minor tasting, the burning the major receiving, and the interval between them the return's last chance.

**Why the Verse Names the Effect Before the Cause**

Sūrah al-Zalzalah states the seeing as the whole of judgment, in the smallest unit imaginable: *“So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it. And whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it”* (99:7–8). Seeing as receiving — the act presented, the doer shown it. Verse 9 withholds the *why* of the sentence for the same reason: the shoulder turned and the shoulders turned are exhibited first, and the exhibition is left to stand as its own justification until verse 10 supplies the verdict's second half.

Luqmān's counsel to his son forbids the same gesture by name: *“And do not turn your nose up to people, nor walk pridefully upon the earth”* (31:18).

Sūrah al-Naḥl prices the transmission, and the price is double: *“Let them bear their burdens in full on the Day of Judgment as well as some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge”* (16:25).

## Verse 22:10

> ˹They will be told,˺ “This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation.”

**These Words Are Spoken to a Face**

The verse is not a description of judgment; it is a line of dialogue inside it. The translation supplies *they will be told*, and the Arabic makes the same move by putting the sentence in direct speech: *dhālika bimā qaddamat yadāka* — that is for what your two hands sent ahead.

The address is singular — *yadāka*, your two hands — and the singularity is part of the staging. Judgment in the Qur'an is never a collective proceeding in which a person can stand inside a crowd; each case is heard alone, as Sūrah Maryam states it: *“And each of them will return to Him on the Day of Judgment all alone”* (19:95). The singular is what makes the scene unshareable: this telling is to one person, and no one standing beside him is addressed by it.

**"What Your Hands Sent Ahead"**

The wording is nearly identical to a verse in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān, which is why the two are always read together: *“This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation”* (3:182). There the sentence is addressed in exactly the same form, and the repetition across two sūrahs confirms that this is a fixed formula of the judgment rather than a description tailored to one group.

**"Hands" Specifically**

The phrase is confirmed by what the Qur'an says the hands will do at the judgment — they will not merely be cited as evidence but will give it: *“On this Day We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak to Us. Their feet will testify to what they used to commit”* (36:65). The mouth that produced the disputation of verses 3 and 8 is sealed first, and then the instruments of action take over the testimony. A deed recorded by the limb that performed it cannot be disowned by the person who ordered it.

**The Clause That Carries the Verse**

The second half is the one the verse exists for: *wa-anna Allāha laysa bi-ẓallāmin li-l-ʿabīd* — and God is not unjust to His servants.

The formula is one of the Qur'an's fixed closings, and its fullest form states both halves of the present verse in a single sentence: *“Whoever does good, it is to their own benefit. And whoever does evil, it is to their own loss. Your Lord is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation”* (41:46).

**Why the Record Is Outside the Person**

The machinery behind "what your hands sent ahead" is the record, and the Qur'an is unusually concrete about it. *“And We have everything recorded precisely”* (78:29). At the judgment the record is produced as an object: *“Read your record. You ˹alone˺ are enough this Day to take account of yourself”* (17:14).

The external record is what makes the second clause of this verse meaningful. If judgment were a matter of divine recollection, the claim of justice would rest on the reliability of a memory; the Qur'an instead places the account outside both parties, legible to the accused, and has him read it himself. Sūrah al-Kahf describes the moment: *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it. They will cry, ‘Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?’”* (18:49).

## Verse 22:11

> And there are some who worship Allah on the verge ˹of faith˺: if they are blessed with something good, they are content with it; but if they are afflicted with a trial, they relapse ˹into disbelief˺, losing this world and the Hereafter. That is ˹truly˺ the clearest loss

**The Test Runs in Both Directions**

The verse then gives the mechanics, and they are symmetrical in form and asymmetrical in outcome. If good comes to him, he is content with it. If a trial strikes him, he flips over onto his face.

The symmetry is in the events: blessing and trial are the ordinary alternations of any human life, and both are described as things that *befall* him, not things he brings about. The asymmetry is in his response. Contentment with the good is not called faith — it is called *iṭmaʾanna*, being at ease, a settling. The trial produces *inqalaba ʿalā wajhih*, a turning over onto the face, the posture of a man who has fallen.

**Al-Ṭabarī's Bedouins, and the Wider Reading**

Other commentators took the verse as a general description of the hypocrites, whose religion is built on a shallow calculation of what is good for them, and whom the slightest discomfort topples.

The general reading is the stronger one, for two reasons. The wording is *wa-mina al-nāsi man* — and among people is he who — the same partitive formula the sūrah has already used twice for types rather than individuals (22:3 — *“˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, and follow every rebellious devil”*, 22:8 — *“˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, guidance, or an enlightening scripture”*). And the reported occasion does not restrict the meaning even if it is historically accurate, since the point of the verse is the mechanism, not the men. The mechanism is that a faith held for its benefits collapses when the benefits do, and that mechanism is not confined to seventh-century Arabia.

**The Same Wager Tested in Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt**

The Qur'an returns to this type and puts a sharper edge on it: *“There are some who say, ‘We believe in Allah,’ but when they suffer in the cause of Allah, they mistake ˹this˺ persecution at the hands of people for the punishment of Allah. But when victory comes from your Lord, they surely say ˹to the believers˺, ‘We have always been with you’”* (29:10). The man of that verse misreads the source of his own trouble — he takes the hostility of people for the displeasure of God — and then reappears to claim the credit when things improve. The brink of 22:11 and the confusion of 29:10 — *“But when victory comes from your Lord, they surely say ˹to the believers˺, “We have always been with you.””* are the same failure seen from two sides: a religion held on the condition that it must pay.

## Verse 22:12

> They call besides Allah what can neither harm nor benefit them. That is ˹truly˺ the farthest one can stray

**"Neither Harms nor Benefits": the Qur'an's Working Test for a God**

The criterion stated here is functional, and it is stated in the same words across the Qur'an. Ibrāhīm puts it to his people in the form of a question: *“Do you then worship—instead of Allah—what can neither benefit nor harm you in any way?”* (21:66). The same formula is put into the mouth of the Messenger as a test his own community should apply to themselves: *“Should we invoke, other than Allah, those ˹idols˺ which cannot benefit or harm us, and turn back to disbelief after Allah has guided us?”* (6:71). It recurs as a flat description of the practice: *“Yet they worship besides Allah what can neither benefit nor harm them”* (25:55). Ibrāhīm's audience is asked the same question in another form: *“Or can they benefit or harm you?”* (26:73).

**They Were Not Thought to Be Creators**

The obvious objection is that nobody in Makkah believed a carved stone had made the sky. The Qur'an knows this, and it says so: *“They worship besides Allah others who can neither harm nor benefit them, and say, ‘These are our intercessors with Allah’”* (10:18).

That verse is the key to this one. The role assigned to the idols was intercession — influence with the real power, a word put in with someone who can act. The Qur'an's reply in 10:18 — *“They worship besides Allah others who can neither harm nor benefit them, and say, “These are our intercessors with Allah.” Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Are you informing Allah of something He does not know in the heavens or the earth? Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate ˹with Him˺!””* is withering precisely because it takes the claim seriously: are you informing God of something He does not know?

Verse 12 of this sūrah dismantles the arrangement from the other end. An intercessor who can neither harm nor benefit has nothing to intercede with. If he cannot move a single atom on his own, his access to the One who moves atoms is worthless, since the whole point of the access was to produce an effect. A patron with no capacity is not a weaker patron; he is no patron at all.

The verdict is *wa-huwa al-ḍalālu al-baʿīd* — and that is the far straying, the straying at a distance.

*The* far straying, as though there were a recognized extreme and this were it.

**Two Ways of Getting This Wrong**

The verse's economy also protects it against two opposite misreadings, and both were live in the audience that first heard it.

The first is the view that the objects of worship are dangerous powers — real, malevolent, requiring appeasement. Verse 12 rules that out directly: a thing that can neither harm nor benefit is not a threat, and treating it as one is the superstition the Qur'an is dismantling. Fear of an idol is as misplaced as hope in it.

Between the two, the Qur'an holds a narrow line that is easy to state and hard to keep: the idol is nothing, and worshipping it is a catastrophe.

## Verse 22:13

> They invoke those whose worship leads to harm, not benefit. What an evil patron and what an evil associate

**From "Neither" to "Harm"**

Verse 12 said the object of worship can neither harm nor benefit. Verse 13 moves the ground: he calls on what harms him rather than benefits him. The escalation is small in wording and large in implication, and it is the sort of movement the Qur'an makes often within a pair of verses.

**Two Words for a Bad Friend**

The verse ends by naming the relationship twice: *labi'sa al-mawlā wa-labi'sa al-ʿashīr* — what an evil *mawlā*, and what an evil *ʿashīr*.

Both terms are institutions before they are insults, and the choice of these two in particular is exact. A *mawlā* is a patron or client, the party to a bond of protection — the arrangement by which a person without a tribe attached himself to one, answered for by it and answerable to it. An *ʿashīr* is a close companion, the person you mix with, the associate of your daily life and, in the tribal vocabulary, a kinsman of your own generation.

Between them they cover the two relationships in which a pre-Islamic Arab located his safety: formal protection and informal company. The verse declares both of them evil in this instance. The god he took as a *mawlā* cannot protect him; the god he took as a daily companion, the one whose company he keeps, turns out to be worse than nobody.

## Verse 22:14

> Indeed, Allah will admit those who believe and do good into Gardens, under which rivers flow. Surely Allah does what He wills

**"Those Who Believe and Do Good"**

The first occurrence of the promise in the Qur'an's arrangement uses the identical phrasing and the identical reward: *“Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow”* (2:25). The stability of the formula across the whole text is the point of it. Salvation is not described differently for different groups; the same two conditions and the same promise are on offer to everyone.

**The Promise That Answers the Failure**

Verses 11 to 13 described three figures: the man on the brink, the man calling on what cannot help, and the man whose patron is worse than nothing. Verse 14 answers all three with a single sentence about a different person.

*Āmanū* — they believed, they made themselves secure in a trust. *Wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti* — and they did the deeds that are sound, that work, that hold up. The pairing matters in both directions. Belief without works is the condition of the man in verse 11, who said the words and was toppled by the first trial. Works without belief would be a moral life with no orientation, which the Qur'an does not recognize as a category.

## Verse 22:15

> Whoever thinks that Allah will not help His Prophet in this world and the Hereafter, let them stretch out a rope to the ceiling and strangle themselves, then let them see if this plan will do away with ˹the cause of˺ their rage

**A Verse With More Than One Door**

This is the most difficult verse in the passage, and the commentaries say so by disagreeing. The disagreements are worth setting out because they turn on identifiable features of the Arabic rather than on taste.

The first question is who the pronoun refers to. Most commentators take "will not help him" as referring to the Prophet ﷺ: whoever supposes that God will not give His Prophet victory in this world and the next. Others read it as referring to the presumptuous man himself — whoever thinks that God will not help *him*.

The second question is who is being addressed. Readings include the Prophet's enemies, who were enraged by his success; the tribes of Asad and Ghaṭafān, who expected him to fail; and, on one reading, a group of believers so impatient for victory against the idolaters that their frustration had curdled. The wording is general — *man kāna yaẓunnu*, whoever thinks — and generality is what allows the readings to coincide.

**"A Rope to the Sky"**

The third question is the image. The Arabic is *fal-yamdud bi-sababin ilā al-samāʾ* — let him stretch out a rope to the *samāʾ*. That word means sky, heaven, or, in ordinary speech, the ceiling of a room. Both senses are live, and both were used by the commentators.

**What the Verse Is Dealing With**

The verse begins with a command that has been read in several ways: whoever thinks that God will not help the Prophet in this world and the next should stretch a rope to the sky and hang himself, and then see whether his plan removes what enrages him. The reading that the older commentators preferred is that the image is of a person trying to cut himself off from the help of God, and the verse ends with a statement that whatever the man does will not change the outcome. The sūrah then states the plain case about the signs and about God's care for His messenger.
**Why the Commentators Differed**

The Latin and modern translations have made heavy weather of the verse, and the traditional commentaries record the disagreement: some read the rope as a means of reaching the sky to stop the revelation, some as a threat of suicide by a man in despair, and some as a picture of a frustrated enemy cutting off his own access to God. The Book's own wording supports the third reading best, since the verse asks the man to see whether his device removes what angers him, which is the success of the message. The disagreement is recorded here so that a reader knows the verse is difficult rather than hidden.
**Where the Passage Goes**

The verses in front of it describe the men who worship God on a shaky footing, taking their religion as a wager (22:11 — *“And there are some who worship Allah on the verge ˹of faith˺: if they are blessed with something good, they are content with it; but if they are afflicted with a trial, they relapse ˹into disbelief˺, losing this world and the Hereafter. That is ˹truly˺ the clearest loss”*), and the verses after it tell the Prophet that the recitation is the clear signs and that God guides whom He wills (22:16 — *“And so We revealed this ˹Quran˺ as clear verses. And Allah certainly guides whoever He wills”*). The sūrah is dealing with the community in Makkah under pressure, and the passage in front of us answers a taunt about the help that had not yet arrived with a sentence about the futility of trying to stop it. The verse after it names the Day that will settle the argument, and the sūrah then returns to the laws of the pilgrimage.

## Verse 22:16

> And so We revealed this ˹Quran˺ as clear verses. And Allah certainly guides whoever He wills

**What "Clear Signs" Means, and What It Does Not**

*Āyātin bayyinātin* — signs that are clear, evident, self-explaining. The Qur'an uses the phrase for previous revelation as well as for its own: *“Indeed, We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ clear revelations. ˹But˺ none will deny them except the rebellious”* (2:99), and in the description of what scripture does: *“He is the One Who sends down clear revelations to His servant to bring you out of darkness and into light”* (57:9).

That is why the same word can be used for the revelation and, in verse 8 of this sūrah, for the scripture the disputant does not have. The illumination is real and the failure to see it is also real. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān makes the same point in its own vocabulary: *“Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood”* (2:256). The standing-out belongs to the truth; whether a person looks is another matter.

**"God Guides Whom He Wills" — the Standard Misreading**

The closing clause is the one readers stumble over. Taken in isolation it sounds like a decree issued at random, and the Qur'an's own usage rules that reading out.

Sūrah al-Kahf states the same pairing without any suggestion of arbitrariness: *“Whoever Allah guides is truly guided. But whoever He leaves to stray, you will never find for them a guiding mentor”* (18:17). The emphasis there falls on the consequence for the person left, not on an unexplained selection.

**Clarity and Guidance Are Two Different Gifts**

The most useful thing this verse does is hold two facts together that readers tend to separate.

The first is that the signs are clear, which means no one is condemned for failing to understand something that was never explained. Accountability in the Qur'an always follows a message that actually arrived, and this verse guarantees that it did.

The second is that guidance is God's to give, which means clarity does not compel belief. A person can see a clear sign and refuse it, and verses 8 to 13 of this very sūrah are a catalogue of people doing exactly that. If clarity automatically produced faith, the disputant of verse 8 could not exist — and he plainly does, and the sūrah spends six verses describing him.

---

## Verse 22:17

> Indeed, the believers, Jews, Sabians, Christians, Magi, and the polytheists—Allah will judge between them ˹all˺ on Judgment Day. Surely Allah is a Witness over all things

**The Sabians: an Identity the Commentators Could Not Settle**

*Al-ṣābiʾūn* appears in the Qur'an alongside the Jews and Christians as a community with a claim to revelation, and the commentators disagreed sharply about who they were — a baptismal sect near the marshes of southern Iraq, a star-worshipping group in Harrān, or people who had left one religion without entering another.

The legal consequence drawn by the jurists from the parallel verse in Sūrah al-Māʾidah was that membership in a named community is not what saves. *“Indeed, the believers, Jews, Sabians and Christians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve”* (5:69). The condition inside that sentence is belief, the Last Day, and sound action — not the label.

**Judgment Deferred Is Not Judgment Denied**

This is the Qur'an's standard posture on inter-religious disagreement, and it is worth stating plainly because it is so often misrepresented. The Qur'an does not say that all communities are equally correct. It says where the adjudication will take place. The communities themselves make counter-claims that cannot both be true — *“The Jews say, ‘The Christians have nothing to stand on’ and the Christians say, ‘The Jews have nothing to stand on,’ although both recite the Scriptures”* (2:113) — and the text's response is not to declare a winner in the abstract but to name the court.

**The Verse's Political Consequence**

A text that defers adjudication to the Day of Resurrection is also a text that declines to authorize coercion in the meantime, and the early community read it that way. Sūrah al-Bayyinah describes the People of the Book and the associators as waiting for *“the clear proof”* to come to them (98:1 — *“The disbelievers from the People of the Book and the polytheists were not going to desist ˹from disbelief˺ until the clear proof came to them”*), and the proof's arrival is what divided them — not the sword.

Sūrah al-Māʾidah states the principle in its most explicit form: *“To each of you We have ordained a code of law and a way of life. If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but His Will is to test you with what He has given ˹each of˺ you. So compete with one another in doing good”* (5:48). Plurality is described as the test, and the competition is in good works rather than in verdicts.

## Verse 22:18

> Do you not see that to Allah bow down ˹in submission˺ all those in the heavens and all those on the earth, as well as the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, and ˹all˺ living beings, as well as many humans, while many are deserving of punishment. And whoever Allah disgraces, none can honour. Surely Allah does what He wills

**How a Mountain Prostrates**

The obvious question is what prostration means when the subject is a star or a stone, and the commentators answered it in two directions that are not mutually exclusive.

One reading takes *sujūd* in its literal sense of bowing down and holds that every creature performs an act proper to its own mode of being, which we do not perceive. Sūrah al-Raʿd is the controlling text: *“To Allah ˹alone˺ bow down ˹in submission˺ all those in the heavens and the earth—willingly or unwillingly—as do their shadows, morning and evening”* (13:15). Sūrah al-Naḥl adds the angels to the same list (16:49 — *“And to Allah ˹alone˺ bows down ˹in submission˺ whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth of living creatures, as do the angels—who are not too proud ˹to do so˺”*), and Sūrah al-Raḥmān includes the stars and the trees in a single line: *“The stars and the trees bow down ˹in submission˺”* (55:6).

The other reading takes the prostration as the creature's total submission to the laws it cannot break — a star's obedience is its orbit. Sūrah al-Isrāʾ holds the two together and explains why the language is not ordinary description: *“There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification”* (17:44).

Abraham's argument is preserved at the moment of the star's setting: *“When the night grew dark upon him, he saw a star and said, ‘This is my Lord!’ But when it set, he said, ‘I do not love things that set’”* (6:76). Verse 18 applies the same test to the whole catalogue at once: everything listed is shown in the act of submission, and submission is the more general form of setting.

**The Reader Is Asked to Prostrate Too**

This is one of two verses in this sūrah at which the Qur'an's reciters bow, and the practice is attested in the ḥadīth literature. ʿUqbah ibn ʿĀmir reported that he asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ whether Sūrat al-Ḥajj contains two prostrations. Was told that it does, and that whoever does not perform them should not recite them — recorded in *Sunan Abī Dāwūd* (1402), where al-Albānī graded the chain weak, though others have accepted it. A related report from ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, also in Abū Dāwūd (1401), counts fifteen prostrations in the Qur'an including two in this sūrah.

Sūrah al-Najm closes on the command verse 18 implies: *“Instead, prostrate to Allah and worship ˹Him alone˺!”* (53:62). The reciter who has heard the catalogue and reaches its end faces the same two options the verse lays out — join the prostrating many, or stand with the many against whom punishment is due. The bowed recitation is the choice made with the body: the list read, the invitation accepted, the reader no longer upright among the upright.

**"Whoever God Disgraces, None Can Honour"**

The point is sovereignty, and it is stated without qualification for the same reason it is stated in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān: *“If Allah helps you, none can defeat you. But if He denies you help, then who else can help you?”* (3:160). Honour in this life is a commodity that human beings trade in and believe they control. The verse places its source somewhere no one can touch, which is simultaneously a warning to the proud and a consolation to the humiliated.

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān places both verbs in the same hand: *“You honour whoever You please and disgrace who You please — all good is in Your Hands”* (3:26). Honouring and disgracing as two motions of one hand, with the good named as what the hand holds. Verse 18's closing is that sentence with its consequence drawn: disgrace from that hand has no appeal, because there is no second source of honour to apply to. The proud man's creditors cannot help him, and the humiliated believer's debtors cannot harm him.

Sūrah Fuṣṣilat draws the conclusion the catalogue implies, as a direct prohibition: *“Do not prostrate to the sun or the moon, but prostrate to Allah, Who created them ˹all˺”* (41:37).

## Verse 22:19

> These are two opposing groups that disagree about their Lord: as for the disbelievers, garments of Fire will be cut out for them and boiling water will be poured over their heads

**"Garments of Fire Cut Out"**

*Qutṭiʿat lahum thiyābun min nārin* — garments of fire have been cut out for them. The verb is from *qaṭṭaʿa*, to cut into pieces, to cut to shape. It is the vocabulary of a tailor working to a pattern, and the preposition *lahum*, for them, marks the garment as made to measure.

**Why Badr Was Proposed**

The most frequently cited occasion is the opening of the Battle of Badr, when three champions came forward from each army: ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, Ḥamzah and ʿUbaydah ibn al-Ḥārith for the Muslims, and three fighters from Quraysh against them.

Every element of the image is pointed. Clothing is what a person uses to cover himself, to appear, and to be ranked by; the Qur'an's paradisal vocabulary uses garments the same way, as the visible sign of a state. Here the covering is fire, and it has been tailored to fit the wearer — the punishment is not an uniform issued to a class but a garment cut for a person.

**When the Garden and the Fire Spoke**

On the reading that takes the two adversaries as the Garden and the Fire themselves, the report is a famous one and it is well attested. Abū Hurayrah narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said that Paradise and the Fire argued: the Fire said, "I have been given the arrogant and the tyrants," and Paradise said, "Why is it that only the weak and the humble enter me?" God then said to Paradise, "You are My mercy, by which I show mercy to whom I will," and to the Fire, "You are My punishment, by which I punish whom I will. Each of you will be filled." It is recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (4850, in the Book of Tafsīr) and in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2846), in the chapter headed "the arrogant will enter the Fire and the humble will enter Paradise.".

The report explains the verse's phrasing on that reading — *ikhtaṣamū fī rabbihim*, they disputed about their Lord, meaning they laid their cases before Him — and it explains why the sūrah goes on to describe the two destinations in matching terms. The Fire is told it will be filled; the inhabitants described in this verse are its filling.

## Verse 22:20

> melting whatever is in their bellies, along with their skin

**One Sentence, Two Surfaces**

The verse is a single clause and it does one thing: it says what the scalding water of the previous verse does. *Yuṣharu bihi mā fī buṭūnihim wa-l-julūd* — what is in their bellies is melted by it, and the skins too.

Sūrah Ibrāhīm names the same totality from the outside in: *“with garments of tar, and their faces covered with flames”* (14:50). Garment worn, face covered — the surface sealed and the meeting-place with the world wrapped in fire. Verse 20 completes the picture from the inside: what the tar garment covers on the skin, the scalding water reaches in the belly. Between the two verses nothing of the sufferer is left undescribed.

**Why the Skin Is Named Separately**

This is why the Qur'an's punishments are so often described at the surface of the body, and why the same tradition renders the reward in the same register. Sūrah al-Kahf uses the identical vocabulary for the punishment of those who reject the truth: *“When they cry for aid, they will be aided with water like molten metal, which will burn ˹their˺ faces”* (18:29). The face is named there for the same reason the skin is named here — not to be lurid but to locate the suffering where a person actually lives, at the place where he meets the world.

Sūrah Fuṣṣilat gives the skin a voice, and the voice testifies: *“They will ask their skin ˹furiously˺, ‘Why have you testified against us?’”* (41:21). The organ of feeling turned witness — what registered every touch now reports it. The skin is named separately in verse 20 for the same reason it speaks there: it is not the wrapping of the person but one of his organs of accountability, the surface at which the world was met and the surface at which the meeting is recalled.

**The Belly: What Is Inside Is Also Reached**

Theologically, the inside matters for a specific reason in this sūrah. The disbelievers' offence has been described as a matter of the heart and the interior: disputing without knowledge (22:3 — *“˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, and follow every rebellious devil”*), worshipping on a brink (22:11 — *“And there are some who worship Allah on the verge ˹of faith˺: if they are blessed with something good, they are content with it; but if they are afflicted with a trial, they relapse ˹into disbelief˺, losing this world and the Hereafter. That is ˹truly˺ the clearest loss”*), arguing from preference rather than from a source (22:8 — *“˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, guidance, or an enlightening scripture”*). A punishment that reaches only the surface would leave the actual site of the disease untouched. The water goes in.

Sūrah al-Baqarah locates the offence where the water goes: *“There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase”* (2:10). Sickness in the interior, increased rather than treated — the disputing, the brink-worship, the preference argued as principle, all of it a condition of the inside. A punishment reaching only the skin would cauterise the surface and leave the sickness; the water that melts *mā fī buṭūnihim* reaches the ward where the disease has been kept.

**Nothing in the Description Is Remedial**

Sūrah al-Humazah names the fire's destination, and it is the interior: *“˹It is˺ Allah's kindled Fire, which rages over the hearts”* (104:6–7). Over the hearts — *taṭṭaliʿu ʿalā al-afʾidah* — the fire reaching the seat of the offence and finding nothing there to refine. The refining fire of this-life trial draws impurity out of metal; the kindled fire mounts over hearts in which nothing remains but the disease. Same element, opposite results — and the difference, as verse 20's silence implies, was made before the fire arrived.

That claim was argued at length in the opening of this sūrah — from the embryo, from the field, from the raising of those in the graves (22:5–7 — *“O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh—fully formed or unformed—in order to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you.”*).

## Verse 22:21

> And awaiting them are maces of iron

*Wa-lahum maqāmiʿu min ḥadīdin* — and for them are hooked rods of iron. The Arabic is a nominal sentence: a prepositional phrase, then a noun.

Sūrah Fāṭir states what the timeless grammar implies: *“As for the disbelievers, they will have the Fire of Hell, where they will not be ˹allowed to be˺ finished by death, nor will its torment be lightened for them”* (35:36). The nominal *wa-lahum maqāmiʿ* works the same way: the rods are not an event approaching in time but equipment belonging to a place, and the belonging has no tense because it has no end.

**What the Word Means**

*Maqāmiʿ* is the plural of *miqmaʿa*, an iron implement with a hooked or angled end. The lexicographers describe it as the bar used to strike a stubborn animal and the hook used to drive or restrain one; the root *q-m-ʿ* carries the sense of subduing, of breaking a thing's resistance.

Sūrah al-Qamar shows the driving in motion: *“On the Day they will be dragged into the Fire on their faces”* (54:48). Dragged, on the face — the posture of a thing that refused to walk and is now moved without its consent. The *miqmaʿa* is the instrument of that motion: what drags must have something to drag with, and the hooked iron is named here while the dragging is shown there. The implement and the action belong to one scene, split across two sūrahs.

**The Instrument Fits the Sin**

The people described in this passage are the ones who turned their shoulders away in pride (22:9 — *“turning away ˹in pride˺ to lead ˹others˺ astray from Allah’s Way.”*), who disputed without knowledge (22:8 — *“˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, guidance, or an enlightening scripture”*), and who were enraged at the thought that God would help someone other than them (22:15 — *“Whoever thinks that Allah will not help His Prophet in this world and the Hereafter, let them stretch out a rope to the ceiling and strangle themselves, then let them see if this plan will do away with ˹the cause of˺ their rage”*). Their defining posture was that they would not be managed.

Sūrah al-Tawbah gives the clearest instance of the mirror, in the branding of hoarders with their own hoard: *“their foreheads, sides, and backs branded with it. ˹It will be said to them,˺ ‘This is the treasure you hoarded for yourselves. Now taste what you hoarded!’”* (9:35). The treasure heated, the body marked where the hoard was kept from reaching — forehead, sides, back. Verse 21 works by the same logic of fittedness: the men who would not be led are met by the tool for leading the unwilling, and the fit is the verdict.

**The Four Verses as One Picture**

Sūrah al-Furqān closes a similar movement the way verse 22 will close this one: *“And when they are tossed into a narrow place inside ˹Hell˺, chained together, then and there they will cry out for ˹instant˺ destruction”* (25:13). Tossed, chained, crying for an end — the narrowing of the place answering the narrowing of every hope. Verses 19 to 22 follow the same narrowing: garment, water, rods, and then the try to leave that produces only return. The specificity the passage accumulates is all about the confinement; the one thing never specified is an exit.

Sūrah al-Ḥadīd describes it as something sent down: *“And We sent down iron with its great might, benefits for humanity, and means for Allah to prove who ˹is willing to˺ stand up for Him and His messengers without seeing Him”* (57:25).

## Verse 22:22

> Whenever they try to escape from Hell—out of anguish—they will be forced back into it, ˹and will be told,˺ “Taste the torment of burning!”

**Returned, and by Whom?**

*Uʿīdū fīhā* — they are returned into it. The verb is passive, in the plural, with no agent named.

Sūrah al-Sajdah stages the identical scene with the verdict attached: *“Whenever they try to escape from it, they will be forced back into it, and will be told, ‘Taste the Fire's torment, which you used to deny’”* (32:20). The same passive return, the same tasting, with the addition this verse leaves implicit — *which you used to deny*. The denial named at the moment of tasting is what answers the question of agency: no guard needs naming, because the return is the denial arriving at its destination.

**"Returned" Is the Resurrection's Own Word**

*Uʿīdū* — they are returned — is from the same root the Qur'an uses for the resurrection of the dead. These people were returned to life, and now they are returned to the Fire.

The echo is deliberate and it is bleak. The verb that names the great hope of the sūrah's opening verses — that God will raise those in the graves (22:7 — *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves”*) — reappears here with a different destination attached. The same act that brought them back now holds them in.

Sūrah Ṭā-Hā gives the root its full cycle, from dust to dust to rising: *“From the earth We created you, and into it We will return you, and from it We will bring you back again”* (20:55). Created, returned, brought back — the three motions of *ʿāda* across a human history. Verse 22's *uʿīdū* is the root turned one more time, after the cycle should have ended: returned to life at the graves, then returned to the Fire at every escape. The resurrection's verb, made into the punishment's.

**The Keepers Who Ask Questions**

The Qur'an's Fire has custodians, and their words are recorded in two passages that illuminate this one. At the arrival, *“its keepers will ask them: ‘Did messengers not come to you from among yourselves, reciting to you the revelations of your Lord and warning you of the coming of this Day of yours?’ The disbelievers will cry, ‘Yes ˹indeed˺! But the decree of torment has come to pass against the disbelievers’”* (39:71). Sūrah al-Mulk records the same exchange in a harder form: *“almost bursting in fury. Every time a group is cast into it, its keepers will ask them, ‘Did a warner not come to you?’”* (67:8).

**Anguish Is the Punishment, Not Its Side Effect**

Sūrah Ibrāhīm describes the same preservation of the sufferer at its limit: *“Death will overwhelm them from every side, yet they will not ˹be able to˺ die”* (14:17). Death arriving from all directions and never arriving — the will intact, the exit sealed. Verse 22's *min ghammin* is that condition from the inside: anguish enough to want out, life enough to keep wanting, and a return that answers every try. What is preserved is precisely what makes the preservation unbearable.

Sūrah al-Nisāʾ builds the same loop around the same word: *“Whenever their skin is burnt completely, We will replace it so they will ˹constantly˺ taste the punishment”* (4:56).

Sūrah al-Māʾidah states the wish and its frustration in the barest terms: *“They will be desperate to get out of the Fire but they will never be able to. And they will suffer an everlasting punishment”* (5:37).

## Verse 22:23

> ˹But˺ Allah will surely admit those who believe and do good into Gardens, under which rivers flow, where they will be adorned with bracelets of gold and pearls, and their clothing will be silk

**Gold and Pearl**

*Yuḥallawna fīhā min asāwira min dhahabin wa-luʾluʾan* — they are adorned in it with bracelets of gold, and pearl. The verb is passive: they are adorned. Nobody in the Garden decorates himself.

The parallel descriptions elsewhere confirm the register: *“They will enter the Gardens of Eternity, where they will be adorned with bracelets of gold and pearls. Their clothing will be silk”* (35:33), and in the fuller account, *“There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold, and wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade, reclining there on ˹canopied˺ couches”* (18:31).

**Silk**

*Wa-libāsuhum fīhā ḥarīr* — and their clothing in it is silk. The word is *ḥarīr*, and the same fabrics are named in the descriptions of the Garden elsewhere: *“dressed in fine silk and rich brocade, facing one another”* (44:53), and *“The virtuous will be ˹dressed˺ in garments of fine green silk and rich brocade, and adorned with bracelets of silver”* (76:21).

Sūrah al-Raḥmān furnishes the Garden down to the lining: *“Those ˹believers˺ will recline on furnishings lined with rich brocade. And the fruit of both Gardens will hang within reach”* (55:54). Brocade underneath, fruit overhead — the texture register verse 23 works in, extended to the furniture. What is promised is not appraised but inhabited: silk against skin, brocade beneath the body, sweetness within arm's length. The reward is described the way a room is described, because it is a room the raised body will live in.

**How the Qur'an Builds These Pairs**

The matching-description technique used here is not unique to this sūrah, and recognizing it elsewhere sharpens the reading here. Sūrah al-Dukhān puts the two destinations four verses apart using the same method: for the punished, *“Like molten metal, it will boil in the bellies, like the boiling of hot water”* (44:45–46); for the rewarded, *“dressed in fine silk and rich brocade, facing one another”* (44:53). Belly and silk, in that order.

It also guards against a common error. Because the two descriptions are built from the same materials, no reader can suppose that one of them is literal and the other figurative. They stand or fall together.

**"Admit" — the Verb Returns**

*Yudkhilu* — He causes them to enter. The same verb the sūrah used in verse 14, in the same form, with the same condition attached: those who believe and do sound deeds.

The punishment of verses 19 to 22 was described almost completely in the passive — garments cut, water poured, no agent named — which leaves a reader wondering who is doing this.

Sūrah al-Nisāʾ names the destination of the admission, and it is not a place but a relation: *“He will admit them into His mercy and grace and guide them to Himself through the Straight Path”* (4:175). Into mercy, into grace — *yudkhiluhum fī raḥmatin minhu* — with guidance continuing after arrival. Verse 23's *yudkhilu* carries the same authorship: the punishment runs as machinery with no agent named, but entry is performed by God, into what belongs to Him, and the entering does not end the bringing.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf gives clothing its theology, at the first dressing of the species: *“O children of Adam! We have provided for you clothing to cover your nakedness and as an adornment. However, the best clothing is righteousness”* (7:26).

## Verse 22:24
> for they have been guided to the best of speech, and they have been guided to the Commendable Path

**The Good Word Elsewhere**

*Al-ṭayyib mina al-qawl* is a phrase with a history in the Qur'an. Sūrah Ibrāhīm builds a parable on it: *“Do you not see how Allah compares a good word to a good tree? Its root is firm and its branches reach the sky”* (14:24). The good word is pictured as something with roots and height, which is to say something alive and load-bearing rather than ornamental.

The paradisal application is made explicit in the scenes of arrival. *“And those who were mindful of their Lord will be led to Paradise in ˹successive˺ groups. When they arrive at its ˹already˺ open gates, its keepers will say, ‘Peace be upon you! You have done well, so come in, to stay forever’”* (39:73). The gates are already open, and the first thing that happens is speech.

The evil word gets the matching parable, and the matching is exact: *“And the parable of an evil word is that of an evil tree, uprooted from the earth, having no stability”* (14:26). Roots and height against uprooting and collapse — the two words pictured as two trees, one load-bearing and one already fallen. Verse 24's guided speech is the first tree's fruit: the inhabitants said the firm word in this life, and the firm word is what they find on every tongue in the next.

**What the Inhabitants Say to Each Other**

The inhabitants are greeted: *“the Gardens of Eternity, which they will enter along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants. And the angels will enter upon them from every gate”* (13:23). They are welcomed with a word — *“where they will be greeted with ‘Peace!’”* (14:23). The greeting is named as a divine greeting on the Day of meeting (33:44 — *“Their greeting on the Day they meet Him will be, “Peace!” And He has prepared for them an honourable reward”*) and as a greeting from the Merciful Lord Himself (36:58 — *“And “Peace!” will be ˹their˺ greeting from the Merciful Lord”*).

And what is absent is named as precisely as what is present: *“There they will never hear any idle talk—only ˹greetings of˺ peace”* (19:62), and *“never to hear any idle talk or lying in it”* (78:35).

**"The Path of the Praised"**

*Wa-hudū ilā ṣirāṭi al-ḥamīd* — and they were guided to the path of the Praised.

Sūrah Sabaʾ joins the same name to the same path, as what the revealed truth guides to: *“and that it guides to the Path of the Almighty, the Praiseworthy”* (34:6). *Ṣirāṭ al-ʿazīz al-ḥamīd* — the path named by its owner's attributes, might guarding it and praise filling it. Verse 24's second guidance is that arrival: the road walked in this life under the name, ending where the name is the sound of the place — praise offered, praise answered, peace spoken from every gate.

**The Verse Closes a Circle Opened in Verse 19**

Sūrah Yūnus states the guidance-to-Garden movement as the mechanism of arrival: *“Surely those who believe and do good, their Lord will guide them ˹to Paradise˺ through their faith, rivers will flow under their feet in the Gardens of Bliss”* (10:9). Guided *through their faith* — the belief itself the instrument of the guiding — and the rivers placed at the sentence's end as where the guided arrive. Verse 24 closes verses 19 to 24 on the same note: the silk and bracelets exhibited, then the guidance named as how the wearers got there.

The petition for guidance opens the Qur'an and never leaves the believer's day: *“Guide us along the Straight Path”* (1:6).

## Verse 22:25

> Indeed, those who persist in disbelief and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah and from the Sacred Mosque—which We have appointed for all people, residents and visitors alike—along with whoever intends to deviate by doing wrong in it, We will cause them to taste a painful punishment

The parenthesis is the verse's most consequential clause: the Sacred Mosque, *which We appointed for mankind, equal are the resident in it and the visitor*.

**Why the Sanctuary Makes the Offence Worse**

Offences committed in such a place are therefore not merely offences with an unfortunate address. They are offences against the institution of safety itself, and the Qur'an treats them on that footing. Committing idolatry anywhere is the gravest wrong; committing it in the House that was purified for the worship of one God (22:26 — *“And ˹remember˺ when We assigned to Abraham the site of the House, ˹saying,˺ “Do not associate anything with Me ˹in worship˺ and purify My House for those who circle ˹the Ka’bah˺, stand ˹in prayer˺, and bow and prostrate themselves”*) is a different order of thing, because it uses the sanctuary for the precise purpose the sanctuary exists to exclude.

That is why the verse names the Sacred Mosque as an object of the hindrance rather than merely its location. The people being turned away are being turned away from a place as well as from a path.

**The Punishment Tasted**

And the adjective is *alīm*, painful — the Qur'an's most frequent description of punishment, and one it applies to the Hereafter rather than to this life. The verse's threats are real in history — the sanctuary was cleared — but the sentence it announces is not a political forecast. It is the verdict that comes after.

**The Equality the Verse States**

The parenthesis in the verse says that the Sacred Mosque was appointed for all people, resident and visitor alike, and the jurists took the sentence as a rule rather than a description. The schools discussed at length whether a man may own and rent out houses in Makkah, and whether a resident may shut out the pilgrims who come late in the season, and the argument on both sides goes back to this clause. The Book makes the sanctuary a place that belongs to humanity, and it refuses to let the people born near it turn the address into a privilege.
**The Sanctity the Prophet Declared**

The Prophet stated the standing of the city in his own words on the day it was opened. He said that God had made Makkah sacred, not the people, and that it was not lawful for anyone who believes in God and the Last Day to shed blood in it or to cut its trees, and he made the same declaration about the game and the lost property that is to be announced rather than picked up. The report is in Bukhārī, and it lies behind the verse's warning about anyone who intends to desecrate the House with wrongdoing. The Book's point is that the protection of the sanctuary does not depend on the tribe that happens to hold the keys.
**The Offence the Verse Widens**

The verse names those who intend wrongdoing in it, and the commentators read the clause as covering both the person who commits the crime and the one who means to. The Sūrah of the Cow names the wrongdoers who bar people from the sacred mosque and seek to ruin it (2:114 — *“Who does more wrong than those who prevent Allah’s Name from being mentioned in His places of worship and strive to destroy them? Such people have no right to enter these places except with fear. For them is disgrace in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter”*), and this verse adds the warning that the intention alone is answered, because the damage to a sanctuary begins in the plan. A house of God is desecrated first by the plan, and the verse says so before it names the painful punishment.

## Verse 22:26

> And ˹remember˺ when We assigned to Abraham the site of the House, ˹saying,˺ “Do not associate anything with Me ˹in worship˺ and purify My House for those who circle ˹the Ka’bah˺, stand ˹in prayer˺, and bow and prostrate themselves

**A Site Given, Not Chosen**

*Wa-idh bawwaʾnā li-Ibrāhīma makāna al-bayti* — and when We settled Abraham at the place of the House. The verb *bawwaʾa* means to give someone a place to alight, to install him somewhere and make it habitable for him.

The significance of the verb is that the location is not Abraham's discovery. He did not survey the valley and choose a site; he was placed at one. The sūrah that has already said that the Sacred Mosque was appointed for all people (22:25 — *“Indeed, those who persist in disbelief and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah and from the Sacred Mosque—which We have appointed for all people, residents and visitors alike—along with whoever intends to deviate by doing wrong in it, We will cause them to taste a painful punishment”*) now says that its founder was assigned its position, and the two together remove any human claim of ownership over it. The man who built it did not select it, and the people who inherited the custodianship did not build it.

**The Site Existed Before the Building Did**

The verse says that Abraham was settled at the *place* of the House — *makāna al-bayti* — not at the House itself. The building came later, and the Qur'an records its construction elsewhere in a phrase that makes the sequence explicit: *“And ˹remember˺ when Abraham raised the foundation of the House with Ishmael, ˹both praying,˺ ‘Our Lord! Accept ˹this˺ from us’”* (2:127).

The verse that names the House as the first established for humanity puts the same point positively: *“Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah—a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people”* (3:96).

**The First Command Is Not About Building**

The priority is instructive for reading the rest of the passage. The House is not being established as a shrine that will then need to be defended against corruption; it is being established as a place whose entire purpose is the exclusion of rivals, and everything else — the circuit, the standing, the sacrifice — is secondary to that. When the sūrah speaks a few verses later about shunning the filth of idols (22:30 — *“That is so. And whoever honours the rituals of Allah, it is best for them in the sight of their Lord. The ˹meat of˺ cattle has been made lawful for you, except what has ˹already˺ been recited to you. So shun the impurity of idolatry, and shun words of falsehood”*), it is returning to this first instruction rather than adding a new one.

The parallel passage in Sūrah al-Baqarah records the same commission in the same order, with the purification commanded first and the worshippers named: *“We made the House a place of return and a sanctuary for the people, ˹saying,˺ ‘Take the standing-place of Abraham as a site of prayer.’ And We entrusted Abraham and Ishmael to purify My House for those who circle it, those who meditate in it, and those who bow and prostrate themselves”* (2:125).

*Li-l-ṭāʾifīna wa-l-qāʾimīna wa-l-rukkaʿi al-sujūd* — for those who circle, those who stand, and those who bow and prostrate.

## Verse 22:27

> Call ˹all˺ people to the pilgrimage. They will come to you on foot and on every lean camel from every distant path

**"On Foot"**

*Rijālan* — on foot, walking. The word is the plural of a man on his own feet, and it is placed first in the list of arrivals.

The detail also guarantees that the pilgrimage is not an elite observance.

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the obligation with the ability clause that keeps it open: *“Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people”* (3:97). *Rijālan* first in verse 27's list is that clause enacted: the walker has found his way on foot, and the verse counts him before the rider.

**"Every Lean Camel"**

*Wa-ʿalā kulli ḍāmirin* — and upon every lean one. The commentators took the animal as the camel, and the leanness as the effect of the journey: a beast arrives thin because it has come a long way.

The valley's provisioning is named as a standing favour, and the fruits arrive from elsewhere: *“Have We not established for them a safe haven ˹in Mecca˺ to which fruits of all kinds are brought as a provision from Us?”* (28:57). Brought — the barren hollow fed by routes it did not build. The lean camels of verse 27 are those routes in motion: beasts arriving thin across country with no pasture, carrying people to a sanctuary whose food comes the same way they do, from far away, as provision.

**What the Call Was Answering**

It is easy to read the command as though the pilgrimage were summoned into existence for its own sake. Abraham's own prayer, recorded in Sūrah Ibrāhīm, supplies the reason the valley was inhabited at all: *“Our Lord! I have settled some of my offspring in a barren valley, near Your Sacred House, our Lord, so that they may establish prayer. So make the hearts of ˹believing˺ people incline toward them and provide them with fruits, so perhaps they will be thankful”* (14:37).

**"From Every Distant Pass"**

*Yaʾtīna min kulli fajjin ʿamīq* — they come from every deep ravine, every distant mountain pass. *Fajj* is a gap between mountains, a defile; *ʿamīq* is deep or far.

The House was made for exactly this gathering, and Sūrah al-Baqarah names its purpose at the founding: *“when We made the Sacred House a centre and a sanctuary for the people”* (2:125). A centre — *mathābah*, a place of return, visited and revisited — for *the people*, all of them. The distant passes of verse 27 are the roads to that centre, and the depth of the ravines measures the width of the summons: a house made for humanity draws from every defile humanity lives behind.

## Verse 22:28

> so they may obtain the benefits ˹in store˺ for them, and pronounce the Name of Allah on appointed days over the sacrificial animals He has provided for them. So eat from their meat and feed the desperately poor

**The Pilgrimage Is Not a Retreat From Commerce**

The Qur'an's willingness to name economic activity as part of the rite distinguishes it from the assumption that holiness requires distance from the market.

Sūrah al-Baqarah makes the same point from the other direction, in a verse that defends trade during the pilgrimage against those who considered it sinful. The logic of both texts is identical: what would corrupt a gathering is not buying and selling but the loss of the remembrance that frames them. The pilgrim is commanded to remember God *during the appointed days* (2:203 — *“And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days.”*), which assumes that he is doing something else during those days from which the remembrance must not distract him — or rather, which the remembrance must pervade.

Sūrah al-Baqarah removes the blame explicitly: *“There is no blame on you for seeking the bounty of your Lord ˹during this journey˺”* (2:198). Trade at the pilgrimage is named *bounty of your Lord* — the same phrase used for lawful provision everywhere else — which settles the scruple by redescription. The gain sought at ʿArafāt is not a distraction smuggled into worship; it is provision, and the verse places it inside the journey rather than outside it.

**"Known Days"**

That is why the commentators had to supply the identification from elsewhere, and why they disagreed. Some took the known days as the first ten days of Dhū al-Ḥijjah, the month in which the pilgrimage falls. Others took them as the days of *tashrīq*, the eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth of the same month, which are named in Sūrah al-Baqarah: *“And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days. Whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind ˹till the third—seeking additional reward˺, so long as they are mindful ˹of their Lord˺”* (2:203).

**"Over What He Provided Them"**

The provision language is the Qur'an's general rule for eating, and Sūrah al-Naḥl states it with gratitude attached: *“So eat from the good, lawful things which Allah has provided for you, and be grateful for Allah's favours, if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺”* (16:114). Verse 28's sacrifice is that sentence enacted: the animal named as provision, the name pronounced over it, and the eating that follows done as a response rather than as mere consumption.

**"And Feed the Poor and Wretched"**

Sūrah al-Māʿūn makes the feeding a test of religion itself: the one who denies the Recompense *“does not encourage the feeding of the poor”* (107:3). The charge is not cruelty but omission — he repulses the orphan and lets the poor go unfed — and the omission is presented as evidence about his creed. Verse 28 commands the positive form of the same act at the pilgrimage's centre: the sacrifice that does not feed the *bāʾis faqīr* has missed the rite's stated purpose, whatever else it accomplished.

The shortest sūrah joins the two halves of verse 28's world in a single line: *“So pray and sacrifice to your Lord ˹alone˺”* (108:2).

## Verse 22:29

> Then let them groom themselves, fulfil their vows, and circle the Ancient House.”

**"Then Let Them End Their Untidiness"**

*Thumma l-yaqḍū tafathahum* — then let them do away with their *tafath*. The word is unusual, and al-Rāzī made a point of saying so: *tafath* is not known from any source outside the interpretive literature. He reports that a Bedouin, asked what it meant, equated it with filth.

That reading is confirmed by the parallel legislation in Sūrah al-Baqarah, which describes the same moment in procedural detail: *“And do not shave your heads until the sacrificial animal reaches its destination”* (2:196). The shaving is the release, and the animal's arrival is what triggers it. The two verses together give the shape of the rite: a period of deliberate untidiness, ended by a specified act, at a specified time.

**"And Let Them Circle the Ancient House"**

*Wa-l-yaṭṭawwafū bi-l-bayti al-ʿatīq* — and let them go around the ancient House. The verse ends where the pilgrimage's central rite is: the circumambulation.

The first sense is the one the Qur'an states elsewhere: *“Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah—a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people”* (3:96). The House is ancient in the specific sense that it is the first, which means the religion practised at it is not a new sect but the restoration of the oldest thing.

**"Freed" — and the Report Behind It**

The religious reading of *ʿatīq* as "freed" therefore makes a claim about ownership that runs through this whole passage: the House is not the property of whoever holds the keys, and no power has ever successfully taken it.

## Verse 22:30

> That is so. And whoever honours the rituals of Allah, it is best for them in the sight of their Lord. The ˹meat of˺ cattle has been made lawful for you, except what has ˹already˺ been recited to you. So shun the impurity of idolatry, and shun words of falsehood

*Uḥillat lakum al-anʿāmu illā mā yutlā ʿalaykum* — the grazing livestock have been made lawful to you except what is recited to you.

**"That Is So" — a Seal on the Passage**

*Dhālika* — that is how it is. The demonstrative closes everything from verse 26 onward: the assignment of the site to Abraham, the purification of the House, the call to the pilgrimage, the arrival on foot and on lean camels, the sacrifice, the release and the circuit.

Verses 31 onward are general instructions to the community; verses 26 to 29 were the pilgrimage's founding charter. *That* is how it is; now for you.

**Cattle Permitted, With the Recited Exceptions**

The exception points backward to revelation already given, and the prohibitions are listed in full in Sūrah al-Māʾidah: *“Forbidden to you are carrion, blood. Swine; what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah; what is killed by strangling, beating, a fall, or by being gored to death; what is partly eaten by a predator unless you slaughter it; and what is sacrificed on altars”* (5:3). The shorter statement in Sūrah al-Baqarah names the core: *“He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah”* (2:173).

The placement of this permission directly after the rites of sacrifice is not incidental. Having just commanded slaughter, the verse clarifies what may be slaughtered and eaten, and the emphasis falls on the name pronounced over the animal: *“Do not eat of what is not slaughtered in Allah’s Name. For that would certainly be ˹an act of˺ disobedience”* (6:121). The meat is lawful; the invocation is what makes it so.

**"Shun the Filth of Idols"**

*Fa-jtanibū al-rijsa mina al-awthāni* — so avoid the filth of the idols. The word *rijs* is the term used for filth that defiles, and it is applied here to the objects themselves rather than to the act of worshipping them.

Sūrah al-Tawbah draws the legal consequence from the same vocabulary: *“Indeed, the polytheists are ˹spiritually˺ impure, so they should not approach the Sacred Mosque after this year”* (9:28). The impurity is not physical dirt — idols are often well-kept objects — but a defilement that attaches to the person who has contact with them, and the verb *ijtanibū*, keep away from, prescribes distance rather than careful handling.

**"And Shun False Speech"**

*Wa-jtanibū qawla al-zūri* — and avoid the speech that is false. The same verb governs both objects, which places the two offences on the same footing.

The Qur'an gives specimens of the speech it means: *“those who take other lords besides Him, ˹saying,˺ ‘We worship them only so they may bring us closer to Allah’”* (39:3), and *“And they attribute ˹angels as˺ daughters to Allah—glory be to Him!— the opposite of what they desire for themselves”* (16:57). Neither is a lie about a neighbour. Both are lies about God, told to justify an arrangement.

## Verse 22:31

> Be upright ˹in devotion˺ to Allah, associating none with Him ˹in worship˺. For whoever associates ˹others˺ with Allah is like someone who has fallen from the sky and is either snatched away by birds or swept by the wind to a remote place

**The Parable of the Fall**

*Fa-ka-annamā kharra mina al-samāʾi* — it is as though he fell from the sky. The comparison is to a falling body, and the point of the image is helplessness.

Sūrah al-Tīn states the height and the drop in two lines: *“Indeed, We created humans in the best form. But We will reduce them to the lowest of the low”* (95:4–5). Best form, then lowest low — the fall of verse 31 told without the parable.

**Two Endings, and Neither Is a Landing**

Then the image forks: *fa-takhṭafuhu al-ṭayru aw tahwī bihi al-rīḥu fī makānin saḥīq* — the birds snatch him away, or the wind sweeps him down into a far-off place.

Sūrah Ibrāhīm gives the drift ending its own parable, in ashes and wind: *“The parable of the deeds of those who disbelieve in their Lord is that of ashes fiercely blown away by wind on a stormy day”* (14:18). A lifetime's deeds as ash, the wind doing what wind does — no captor, no sentence pronounced, only dispersal. Verse 31's *saḥīq*, the far-off place, is where such ashes land: somewhere no one arrives at by accident, because the scattering was the deed's own weightlessness made visible.

**Why the Image Comes After the Rites**

Sūrah al-Tawbah weighs the sanctuary's services against belief and finds them unequal: *“Do you ˹pagans˺ consider providing the pilgrims with water and maintaining the Sacred Mosque as equal to believing in Allah and the Last Day and struggling in the cause of Allah? They are not equal in Allah's sight”* (9:19). Water provided, House maintained — the very rites verses 26 to 30 legislate — set against faith and found lighter. Verse 31's parable is that verdict in imagery: the rites performed without the turning are the falling man's motions on the way down.

**The Wind and the Birds Are Not Agents**

That absence is theologically important. Nobody is dragging the associator anywhere. He fell, and what happens to him afterwards is what happens to anything that falls. The punishment in this parable is not inflicted; it is what a fall is.

Sūrah al-Isrāʾ states the reflexivity as a rule of history: *“If you act rightly, it is for your own good, but if you do wrong, it is to your own loss”* (17:7). Good kept, evil suffered — by the doer, in both cases. The parable of verse 31 needs no captor because the Qur'an's moral world needs none: the fall is what association is, the birds and the wind are what the air holds, and the landing is the act arriving where acts of its kind arrive.

The Qur'an adopts the term and binds it to Abraham: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘No! We follow the faith of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist’”* (2:135).

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the testimony with its witnesses named: *“Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him — and so are the angels and people of knowledge”* (3:18).

## Verse 22:32

> That is so. And whoever honours the symbols of Allah, it is certainly out of the piety of the heart

**Shaʿāʾir: Emblems, Not Merely Rituals**

*Wa-man yuʿaẓẓim shaʿāʾira Allāhi* — and whoever magnifies the emblems of God. The word *shaʿāʾir* is broader than the word rendered "rituals.".

Al-Qurṭubī connected it to the root *sh-ʿ-r*, which carries the sense of knowing and of being conscious of something, and on that basis described the *shaʿāʾir* as those things in which God has placed a means by which He makes Himself known or teaches. That is why the term covers not only the rites themselves but the places at which they are performed and the animals with which they are carried out.

Sūrah al-Baqarah gives a concrete instance: *“Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺”* (2:158). Two low hills are called emblems of God, which settles the question of what kind of thing an emblem is. It is not intrinsically impressive. It is a marker that points.

**"It Is From the Reverence of Hearts"**

The tradition locates reverence in the same place. Abū Hurayrah reported that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said, "Reverence is here," and pointed to his chest three times; in the same report he said that God does not look at your bodies or your faces but looks at your hearts. It is recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2564).

The placement of *qulūb*, hearts, at the end of the sentence gives it the emphasis. The question the verse answers is where an action's value is registered, and the answer is not the action. Magnifying an emblem is outward; what makes it count is the state of the person doing it, and that state is invisible.

The warning has a particular edge here because the rites in question were inherited.

**What Magnification Looks Like in Practice**

Al-Rāzī took it as an attitude: to magnify the rites is to treat the worship of God through the pilgrimage as a weighty matter rather than a formality. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī took it more materially, in the context of the sacrificial animals: that they should be well fed, sound and good-looking, and that a sick or injured animal should not be offered.

The second reading is the more searching, because it tests the first. A person who believes the rite is weighty will bring an animal he is not ashamed of, and the verse that was rejected in Sūrah al-Baqarah sets the standard: *“He replied, ‘Allah says, “It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields; wholesome and without blemish”’”* (2:71). What is offered is measured by what it cost.

## Verse 22:33

> You may benefit from sacrificial animals for an appointed term, then their place of sacrifice is at the Ancient House

**The Animal Has a Life Before It Has a Destination**

*Lakum fīhā manāfiʿu ilā ajalin musamman* — for you in them are benefits, until a stated term. The sentence grants use before it imposes an end.

The ruling corrects a specific practice. Pre-Islamic custom included animals set aside for idols — the *baḥīrah*, *sāʾibah*, *waṣīlah* and *ḥām* — whose milk and backs were declared forbidden to their owners. Sūrah al-Māʾidah rejects these categories outright: *“Allah has never ordained the ˹so-called˺ baḥîrah, sâ'ibah, waṣîlah, and ḥâm camels. But the disbelievers just fabricate lies about Allah, and most of them lack understanding”* (5:103).

**Superstition With an Economic Shape**

This is consistent with the vocabulary of verse 28, where the sacrificial animal was described as *what He provided them*. Provision is for use. A gift that may not be touched is not provision, and the Qur'an's legislation throughout refuses to create categories of untouchable things.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf puts the question the verse implies, as a challenge to the inventors of prohibition: *“Who has forbidden the adornments and lawful provisions Allah has brought forth for His servants?”* (7:32). The question has no answer, because no one with authority issued the ban — which is what exposes the economics. An animal removed from milk and riding by a custom no one ordained impoverishes its owner for no one's benefit; verse 33's benefit clause restores the yield by asking, in practice, who took it away.

**"Then Their Place of Sacrifice Is at the Ancient House"**

*Thumma maḥilluhā ilā al-bayti al-ʿatīq* — then its place of release is at the Ancient House. The word *maḥill* is the place where something becomes permissible, where the prohibition lifts and the act is completed.

The destination mattered enough to stop an army. At Ḥudaybiyah the Muslims were turned back with *“the sacrificial animals”* prevented *“from reaching their destination”* (48:25) — the rite suspended a day's march from its place. *Maḥill* is not a convenience but a condition: the release happens there or it does not happen, which is why the sūrah fixes the sacrifice to a single sanctuary rather than leaving it to households. A rite that must arrive somewhere cannot be privatised.

**What the Benefit Clause Excludes**

The distinction is not arbitrary. A benefit is something the animal produces while remaining the animal that was dedicated. A sale converts it back into ordinary wealth, and a substitution changes what was vowed. Both undo the dedication rather than use the dedicated thing, and the verse's boundary — benefits *until a stated term* — is drawn to permit the first and refuse the second.

There is a principle here that runs through the sūrah's treatment of property generally. Nothing a person owns is his in the absolute sense: cattle are lawful *except what has been recited* (22:30 — *“The ˹meat of˺ cattle has been made lawful for you, except what has ˹already˺ been recited to you.”*), wealth is *what We provided them* (22:35 — *“those whose hearts tremble at the remembrance of Allah, who patiently endure whatever may befall them, and who establish prayer and donate from what We have provided for them”*), and a dedicated animal may be used but not liquidated. Ownership in this sūrah is always real, always limited, and always referred back to the giver.

Sūrah al-Anʿām applies the same phrase to the human lifespan, with the resurrection as its second term: *“He is the One Who created you from clay, then appointed a term ˹for your death˺ and another known only to Him ˹for your resurrection˺”* (6:2).

Pregnancies run to one, nations run to one (7:34 — *“For each community there is an appointed term. When their time arrives, they can neither delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it”*), lives end at one, and now a dedicated animal's usefulness expires at one.

## Verse 22:34

> For every community We appointed a rite of sacrifice so that they may pronounce the Name of Allah over the sacrificial animals He has provided for them. For your God is only One God, so submit yourselves to Him ˹alone˺. And give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to the humble

**Every Community Got a Rite**

*Wa-li-kulli ummatin jaʿalnā mansakan* — and for every community We appointed a rite. The word *mansak* refers here to sacrifice specifically, though some commentators took it as religious ritual generally and others as the place at which the ritual is performed.

Sūrah al-Māʾidah states the same principle for law and way of life: *“To each of you We have ordained a code of law and a way of life. If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but His Will is to test you with what He has given ˹each of˺ you”* (5:48). Sūrah al-Naḥl states it for messengers: *“We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, ‘Worship Allah and shun false gods’”* (16:36), and Sūrah Yūnus for the warning itself: *“And for every community there is a messenger”* (10:47).

**Why the Rite Was Given: to Mention the Name**

The prohibition matches the purpose clause exactly: *“Do not eat of what is not slaughtered in Allah's Name. For that would certainly be ˹an act of˺ disobedience”* (6:121). The name pronounced *over* the animal is what separates the eaten from the uneaten — not the species, not the method, not the hunger of the eater. Verse 34 states the institution positively and Sūrah al-Anʿām states it negatively, and between them they define sacrifice completely by the invocation: with the name it is provision received, without it disobedience committed.

**"Your God Is One God"**

The Qur'an states the first half of this claim in its baldest form elsewhere: *“Your God is ˹only˺ One God. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”* (2:163).

Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ joins the same two clauses — oneness, then the call to submit: *“Say, ‘What has been revealed to me is this: Your God is only One God.’ Will you then submit?”* (21:108). The whole of revelation compressed into one sentence and one question. Verse 34 keeps the same order — the God named as one, the submission commanded next — because the inference only runs in that direction: from who God is to what the response must be, never the reverse.

**"So Submit to Him"**

Abraham is the Qur'an's portrait of the imperative obeyed: *“When his Lord ordered him, ‘Submit,’ he responded, ‘I submit to the Lord of all worlds’”* (2:131). Command and compliance with nothing between them — no delay, no condition, no negotiation of terms. The sūrah that commissioned Abraham to purify the House (22:26 — *“And ˹remember˺ when We assigned to Abraham the site of the House, ˹saying,˺ “Do not associate anything with Me ˹in worship˺ and purify My House for those who circle ˹the Ka’bah˺, stand ˹in prayer˺, and bow and prostrate themselves”*) commands the same submission here, and the founder's answer stands as the definition of what the word requires: the whole self handed over, at the word, to the Lord of everything.

**The Good News Is Addressed to the Humble**

The good news has the same addressees wherever the formula appears. Sūrah al-Zumar names them by what they turned from and to: *“And those who shun the worship of false gods, turning to Allah ˹alone˺, will have good news. So give good news to My servants”* (39:17). Shunning and turning — the two motions of the *mukhbit*, whose heart has gone still toward every claimant except one. The good news is not announced to the crowd and hoped to land; it is delivered to the still, who are the only ones positioned to receive it.

The fathers' objection is recorded in its standard form in Sūrah al-Baqarah: *“When it is said to them, ‘Follow what Allah has revealed,’ they reply, ‘No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing.’ ˹Would they still do so,˺ even if their forefathers had ˹absolutely˺ no understanding or guidance?”* (2:170).

## Verse 22:35

> those whose hearts tremble at the remembrance of Allah, who patiently endure whatever may befall them, and who establish prayer and donate from what We have provided for them

**The Body Keeps Score**

The second verse often paired with this one is about a different organ: *“When they listen to what has been revealed to the Messenger, you see their eyes overflowing with tears for recognizing the truth”* (5:83).

The pairing is deliberate in the Qur'an's psychology. Faith is not described as an interior state with occasional outward effects; it is described as a state that registers on the body — hearts that tremble, skin that softens, eyes that fill. The commentators who wrote on this verse, including al-Qushayrī, took the trembling as increasing in proportion to a person's awareness of God's self-disclosure in the world, which makes it a function of attention rather than of temperament.

Sūrah al-Raʿd names the second half of the heart's response, after the trembling: *“those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort”* (13:28). Trembling first, comfort after — the same sequence Sūrah al-Zumar traced from fear to softening. The body keeps score in both directions: the mention of God moves the heart, and then it settles it. A humility that stopped at trembling would be alarm; the comfort is what makes it worship.

**The Second Mark: Endurance Without Commentary**

*Wa-l-ṣābirīna ʿalā mā aṣābahum* — and those who are patient over what befalls them. The preposition is *ʿalā*, which carries the sense of staying on top of something, holding one's place under a load.

The phrasing *mā aṣābahum* — whatever strikes them — is deliberately wide.

Sūrah al-Baqarah gives the patient their sentence, and the sentence explains the preposition: *“who, when faced with a disaster, say, ‘Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return’”* (2:156). Belonging stated, return faced — the staying-on-top that *ʿalā* describes is not stoicism but ownership acknowledged.

**Why the Humble Are Described Just Before the Fighting Begins**

The placement of this description is one of the sūrah's most deliberate choices, and it is easy to miss. Three verses from here, permission to fight will be granted (22:39 — *“Permission ˹to fight back˺ is ˹hereby˺ granted to those being fought, for they have been wronged. And Allah is truly Most Capable of helping them ˹prevail˺”*), and the community that receives it is the community described here.

Read the other way round, the sequence is a warning. The sūrah is describing the people whose good news the Prophet is told to announce, and the next passage will describe what happens when a community is established in the land (22:41 — *“˹They are˺ those who, if established in the land by Us, would perform prayer, pay alms-tax, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil.”*). The risk being guarded against is not defeat. It is the loss of the tremor.

**What the List Is For**

Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ states what the apparatus exists to produce, on the Day it is all that counts: *“Only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺”* (26:89). The four marks of verse 35 are that heart described while it is still in the world — trembling, enduring, praying, giving. The sacrifices of verses 28 to 37 were the outward machinery; this list is the product the machinery was built to make.

The same phenomenon is described elsewhere in the same physical vocabulary: *“which causes the skin ˹and hearts˺ of those who fear their Lord to tremble, then their skin and hearts soften at the mention of ˹the mercy of˺ Allah”* (39:23).

The Qur'an's opening portrait of the righteous joins the same two acts: *“who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them”* (2:3).

## Verse 22:36

> We have made sacrificial camels ˹and cattle˺ among the symbols of Allah, in which there is ˹much˺ good for you. So pronounce the Name of Allah over them when they are lined up ˹for sacrifice˺. Once they have fallen ˹lifeless˺ on their sides, you may eat from their meat, and feed the needy—those who do not beg, and those who do. In this way We have subjected these ˹animals˺ to you so that you may be grateful

**Camels as Emblems**

*Wa-l-budna jaʿalnāhā lakum min shaʿāʾiri Allāhi* — and the sacrificial camels, We have made them for you among the emblems of God. The word *budn* refers to the large sacrificial animals; the jurists differed over whether it is restricted to camels or includes cattle, and both readings are defensible.

The classification is the point. An animal is being called a *shaʿīrah*, an emblem of God — the same word the sūrah used two verses before for the thing whose magnification comes from the reverence of hearts (22:32 — *“That is so. And whoever honours the symbols of Allah, it is certainly out of the piety of the heart”*), and the same word used in Sūrah al-Baqarah for the two hills of Ṣafa and Marwah (2:158 — *“Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah.”*).

Sūrah al-Baqarah applies the same word to the two hills of the pilgrimage walk: *“Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah”* (2:158). Hills and camels under one term — *shaʿāʾir* — which shows what the category holds: ordinary features of the world, stone and beast, taken up as pointers. The hill does not change when the pilgrim walks it, and the camel does not change when it is dedicated. Emblemhood is not a property added to the thing; it is a meaning the thing is made to carry.

**"In Them Is Good for You"**

Sūrah al-Naḥl itemises the good before any rite is mentioned: *“And He created the cattle for you as a source of warmth, food, and ˹many other˺ benefits”* (16:5). Warmth, food, benefits — the animal profitable to its owner in every direction before a single one is slaughtered. Verse 36's *lakum fīhā khayr* is that sentence recalled at the moment of sacrifice: the command to kill arrives only after the Creator has restated, in the widest word available, that the whole arrangement favours the giver.

**"When They Have Fallen on Their Sides"**

Sūrah al-Insān describes the feeding at its most costly, where the food is loved and given anyway: *“and give food — despite their desire for it — to the poor, the orphan, and the captive”* (76:8). Verse 36's asymmetry belongs to the same economy: the sacrificer permitted his share, the poor commanded theirs, and the binding part the part that leaves the household.

**"Thus We Have Subjected Them to You"**

*Ka-dhālika sakhkharnāhā lakum* — in that way We have made them subservient to you. The verb *sakhkhara* is used throughout the Qur'an for the created order placed at human disposal: *“He has subjected the ships for your service, sailing through the sea by His command”* (14:32), *“And He has subjected for your benefit the day and the night, the sun and the moon”* (16:12). The same claim for animals, *“so that you may sit firmly on their backs, and remember your Lord’s blessings once you are settled on them”* (43:13).

The rule for eating is stated as a test of belief itself: *“So eat only of what is slaughtered in Allah's Name if you truly believe in His revelations”* (6:118).

Sūrah al-Baqarah describes the first kind in a passage that is almost a commentary on this phrase: *“˹Charity is˺ for the needy who are too engaged in the cause of Allah to move about in the land ˹for work˺. Those unfamiliar with their situation will think they are not in need ˹of charity˺ because they do not beg”* (2:273).

## Verse 22:37

> Neither their meat nor blood reaches Allah. Rather, it is your piety that reaches Him. This is how He has subjected them to you so that you may proclaim the greatness of Allah for what He has guided you to, and give good news to the good-doers

**The Address Switches to the Singular**

The last sentence of the verse changes grammatical person. Everything before has been plural — *your* piety, *He has subjected them to you* — and then the closing command, *give good news*, is addressed to a single person: the Prophet ﷺ.

**God Is Not Fed**

The religious claim underneath the verse is the one the Qur'an makes with unusual bluntness elsewhere: God has no needs. A sacrifice that feeds the deity belongs to a religion of a different kind, and the Qur'an's insistence on this point is what makes its sacrificial law coherent.

Sūrah al-Dhāriyāt states the disclaimer in the first person: *“I seek no provision from them, nor do I need them to feed Me”* (51:57). No provision sought, no feeding needed — the two halves of every food-offering refused together. Verse 37 is that refusal applied to the sacrifice: the meat cannot be His portion because He takes no portion, which is why it is all available for the sideways distribution — eaten by the household, given to the poor — with nothing held back for heaven.

The office is defined where the Prophet's limits are drawn: *“We have surely sent you with the truth ˹O Prophet˺ as a deliverer of good news and a warner. And you will not be accountable for the residents of the Hellfire”* (2:119). Good news and warning as the whole commission, accountability for results explicitly removed. The singular *bashshir* in verses 34 and 37 is that office exercised: the community receives the ruling in the plural, and the Prophet alone is told to announce its happy ending.

**"That You May Magnify God for Guiding You"**

The formula recurs where another pillar closes, and the occasion shows what it is for: *“so that you may complete the prescribed period and proclaim the greatness of Allah for guiding you, and perhaps you will be grateful”* (2:185). *Li-tukabbirū Allāha ʿalā mā hadākum* — the same words, closing Ramaḍān as they close the sacrifice. Fasting completed, pilgrimage offered, and both sealed with magnification *for the guidance* rather than for the strength to perform: the rite is received before it is performed, and the takbīr confesses the order.

**Good News to the Good-Doers**

*Wa-bashshir al-muḥsinīna* — and give good news to the good-doers. *Iḥsān* is the word for doing a thing well, for excellence that exceeds the requirement.

Sūrah al-Naḥl closes on the company the good-doers keep: *“Surely Allah is with those who shun evil and who do good ˹deeds˺”* (16:128). With-ness as the reward of *iḥsān* — not payment rendered but presence granted. The good news of verse 37 is that presence announced in advance: the slaughter done properly, for the right reason, with the meat reaching its people, places the doer where God is with him, which is the only arrival the verse promises.

Sūrah Fāṭir names what does ascend, and the cargo is exactly what verse 37 allows: *“To Him ˹alone˺ good words ascend, and righteous deeds are raised up by Him”* (35:10).

## Verse 22:38

> Indeed, Allah defends those who believe. Surely Allah does not like whoever is deceitful, ungrateful

**"God Loves Not Every Treacherous Ingrate"**

The same verdict is pronounced where treachery is most tempting — in the handling of treaties with a party expected to break them: *“And if you ˹O Prophet˺ see signs of betrayal by a people, respond by openly terminating your treaty with them. Surely Allah does not like those who betray”* (8:58). Even anticipating the other side's betrayal does not license one's own; the instruction is to end openly, in daylight, rather than to answer secret breach with secret breach. Verse 38 is that ruling stated as a principle before the permission arrives.

**The Root Shared With Verse 40**

The word used here reappears two verses later in the famous statement that had God not repelled some people by means of others, monasteries and synagogues and mosques would have been destroyed.

The principle is stated as general history in Sūrah al-Baqarah, in the aftermath of David and Goliath: *“Had Allah not repelled a group of people by ˹the might of˺ another, corruption would have dominated the earth, but Allah is Gracious to all”* (2:251). The same *dafʿ*, the same unnamed agents, the same counterfactual — what is preserved there is the earth from corruption, what is preserved here is the believers from their persecutors and worship from destruction.

*Inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu kulla khawwānin kafūr* — God does not love any treacherous, ungrateful person. Two vices are named in one phrase, and they belong together.

*Khawwān* is the intensive form: a person given to betrayal, for whom treachery is a habit rather than a lapse. *Kafūr* is likewise intensive — not merely ungrateful but a denier of what he has been given. The pairing describes a character rather than an act: the man who betrays trusts and then denies that he was ever trusted with anything.

Sūrah al-Māʾidah states the boundary in its most demanding form: *“Do not let the hatred of a people lead you to injustice. Be just! That is closer to righteousness”* (5:8). The hatred is not denied — the believers hated their persecutors with reason — but it is disqualified as a ground for action. That is the same structure as verses 38 and 39 taken together: the injury is real, the permission is granted, and the hatred the injury produced is not permitted to write the rules of the response.

**Treachery Has No Good Ends**

The tradition recorded a corresponding image for the Day of Resurrection, and the report al-Qurṭubī cites is a well-attested one. ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said: "A flag will be fixed on the Day of Resurrection for every betrayer, and it will be announced, 'This is the betrayal of so-and-so, the son of so-and-so.'" It is recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (6178, and again at 6966).

The image is exact. Betrayal is a hidden act — that is what makes it betrayal — and the punishment announced for it is that it will be made public, with the betrayer's name and his father's name attached to it. The thing he did in secret becomes the thing he is known by in front of everyone.

The prohibition's positive form is the keeping of pledges: *“Honour Allah's covenant when you make a pledge, and do not break your oaths after confirming them, having made Allah your guarantor”* (16:91).

## Verse 22:39

> Permission ˹to fight back˺ is ˹hereby˺ granted to those being fought, for they have been wronged. And Allah is truly Most Capable of helping them ˹prevail˺

**The Verse That Changed the Community's Posture**

*Udhina li-alladhīna yuqātalūna bi-annahum ẓulimū* — permission is granted to those who are being fought, because they have been wronged.

Some place its revelation at the point of the Hijrah itself, and al-Qurṭubī records that when Abū Bakr heard it he remarked that he had known all along that combat would eventually be permitted. Al-Ṭabarī connects it instead with a party of believers who set out to leave Makkah, were intercepted and turned back by their persecutors, and were then granted leave to resist.

**The Grammar of Permission**

*Udhina* is a passive verb with no agent named. Nobody is doing the permitting in the sentence, which is the standard Qur'anic way of attributing an authorisation to God without making Him the subject of a finite verb.

The word matters more than it looks. What is granted is permission — *idhn* — not obligation and not enthusiasm. A command would have made fighting a duty of the same order as the prayer; a mere concession would have implied it was tolerated but regrettable. Permission sits precisely between them: it is lawful, it is not mandatory on every individual, and it is not celebrated.

That is the register the whole passage keeps. The sūrah has just forbidden treachery (22:38 — *“Indeed, Allah defends those who believe. Surely Allah does not like whoever is deceitful, ungrateful”*) and will immediately state the grievance that justifies the fighting (22:40 — *“˹They are˺ those who have been expelled from their homes for no reason other than proclaiming: “Our Lord is Allah.” Had Allah not repelled ˹the aggression of˺ some people by means of others, destruction would have surely claimed monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which Allah’s Name is often mentioned. Allah will certainly help those who stand up for Him. Allah is truly All-Powerful, Almighty”*) and the conditions attached to the power it may produce (22:41 — *“˹They are˺ those who, if established in the land by Us, would perform prayer, pay alms-tax, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil. And with Allah rests the outcome of all affairs”*). Nothing here is a licence.

**"Those Who Are Being Fought"**

The participle is passive: *yuqātalūna*, those who are fought against.

This is why the verse is so often cited in discussions of the Islamic law of war. The permission is not attached to disbelief, to proximity, or to any quality of the opponent.

The restriction to the party that was attacked is stated as law elsewhere, and the wording leaves no room for extension: *“Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you, but do not exceed the limits. Surely Allah does not like transgressors”* (2:190). The permission of this verse and the limit of that one are the same ruling seen from two sides: the object of force may respond, and the response has a ceiling.

**Why the Permission Falls Inside a Pilgrimage Sūrah**

The placement is worth a moment, because it looks like a digression and is not. This is the sūrah of the pilgrimage — the sūrah that was named for the rite its audience had been prevented from performing, and that declared the Sacred Mosque open to resident and visitor alike (22:25 — *“Indeed, those who persist in disbelief and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah and from the Sacred Mosque—which We have appointed for all people, residents and visitors alike—along with whoever intends to deviate by doing wrong in it, We will cause them to taste a painful punishment”*).

The sūrah has already said what the Mosque is for: it was made open to the resident and the visitor alike, and whoever intends to profane it in deviation will be made to taste a painful punishment (22:25 — *“Indeed, those who persist in disbelief and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah and from the Sacred Mosque—which We have appointed for all people, residents and visitors alike—along with whoever intends to deviate by doing wrong in it, We will cause them to taste a painful punishment”*). Those are the terms in which the fighting is authorised. The people who were kept out of the House by force are being permitted to remove the force, and nothing broader than that is licensed by the verse.

## Verse 22:40

> ˹They are˺ those who have been expelled from their homes for no reason other than proclaiming: “Our Lord is Allah.” Had Allah not repelled ˹the aggression of˺ some people by means of others, destruction would have surely claimed monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which Allah’s Name is often mentioned. Allah will certainly help those who stand up for Him. Allah is truly All-Powerful, Almighty

**The Grievance, Stated in One Sentence**

*Alladhīna ukhrijū min diyārihim bi-ghayri ḥaqqin illā a yaqūlū rabbunā Allāh* — those who were driven out of their homes without any right, except that they say, "Our Lord is God.".

The same expulsion was plotted against the Prophet himself, and Sūrah al-Anfāl names all three options his opponents weighed: *“when the disbelievers conspired to capture, kill, or exile you”* (8:30). Capture, killing, exile — the sentence of verse 40 records which of the three was carried out against the community. The verse's *illā* — for nothing except saying *our Lord is God* — is what turns the exile from a political fact into the pattern injury: no crime preceded it, so no justification can follow it.

**The Expulsion Was the Grievance the Qur'an Cited Most**

The commentators note that this grievance is among the most frequently cited in the Qur'an against the Quraysh. The same expulsion is named in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where it is the ground of the permission to fight: *“Kill them wherever you come upon them and drive them out of the places from which they have driven you out. For persecution is far worse than killing”* (2:191).

Sūrah al-Baqarah ranks the expulsion above fighting in the sacred months, in a verse that weighs the two directly: *“hindering ˹others˺ from the Path of Allah, rejecting Him, and expelling the worshippers from the Sacred Mosque is ˹a˺ greater ˹sin˺ in the sight of Allah. For persecution is far worse than killing”* (2:217). The expulsion from the Mosque is not only listed among the injuries; it is placed on the scale against a violation the audience already regarded as grave, and found heavier. That ranking is what makes verse 40's grievance the ground of verse 39's permission.

**"Had God Not Repelled Some People by Others"**

*Wa-law-lā dafʿu Allāhi al-nāsa baʿḍahum bi-baʿḍin* — and were it not for God's repelling of people, some of them by means of others.

Sūrah Hūd states the counterfactual from the other side — what happens where no restraint exists: *“If only there had been among the ˹destroyed˺ peoples before you virtuous individuals who forbade corruption in the land — other than the few We had saved”* (11:116). The destroyed peoples are described as lacking exactly what verse 40 describes God supplying: a check, a resistance, a party that pushes back. Where the repelling is absent the corruption runs to its end; where it is present — through whomsoever God wills — monasteries and mosques alike survive it.

**"God Will Help Those Who Help Him"**

*Wa-la-yanṣuranna Allāhu man yanṣuruhu* — God will certainly help whoever helps Him.

Sūrah Muḥammad states the same promise as a direct address to the believers, with the mechanism named: *“O believers! If you stand up for Allah, He will help you and make your steps firm”* (47:7). Standing up, help given, steps made firm — the promise of verse 40 with its middle term supplied. The help is not described as victory in the field but as firmness of step, which is the form divine help takes for a community that has just been told its victory is God's to give (22:39 — *“Permission ˹to fight back˺ is ˹hereby˺ granted to those being fought, for they have been wronged. And Allah is truly Most Capable of helping them ˹prevail˺”*) and its defence is God's own act (22:38 — *“Indeed, Allah defends those who believe. Surely Allah does not like whoever is deceitful, ungrateful”*).

The general reading of *masājid* has a verse that supports it directly: *“The places of worship are ˹only˺ for Allah, so do not invoke anyone besides Him”* (72:18).

## Verse 22:41

> ˹They are˺ those who, if established in the land by Us, would perform prayer, pay alms-tax, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil. And with Allah rests the outcome of all affairs

**Power Is Described as Something Granted**

*Alladhīna in makkannahum fī al-arḍi* — those who, if We establish them in the land. The verb is *makkannā*, from *makkana*: to give someone a firm place, to put him in a position where he can act.

Joseph is the Qur'an's worked example of the same verb: *“This is how We established Joseph in the land to settle wherever he pleased”* (12:56). A man sold into slavery, imprisoned on a false charge, and then placed where he could act — the establishment arrives as a grant after utter powerlessness, not as the consolidation of a victory. The verse's closing clause is the one this sūrah's audience needed: the reward of the good-doers is never discounted, which means the interval of powerlessness was not a loss.

**The Sentence Is a Conditional, Not a Prediction**

The conditional has a counterpart in Sūrah al-Nūr, where the same establishment is stated as a promise with its condition attached: *“Allah has promised those of you who believe and do good that He will certainly make them successors in the land … ˹provided that˺ they worship Me, associating nothing with Me”* (24:55). Promise and condition travel together — succession granted, worship stated. Verse 41 here is the same structure with the four acts filling in what the worship looks like once the promise is kept.

**The Fourth Item Is the Dangerous One**

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān does not lay the fourth item on every individual as a personal license. It lays it on *a group among* the community: *“Let there be a group among you who call ˹others˺ to goodness, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil — it is they who will be successful”* (3:104).

**Who Is Being Described**

The commentators proposed several referents. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī took the verse as describing the conditions laid on whoever God establishes in the land, meaning those given sovereignty. Others took it as a description of the Emigrants specifically, or of the Muslims generally.

The conditional reading has a named precedent. David was told what his establishment was for in the same terms: *“O David! We have surely made you an authority in the land, so judge between people with truth. And do not follow ˹your˺ desires or they will lead you astray from Allah's Way”* (38:26). Authority granted, purpose stated, desire warned against — the three elements of verse 41 in a single address to a king. Whoever holds power stands where David stood, under the same instruction.

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān makes the same acts the definition of the community's rank: *“You are the best community ever raised for humanity — you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah”* (3:110).

Moses stated the same closing clause to a people with no power at all: *“Seek Allah's help and be patient. Indeed, the earth belongs to Allah ˹alone˺. He grants it to whoever He chooses of His servants. The ultimate outcome belongs ˹only˺ to the righteous”* (7:128).

## Verse 22:42
> If they deny you ˹O Prophet˺, so did the people of Noah before them, as well as ˹the tribes of˺ ’Ȃd and Thamûd

**Denial Is Presented as the Pattern, Not the Emergency**

*Wa-in yukadhdhibūka fa-qad kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥin* — and if they deny you, the people of Noah denied before them.

That is the Qur'an's most consistent consolation to its messenger, and it appears in the same form elsewhere: *“If they deny you, so did those before them. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs, divine Books, and enlightening Scriptures”* (35:25). The point is never that rejection is easy. It is that rejection is what happens to messengers, and that a messenger who is being rejected is not failing because he is rejected.

Sūrah al-Anʿām adds what the precedent cost and what it produced: *“Indeed, messengers before you were rejected but patiently endured rejection and persecution until Our help came to them. And Allah's promise ˹to help˺ is never broken”* (6:34). Rejection, endurance, help — the three are presented as a sequence with the middle term supplied by the messenger. The consolation is therefore not that denial is unreal but that it is the first term of a completed pattern, and the help at the end of it is promised rather than merely hoped for.

**The List That Follows Is Arabian**

Sūrah Ibrāhīm uses the same opening for the same purpose: *“Have you not ˹already˺ received the stories of those who were before you: the people of Noah, ’Âd, Thamûd, and those after them? Only Allah knows how many they were”* (14:9).

Thamūd's dwellings were the most visible item on the route the audience travelled: *“Indeed, the residents of the Stone Valley also denied the messengers”* (15:80). The Stone Valley — al-Ḥijr, the carved facades the Syrian caravans passed — is named as a place rather than only a people, which is what makes the list unanswerable for its first hearers. Noah belongs to transmitted memory; ʿĀd and Thamūd belong to the road the merchants walked twice a year, with the houses of the destroyed still standing in the rock.

**What Was Denied Was a Messenger**

This matters for how the catalogue functions. The parallel is not between the Makkans' theology and the theology of ʿĀd; the parallel is between the Makkans' treatment of their messenger and the treatment of Hūd, Ṣāliḥ and Shuʿayb. Sūrah Hūd records what each of them said, and the message is identical in every case: *“And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd. He said, ‘O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him’”* (11:50).

Noah's sentence is recorded in the same words Hūd used: *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people. He said, ‘O my people! Worship Allah — you have no other god except Him’”* (7:59). The first messenger on the list and the Arabian ones spoke one sentence with different names attached, which is what makes the catalogue a single charge rather than nine different ones. What was denied in every case was the same claim from a brother's mouth — and what the Makkans were denying was that same sentence again.

**Why the Prophet Is Told This Now**

The delay is then given its universal form, and the verse that states it removes the last comfort of the denier: *“If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term”* (16:61). The silence the Makkans were enjoying was not a verdict in their favour but the same deferral extended to everyone, including the believers. To be inside the interval is not to be acquitted; it is to be given an appointed term, which expires whether or not anyone is watching the clock.

## Verse 22:43

> the people of Abraham, the people of Lot

The phrase *qawmu Ibrāhīma* — the people of Abraham — is the standard Qur'anic formula for the community a messenger was sent to, and its assumptions repay attention.

**Lot's People and the Nature of Their Refusal**

Lot's community is the one item on the list whose specific crime is reported in detail elsewhere in the Qur'an, and it was not idolatry in the ordinary sense.

The destruction is described with a specificity the catalogue form deliberately withholds here: *“And We turned the cities ˹of Sodom and Gomorrah˺ upside down and rained upon them stones of baked clay”* (15:74). Inversion from below and bombardment from above — the town assaulted in both directions at once. The compression of verse 43 is doing the opposite work: it reduces that detailed catastrophe to two words of a list, because the catalogue's argument runs on number rather than on horror.

**What "His People" Means for the Verdict**

The rule is stated as a principle of revelation itself: *“We have not sent a messenger except in the language of his people to clarify ˹the message˺ for them”* (14:4). Language here means more than vocabulary — it means the whole shared world of kinship, custom and reference inside which a claim can be understood. That is what makes the rejection on this list a refusal rather than a misunderstanding: each community was addressed in the phrase it thought in, by a man it had watched grow up.

**Every Messenger on This List Was an Insider**

One feature runs through the whole catalogue and is easy to miss because the verses are so compressed. Every messenger named was sent to *his own* people, and the Qur'an is careful to say so.

*“And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ”* (7:73). *“And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd”* (11:50). The word translated "brother" is used repeatedly and with emphasis: Shuʿayb is called the brother of the people of Midian (7:85 — *“And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib.”*), and the pattern holds for every community on the list.

That is precisely the objection the Qur'an records against Muhammad ﷺ — that he was a man like them, that he ate and walked in the market, and that no angel had been sent down with him. The catalogue's answer is that this has always been the arrangement.

**Why the Kaʿbah's Neighbours Should Have Recognised This**

One further consideration. Abraham's people and Lot's people were the communities nearest to the audience's own geography, and their ruins were known. ʿĀd and Thamūd were in the peninsula; Lot's towns were on the trade route to Syria that Makkah's caravans used every year.

Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt makes the geographical point explicit, and it is addressed to the same audience: *“You ˹Meccans˺ certainly pass by their ruins day and night. Will you not then understand?”* (37:137–138). Twice daily — the caravans went out in the morning and came back at night past the same remains. The ruins were not a destination requiring a journey but a sightline on the commute, which is why the verse treats the failure as one of understanding rather than of information.

The household link is stated as a conversion: *“So Lot believed in him. And Abraham said, ‘I am emigrating ˹in obedience˺ to my Lord’”* (29:26).

## Verse 22:44

> and the residents of Midian. And Moses was denied ˹too˺. But I delayed ˹the fate of˺ the disbelievers ˹until their appointed time˺ then seized them. And how severe was My response

**Midian and Its Messenger**

The catalogue closes with the people of Midian, to whom the Qur'an sent Shuʿayb. The account is preserved in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf: *“And to the people of Midian We sent their brother Shu’aib. He said, ‘O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has already come to you from your Lord. So give just measure and weight, do not defraud people of their property, nor spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order’”* (7:85).

**"But I Granted the Disbelievers Respite"**

The sūrah has been preparing this since verse 1. The Hour is *coming* (22:7 — *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves”*) rather than arrived; Iblīs was *delayed* in the parallel sūrahs; the man who wanted the punishment hurried is told in the next verse that a day with God is a thousand years. Delay is the sūrah's most consistent claim about how God runs the world, and this verse states it as a matter of history.

**"Then I Seized Them"**

*Thumma akhadhtuhum* — then I took them. The verb is plain and physical, and *thumma* places it after an interval of unspecified length.

The suddenness is the point, and the Qur'an states it as a rule rather than as a detail of these nine cases: *“˹Imagine˺ how many societies We have destroyed! Our torment took them by surprise ˹while sleeping˺ at night or midday”* (7:4). The seizure arrives in the middle of ordinary life, at the hour when a community is least expecting it — which is what makes the interval preceding it so misleading to the people inside it. Length followed by suddenness is one pattern, not two events.

**"And How Was My Denial"**

Both senses are present in the Arabic, and the ambiguity is a deliberate piece of lexical justice. They denied; the response is described with the same root. The punishment is named as a matching refusal rather than as an unrelated act of force. The same closing question appears elsewhere in the Qur'an in the same form, which confirms that the phrase is a fixed formula: *“Then I seized those who persisted in disbelief. How severe was My response!”* (35:26). The whole catalogue of verses 42 to 44 comes down to two verbs at the end: *amlaytu*, I gave rein, and *akhadhtu*, I took.

## Verse 22:45

> Many are the societies We have destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, leaving them in total ruin. ˹Many are˺ also the abandoned wells and lofty palaces

**The Argument Is Empirical**

Sūrah al-Rūm draws the conclusion the Qur'an draws from this evidence: *“Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them? They were far superior in might; they cultivated the land and developed it more than these ˹Meccans˺ ever have. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs. Allah would have never wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves”* (30:9).

**"How Many a Town"**

*Wa-ka-ayyin min qaryatin ahlaknāhā* — and how many a town did We destroy. The construction *ka-ayyin* is the Arabic phrase for an uncounted large number, and it is always used in the Qur'an to introduce a claim that could be documented but will not be enumerated.

**"Fallen Upon Its Roofs"**

The ruined town is used elsewhere as the site of a resurrection argument, and the picture is the same one: *“Or ˹are you not aware of˺ the one who passed by a city which was in ruins. He wondered, ‘How could Allah bring this back to life after its destruction?’”* (2:259). What the ruins provoke in both places is the same question about whether what is over is finally over — and in this sūrah the answer is directed at the living rather than the dead.

The Qur'an's appeal here is to evidence, and it is worth being precise about what kind.

**Why the Ruins Did Not Settle the Question**

The next verse supplies the diagnosis, and it is the harder claim of the two. The ruins are on the trade routes and everyone has seen them; what is missing is not evidence but the faculty that reads it. Sūrah Muḥammad puts the same question in the same words, and the complaint is that the travelling happened and the learning did not: *“Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those before them? Allah annihilated them, and a similar fate awaits the disbelievers”* (47:10).

## Verse 22:46

> Have they not travelled throughout the land so their hearts may reason, and their ears may listen? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind

**The Question Assumes the Answer Is No**

*A-fa-lam yasīrū fī al-arḍi* — have they not travelled in the land? The interrogative expects a negative answer only in the rhetorical sense: of course they have travelled. The Quraysh were merchants; their caravans went to Syria in the summer and to Yemen in the winter, and the routes passed directly through the ruins of the communities named in the previous verses.

The audience's travelling was not incidental to them; it was the basis of their prosperity. A sūrah of the Qur'an is addressed to Quraysh precisely in those terms — their security and their two annual caravans, to Yemen in the winter and to Syria in the summer (106:1–2 — *“secure in their trading caravan ˹to Yemen˺ in the winter and ˹Syria˺ in the summer”*). The routes those caravans took ran past the ruins named in verses 42 to 44. The question therefore lands on people who had passed the evidence twice a year for their entire commercial lives and had drawn nothing from it.

**"It Is Not the Eyes That Go Blind"**

*Fa-innahā lā taʿmā al-abṣāru* — for it is not the eyesights that are blind. The word *abṣār* is the plural of *baṣar*, eyesight or insight, and the verse denies the defect there.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf states the condition in its fullest form, and the organ is the same one: *“Indeed, We have destined many jinn and humans for Hell. They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with. They are like cattle”* (7:179). The failure is not a missing faculty but an unused one, which is why the same sūrahs can describe these people as having hearts, eyes and ears and still say that they do not perceive.

**"But the Hearts Which Are in the Breasts"**

*Wa-lākin taʿmā al-qulūbu allatī fī al-ṣudūr* — but blind are the hearts that are in the breasts.

The same insistence appears in a well-known ḥadīth. Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr reported that the Prophet ﷺ said: "In the body there is a piece of flesh. If it is sound, the entire body is sound, and if it is corrupt, the entire body is corrupt — it is the heart." It is recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (52) and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (1599). The report's logic is the verse's logic: the condition of the whole person follows the condition of a particular organ.

The question the Qur'an poses about the Qur'an is posed in the same vocabulary, and it places the fault in the same organ: *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Or are there locks upon their hearts?”* (47:24). The lock image and the blindness image are the same diagnosis from two directions — the apparatus is intact and the reception is not happening, so the defect must be in the receiver.

**What the Verse Requires of a Traveller**

The sūrahs that repeat this appeal frame it identically, as a demand to see *the end of those before them* rather than merely their remains: *“Say, ‘Travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers’”* (6:11). *“Similar situations came to pass before you, so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers”* (3:137). The object of the seeing is always a conclusion, never a sight.

## Verse 22:47

> They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment. And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting

**The Taunt**

*Wa-yastaʿjilūnaka bi-l-ʿadhābi* — and they ask you to hurry the punishment. The verb is the intensive form of "to hasten": they keep asking, and the asking is a form of mockery rather than a request for information.

The demand is recorded elsewhere in the same words, and in Makkah it was once phrased as a prayer: *"O Allah! If this is indeed the truth from You, then rain down stones upon us from the sky or overcome us with a painful punishment"* (8:32). Sūrah Yūnus supplies the answer the demand invites: *"Imagine if His torment were to overcome you by night or day—do the wicked realize what they are ˹really˺ asking Him to hasten? Will you believe in it only after it has overtaken you? Now? But you always wanted to hasten it!"* (10:50–51). The reply exposes the request as something other than a request for evidence. A punishment that arrives is the end of the period in which evidence could be answered at all, and the taunt is therefore a demand that the testing be brought to a close.

**"A Day With Your Lord Is Like a Thousand Years"**

The commentators proposed several referents. Some took the day as one of the six days of creation mentioned elsewhere in the Qur'an, comparing it with the statement that a day with God measures a thousand years by human reckoning (32:5 — *“He conducts every affair from the heavens to the earth, then it all ascends to Him on a Day whose length is a thousand years by your counting”*). Others took it as one of the days of the Hereafter, meaning the Day of Resurrection or the day of reckoning itself. Al-Rāzī recorded a further reading: that a day of punishment will *feel* to the sufferer like a thousand years, so the delay is not the point — the duration, once it begins, is.

The figure has a larger neighbour that the commentators had to reconcile with it: *"˹through which˺ the angels and the ˹holy˺ spirit will ascend to Him on a Day fifty thousand years in length"* (70:4). Their answers differed — that the thousand and the fifty thousand describe different days, or different experiences of one day, or the reckoning as a whole against a single stage of it — but they agreed on what the numbers are doing. Neither is being offered as a measurement.

**The Taunt's Own Logic**

The sūrah has already said as much about the Hour: once the signs arrive, believing does nothing for a person who did not believe before (6:158 — *“On the Day your Lord’s signs arrive, belief will not benefit those who did not believe earlier or those who did no good through their faith.”*). The demand to hurry the punishment is, structurally, a demand that God settle the question in a way that removes the need for faith — and the whole design of the testing period is that the question is *not* settled that way.

The delay the mockers cite against the Prophet is the same delay that keeps them alive to object. *"If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term"* (16:61). The respite is not a gap in the system; it is the system. Every day of it is a day in which the objection can still be withdrawn, and the taunt is an argument against the very arrangement that makes the taunter's existence possible.

**What the Verse Does for the Prophet**

What he is given is not a date. It would be a misreading to hear the verse as "wait a little longer," because nothing in it promises that the wait will be short measured in his years — and the sūrah will go on to describe a community that emigrates, fights. Is tested long before it returns to the sanctuary (22:39–41 — *“Permission ˹to fight back˺ is ˹hereby˺ granted to those being fought, for they have been wronged. And Allah is truly Most Capable of helping them ˹prevail˺”*, 22:58 — *“As for those who emigrate in the cause of Allah and then are martyred or die, Allah will indeed grant them a good provision. Surely Allah is the Best Provider”*).

The same claim about God appears on the other side of the argument, in the prayer of the believers: *"Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity for the ˹promised˺ Day—about which there is no doubt. Surely Allah does not break His promise"* (3:9).

## Verse 22:48

> Many are the societies whose end We delayed while they did wrong, then seized them. And to Me is the final return

**The Verse Restates Verse 45 With One Word Changed**

*Wa-ka-ayyin min qaryatin amlaytu lahā wa-hiya ẓālimatun thumma akhadhtuhā* — and how many a town did I grant respite while it was doing wrong, then I seized it.

The sentence is almost identical to verse 45, and the difference is one verb. Verse 45 said *ahlaknāhā*, We destroyed it. Verse 48 says *amlaytu lahā*, I gave it rein. The destruction is still there, at the end, but the emphasis has moved from the end to the interval.

**Delay as a Deliberate Act**

Sūrah al-Anʿām states the mechanism: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair!”* (6:44). The gifts keep arriving, the warnings stop feeling urgent, and the recipient concludes that the silence of heaven is a verdict.

**"Then I Seized It"**

*Thumma akhadhtuhā* — then I took it. As in verse 44, the verb is plain and the *thumma* marks an interval whose length is not stated.

The pairing of the two clauses is the whole doctrine: a long silence, then a single action. The sūrah is describing the same pattern it has described three times already — in the respite granted to the deniers of verse 44, in the Hour that is coming rather than arrived (22:7 — *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves”*), and now here.

## Verse 22:49

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O humanity! I am only sent to you with a clear warning

**The Prophet Is Told What to Say About Himself**

*Qul yā ayyuhā al-nāsu innamā anā lakum nadhīrun mubīn* — Say: O humanity, I am only, for you, a clear warner.

The verse begins with the imperative *qul*, say. The Qur'an uses this formula when it wants the Prophet to announce something in the first person, and what he is told to announce here is a description of his own office rather than a doctrine.

The same imperative opens the comparable definitions elsewhere, and the repetition tells the reader that this is a fixed reply to a fixed objection. When the disbelievers ask why no sign comes down, the answer given to the Prophet is: *"You ˹O Prophet˺ are only a warner. And every people had a guide"* (13:7). When he is told to state the limits of his own knowledge, the answer takes the same shape: *"Say, 'I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the Will of Allah… I am only a warner and deliverer of good news for those who believe'"* (7:188). The office is defined by the imperative in every case, as though the definition were something the Prophet needed to hear as much as his opponents did.

**What a Warner Is Not**

The Qur'an states the exemption in so many words: *"We have surely sent you with the truth ˹O Prophet˺ as a deliverer of good news and a warner. And you will not be accountable for the residents of the Hellfire"* (2:119).

**Why the Warning Must Be "Clear"**

*Mubīn* means clear, evident, manifest — the same adjective the sūrah used for the verses sent down in 22:16 — *“And so We revealed this ˹Quran˺ as clear verses. And Allah certainly guides whoever He wills”* and for the scripture the disputant lacked in 22:8 — *“˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, guidance, or an enlightening scripture”*.

Clarity is a moral requirement in the Qur'an, not a stylistic preference. A warning that could reasonably be misunderstood leaves the person warned with an excuse, and the entire edifice of judgment requires that no one has one. The messengers were sent with clear proofs and clear warnings for exactly that reason.

**A Refrain on Many Messengers' Lips**

The formula is not unique to Muhammad ﷺ, and the Qur'an puts it into the mouths of the messengers whose communities were just listed. Noah says it, Hūd says it, Ṣāliḥ says it, Shuʿayb says it, and the Prophet is told to say it in the same words.

The same unity is stated from God's side in the verse that gives the purpose of sending them at all: *"We have sent messengers only as deliverers of good news and as warners. Whoever believes and does good, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve"* (6:48). The two functions are named together there, as they are in 7:188 — *“I am only a warner and deliverer of good news for those who believe.””*, and the pairing is the reason the office has the shape it has: good news can be refused, and a warning can be heeded, and neither of those is possible unless the recipient is left free.

**The Address Is the Widest One Available**

The same formula with which this sūrah opened (22:1 — *“O humanity! Fear your Lord, for the ˹violent˺ quaking at the Hour is surely a dreadful thing”*), and it is not the formula most of the Medinan passages use.

It is also consistent with the verse that appears earlier in this sūrah in which God is described as judging between all the communities by name (22:17 — *“Indeed, the believers, Jews, Sabians, Christians, Magi, and the polytheists—Allah will judge between them ˹all˺ on Judgment Day. Surely Allah is a Witness over all things”*). The message goes out to the same breadth as the judgment will cover.

## Verse 22:50

> So those who believe and do good will have forgiveness and an honourable provision

**"A Noble Provision"**

*Wa-rizqun karīm* — and a noble provision. The adjective is *karīm*, a word with a wide field: generous, noble, honourable, plentiful, fine.

The formula is not the sūrah's own. It appears in almost these words in Sūrah al-Anfāl, attached to a description of the believers' character: *“It is they who are the true believers. They will have elevated ranks, forgiveness, and a honourable provision from their Lord”* (8:4). The recurrence is what settles the meaning: this is the standing vocabulary of the reward, and *karīm* is doing the same work in both places — describing the standing of the recipient as much as the size of the gift.

**Provision, Not Wages**

That distinction carries weight in a sūrah that has been insistent about grace. Verse 14 said God *admits* the believers to the Gardens; verse 23 said they are *adorned*, passively; and this verse offers provision rather than remuneration. Nothing in the sūrah's vocabulary of reward suggests a transaction that God is settling.

The word's range elsewhere confirms the reading. Provision in the Qur'an is what God gives to creatures who have done nothing to earn it: *“There is no moving creature on earth whose provision is not guaranteed by Allah”* (11:6). To make the reward *rizq* rather than a wage is to place the believers' recompense in the same category as the sustenance of everything that lives — given, guaranteed, and not negotiated.

There is a third omission worth naming. The verse does not say that the two halves come separately, or that the provision is deferred in its entirety to the Hereafter. Read in a sūrah that has just promised to settle the believers in a land and establish their religion (22:41 — *“˹They are˺ those who, if established in the land by Us, would perform prayer, pay alms-tax, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil.”*), the promise sits naturally across both lives, and the sūrah is content to leave the dating open.

## Verse 22:51

> But those who strive to discredit Our revelations, they will be the residents of the Hellfire.”

**Why Effort Is the Aggravating Factor**

The word appears elsewhere in exactly this construction, and the parallel confirms the second reading: *"As for those who strive to discredit Our revelations, it is they who will suffer the ˹worst˺ torment of agonizing pain"* (34:5).

**Not Disbelief but Campaigning**

*Wa-alladhīna saʿaw fī āyātinā muʿājizīna* — and those who strove against Our signs, seeking to frustrate.

The same imperative is addressed to the believers about the Friday prayer: *"O believers! When the call to prayer is made on Friday, then proceed ˹diligently˺ to the remembrance of Allah and leave off ˹your˺ business"* (62:9).

**What the Campaign Looked Like**

The Qur'an records the specific tactics, and they are worth listing because they are not what modern readers expect. The opponents did not typically argue that God does not exist. They disputed *about the signs* (40:35 — *“those who dispute Allah’s signs with no proof given to them. How despicable is that for Allah and the believers! This is how Allah seals the heart of every arrogant tyrant.””*), they plotted against them (10:21 — *“When We give people a taste of mercy after being afflicted with a hardship, they swiftly devise plots against Our revelations! Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah is swifter in devising ˹punishment˺. Surely Our messenger-angels record whatever you devise.””*), and they deviated about them (41:40 — *“Indeed, those who abuse Our revelations are not hidden from Us. Who is better: the one who will be cast into the Fire or the one who will be secure on Judgment Day? Do whatever you want. He is certainly All-Seeing of what you do”*).

Two further tactics are recorded from Makkah, and both are tries to change the terms of the exchange rather than to answer it. One was to ask for the message itself to be revised: *"Bring us a different Quran or make some changes in it"* (10:15) — a request framed as a negotiation, to which the Prophet was told to answer that he does not alter what is revealed to him. Another was to claim the ability to match it and then decline to bother: *"We have already heard ˹the recitation˺. If we wanted, we could have easily produced something similar. This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but ancient fables!"* (8:31). The claim of ability is unfalsifiable by design, and the demand for a spring to gush from the earth (17:90–93 — *“They challenge ˹the Prophet˺, “We will never believe in you until you cause a spring to gush forth from the earth for us”*) belongs to the same family: conditions set so that the sign, if given, would be reclassified as well.

**"The Companions of the Fire"**

*Ulāʾika aṣḥābu al-jaḥīmi* — those are the companions of the Fire.

The Qur'an uses the same noun for both destinations in a single comparison, and the inequality it draws is between the two companies rather than between two locations: *"The residents of the Fire cannot be equal to the residents of Paradise. ˹Only˺ the residents of Paradise will be successful"* (59:20). Elsewhere the companionship is stated with the adverb that settles the question: *"But those who disbelieve and deny Our signs will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever"* (2:39). Belonging is permanent on both sides, and the sūrah's point is that the permanence is what the striving was building all along.

The sūrah has already graded offences by whether they stay private, and it does so again here. Verse 9 described people who disputed, and then described people who disputed *to lead others astray* — the purpose clause was what doubled the sentence.

The principle behind the grading is stated in a single line of Sūrah al-Najm, using the same root as this verse: *"and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards"* (53:39). What a person gets is what he worked for, and the work is counted as his own. That is why the verse can describe the punishment as the appropriate outcome rather than an arbitrary one: the strivers spent their effort making the signs ineffective, and what they receive is the one condition in which the signs are no longer available to be acted on.

## Verse 22:52

> Whenever We sent a messenger or a prophet before you ˹O Prophet˺ and he recited ˹Our revelations˺, Satan would influence ˹people’s understanding of˺ his recitation. But ˹eventually˺ Allah would eliminate Satan’s influence. Then Allah would ˹firmly˺ establish His revelations. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise

**What the Verse Actually Says**

*Wa-mā arsalnā min qablika min rasūlin wa-lā nabiyyin illā idhā tamannā alqā al-shayṭānu fī umniyyatihi* — We sent no messenger or prophet before you but that, when he *tamannā*, Satan cast something into his *umniyyah*.

The two words govern every reading of the verse. *Tamannā* means to long for, to wish, to desire. *Umiyyah*, from the same root, means a longing, a desire, or, in several Qur'anic occurrences, a false hope — as in the hearsay of 2:78 — *“And among them are the illiterate who know nothing about the Scripture except lies, and ˹so˺ they ˹wishfully˺ speculate”*, the wishes of 2:111 — *“The Jews and Christians each claim that none will enter Paradise except those of their own faith. These are their desires. Reply, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Show ˹me˺ your proof if what you say is true.””*, the desires of 4:119 — *“I will certainly mislead them and delude them with empty hopes. Also, I will order them and they will slit the ears of cattle and alter Allah’s creation.” And whoever takes Satan as a guardian instead of Allah has certainly suffered a tremendous loss”*, and the false hopes of 57:14 — *“But you chose to be tempted ˹by hypocrisy˺, ˹eagerly˺ awaited ˹our demise˺, doubted ˹the truth˺, and were deluded by false hopes until Allah’s decree ˹of your death˺ came to pass.”*. A secondary sense of "recitation" exists and is the one many commentators adopted, but it is rare and it cuts against the Qur'an's own usage of the term.

The translation used here takes *umniyyah* as the recitation the messenger delivered, and reads the casting as interference with how that recitation lands rather than with what was revealed.

**The Story of the Cranes**

No verse in this sūrah comes trailing a longer and more contested appendix. A number of early commentators connected this verse — along with Sūrah 53's verses on the goddesses and the passage at 17:73–75 — to an account known as the "story of the cranes," and in Western literature as the "Satanic Verses.".

The passage at Sūrah al-Isrāʾ that the account leans on says the opposite of what the account needs: *“They definitely ˹thought they˺ were about to lure you away from what We have revealed to you … hoping that you would attribute something else to Us falsely”* (17:73). *About* to lure — *la-kādū*, they nearly did — and the nearly is the whole point. The verse records a try that did not succeed, which is why it cannot support a story about an interpolation that did. What the Qur'an says its enemies almost achieved, the appendix claims they achieved; the two cannot both be read as history.

**Why the Account Fails**

*Theology.* The prophets are held to be protected from error of this kind. Al-Qurṭubī rejects the premise outright, citing Satan's own testimony: *“I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me”* (14:22). If Satan could seize a person's speech, no one could withstand it.

*Literary fit.* The verse's two halves are not joined in the manner the account requires. God first wipes out what Satan cast, and afterwards establishes His signs — so the establishing follows the erasure rather than being produced by it, and it attaches most naturally to verse 51: they labour to frustrate the signs, and God makes them firm. The alleged interpolation would also contradict the sūrah it supposedly entered, which argues that intercession belongs to God alone (53:26 — *“˹Imagine˺ how many ˹noble˺ angels are in the heavens! ˹Even˺ their intercession would be of no benefit whatsoever, until Allah gives permission to whoever He wills and ˹only for the people He˺ approves”*).

Sūrah al-Anʿām states the standing feature as a rule about every prophet: *“And so We have made for every prophet enemies — devilish humans and jinn — whispering to one another with elegant words of deception”* (6:112).

The Qur'an describes the Prophet as grief-stricken at his people's refusal — *“Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial”* (18:6) — which is the condition this verse speaks to.

Sūrah al-Raʿd gives the erasure-and-establishment its parable, in foam and metal: *“The ˹worthless˺ residue is then cast away, but what benefits people remains on the earth”* (13:17).

## Verse 22:53

> All that so He may make Satan’s influence a trial for those ˹hypocrites˺ whose hearts are sick and those ˹disbelievers˺ whose hearts are hardened. Surely the wrongdoers are totally engrossed in opposition

**A Fitnah Is Not a Temptation Only**

The word *fitnah* covers trial, testing, temptation and sedition, and the Qur'an uses it for all of them. The root sense is of putting something to the test by fire — assaying metal to see what it is.

The word's other occurrences confirm that the fire in the root is the point. Persecution is called *fitnah* and declared worse than killing (2:191 — *“For persecution is far worse than killing.”*); war is to be waged *until there is no more fitnah* (8:39 — *“Fight against them until there is no more persecution—and ˹your˺ devotion will be entirely to Allah. But if they desist, then surely Allah is All-Seeing of what they do”*). In both, the trial is external pressure applied to a community to see what it is made of. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt applies the same word to the believers themselves: *"Do people think once they say, 'We believe,' that they will be left without being put to the test?"* (29:2). A *fitnah* is therefore not a trap set for the weak but the ordinary condition of a claim that has been announced; what differs here is that the pressure is an ambiguity in the revelation rather than a persecution outside it.

**"Those in Whose Hearts Is Sickness"**

The commentators understood it as covering hypocrisy, doubt, ignorance and the absence of settled faith. The usage supports them: *“There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase”* (2:10), with the same phrase applied elsewhere to the waverers (9:125 — *“But as for those with sickness in their hearts, it has increased them only in wickedness upon their wickedness, and they die as disbelievers”*; 33:12 — *“And ˹remember˺ when the hypocrites and those with sickness in their hearts said, “Allah and His Messenger have promised us nothing but delusion!””*) and to those who say one thing and believe another (47:20 — *“Yet when a precise sûrah is revealed, in which fighting is ˹explicitly˺ mentioned, you see those with sickness in their hearts staring at you like someone in the throes of death.”*).

The sickness also has a visible symptom, and the Qur'an names it: calculation. *"You see those with sickness in their hearts racing for their guardianship, saying ˹in justification˺, 'We fear a turn of fortune will strike us.' But perhaps Allah will bring about ˹your˺ victory or another favour by His command, and they will regret what they have hidden in their hearts"* (5:52). What is diseased is the faculty that weighs claims, so the person with the sickness divides his allegiances by expected outcome rather than by truth — which is precisely the disposition an ambiguous event will expose.

**"Those Whose Hearts Are Hardened"**

The sick heart is unsettled; the hard heart has settled, in the wrong direction. One wavers and the other has stopped moving. The Qur'an's vocabulary for hardness is precise: *“Can ˹the misguided be like˺ those whose hearts Allah has opened to Islam, so they are enlightened by their Lord? So woe to those whose hearts are hardened at the remembrance of Allah!”* (39:22). Hardness is defined by what it does not respond to.

Sūrah al-Ḥadīd gives the mechanism and the cure in one verse: *“Has the time not yet come for believers’ hearts to be humbled at the remembrance of Allah and what has been revealed of the truth. Not be like those given the Scripture before—˹those˺ who were spoiled for so long that their hearts became hardened?”* (57:16).

**"The Wrongdoers Are in Far Schism"**

The grammar turns the opposition into a location. They are *fī shiqāqin*, in a schism, not merely holding one: the fracture is the element they now occupy, and the preposition is the same one the verse used for the sickness in their hearts. The same construction elsewhere measures belief by its opposite: *"So if they believe in what you believe, then they will indeed be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, they are simply opposed ˹to the truth˺"* (2:137), where the Arabic has the same *fī shiqāqin*. Opposition in this usage is not an argument a person makes; it is a position he stands in, and the distance is measured from the truth rather than from the community he is arguing with.

**Why a Test Is Not a Trap**

The mechanism is not unique to this episode, and the Qur'an describes it for revelation as a whole: *"Whoever Allah wills to guide, He opens their heart to Islam. But whoever He wills to leave astray, He makes their chest tight and constricted as if they were climbing up into the sky"* (6:125).

## Verse 22:54

> ˹This is˺ also so that those gifted with knowledge would know that this ˹revelation˺ is the truth from your Lord, so they have faith in it, and so their hearts would submit humbly to it. And Allah surely guides the believers to the Straight Path

**Who "Those Given Knowledge" Are**

The Qur'an uses this phrase with a consistent profile, and it is worth assembling.

They are the ones who recognise the message when they hear it: *“Indeed, when it is recited to those who were gifted with knowledge before it ˹was revealed˺, they fall upon their faces in prostration”* (17:107). They are contrasted with people who prefer this life: *“But those gifted with knowledge said, ‘Shame on you! Allah’s reward is far better for those who believe and do good’”* (28:80). The revelation is described as residing in them: *“But this ˹Quran˺ is ˹a set of˺ clear revelations ˹preserved˺ in the hearts of those gifted with knowledge. And none denies Our revelations except the ˹stubborn˺ wrongdoers”* (29:49), and *“Those gifted with knowledge ˹clearly˺ see that what has been revealed to you from your Lord ˹O Prophet˺ is the truth”* (34:6).

**Knowledge Produces Humility, Not Certainty of a Different Kind**

*Fa-yuʾminū bihi fa-tukhbita lahu qulūbuhum* — and so they believe in it, and their hearts humble themselves to it.

The sequence is worth holding on to. Knowledge comes first, then faith, then humility — and the order rules out a familiar error.

Sūrah al-Anfāl defines the believer by the same sequence verse 54 traces: *“The ˹true˺ believers are only those whose hearts tremble at the remembrance of Allah, whose faith increases when His revelations are recited to them, and who put their trust in their Lord”* (8:2).

**Why the Same Event Produces Opposite Results**

That is also why the sūrah can hold the two verses together without tension. Nothing in verse 54 rescinds verse 53. Both are describing what a test does.

Sūrah al-Baqarah states the principle for parables, and it holds for every sign: *“Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious”* (2:26). The same parable strays and guides; the straying falls only on the rebellious, which means the outcome was in the recipient before it was in the sign. That is the mechanism verse 54 withholds and verse 53 implies: nothing is added to either party except the occasion, and the occasion reveals what each had been cultivating all along.

**The Verse Answers a Question the Sūrah Has Been Asked Twice**

Twice in this sūrah the Prophet's opponents have demanded something that would settle matters by force: the punishment, hurried up (22:47 — *“They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment. And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting”*), and now the interference that they hoped would discredit the message.

The demand for a settling sign has a history, and Sūrah al-Isrāʾ gives the reason it is refused: *“Nothing keeps Us from sending the ˹demanded˺ signs except that they had ˹already˺ been denied by earlier peoples. And We gave Thamūd the she-camel as a clear sign, but they wrongfully rejected it”* (17:59). The demanded demonstration was already tried — the she-camel, visible and undeniable — and the result was rejection followed by destruction. Verse 54's refusal belongs to that history: the interference is not removed because removal never settled anyone, and the knowledgeable are those who know without it.

Sūrah al-Isrāʾ states the two-directional effect as a rule about the revelation itself: *“We send down the Quran as a healing and mercy for the believers, but it only increases the wrongdoers in loss”* (17:82).

The guidance is described as increasing, not merely given: *“As for those who are ˹rightly˺ guided, He increases them in guidance and blesses them with righteousness”* (47:17).

## Verse 22:55

> Yet the disbelievers will persist in doubt about this ˹revelation˺ until the Hour takes them by surprise, or the torment of a terminating Day comes to them

**Doubt as a Permanent Posture**

*Wa-lā yazālu alladhīna kafarū fī miryatin minhu* — and those who disbelieve will not cease to be in doubt about it.

The same verb of unbroken continuance describes the human condition elsewhere, and the resemblance is exact: *“Had your Lord so willed, He would have certainly made humanity one single community ˹of believers˺, but they will always ˹choose to˺ differ”* (11:118). *Lā yazālūna* in both places — they will not cease.

**"Until the Hour Comes Upon Them Suddenly"**

The suddenness is stated as a rule about the Hour wherever the question of its timing is raised: *“It is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth and will only take you by surprise”* (7:187). *Baghtatan* in both verses — the same adverb, the same refusal of a schedule.

**"Or the Punishment of a Barren Day"**

The adjective has one other appearance as an instrument of divine action, and it is a wind: *“when We sent against them the devastating wind”* (51:41) — in the Arabic, *al-rīḥ al-ʿaqīm*, the barren wind. A wind that carries no rain, pollinates nothing, and leaves nothing growing after it passes: the same sterility, applied to a day. What makes both barren is not violence but aftermathlessness — the wind passes and nothing grows; the day comes and no next day repairs it.

**Two Arrivals, Not Two Alternatives**

Sūrah al-Qamar names the farther horizon in terms that match the nearer one: *“Better yet, the Hour is their appointed time — and the Hour will be most catastrophic and most bitter”* (54:46). *Mawʿid* — an appointment, set whether or not the party attends. The two arrivals in this verse are the same appointment viewed at two distances: a day in history that ends the doubters' calculation, and the Day that ends calculation itself. The disjunction is not offering a choice of fates; it is covering both horizons so that neither can be mistaken for an escape from the other.

**Where the Argument Has Arrived**

The arrival is then shown from the inside, at the moment the doubt finally breaks: *“But their faith was of no benefit to them when they saw Our torment. This has ˹always˺ been Allah's way ˹of dealing˺ with His ˹wicked˺ servants”* (40:85). The doubt ends exactly as verse 55 said it would — with the event, not with an argument — and the ending does the doubter no good, because the faith that the event produces is not the faith that was asked for. The sūrah's argument therefore closes the way it opened: the Hour was announced in verse 1, and the last word on those who doubted it is that they will go on doubting until it takes them.

## Verse 22:56

> All authority on that Day is for Allah ˹alone˺. He will judge between them. So those who believe and do good will be in the Gardens of Bliss

**Sovereignty on That Day**

*Al-mulku yawmaʾidhin lillāh* — the dominion on that Day belongs to God. The sentence is a noun sentence with a fronted predicate, which in Arabic gives it the force of exclusivity: the authority is God's, and on that Day it is visibly, undeniably His.

Sūrah al-Furqān states the formula with the attribute that decides its tone: *“True authority on that Day will belong ˹only˺ to the Most Compassionate. And it will be a hard day for the disbelievers”* (25:26). The Most Compassionate — *al-Raḥmān* — as the holder of undivided authority, and the hardness of the day falling only on one party. Verse 56's sovereignty is that sentence without the second clause: dominion God's, judgment His act, and the Gardens receiving those the compassion covers.

**The Same Claim Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The statement is one of the Qur'an's fixed formulae for the Resurrection, and the wording is nearly identical wherever it appears: *“the Day all will appear ˹before Allah˺. Nothing about them will be hidden from Him. ˹He will ask,˺ ‘Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme!’”* (40:16).

Sūrah al-Infiṭār states it with an added consequence: *“˹It is˺ the Day no soul will be of ˹any˺ benefit to another whatsoever, for all authority on that Day belongs to Allah ˹entirely˺”* (82:19). The two halves belong together. Human authority in this life includes the ability to intercede, to protect, to pull strings. On that day there is none of it, and the reason given is that the authority is not divided.

Sūrah al-Anʿām pairs the same claim with the act of creation itself: *“All authority is His ˹alone˺ on the Day the Trumpet will be blown. He is the Knower of all—seen or unseen”* (6:73).

**"He Will Judge Between Them"**

Sūrah al-Zumar stages the judgment verse 56 announces, with every element in place: *“The earth will shine with the light of its Lord, the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, the prophets and the witnesses will be brought forward — and judgment will be passed on all with fairness. None will be wronged”* (39:69). Light, record, witnesses, fairness — the full apparatus of the *yaḥkumu baynahum*. Verse 56 states the act in two words; the staging shows what the two words contain, and the closing *none will be wronged* is verse 10's verdict executed.

**The Gardens of Bliss**

*Fa-alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti fī jannāti al-naʿīm* — so those who believe and do sound deeds are in Gardens of bliss.

Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn gives the state its shortest description, as the standing of the righteous: *“Surely the virtuous will be in bliss”* (83:22). *Naʿīm* alone — no garden named, no river, no furnishing. The word by itself carries the whole condition: ease without edge, life without sharpness or scarcity. Verse 56's *jannāti al-naʿīm* adds the place to the state, but the state is what the word promises, and the sūrah is content to let it stand nearly bare.

Sūrah al-Rūm diagnoses the misreading the verse corrects: *“They ˹only˺ know the worldly affairs of this life, but are ˹totally˺ oblivious to the Hereafter”* (30:7).

Sūrah Yā-Sīn pictures the bodily reward in a single line of activity: *“Indeed, on that Day the residents of Paradise will be busy enjoying themselves”* (36:55).

## Verse 22:57

> But those who disbelieve and deny Our revelations, it is they who will suffer a humiliating punishment

**"A Humiliating Punishment"**

Sūrah al-Jāthiyah attaches the same adjective to the same offence: *“And whenever they learn anything of Our revelations, they make a mockery of it. It is they who will suffer a humiliating punishment”* (45:9). What was made small in public is made small in public; the *muhīn* answers the mockery measure for measure.

**The Second Half of the Judgment**

Verse 56 gave the authority, the judgment and the reward. Verse 57 gives the other outcome, and the two verses are one sentence in argument.

The authority under which the distribution is made is staged as a question with only one possible answer: *“˹He will ask,˺ ‘Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah — the One, the Supreme!’”* (40:16). The courtroom of verses 56 and 57 is the same scene — sovereignty announced, judgment executed, no second claimant present to object. What these two verses add to the sūrah's earlier descriptions of the two outcomes is exactly that setting: Gardens and Fire already described, now distributed by a voice that asks and answers in the same breath.

**Two Conditions Joined**

The double condition is a fixed formula, and Sūrah al-Taghābun states it with the outcome attached: *“As for those who disbelieve and reject Our revelations, they will be the residents of the Fire, staying there forever”* (64:10). State and act together — *kafarū wa-kadhdhabū* — in both places, which confirms that the verse is not describing two groups but one group under two descriptions. Refusal on the inside, denial uttered on the outside; the sūrah has been tracking both since verse 3, and here they are named together as the single criterion.

**Humiliation Is the Natural Answer to Arrogance**

The logic is stated as a general rule about pride and worship: *“Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled”* (40:60). Arrogance toward worship and humiliation in Hell are presented as cause and effect, not as coincidence — the refusal to be small before God produces smallness before everything. The sūrah's three figures of pride each refused a different kind of smallness — the shoulder turned, the rage at another's help, the striving against the signs — and the single adjective answers all three.

**The Verse's Pairing With 56**

The non-communal criterion is stated in its most explicit form in Sūrah al-Baqarah: *“Indeed, the believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians — whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good will have their reward with their Lord”* (2:62). Four communities named, and then the criterion cuts across all four — belief and good deeds, not membership. Verses 56 and 57 are that principle executed: the judgment verse 17 deferred is carried out here, and the names of the communities do not appear in it.

## Verse 22:58

> As for those who emigrate in the cause of Allah and then are martyred or die, Allah will indeed grant them a good provision. Surely Allah is the Best Provider

**"God Will Provide Them a Good Provision"**

The commentators differed about when this provision arrives. Many took it as the provision of the Garden, owed to the emigrant in the Hereafter. Others took it as a present provision — that the martyr is sustained now, in the interval between death and resurrection, rather than only after it. The second reading has a strong parallel in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān, which uses the same root: *"Never think of those martyred in the cause of Allah as dead. In fact, they are alive with their Lord, well provided for"* (3:169). On that reading the promise of this verse is not deferred compensation but a description of the present state of the dead, and the dead are described as being provided for rather than as waiting.

**What Emigration Was**

*Hājarū fī sabīli Allāh* — those who emigrated in the way of God. The verb shares a root with *hijrah*, the emigration, and the word carries the sense of cutting a tie and going.

The sūrah has already named this act as a crime committed against the believers: they were expelled from their homes (22:40 — *“˹They are˺ those who have been expelled from their homes for no reason other than proclaiming: “Our Lord is Allah.””*). Emigration is the other side of that expulsion — the same departure, described from the point of view of the person who chose it rather than the people who imposed it.

Sūrah al-Nisāʾ states the promise attached to it in unambiguous terms: *“Whoever leaves their home, emigrating ˹in the cause of˺ Allah and His Messenger, and then dies, their reward is guaranteed by Allah”* (4:100). The guarantee is tied to the departure and the intention, and death overtaking the emigrant does not interrupt it.

The intention is what makes the act what it is. The Prophet ﷺ said so in the report that opens al-Bukhārī's collection: actions are reckoned by their intentions. A person has what he intended — so that whoever emigrates for God and His Messenger has emigrated for God and His Messenger, while whoever emigrates for some worldly gain or for a woman he wishes to marry has emigrated for that (Bukhārī 1; Muslim 1907). That is why the verse's phrase *fī sabīli Allāh* is not decorative. Emigration is not defined by the distance travelled but by what the traveller left for.

**"He Is the Best of Providers"**

The argument is generalised elsewhere, and the generalisation is directed at exactly this fear. *"How many are the creatures that cannot secure their provisions! ˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ provides for them and you ˹as well˺"* (29:60). Provision is not distributed according to who holds the means of producing it; if it were, the argument runs, the animals would starve. The same point is turned into a prohibition: *"Do not kill your children for fear of poverty. We provide for them and for you"* (17:31). The emigrant who has walked out of his clan's protection has not walked out of the arrangement described in those two verses.

**Death Is Not the Variable**

It refused to grade the poor who ask against the poor who do not (22:36 — *“We have made sacrificial camels ˹and cattle˺ among the symbols of Allah, in which there is ˹much˺ good for you. So pronounce the Name of Allah over them when they are lined up ˹for sacrifice˺. Once they have fallen ˹lifeless˺ on their sides, you may eat from their meat, and feed the needy—those who do not beg, and those who do. In this way We have subjected these ˹animals˺ to you so that you may be grateful”*), it refused to grade the emigrant's manner of dying, and it will close by commanding a striving that is defined by its object rather than by its form (22:78 — *“Strive for ˹the cause of˺ Allah in the way He deserves, for ˹it is˺ He ˹Who˺ has chosen you, and laid upon you no hardship in the religion—the way of your forefather Abraham.”*).

The sūrah has just stated the two destinations at the judgment (22:56–57 — *“All authority on that Day is for Allah ˹alone˺. He will judge between them. So those who believe and do good will be in the Gardens of Bliss But those who disbelieve and deny Our revelations, it is they who will suffer a humiliating punishment”*), and it now applies the reward to the people who had done the hardest thing the community had been asked to do so far.

## Verse 22:59

> He will certainly admit them into a place they will be pleased with. For Allah is truly All-Knowing, Most Forbearing

**"He Will Admit Them" — the Verb of Grace Returns**

*La-yudkhilannahum mudkhalan yarḍawnahu* — He will surely admit them an entrance they are pleased with.

The emphatic form is worth pausing on: the prefixed *la-* and the doubled *n* are the strongest construction Arabic has for a future act, and the sūrah uses it for entry in exactly the places where it wants the outcome removed from human achievement. The believers *are admitted*; they do not arrive. Everything the emigrants did was on the leaving side of this promise, and everything on the arriving side is done to them.

**"An Entrance They Are Pleased With"**

*Mudkhalan yarḍawnahu* — an entering-place that they are content with.

The object of the verb is not a place but an *entering*, a verbal noun. What is described is the manner and the experience of arriving rather than the coordinates of a destination. The sūrah does not say where they go; it says that the going-in will be such that they are pleased with it.

The relative clause is the substance of the promise. Contentment is internal and cannot be conferred by the quality of a place alone — a person can be in a garden and be wretched. What is promised here is the removal of that possibility.

Contentment is the Qur'an's own term for the furthest point of the reward, and it is used reciprocally — the servant is pleased and the Lord is pleased. The close of Sūrah al-Bayyinah gives the same double: *“Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him”* (98:8). What is promised here is one half of that exchange, and it is stated as an internal state rather than as an inventory, because a person can be given everything and still be out of place.

**Why Contentment Is the Right Promise for Emigrants**

The people addressed had left their homes, their property, their clan and, in many cases, their families. What an emigrant loses is not mainly comfort; it is the sense of being where one belongs.

Sūrah al-Fajr states the same promise in the direct address to the soul at peace: *“Return to your Lord, well pleased ˹with Him˺ and well pleasing ˹to Him˺. So join My servants, and enter My Paradise”* (89:27–30). The contentment and the entry are the same event.

**Forbearance Is the Sūrah's Own Attribute**

The chapter's entire middle section is an argument about delay: communities given rein before being seized (22:44 — *“But I delayed ˹the fate of˺ the disbelievers ˹until their appointed time˺ then seized them.”*, 22:48 — *“Many are the societies whose end We delayed while they did wrong, then seized them. And to Me is the final return”*), a punishment whose arrival is measured on a different clock (22:47 — *“They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment. And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting”*), a warning delivered by a messenger who is not responsible for the outcome (22:49 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O humanity! I am only sent to you with a clear warning”*). Forbearance is the divine name for all of that.

Naming it here, at the close of the promise to the emigrants, is the sūrah's way of saying that the delay cost them nothing. Those who died before the victory are described as entering a place they are pleased with, and the One who admits them is the One who was never in a hurry.

## Verse 22:60
> That is so. And whoever retaliates in equivalence to the injury they have received, and then are wronged ˹again˺, Allah will certainly help them. Surely Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving

**"Whoever Retaliates With the Like of What He Suffered"**

Sūrah al-Naḥl states the rule and then the preference in one sentence: *“If you retaliate, then let it be equivalent to what you have suffered. But if you patiently endure, it is certainly best for those who are patient”* (16:126). Sūrah al-Shūrā does the same: *“The reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. But whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah”* (42:40).

**The Fourth Seal**

*Dhālika* — that is how it is. The demonstrative appears here for the fourth time in the sūrah, closing the passage on the emigrants just as it closed the passages on the pilgrimage (22:30 — *“That is so. And whoever honours the rituals of Allah, it is best for them in the sight of their Lord. The ˹meat of˺ cattle has been made lawful for you, except what has ˹already˺ been recited to you. So shun the impurity of idolatry, and shun words of falsehood”*) and the rites (22:32 — *“That is so. And whoever honours the symbols of Allah, it is certainly out of the piety of the heart”*).

The sūrah uses the pointer in two distinct ways, and the difference is instructive. At 22:30 — *“That is so. And whoever honours the rituals of Allah, it is best for them in the sight of their Lord. The ˹meat of˺ cattle has been made lawful for you, except what has ˹already˺ been recited to you. So shun the impurity of idolatry, and shun words of falsehood”* and 22:32 — *“That is so. And whoever honours the symbols of Allah, it is certainly out of the piety of the heart”* it stands alone, sealing a block of instruction: what has been said is to be taken as stated. At 22:60 and 22:62 — *“That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth and what they invoke besides Him is falsehood, and Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Most High, All-Great”* it is followed by *bi-anna*, because: a ground is about to be supplied. The retaliator's permission and the promise of help are therefore not asserted twice in a row. The first is asserted and sealed, and the four verses that follow give the reasons.

*Wa-man ʿāqaba bi-mithli mā ʿūqiba bihi* — and whoever retaliates with the equivalent of what he was punished with.

The question of the sacred month — which is where the reported occasion of this verse bites — is settled on the same principle of measure rather than on the calendar. *"˹There will be retaliation in˺ a sacred month for ˹an offence in˺ a sacred month, and all violations will bring about retaliation"* (2:194). The sanctity of the month is real, and so is the right of reply within it; what is prohibited is the excess, not the response.

**Equivalence Is What Separates a Response From Revenge**

The Qur'an's law of retaliation in cases of homicide rests on exactly this logic, and it is stated with an explicit preference for waiving it: *“But if the offender is pardoned by the victim’s guardian, then bloodmoney should be decided fairly and payment should be made courteously. This is a concession and a mercy from your Lord”* (2:178).

*Inna Allāha la-ʿafuwwun ghafūr* — God is certainly Pardoning, Forgiving.

*"Let them pardon and forgive. Do you not love to be forgiven by Allah?"* (24:22).

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Ethics of Force**

Fighting is permitted, to those who were fought, because they were wronged (22:39 — *“Permission ˹to fight back˺ is ˹hereby˺ granted to those being fought, for they have been wronged. And Allah is truly Most Capable of helping them ˹prevail˺”*). The grievance is specified: expulsion for a creed (22:40 — *“˹They are˺ those who have been expelled from their homes for no reason other than proclaiming: “Our Lord is Allah.” Had Allah not repelled ˹the aggression of˺ some people by means of others, destruction would have surely claimed monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques in which Allah’s Name is often mentioned. Allah will certainly help those who stand up for Him. Allah is truly All-Powerful, Almighty”*). Power, if granted, carries four duties, two of them public (22:41 — *“˹They are˺ those who, if established in the land by Us, would perform prayer, pay alms-tax, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil. And with Allah rests the outcome of all affairs”*). Treachery is forbidden (22:38 — *“Indeed, Allah defends those who believe. Surely Allah does not like whoever is deceitful, ungrateful”*).

Retaliation is permitted in exact measure, never in excess (22:60). And the God who authorises all of it is described as pardoning and forgiving.

The set also has an outer boundary that the sūrah assumes rather than states, and it is worth stating because the objection is predictable. Force is directed at those who wage it: *"Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you, but do not exceed the limits. Surely Allah does not like transgressors"* (2:190). Read with verse 60, the shape of the ethic is a target, a measure and a prohibition on excess — a permission that is hedged on every side, addressed to a community that had been expelled from its homes and is being told to count before it strikes.

## Verse 22:61

> That is because Allah causes the night to merge into the day, and the day into the night. Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing

**The Verb: Entering, Not Replacing**

*Yūliju* means to insert, to cause to enter, to make one thing pass into another. It is not the verb for replacing or for following.

The choice is precise, and the parallel verses use the same word: *“Do you not see that Allah causes the night to merge into the day and the day into the night. Has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term”* (31:29), and in the fuller statement, *“He wraps the night around the day, and wraps the day around the night”* (39:5).

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān makes the connection explicit: *“You cause the night to pass into the day and the day into the night. You bring forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And You provide for whoever You will without limit”* (3:27).

**Why This Sign Above All Others**

Sūrah Yūnus states the argumentative function in a single line: *“Surely in the alternation of the day and the night, and in all that Allah has created in the heavens and the earth, there are truly signs for those mindful ˹of Him˺”* (10:6). Sūrah al-Furqān adds the purpose: *“And He is the One Who causes the day and the night to alternate, ˹as a sign˺ for whoever desires to be mindful or to be grateful”* (25:62).

**"Hearing, Seeing"**

*Wa-anna Allāha samīʿun baṣīr* — and God is Hearing, Seeing.

The pairing is used the same way at the opening of Sūrah al-Mujādilah, in a case that is likewise about a complaint brought by someone with no power to enforce it: *"Indeed, Allah has heard the argument of the woman who pleaded with you ˹O Prophet˺ about her husband, and appealed to Allah. Allah has heard your exchange. Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing"* (58:1). The hearing is announced before the ruling is given, because the first thing an unheard party needs is to have been heard. That is what the two names do at the end of this verse: they tell the wronged party that the case is already complete.

**The Verse in the Chain**

Verses 61 to 64 are a chain of four verses each beginning with a causal particle: because God merges night into day (v. 61), because God is the Truth (v. 62), because God sends rain (v. 63), because to Him belongs everything (v. 64).

The four grounds also answer four different objections, and that is why there are four of them rather than one. The alternation of night and day answers "the situation cannot change." God's being the Truth answers "the cause is not worth changing it for." The rain that revives dead ground answers "the cause is finished." Ownership of everything answers "there is nothing left to give." None of the four is redundant. None of them is an argument about the believers' numbers or equipment.

## Verse 22:62

> That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth and what they invoke besides Him is falsehood, and Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Most High, All-Great

The word translated as falsehood is *bāṭil*, from a root meaning to be void, to come to nothing, to fail.

**The Objection This Answers**

Sūrah Luqmān states the entire verse almost identically, which confirms that this is a fixed formula rather than a remark tailored to one audience: *“That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth and what they invoke besides Him is falsehood, and ˹because˺ Allah ˹alone˺ is the Most High, All-Great”* (31:30).

The conduit version of the objection is recorded in the Qur'an in its own words. It is answered the same way: *“They worship besides Allah others who can neither harm nor benefit them, and say, ‘These are our intercessors with Allah.’ Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, ‘Are you informing Allah of something He does not know in the heavens or the earth?’”* (10:18). The reply is a question about warrant rather than about power. An intercessor who was appointed would be a different matter completely; the claim being rejected is that one may be nominated.

**Two Predicates, One Shape**

The emphatic pronoun *huwa* in each clause does the rest. God *is* the Truth: not a truth among truths, but that which truly is. What is called upon besides Him *is* the false: not weaker, not lesser, but the thing that comes to nothing.

**"The Most High, the Great"**

*Wa-anna Allāha huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-kabīr* — and God is the Most High, the Great.

The two names are chosen for their fit with what precedes. If what is worshipped besides God is null, the reason is not that it is weak in a competition among powers. It is that the distance between it and God is not a distance at all: God is *al-ʿaliyy*, the High, above comparison, and *al-kabīr*, the Great, beside whom greatness is not a measurable property.

The two names close the most famous verse in the Qur'an in the same form, and the context there is the same context: *“For He is the Most High, the Greatest”* (2:255). In both places the names follow a statement of what belongs to God and of what no one can do without His permission. The argument is not that He is highest in a ranking but that the ranking is His to make.

## Verse 22:63

> Do you not see that Allah sends down rain from the sky, then the earth becomes green? Surely Allah is Most Subtle, All-Aware

**The Verb: "Becomes"**

*Fa-tuṣbiḥu* — and it *becomes*, in the morning. The verb carries the sense of entering a state, and in this form it is used for what happens at daybreak.

Morning is the Qur'an's appointed hour for the arrival of what was promised, and the phrase runs through the narratives of destruction as well as the signs of mercy: *“Their appointed time is the morning. Is the morning not near?”* (11:81). The verb *tuṣbiḥu* places the earth's revival inside that larger pattern: nobody watches the change happen, and everyone wakes to find it done.

**"Green"**

Green is also the Qur'an's colour for the Garden, and the correspondence is exact enough to read as deliberate: *“There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold, and wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade”* (18:31). The field after rain and the inhabitants of Paradise share a colour, which means the sign and the thing signified resemble each other — the earth's annual greening is not only evidence for the Resurrection but a preview of what it looks like.

**Laṭīf: Subtle, and Reaching Into the Fine Places**

The pairing of the two names is itself a formula, and Sūrah al-Mulk states it as a rhetorical question that answers itself: *“How could He not know His Own creation? For He ˹alone˺ is the Most Subtle, All-Aware”* (67:14). Subtlety and awareness belong together because they are the same perfection viewed from two sides — action that reaches everywhere, knowledge that misses nothing. The One who made the seed knows where it lies, which is why the rain finds it.

**Khabīr: Aware**

Sūrah Luqmān attaches the same pair of names to the smallest hidden thing imaginable: *“˹Even˺ if a deed were the weight of a mustard seed — be it ˹hidden˺ in a rock or in the heavens or the earth — Allah will bring it forth. Surely Allah is Most Subtle, All-Aware”* (31:16). A mustard seed inside a rock is the limit case of the concealed, and the verse places it in the same sentence as these two names. The root in the soil is found; the deed in the rock will be found; nothing hidden is hidden from the *khabīr*.

**Why the Rain Sign Carries More Weight Than Any Other**

Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah presses the dependence one step further, from the field to the drinker: *“Have you considered the water you drink? Is it you who bring it down from the clouds, or is it We Who do so?”* (56:68–69). Nobody in the audience could answer *we do* — the caravans did not manufacture rain. A sign that every person drinks daily, that no person can produce, and that decides between a living year and a famine leaves no room for either dismissal or self-credit, which is why the Qur'an returns to it more often than to any other.

Sūrah Fuṣṣilat states the sign and the inference in their most explicit form, with the conclusion drawn inside the verse: *“And among His signs is that you see the earth devoid of life, but as soon as We send down rain upon it, it begins to stir ˹to life˺ and swell. Indeed, the One Who revives it can easily revive the dead. He is certainly Most Capable of everything”* (41:39).

## Verse 22:64

> To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy

**The Claim of Ownership**

*Lahu mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍ* — to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.

The same claim appears at the head of the closing verses of Sūrah al-Baqarah, and the consequence drawn there is the one this sūrah is also drawing: *“To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Whether you reveal what is in your hearts or conceal it, Allah will call you to account for it”* (2:284). Ownership and accountability are a single claim. The sūrah has spent its length establishing the first to make the second unavoidable.

**What Ownership Excludes**

Nothing belongs to the idols, which is the argument of verse 62 restated as a property claim. Nothing belongs to the worshipper in the absolute sense, which is why the sūrah keeps describing wealth as *what We provided you* (22:28 — *“so they may obtain the benefits ˹in store˺ for them, and pronounce the Name of Allah on appointed days over the sacrificial animals He has provided for them. So eat from their meat and feed the desperately poor”*, 22:35 — *“those whose hearts tremble at the remembrance of Allah, who patiently endure whatever may befall them, and who establish prayer and donate from what We have provided for them”*) and why the sacrificial animal may be used but not sold. And nothing that a person surrenders is lost, since it was never his to begin with — which is the argument behind the promise to the emigrants in verses 58 and 59.

**Al-Ghanī: the Self-Sufficient**

*Wa-inna Allāha la-huwa al-ghaniyyu al-ḥamīd* — and God, He is the Self-Sufficient, the Praised.

*Ghanī* means rich, free of need, self-enough. The word is used for a person of independent means, and applied to God it means that nothing outside Him supplies anything to Him.

**Al-Ḥamīd: the Praised**

This is the second of the sūrah's two uses of the root. The *path of the Praised* appeared at verse 24 as the destination of the people of the Garden, and the sūrah's closing verse will command the believers to hold fast to God as their Guardian.

That application is what the sūrah's closing command will name as holding fast. A person who believes that everything belongs to God has two available responses: to clutch at what he has been given, or to hold on to the Giver. The sūrah has described the first in a dozen figures — the disputer without knowledge, the man on the brink, the worshipper of what a fly can rob — and it will close by naming the second as the whole of what is asked (22:78 — *“So establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and hold fast to Allah.”*).

## Verse 22:65

> Do you not see that Allah has subjected to you whatever is in the earth as well as the ships ˹that˺ sail through the sea by His command? He keeps the sky from falling down on the earth except by His permission. Surely Allah is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to humanity

**The Subjection Is Not a Licence**

Sūrah al-Baqarah holds the Qur'an's longest catalogue of the same signs. The closing line names their audience: *“the ships that sail the sea for the benefit of humanity; the rain sent down by Allah from the skies, reviving the earth after its death … ˹in all of this˺ are surely signs for people of understanding”* (2:164).

**What "Subjected" Means**

*Sakhkhara* means to make something subservient, to put it at someone's disposal. The sūrah has used the word before, in verse 36, for the sacrificial camels, and the meaning is the same: a thing stronger than the person is made usable by him.

The word's asymmetry is what makes it a sign rather than a fact about human cleverness. The earth and everything in it is not weaker than a human being; the animals are stronger, the soils and waters are indifferent to him, and the whole arrangement could have been otherwise. That it is arranged for him is the claim, and the sūrah repeats it elsewhere in the same vocabulary: *“Have you not seen that Allah has subjected for you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, and has lavished His favours upon you, both seen and unseen?”* (31:20).

The travellers' own sentence confesses the asymmetry: *“Glory be to the One Who has subjected these for us, for we could have never done so ˹on our own˺”* (43:13). *Wa-mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn* — we were not its match, not up to subduing it. The camel beneath the rider and the ship beneath the merchant are alike stronger than their users; the subjection is confessed as gift at the moment of use. Verse 65 generalises the confession from the mount to the earth and everything in it.

**The Ships**

Sūrah al-Jāthiyah states it as a purpose: *“Allah is the One Who has subjected the sea for you so that ships may sail upon it by His command, and that you may seek His bounty”* (45:12). Sūrah Ibrāhīm pairs it with the rivers: *“He has subjected the ships for your service, sailing through the sea by His command, and has subjected the rivers for you”* (14:32).

**The Exception**

Sūrah al-Mulk asks what withdrawal would look like, and the question is geological: *“Do you feel secure that the One Who is in heaven will not cause the earth to swallow you up as it quakes violently?”* (67:16). The ground opened, the city taken down — the permission of verse 65's *illā bi-idhnih* imagined as revoked. The security the audience feels is real but conditional, and the verse names the condition by threatening it: the sky held and the earth firm are both permissions, and permissions can be lifted.

Sūrah al-Anʿām attaches the due to the harvest in the same breath as the eating: *“Eat from the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest, but do not waste”* (6:141). Eating permitted, dues commanded, waste forbidden — the subjection of the gardens hedged on both sides. Verse 65's earth is subjected under the same terms: use granted, obligation attached, and the closing attributes of kindness and mercy naming the giver rather than licensing the gifted.

The pair closes a verse about the community's hardest test, with the same universal object: *“Surely Allah is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to humanity”* (2:143).

## Verse 22:66

> And He is the One Who gave you life, then will cause you to die, and then will bring you back to life. ˹But˺ surely humankind is ever ungrateful

**Three Verbs, and the Sūrah's Whole Argument in One Line**

*Wa-huwa alladhī aḥyākum thumma yumītukum thumma yuḥyīkum* — and He is the One who gave you life, then will cause you to die, then will give you life again.

Sūrah al-Baqarah states the same three verbs in the same order, and then draws the conclusion this verse leaves implicit: *“How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned”* (2:28). The sequence there ends where this sūrah's argument ends — not with life restored as a bare fact but with a return, which is why the charge of ingratitude follows the verbs rather than preceding them.

**The Sequence Matches the Sūrah's Own Structure**

The sūrah began by addressing people who are alive and in doubt about what follows (22:5 — *“O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh—fully formed or unformed—in order to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you.”*). It described the death of the earth and its revival under rain. It described communities that were given life, then taken. It will close by commanding a striving from people who will be brought back. Every movement of the chapter has been inside this sequence.

The *thumma* matters more than it looks. It is the particle of succession with an interval, and the Qur'an uses it for this sequence wherever the interval needs to be felt: *“After that you will surely die, then on the Day of Judgment you will be resurrected”* (23:15–16). Death is not the next item on a list. It is separated from what follows by a gap whose length belongs to God, which is the sūrah's standing claim about every divine act — rein given, then seizure (22:44 — *“and the residents of Midian. And Moses was denied ˹too˺. But I delayed ˹the fate of˺ the disbelievers ˹until their appointed time˺ then seized them. And how severe was My response”*), a day like a thousand years (22:47 — *“They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment. And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting”*), help that arrives after the waiting (22:60 — *“That is so. And whoever retaliates in equivalence to the injury they have received, and then are wronged ˹again˺, Allah will certainly help them. Surely Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving”*).

**"Man Is Ungrateful"**

The subject is *al-insān*, the human being as such, not a particular community. The verse is not naming the Quraysh; it is naming the species. The sūrah has already given the evidence — the man on the brink who was content with the gift and forgot the giver (22:11 — *“And there are some who worship Allah on the verge ˹of faith˺: if they are blessed with something good, they are content with it; but if they are afflicted with a trial, they relapse ˹into disbelief˺, losing this world and the Hereafter. That is ˹truly˺ the clearest loss”*), the man who wanted the punishment hurried (22:47 — *“They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment. And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting”*), and the people who saw the ruins and drew no conclusion (22:46 — *“Have they not travelled throughout the land so their hearts may reason, and their ears may listen? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind”*).

The charge is repeated as a standing description of the species in Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt, in even barer form: *“Surely humankind is ungrateful to their Lord”* (100:6). No occasion is named and no community is specified, which confirms that *kafūr* here is not a verdict on the sūrah's opponents but a premise of its anthropology — the thing about human beings that makes revelation necessary rather than optional.

**The Verse's Honesty About the Species**

The Qur'an does state the exception, but it states it elsewhere, and the narrowness of the exception is what gives this verse its force: *“except those who have faith, do good, and urge each other to the truth, and urge each other to perseverance”* (103:3). Four conditions, all of them active and two of them social — faith held, good done, truth urged, patience urged. The default needs no qualification; the departure from the default needs all four.

The parity argument is stated as a direct answer to the doubter's question in Sūrah Yā-Sīn. The answer turns on exactly this point — that the third act is not a new kind of thing: *“And they argue with Us— forgetting they were created—saying, ‘Who will give life to decayed bones?’ Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time. He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being’”* (36:78–79).

## Verse 22:67

> For every community We appointed a code of life to follow. So do not let them dispute with you ˹O Prophet˺ in this matter. And invite ˹all˺ to your Lord, for you are truly on the Right Guidance

**What the Prophet Is Told to Do Instead**

*Wa-dʿu ilā rabbika* — and call to your Lord.

A dispute takes place on the ground the disputant has chosen: which rite, which community, which practice is correct. An invitation takes place on ground the caller chooses: the Lord. The sūrah's instruction is to decline the first and keep to the second, which is also the strategy of verse 49, where the Prophet was told to announce his office rather than to produce the punishment.

The standing formulation of the same instruction makes clear that the verse is not banning argument as such. *"Invite ˹all˺ to the Way of your Lord with wisdom and kind advice, and only debate with them in the best manner"* (16:125). Debate conducted well is part of the invitation. What verse 67 withdraws from is a debate conducted on the opponent's terms, about a matter that was appointed rather than argued.

**"You Are Indeed on Straight Guidance"**

*Innaka la-ʿalā hudan mustaqīm* — you are certainly upon a guidance that is straight.

The same assurance is given to him elsewhere in nearly the same words, and the parallel shows what is being protected: *"You ˹O Prophet˺ are truly one of the messengers, upon the Straight Path"* (36:3–4). In both places the statement precedes the outcome. The Prophet is not told that the disputants will be convinced; he is told that his position does not depend on whether they are.

**The Dispute the Sūrah Has in View**

The first is the more natural reading of the grammar, since the verb is a prohibition addressed in the second person with *them* as the object. It is also the reading that fits the sūrah, which has repeatedly told the Prophet what his job is and what it is not (22:49 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O humanity! I am only sent to you with a clear warning”*).

Either way, the verse is not a general prohibition of discussion. The Qur'an prescribes argument elsewhere and prescribes its manner: *"Do not argue with the People of the Book unless gracefully, except with those of them who act wrongfully"* (29:46). What is withdrawn in this verse is narrower — a dispute about an appointed difference, with people the sūrah has already described as disputing without knowledge.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Closing**

That is the right place for it. A community that has been told it is right, and told that the adjudication is deferred to God (22:17 — *“Indeed, the believers, Jews, Sabians, Christians, Magi, and the polytheists—Allah will judge between them ˹all˺ on Judgment Day. Surely Allah is a Witness over all things”*, 22:56 — *“All authority on that Day is for Allah ˹alone˺. He will judge between them. So those who believe and do good will be in the Gardens of Bliss”*), needs to know what to do with people who disagree — and the answer the sūrah gives is not argument.

The three verses also close the question that the sūrah has held open since its middle. Verse 69 will state that God will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection about what they differed about, which is the same deferral that verse 17 made for the six communities and verse 56 made for the judgment generally. Between the deferral and the day, the instruction is to invite and not to dispute — a rule for the interval rather than a solution to the disagreement.

## Verse 22:68

> But if they argue with you, then say, “Allah knows best what you do.”

**What to Say, and Why That**

*Fa-qul Allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā taʿmalūn* — then say: God knows best what you do.

Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ records the same disengagement as the practice of the believers, with the account books kept separate: *“When they hear slanderous talk, they turn away from it, saying, ‘We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. Peace ˹is our only response˺ to you! We want nothing to do with those who act ignorantly’”* (28:55). Deeds assigned to their doers, peace offered once, ignorance declined as a conversation partner. Verse 68's single sentence is that paragraph reduced to its operative clause: the record invoked, the exchange ended.

**The Knowledge Invoked Is the Sūrah's Own Theme**

Sūrah al-Mujādalah states what the invoked knowledge will do on the day it is acted on: *“He will then inform them of what they have done. Allah has kept account of it all, while they have forgotten it”* (58:6). Kept by God, forgotten by them — the asymmetry is what makes verse 68's sentence a warning rather than a shrug. The disputant who will not stop arguing has forgotten most of what he has done; the record he is being referred to has forgotten none of it, and the informing is dated to the resurrection of all together.

**Disengagement Is Not Defeat**

The disengagement is the Qur'an's portrait of the ideal believer's manners, not a concession to weakness: *“The ˹true˺ servants of the Most Compassionate are those who walk on the earth humbly, and when the foolish address them ˹improperly˺, they only respond with peace”* (25:63). Responding with peace to provocation is listed as the defining behaviour of the servants of the Merciful — placed first in the catalogue, before the night prayer and the charity. What verse 68 prescribes for the Prophet is what the Qur'an prescribes as the mark of every believer grown to full stature.

**The Verse's View of Argument Itself**

The assumption is that some arguments cannot be won, and that this is a fact about the other party rather than about the case. The sūrah has described people who dispute without knowledge (22:8 — *“˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, guidance, or an enlightening scripture”*), whose hearts are sick (22:53 — *“All that so He may make Satan’s influence a trial for those ˹hypocrites˺ whose hearts are sick and those ˹disbelievers˺ whose hearts are hardened.”*), whose hearts are hard (22:53 — *“All that so He may make Satan’s influence a trial for those ˹hypocrites˺ whose hearts are sick and those ˹disbelievers˺ whose hearts are hardened.”*), and who will not cease to be in doubt until the Hour arrives (22:55 — *“Yet the disbelievers will persist in doubt about this ˹revelation˺ until the Hour takes them by surprise, or the torment of a terminating Day comes to them”*). Against that description, more argument is not only futile; it is a category error.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf compresses the whole posture into three imperatives: *“Be gracious, enjoin what is right, and turn away from those who act ignorantly”* (7:199). Graciousness toward people, right commanded where it can be heard, and turning away where it cannot — the third imperative is not a failure of the second but its boundary. Verse 68's rule belongs to that third clause: the argument that cannot be won is not fought harder but left, and the leaving is itself commanded.

**Where This Leaves the Believer**

The cost of forgoing victory has its own promise attached, in the verse on repelling evil with good: *“Good and evil cannot be equal. Respond ˹to evil˺ with what is best, then the one you are in a feud with will be like a close friend”* (41:34). The single sentence of verse 68 is a small instance of the best response — knowledge named instead of insult returned — and the verse holds out what disengagement rarely promises: that the feud itself may end, not by being won but by being answered with something it cannot feed on.

The restraint has its own technical term, given to the Prophet as a standing command: *“Be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say, and depart from them courteously”* (73:10) — in the Arabic, *wahjurhum hajran jamīlan*, a gracious avoidance.

The God-knows-best formula closes disputes the Qur'an declines to adjudicate here: *“Indeed, your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided”* (6:117).

## Verse 22:69

> Allah will judge between you ˹all˺ on Judgment Day regarding your differences

**The Deferral Has a Date**

Sūrah al-Sajdah states the date in its barest form: *“Indeed, your Lord will decide between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their differences”* (32:25). No parties named, no dispute specified — only the Judge, the day and the scope. The bareness is what makes the formula reusable: it fits the Sabbath dispute, the communal claims of verse 17, and the rites of verse 67 without a word changed, because the only content it carries is that the settling is certain and scheduled.

**"Concerning That in Which You Differ"**

The scope of the phrase is wider than the context might suggest. The immediate disagreement is about the rites, since verse 67 has just said that every community was given its own.

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān states the same deferral for the disputes of the People of the Book: *“Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and I will settle all your disputes”* (3:55). Sūrah al-Māʾidah does the same for the plurality of laws (5:48 — *“We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ this Book with the truth, as a confirmation of previous Scriptures and a supreme authority on them.”*).

A dispute referred to an indefinite future has been shelved. A dispute referred to a named day that the sūrah has spent its length establishing — the Hour that is coming (22:7 — *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves”*), the day on which sovereignty is God's alone (22:56 — *“All authority on that Day is for Allah ˹alone˺. He will judge between them. So those who believe and do good will be in the Gardens of Bliss”*) — has been scheduled.

**What the Deferral Does for the Community**

The sūrah has just legislated the limits of force (22:39–41 — *“Permission ˹to fight back˺ is ˹hereby˺ granted to those being fought, for they have been wronged. And Allah is truly Most Capable of helping them ˹prevail˺”*, 22:60 — *“That is so. And whoever retaliates in equivalence to the injury they have received, and then are wronged ˹again˺, Allah will certainly help them. Surely Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving”*). This verse supplies the other half of that legislation: the disputes that cannot be settled by force are not abandoned, they are reserved.

Sūrah al-Kāfirūn gives the same release in its most compact form: *“You have your way, and I have my Way”* (109:6). Neither silencing nor conceding — the two ways named side by side, with the adjudication left to the Judge both parties will meet. That is the social form of verse 69's theology: a community can live the interval honestly only if it is forbidden to force the verdict early, which is why the sūrah legislates the limits of force and the reservation of judgment in the same breath.

**The Verse's Connection to Verse 17**

The verb is the same one used at verse 17: *yaḥkumu bayna*, He judges between. There the parties were named — believers, Jews, Sabians, Christians, Magians and associators — and the sūrah declined to rank them.

The formula's application to an inter-communal dispute is recorded in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where each party nullifies the other: *“The Jews say, ‘The Christians have nothing to stand on’ and the Christians say, ‘The Jews have nothing to stand on,’ although both recite the Scriptures … Surely Allah will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute”* (2:113). Both reciting scripture, each denying the other — the precise situation verse 69 addresses, with the rites standing in for the scriptures.

The formula is fixed across sūrahs, and Sūrah al-Naḥl applies it to a dispute about the Sabbath: *“surely your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their disputes”* (16:124).

The interval has a stated purpose, and Sūrah al-Mulk names it in the verse that opens the sūrah's argument: *“˹He is the One˺ Who created death and life to test which of you is best in deeds”* (67:2).

The Judge's qualification is stated as a question that answers itself: *“Is Allah not the most just of all judges?”* (95:8).

## Verse 22:70

> Do you not know that Allah ˹fully˺ knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth? Surely it is all ˹written˺ in a Record. That is certainly easy for Allah

**"Indeed That Is in a Book"**

The Qur'an's insistence on the record is a claim about the kind of knowledge God has. It is not a general awareness or an intuition; it is an entry. Sūrah Luqmān makes the scale explicit: *“˹Even˺ if a deed were the weight of a mustard seed—hidden in a rock or in the heavens or the earth—Allah will bring it forth”* (31:16). The same claim is made elsewhere in the same vocabulary: *“He knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth”* (3:29) and *“He knows whatever is in the heavens and on the earth”* (5:97).

**The Singular "Heaven"**

The Arabic here has *al-samāʾ* in the singular rather than the plural *al-samāwāt* used in verse 64. This is the only place in the Qur'an where the singular form appears in a statement of this kind, and the variation is worth noting precisely because it is unusual.

The plural is what the Qur'an uses when it is counting or when it is describing structure: *"So He formed the heaven into seven heavens in two Days, assigning to each its mandate"* (41:12). What is at issue in this verse is not structure but coverage. A hearer who is told that the seven heavens are in the record might wonder about what lies between them; a hearer who is told that the heaven is in the record, taken as one undivided expanse, is given no gap to insert anything into. The singular is the more exhaustive word in a sentence about exhaustive knowledge.

**Why a Record Rather Than a Memory**

That matters for the judgment the sūrah has been describing. A verdict based on recollection could in principle be disputed on the ground of error. A verdict based on a written entry that the accused is invited to read cannot — which is why the Qur'an's judgment scenes show the record being produced rather than the charges being announced (17:14 — *“˹And it will be said,˺ “Read your record. You ˹alone˺ are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself.””*; 18:49 — *“And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it.”*; 69:19 — *“As for those given their records in their right hand, they will cry ˹happily˺, “Here ˹everyone˺! Read my record”*).

Sūrah al-Kahf describes what happens when the document is opened, and the reaction is not a dispute about facts: *"And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it. They will cry, 'Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?'"* (18:49). Sūrah al-Qamar gives the same property in two lines: *"Everything they have done is ˹listed˺ in ˹their˺ records. Every matter, small and large, is written ˹precisely˺"* (54:52–53). Completeness is the attribute that makes a record a record rather than a summary, and it is the attribute this verse is claiming.

**"That Is Easy for God"**

The same formula is attached elsewhere to the act that readers find hardest to accept, and the comparison is what gives the word its force: *"And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him"* (30:27). The comparative *even easier* is not measuring two labours against each other; it is pointing out that the second act follows from the first by the same power.

**The Record and the Sūrah's Own Argument**

The record is also what makes the sūrah's repeated deferrals coherent. God is said to judge between the communities on the Day of Resurrection (22:17 — *“Indeed, the believers, Jews, Sabians, Christians, Magi, and the polytheists—Allah will judge between them ˹all˺ on Judgment Day. Surely Allah is a Witness over all things”*), sovereignty on that day is His (22:56 — *“All authority on that Day is for Allah ˹alone˺. He will judge between them. So those who believe and do good will be in the Gardens of Bliss”*), and the Prophet is told that God will judge the disputed matter (22:69 — *“Allah will judge between you ˹all˺ on Judgment Day regarding your differences”*). Every one of those is a postponement, and a postponement is only just if nothing is lost in the interval. The Book is the answer to that worry: the deferral costs the wronged party nothing, because the case does not decay while it waits.

The Qur'an also describes how the entries get made, and the description turns the doctrine into something a person lives inside rather than something he waits for: *"while you are certainly observed by vigilant, honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺. They know whatever you do"* (82:10–12).

## Verse 22:71

> Yet they worship besides Allah that for which He has sent down no authority, and of which they have no knowledge. The wrongdoers will have no helper

**What "Sulṭān" Means Here**

*Sulṭān* is the word for authority, warrant, proof, and the power by which one thing prevails over another. Its range is wide and all of it is in play.

In another it means intellectual demonstration — the argument by which a claim is established — and al-Rāzī took it in that sense here, linking it to the second half of the verse. In a third it means sheer power, the sense in which Satan says at the judgment: *“I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me”* (14:22).

**The Burden of Proof Is Placed on the Worshipper**

The Qur'an makes the inheritance explicit when it wants to, and the wording it uses closes the remaining exit. Of the idols it says that the worshippers have *"mere names that you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized"* (53:23), and of the people it says that *"most of them follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions. ˹And˺ surely assumptions can in no way replace the truth"* (10:36). The verse is not demanding that the worshippers produce a demonstration of metaphysics. It is asking what they are relying on, and naming the answer before they give it.

**"The Wrongdoers Will Have No Helper"**

*Wa-mā li-l-ẓālimīna min naṣīr* — and the wrongdoers have no helper.

The same sentence is put into Abraham's mouth in Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt, addressed to exactly this arrangement, and it adds what the absence will feel like from the inside: *"You have taken idols ˹for worship˺ instead of Allah, only to keep ˹the bond of˺ harmony among yourselves in this worldly life. But on the Day of Judgment you will disown and curse one another. Your home will be the Fire, and you will have no helper!"* (29:25). The alliance dissolves in both directions: the gods do not come, and the worshippers turn on each other.

**Why "Wrongdoers" Rather Than "Disbelievers"**

The sūrah used the same word at verse 25 for those who bar people from the Sacred Mosque, and it will use it at verse 72 for the rage on the disbelievers' faces. In every case the wrong is something done rather than something merely believed.

The Qur'an names the same act as the worst instance of the same category, and the naming is what settles the question of why *ẓālimīn* and not something milder: *"Never associate ˹anything˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺, for associating ˹others with Him˺ is truly the worst of all wrongs"* (31:13). The verse is not softening the charge by calling it wrongdoing. It is using the word the Qur'an reserves for the gravest misplacement there is.

The Qur'an makes this same challenge in the same form elsewhere, and the challenge is what makes the argument an argument rather than a denunciation: *“Call upon those you claim ˹to be divine˺ besides Allah. They do not have ˹even˺ an atom’s weight either in the heavens or the earth, nor do they have any share in ˹governing˺ them”* (34:22).

## Verse 22:72

> Whenever Our clear revelations are recited to them, you ˹O Prophet˺ recognize rage on the faces of the disbelievers, as if they are going to snap at those who recite Our revelations to them. Say, “Shall I inform you of something far more enraging than that? ˹It is˺ the Fire with which Allah has threatened those who disbelieve. What an evil destination!”

**The Face Betrays What the Argument Conceals**

*Wa-idhā tutlā ʿalayhim āyātunā bayyinātin taʿrifu fī wujūhi alladhīna kafarū al-munkara* — and when Our signs are recited to them as clear proofs, you recognise the denial in the faces of those who disbelieve.

The sūrah has been interested in faces before. Verse 11 described a man turned over onto his; verse 9 described a shoulder turned away. Here the face is where the state of the heart becomes visible, and the verb *taʿrifu*, you recognise, implies something that can be read without being told.

The Qur'an treats the face as a readable surface elsewhere, and always at moments of exposure. Sūrah Āl ʿImrān makes it the index of the verdict: *"On that Day some faces will be bright while others gloomy. To the gloomy-faced it will be said, 'Did you disbelieve after having believed?'"* (3:106). The same contrast opens Sūrah al-Qiyāmah and Sūrah ʿAbasa (75:22 — *“On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright”*; 80:38–40 — *“On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright”*). What happens here is the same disclosure in miniature: an interior state becoming legible on a face, in public, before anyone has been told anything.

*Al-munkar* is the Qur'an's word for what is to be forbidden — the same word the believers are commanded to prohibit: *"enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong"* (3:104).

**"The Fire Which God Has Promised the Disbelievers"**

*Al-nāru waʿadahā Allāhu alladhīna kafarū* — the Fire, which God has promised to those who disbelieve.

The symmetry is the part that deserves attention, because the same verb is used for what the believers are given: *"Allah has promised the believers, both men and women, Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever"* (9:72). One verb, two destinations, and the certainty is identical on both sides. The verse is not saying that God has issued a threat which may or may not be carried out. It is saying that these people have an appointment, in the same sense and from the same source as the believers have theirs.

**"What an Evil Destination"**

*Wa-biʾsa al-maṣīr* — and what an evil place of arrival.

The formula is not peculiar to this context; Sūrah al-Taghābun closes the fate of the rejecters with it word for word: *"As for those who disbelieve and reject Our revelations, they will be the residents of the Fire, staying there forever. What an evil destination!"* (64:10). Identical wording, identical position after a description of rejection — which tells the reader how to hear it. It is the settled term for an arrival that cannot be reversed, and the sūrah is using a fixed formula rather than inventing an insult.

**The Reaction Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

Sometimes the response is mockery: *“When Our clear revelations are recited to them, the disbelievers ask the believers ˹mockingly˺, ‘Which of the two of us is better in status and superior in assembly?’”* (19:73). Sometimes it is a charge of sorcery: *“Whenever Our clear revelations are recited to them, the disbelievers say of the truth when it has come to them, ‘This is pure magic’”* (46:7). Sometimes it is dismissal: *“whenever Our revelations are recited to him, he says, ‘Ancient fables!’”* (68:15).

The Qur'an uses the same interrogative frame when it wants to expose a ranking the audience has got backwards: *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Shall I inform you of those who deserve a worse punishment from Allah ˹than the rebellious˺?'"* (5:60).

## Verse 22:73

> O humanity! A lesson is set forth, so listen to it ˹carefully˺: those ˹idols˺ you invoke besides Allah can never create ˹so much as˺ a fly, even if they ˹all˺ were to come together for that. And if a fly were to snatch anything away from them, they cannot ˹even˺ retrieve it from the fly. How powerless are those who invoke and those invoked

**A Parable Announced Before It Is Told**

*Yā ayyuhā al-nāsu ḍuriba mathalun fa-stamiʿū lahu* — O humanity, a parable is struck, so listen to it.

The listening is commanded wherever recitation happens, and Sūrah al-Aʿrāf attaches the reason: *“When the Quran is recited, listen to it attentively and be silent, so you may be shown mercy”* (7:204). Verse 73's *fa-stamiʿū lahu* is that command sharpened for a single parable: the argument that follows is short, and missing a word of it means missing all of it.

**The Second Half Is Worse Than the First**

The scenario also echoes Abraham's confrontation with his people, where he broke the idols and left the largest one intact, and his people were forced to concede the point themselves: *“You already know that those ˹idols˺ cannot talk”* (21:65). In both passages the reduction is total, and in both it is achieved by the idolaters' own admission of what an idol can do.

Abraham drew the conclusion aloud, and the verse preserves his contempt: *“Shame on you and whatever you worship instead of Allah! Do you not have any sense?”* (21:67). The idols exposed first — silent, unanswering — and then the worshippers addressed with the question the exposure earned. Verse 73 follows the same two beats in reverse density: the demonstration compressed into the fly, the verdict compressed into the closing line. In both passages the order is demonstration first, contempt after, because the contempt has to be earned by the proof.

**The Fly in the Prophetic Teaching**

Abū Hurayrah narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said: *“If a housefly falls into the drink of any of you, let him dip it all in and then throw it away, for on one of its wings is a disease and on the other is a cure”* — recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (3320). Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī's version adds that the fly protects itself with the diseased wing, and the report also appears in *Sunan Abī Dāwūd* (3844, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī) and *Sunan Ibn Mājah* (3505).

**Why This Parable Rather Than Another**

The reduction is the method. Earlier arguments established that the idols cannot help (22:12 — *“They call besides Allah what can neither harm nor benefit them. That is ˹truly˺ the farthest one can stray”*), that they harm (22:13 — *“They invoke those whose worship leads to harm, not benefit. What an evil patron and what an evil associate”*), that no warrant was sent down for them (22:71 — *“Yet they worship besides Allah that for which He has sent down no authority, and of which they have no knowledge. The wrongdoers will have no helper”*), and that God is the only truth (22:62 — *“That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth and what they invoke besides Him is falsehood, and Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Most High, All-Great”*). The parable of the fly takes all of that and makes it small enough to hold in one image: the thing you are praying to cannot swat an insect.

The parable belongs to a stated method, and Sūrah al-Zumar names its purpose: *“We have certainly set forth every ˹kind of˺ lesson for people in this Quran, so perhaps they will be mindful”* (39:27). *Every* kind of lesson — the fly included — set forth *so that* remembrance follows. The parable is therefore not decoration on the argument but one of its kinds, and the sūrah deploys it last because reduction is the last move: once the idol has been measured against an insect and found weaker, mindfulness is the only thing left to ask for.

The Qur'an uses the same logic elsewhere in the same vocabulary: *“Do they associate ˹with Allah˺ those ˹idols˺ which cannot create anything, but are in fact created”* (7:191), and the challenge issued directly: *“Those ˹idols˺ you invoke besides Allah are created beings like yourselves. So call upon them and see if they will answer you, if your claims are true!”* (7:194).

Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt strikes the matching parable for the other half of false worship — not the strength claimed but the shelter sought: *“The parable of those who take protectors other than Allah is that of a spider spinning a shelter. And the flimsiest of all shelters is certainly that of a spider, if only they knew”* (29:41).

## Verse 22:74

> They have not shown Allah the reverence He deserves. Surely Allah is All-Powerful, Almighty

**The Idiom of Measuring**

*Mā qadarū Allāha ḥaqqa qadrih* — they did not measure God with the measure due to Him.

The verb *qadara* and the noun *qadr* belong to the vocabulary of measuring, estimating, and assigning a thing its proper worth. The phrase is an phrase for failing to size someone up correctly — for treating a great thing as small.

The phrase appears three times in the Qur'an, and the comparisons are what fix its meaning. In Sūrah al-Anʿām the same sentence is directed at people who refused the possibility of revelation itself: *"And they have not shown Allah His proper reverence when they said, 'Allah has revealed nothing to any human being'"* (6:91). In Sūrah al-Zumar it is attached to the largest image of divine scale in the scripture, the earth held in a grip and the heavens rolled up in a hand (39:67 — *“They have not shown Allah His proper reverence—when on the Day of Judgment the ˹whole˺ earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand.”*). One case is a denial that God speaks to men, one is a denial that He is as large as He is, and the third is the one in front of us. All three are failures of estimation, and in none of them is the existence of God the point at issue.

**How a Person Measures God**

The commentators put it two ways that amount to the same thing: al-Rāzī took the failure as a failure to give God the honour due to Him, since they set up stones beside Him; al-Ṭabarī took it as a failure to know Him as He ought to be known. Both are failures of estimation rather than of existence.

**The Parallel in Sūrah al-Zumar**

The wording of that verse makes the connection unmissable: *"They have not shown Allah His proper reverence—when on the Day of Judgment the ˹whole˺ earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand"* (39:67). The adverb *when* is doing the work. The failure of estimation is located at the moment of the largest possible disclosure, which is the sūrah's point exactly: the estimate is wrong now because it will be shown to be wrong then.

**The Charge Is Not Only for Idolaters**

A person can avoid idols completely and still hold an estimate of God that is too small — a God who is mainly a resource, or a God whose commands are negotiable, or a God whose warnings are understood as rhetoric. Every one of those is a failure of measurement, and the sūrah has described people in exactly those terms: the man on the brink who assumed the religion was a transaction (22:11 — *“And there are some who worship Allah on the verge ˹of faith˺: if they are blessed with something good, they are content with it; but if they are afflicted with a trial, they relapse ˹into disbelief˺, losing this world and the Hereafter. That is ˹truly˺ the clearest loss”*), and the doubters who concluded from a delay that nothing was coming (22:47 — *“They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment. And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting”*).

## Verse 22:75

> Allah selects messengers from both angels and people, for Allah is truly All-Hearing, All-Seeing

**Hearing and Seeing Close the Argument**

The pair opens a sūrah, and the occasion shows what the hearing covers: *“Indeed, Allah has heard the argument of the woman who pleaded with you ˹O Prophet˺ about her husband, and appealed to Allah. Allah has heard your exchange. Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing”* (58:1). A private complaint, heard twice over — the pleading and the exchange both registered. The mockery verse 75 answers is public and the complaint of Sūrah al-Mujādalah was private; the same two attributes cover both, which is the point of closing the selection verse with them. Nothing said about the chosen goes unheard, and no choosing is done unseen.

**Why a Human Messenger Is Fitting**

The sūrahs that answer this objection directly give two reasons, and both are worth having.

The first is that an angel would settle the matter by force of appearance. *“They say, ‘Why has no ˹visible˺ angel come with him?’ Had We sent down an angel, the matter would have certainly been settled ˹at once˺, and they would have never been given more time ˹to repent˺”* (6:8). Faith extracted by a visible angel is not faith, and the testing period would be over.

The second is that an angel would have to appear as a man anyway: *“And if We had sent an angel, We would have certainly made it ˹assume the form of˺ a man—leaving them more confused than they are”* (6:9). Sūrah al-Isrāʾ supplies the principle that governs the whole question: *“Had there been angels walking the earth, well settled, We would have surely sent down for them an angel from heaven as a messenger”* (17:95).

**The Objection's Own Incoherence**

The Qur'an records the objection in its own words, and the form it takes is worth noting: *“What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in marketplaces for a living?”* (25:7).

The demands escalate in the next breath, which exposes what they are: *“or a treasure had been cast down to him, or he had had a garden from which he may eat!”* (25:8). Angel, treasure, garden — each demand abandoned for a larger one before it can be met, and the sequence ends with the charge that the believers follow a bewitched man. A request for evidence stops when evidence arrives; a performance of contempt changes its terms.

**The Selection Is Not a Compromise**

Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ states the prerogative as an exclusion: *“Your Lord creates and chooses whatever He wills — the choice is not theirs”* (28:68). Creation and choice joined in one clause, and the second half addressed directly to the objectors: *mā kāna lahum al-khiyarah* — no share of the choosing belongs to them. Verse 75 asserts the right; Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ denies the rival claim. Between them they close the question the objection tried to open — who decides the fitness of a messenger — by removing the audience from the decision completely.

**The Verse's Connection to Verse 74**

The mismeasurement takes its gravest form where revelation itself is denied to human recipients: *“And they have not shown Allah His proper reverence when they said, ‘Allah has revealed nothing to any human being’”* (6:91). *Mā qadarū Allāha ḥaqqa qadrih* — the same charge as verse 74, attached to the same objection as verse 75. The reply given there is devastating in its economy: *who then revealed the Book of Moses?* — the objectors' own scripture turned into evidence against them. Verses 74 and 75 together are that argument restaged: the measuring corrected, the chosen defended, the deniers left with a Book they cannot account for.

The verb is the Qur'an's standing term for divine election, and Sūrah Āl ʿImrān applies it across history: *“Indeed, Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ʿImrān above all people ˹of their time˺”* (3:33).

Gabriel is identified as the one who carried the revelation down: *“Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know that he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah's Will”* (2:97).

## Verse 22:76
> He knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them. And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺

**Why the Clause Follows the Selection of Messengers**

The pronoun refers most immediately to the messengers of verse 75.

That placement is pointed. God chooses messengers, and He knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them — which is to say that the selection was not made in ignorance of what each of them would face. The rejections, the expulsions, the years in which nothing appeared to happen: all of it was known before the selection was made.

For the Prophet hearing this, that is the substance of the verse.

**The Sūrah's Attribute Pattern**

Verses 75 and 76 are part of a closing sequence in which the sūrah names the divine attributes in rapid succession: Hearing and Seeing (v. 75), knowing all and the return (v. 76).

The sequence is not confined to the last two verses.

From verse 59 onward the sūrah has been closing its paragraphs with the names of God. It ends one with the Most Forbearing (22:59 states it — *“He will certainly admit them into a place they will be pleased with. For Allah is truly All-Knowing, Most Forbearing”*), another with the Ever-Pardoning and All-Forgiving (22:60 states them — *“Surely Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving”*), another with the All-Hearing and All-Seeing (22:61 states them — *“That is because Allah causes the night to merge into the day, and the day into the night. Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing”*), another with the Truth and the Most High (22:62 states them — *“That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth and what they invoke besides Him is falsehood, and Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Most High, All-Great”*), another with the Most Subtle and All-Aware (22:63 states them — *“Do you not see that Allah sends down rain from the sky, then the earth becomes green? Surely Allah is Most Subtle, All-Aware”*), another with the Self-Sufficient and Praiseworthy (22:64 states them — *“To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy”*), and another with the Ever Gracious and Most Merciful (22:65 states them — *“Surely Allah is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to humanity”*). This verse closes in the same way, naming God as hearing, seeing and knowing (22:76 states all three), which is the note the whole passage has been building.

The reader who follows the closing words of the paragraphs from verse 59 to verse 76 is given a complete account of the divine character in eighteen verses — and it is placed at the end of the sūrah on purpose, because everything the chapter demanded is being rested on who is making the demand.

**The Verse as a Bridge**

Verses 74 to 76 form an unit: the failure to measure God, the right of selection, and the knowledge and return that make the selection meaningful.

The bridge is also a reply to the sūrah's own opening. Verse 1 told humanity to fear its Lord because the Hour is a dreadful thing, and the whole chapter has been spent showing that the Hour is real. What verses 74 to 76 add is the standing implication: if matters are returned, then the things people do with them are not lost. A command to strive makes sense only against that background, and the sūrah places the premise immediately before the command.

**The Clause Answers the Sūrah's Hardest Question**

Every sūrah that promises a judgment owes its reader an answer to one objection: why is the judgment taking so long?

This sūrah has taken the question more seriously than most. It devoted three verses to the demand that the punishment be hurried (22:47 — *“They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment. And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting”*), answered that the measures of time are incommensurable, and then documented the pattern in the ruins of nine communities (22:42–48 — *“If they deny you ˹O Prophet˺, so did the people of Noah before them, as well as ˹the tribes of˺ ’Ȃd and Thamûd”*). Verse 76 is the last word on it. All matters are returned, and the return has a destination that is not in question.

Those three answers were given separately and they belong together. Verse 47 said the delay is not a delay on the only clock that matters. Verse 70 said nothing is being lost while the wait continues, because it is all in a record. Verse 76 says where the record is going. A community that had been taunted for ten years about a punishment that had not arrived is given, in closing, a reason for the wait, an account of what is happening during it, and a destination at the end of it.

## Verse 22:77

> O believers! Bow down, prostrate yourselves, worship your Lord, and do ˹what is˺ good so that you may be successful

**The Address Changes**

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū* — O you who believe.

This is the first time in the sūrah that the believers are addressed by this formula. For seventy-six verses the sūrah has said *O humanity* (22:1 — *“O humanity! Fear your Lord, for the ˹violent˺ quaking at the Hour is surely a dreadful thing”*, 22:49 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O humanity! I am only sent to you with a clear warning”*, 22:73 — *“O humanity! A lesson is set forth, so listen to it ˹carefully˺: those ˹idols˺ you invoke besides Allah can never create ˹so much as˺ a fly, even if they ˹all˺ were to come together for that. And if a fly were to snatch anything away from them, they cannot ˹even˺ retrieve it from the fly. How powerless are those who invoke and those invoked”*) or spoken about the believers in the third person. Now, at the very end, it turns and speaks to them directly.

The address introduces obligation wherever it appears, and Sūrah al-Māʾidah opens on the pattern: *“O believers! Honour your obligations”* (5:1). The vocative is a summons to a covenant already made — not *become* believers but *you who believe*, act as what you are. Verse 77 is that summons placed at the end of the proof rather than the beginning of the law: the obligations that follow — bowing, prostration, worship, good deeds — arrive as the answer the argument was building toward, not as demands issued before it.

**The Second Prostration of This Sūrah**

ʿUqbah ibn ʿĀmir reported that he asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ whether there are two prostrations in Sūrat al-Ḥajj, and was told: *“Yes, and whoever does not perform them should not recite them”* — recorded in *Sunan Abī Dāwūd* (1402), where al-Albānī graded the chain weak, though others have accepted it. A related report from ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, also in Abū Dāwūd (1401), counts fifteen prostrations in the Qur'an, among them two in this sūrah. The first is at verse 18.

**What the Bowing Means**

The sūrah has also been insistent that the inward state is what matters (22:32 — *“That is so. And whoever honours the symbols of Allah, it is certainly out of the piety of the heart”*, 22:37 — *“Neither their meat nor blood reaches Allah. Rather, it is your piety that reaches Him. This is how He has subjected them to you so that you may proclaim the greatness of Allah for what He has guided you to, and give good news to the good-doers”*). Placing that insistence beside a command to perform a physical act resolves a question readers often force into a contradiction: the inward state is what the act is for, and the act is how the inward state is expressed. Neither is the other's rival.

The first sūrah revealed ends on the same act, joined to its result: *“Rather, ˹continue to˺ prostrate and draw near ˹to Allah˺”* (96:19). Prostration and nearness in a single imperative — the lowest posture producing the closest approach, which is the paradox the act embodies. The face on the ground is not abasement accepted but distance closed: revelation began by commanding the Prophet to the ground, and this sūrah all but ends by commanding the believers to join him there.

**"Do Good" Is the Command That Has No Limit**

Sūrah al-Baqarah makes the open command a competition: *“So compete with one another in doing good. Wherever you are, Allah will bring you all together ˹for judgment˺”* (2:148). Verse 77's fourth imperative is that race placed last in the list, after the specified acts are complete: the bowing has a form and the worship has its rites, but the good has only a direction, and the sūrah sends the believers running in it.

**Why This Comes Before the Final Verse**

The sūrah's last verse will command striving — a word that, in the sūrah's own context, has just been used for fighting (22:39 — *“Permission ˹to fight back˺ is ˹hereby˺ granted to those being fought, for they have been wronged. And Allah is truly Most Capable of helping them ˹prevail˺”*), for emigration (22:58 — *“As for those who emigrate in the cause of Allah and then are martyred or die, Allah will indeed grant them a good provision. Surely Allah is the Best Provider”*) and for the campaign against the signs (22:51 — *“But those who strive to discredit Our revelations, they will be the residents of the Hellfire.””*).

The striving that follows has its own promise, and the promise explains the ordering: *“As for those who struggle in Our cause, We will surely guide them along Our Way. And Allah is certainly with the good-doers”* (29:69). Struggle first, guidance following — the guidance is the *result* of the striving, not its precondition. Verse 77 places the bowing before the striving for the same reason the promise places the striving before the guidance: each act prepares the next, and the person who strives without first having bowed has started the sequence in the middle.

The pairing of prostration and bowing is commanded elsewhere as the shape of devotion itself: *“O Mary! Be devout to your Lord, prostrate yourself ˹in prayer˺ and bow along with those who bow down”* (3:43).

Sūrah Āl ʿImrān closes the way this sūrah nearly does — imperatives stacked, success hoped: *“O believers! Patiently endure, persevere, stand on guard, and be mindful of Allah, so you may be successful”* (3:200).

## Verse 22:78

> Strive for ˹the cause of˺ Allah in the way He deserves, for ˹it is˺ He ˹Who˺ has chosen you, and laid upon you no hardship in the religion—the way of your forefather Abraham. ˹It is Allah˺ Who named you ‘the ones who submit’ ˹in the˺ earlier ˹Scriptures˺ and in this ˹Quran˺, so that the Messenger may be a witness over you, and that you may be witnesses over humanity. So establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and hold fast to Allah. He ˹alone˺ is your Guardian. What an excellent Guardian, and what an excellent Helper

**"And Has Not Laid Upon You Any Hardship in the Religion"**

*Wa-mā jaʿala ʿalaykum fī al-dīni min ḥaraj* — and He has placed no *ḥaraj* upon you in the religion.

The jurists filled the phrase with content: the concessions granted under difficulty, such as shortening the prayers while travelling and praying seated or lying down when ill, and the general ruling that no sin is without a way out of it. Sūrah al-Baqarah states the principle in a form that covers every obligation: *“Allah intends ease for you, not hardship”* (2:185), and *“Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford”* (2:286).

**"The Creed of Your Father Abraham"**

*Millata abīkum Ibrāhīm* — the creed of your father Abraham.

The word *millah* means a religion considered as a way rather than as a set of propositions, and the Qur'an attaches it to Abraham consistently: followers of his creed are commanded to follow it in sūrah after sūrah (3:95 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah has declared the truth. So follow the Way of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.””*; 4:125 — *“And who is better in faith than those who ˹fully˺ submit themselves to Allah, do good, and follow the Way of Abraham, the upright? Allah chose Abraham as a close friend”*; 6:161 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Surely my Lord has guided me to the Straight Path, a perfect way, the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists.””*; 16:123 — *“Then We revealed to you ˹O Prophet, saying˺: “Follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists.””*). In nearly every occurrence the word *ḥanīf* appears with it, which returns the reader to verse 31 of this sūrah and to the turning that the whole chapter has been demanding.

**"He Named You Submitters Before, and in This"**

Two readings were recorded. Some took the naming as Abraham's prayer, recorded in Sūrah al-Baqarah, that God make him and his progeny a community submitting to Him (2:128 — *“Our Lord! Make us both ˹fully˺ submit to You and from our descendants a nation that will submit to you. Show us our rituals, and turn to us in grace. You are truly the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful”*) — which would make the name ancient and petitioned for. Others took the subject as God: He named you submitters in the earlier scriptures and now in this recitation.

**The Closing Commands**

The verb *iʿtaṣimū* means to grip, to take hold of something for protection. The Qur'an uses the same image elsewhere of holding fast to a rope (3:103 — *“And hold firmly to the rope of Allah and do not be divided.”*), and the image is of a person holding on rather than of a person standing on his own.

The holding-fast is commanded to the community as its unity, with the rope named: *“And hold firmly to the rope of Allah and do not be divided”* (3:103). *Wa-ʿtaṣimū bi-ḥabli Allāh* — grip the rope, refuse division. Verse 78's *wa-ʿtaṣimū bi-Llāh* shortens the image to its essence: the rope is God Himself, and the holding is the sūrah's last instruction after seventy-seven verses of reasons. Prayer established, alms given, and then the grip — the religion ending where it began, with a person holding on.

**The Last Words**

The sūrah that opened with an earthquake closes with two nouns: *mawlā*, the protecting patron, and *naṣīr*, the ally who comes when called. Both are the words the sūrah used for what the idolaters thought they had — a patron (22:13 — *“They invoke those whose worship leads to harm, not benefit. What an evil patron and what an evil associate”*) and a helper (22:71 — *“Yet they worship besides Allah that for which He has sent down no authority, and of which they have no knowledge. The wrongdoers will have no helper”*) — and both are here returned to their true owner.

Sūrah al-Anfāl closes an address to the fledgling community in the identical words: *“then know that Allah is your Protector. What an excellent Protector, and what an excellent Helper!”* (8:40). *Niʿma al-mawlā wa-niʿma al-naṣīr* — the same superlatives, the same two nouns, spoken to believers facing an enemy they could not match. Verse 78 borrows the ending because the situation rhymes: a community told to strive beyond its strength is left with the Patron who is beyond every strength. The sūrah's last sentence is older than the sūrah, and it holds.

Sūrah Fāṭir names what the choosing conveys, and it is the Book itself: *“Then We granted the Book to those We have chosen from Our servants”* (35:32).
