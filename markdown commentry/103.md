# Sūrah al-ʿAṣr (Chapter 103) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary
## Sūrah al-ʿAṣr 103:1

> By the ˹passage of˺ time!

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath by Time That Is Running Out**

*Wa-al-ʿaṣr* — by *al-ʿaṣr*. The word can mean late afternoon, the last part of the day, the afternoon prayer, an epoch, or time as such. The translation chooses “the passage of time,” which is the widest sense and a fair umbrella. Lexically *ʿaṣara* is to squeeze, press, extract. Time that is *ʿaṣr* is time being pressed, the day squeezed toward night, a life squeezed toward death, an age squeezed toward its end. God swears by that pressure. An oath by time is not an oath by a calm river. It is an oath by a shrinking resource. The *jawāb* will be that man is in loss. The oath already explains why: if time is being squeezed, standing still is falling.

**Prayer, Epoch, and Afternoon Light**

Many jurists and exegetes, including paths recorded from al-Bayḍāwī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Zamakhsharī, prefer the ʿaṣr prayer, often identified with the “middle prayer” of *“Be mindful of the prayers—especially the middle prayer”* (2:238), though that identification is itself disputed. Some say the era of the Prophet, because of its *faḍl* in the return of prophethood. Some say time in general. The prayer-reading has a moral bite: the day has a named hour that people miss, and missing it is a picture of missing a life. The epoch-reading has a historical bite: the species itself is in a late afternoon. The time-reading is the one that makes the next verse a law for every hour. None of these needs to be killed for the others. The squeezed afternoon is a good first picture because everyone has seen a day fail.

**A Sūrah Companions Would Not Part Without**

Ibn Kathīr records that when two Companions met, they would not part until one had recited *al-ʿAṣr* and they had given *salām*. The report, however one grades a given chain, matches the sūrah's size and density. Three verses were treated as a complete counsel. Al-Shāfiʿī's word is also famous: if people pondered this sūrah, it would suffice them. That is not a claim that the rest of the Book is extra. It is a claim that the axis — time, loss, the four exceptions — is the whole message in miniature. Verse 1 is the first quarter of that miniature: the clock is a witness.

**Time as the Hidden Subject of Takāthur**

The previous sūrah ended with a question about pleasures. This sūrah begins with time. The link taught by some is that vying for increase is how people waste the thing they will be asked about, and time is that thing's vessel. Whether or not the canonical order is the chronological order, the muṣḥaf's sequence is a teaching: after “you will be asked about *naʿīm*” comes “by the squeezed day.” Pleasure eats hours. The oath puts the hours on the stand before the eater is named.

**Why God Swears by a Passing Thing**

Oaths in the Book take signs: dawn, fig, wind, chargers. Time is less a thing than a measure. Swearing by it makes the measure itself a sign. You do not own what you are measured with. *“He is the One Who created night and day, the sun and the moon, each floating in its orbit”* (21:33). *Al-ʿaṣr* as afternoon is one slice of that orbit. As epoch it is the slice that contains us. As prayer it is a commanded stop inside the slice. The verse is only two words in Arabic. They are enough to put every later excuse — “I will, later” — under an oath that later is the thing being squeezed.

**The Arabic Still Has Work After the First Pass**

The line of 103:1 is short in the muṣḥaf and long in the mouth of a teacher who will not stop at a gloss. al-ʿAṣr places this verse inside a sūrah whose name is already an argument, and the local theme — the squeezed afternoon — does not exhaust the grammar. Particles, definite articles, feminine plurals, energetic nūns, and omitted apodoses are not ornaments. They are how the threat or the oath actually works. A reciter who only translates into a slogan has dropped the tools. Classical iʿrāb on a verse like this is not a hobby for specialists. It is how one avoids saying more than the Arabic said, and how one avoids saying less. Where the translation uses brackets, those brackets are tafsīr. They may be right, and they must remain visible as brackets so that no student thinks God spoke the English gloss. Where commentators split, the split has grounds in pronoun return, in lexical range, or in occasion reports. Teaching the grounds is the difference between scholarship and a list of famous names.

**Conduct Follows if the Verse Is Believed**

Belief in 103:1 is not a nod during tarāwīḥ. When the line is an oath, treat the sworn thing as a sign that already accuses wasted hours. A question in God's mouth means stop pretending folklore has the answer. A threat means leave the type while tawbah is open. An exception-clause means become the verbs, not wear a group label. al-ʿAṣr is recited in two rakʿahs by people who then return to counting, mocking, or sleeping through ʿaṣr. That return is the opposite of tafsīr. Ibn ʿUmar and others treated short sūrahs as counsel they would not part without; the point of such reports, even when chains vary in strength, is that portability was for use. Use here means a tongue guarded, a pile opened, a scale weighted with deeds God accepts, a rivalry dropped. The verse does not need a new miracle to be heavy. It needs a day that looks different after it.

**A Checked Parallel, Not a Stolen Verse**

*“Be mindful of the prayers—especially the middle prayer”* (2:238) belongs to another place in the Book, and it is brought here only as a witness, not as a second assignment for this commentary. If ʿaṣr is the prayer, this oath puts a named hour in the witness box; if ʿaṣr is time, the prayer is still one way of honouring the squeeze. Cross-reference is a servant of the present line. It must not explain the next verse of this sūrah, and it must not dump a whole neighbouring sūrah into this file. The discipline is: one extra checked fact, tagged, then back to 103:1. Occasions of revelation, when reported, are historical helps. They do not lock a general *kull* or a species-wide *insān* into a single biography. Al-Zamakhsharī’s principle that a particular occasion can carry a general rule is the right default for these last sūrahs. Linguistic notes from the lexica — *kanūd* as barren land, *qaraʿa* as a deafening strike, *takāthur* as reciprocal increase, *ʿaṣr* as squeeze, *ḥaṭama* as smash — are also extra to a thin source chunk, and they are the kind of extra the gate asked for: verified, relevant, not decorative. The present verse remains the only defendant. The parallel is a lamp, not a second courtroom.

## Sūrah al-ʿAṣr 103:2

> Surely humanity is in ˹grave˺ loss,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Jawāb: Man Is in Loss**

*Inna al-insāna la-fī khusr* — truly the human being is in loss. This is the answer to the oath. *Khusr* is loss in trade, a deal that ends in deficit, ruin of capital. The extra *lām* and *inna* refuse a mild reading. *Al-insān* is the species in its default, or, as some say, the disbeliever. Al-Qurṭubī records both. The species-reading is stronger next to verse 3's exception: you do not except a subset from a subset; you except a subset from the whole. Humanity as such, left to the squeeze of time without the four traits of verse 3, is in deficit.


The Qur'an names losers as those who lose themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection: *“Say, ‘The ˹true˺ losers are those who will lose themselves and their families on Judgment Day. That is indeed the clearest loss’”* (39:15). Also *“There are some who worship Allah on the verge… He has lost both this world and the Hereafter. That is truly the clearest loss”* (22:11). *Khusr* is not only money. It is the self. Time is the capital. Deeds are the trade. The ungrateful fierce lover of goods from *al-ʿĀdiyāt* and the competitor of *at-Takāthur* are case studies of this line. *“The disbelief of the disbelievers only increases them in loss”* (35:39).


*Fī khusr* is a container. They are inside it, now. The afternoon oath explains the present tense: while the day is being squeezed, a life not spent on the exception-clause is already a losing trade, not a trade that will be reviewed later and then perhaps fail. Later will only make the loss *mubīn*, clear. Now it is already *khusr*. That doctrine kills the idea of a morally neutral waiting. Waiting is spending capital. *“By the time”* and “in loss” are almost one idea in two verses.


*“Then We reduced him to the lowest of the low, except those who believe and do good”* (95:5–6) is the sister of 103:2–3. *Tīn* swears by fig and olive and Sinai and a secure town; *ʿaṣr* swears by time. Both then drop man and except the workers of faith. The parallel is a reason to take *al-insān* as the kind, not only Makkan pagans. The exception would be odd if the rule were only about one tribe.

**Grave in Brackets**

The translator adds “grave” to loss. The Arabic is *khusr* without an adjective. The adjective is tafsīr of intensity, matching *al-khusrān al-mubīn* elsewhere. It is allowed as English emphasis if no one thinks a second Arabic word is there. The loss is grave because the capital is a whole self and a whole span of *ʿaṣr*. A small loss you can recoup. This one, at death, you cannot. Verse 3 is the only stated recoup in the sūrah.

## Sūrah al-ʿAṣr 103:3

> except those who have faith, do good, and urge each other to the truth, and urge each other to perseverance.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Traits, One Exception**

*Illā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt wa-tawāṣaw bi-al-ḥaqqi wa-tawāṣaw bi-al-ṣabr* — except those who believed, did righteous deeds, enjoined one another to truth, and enjoined one another to patience. The exception is a community, not a lone mystic only. Two verbs are inner-personal (faith, work); two are reciprocal (*tawāṣaw*). Rescue from *khusr* is not a private spirituality that never speaks. It is faith that becomes work and work that becomes mutual counsel.


The Book's most common saved group is *alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt*. Faith without work is incomplete in Qur'anic speech; work without faith is not the work that saves. This sūrah could have stopped at the pair, as 95:6 almost does. It adds *tawāṣī* twice. The addition is the sūrah's signature. A believing worker who never urges another still lives in a world of loss around him and may not remain. The reciprocal form says: they keep each other.


Truth may be God, the Qur'an, *tawḥīd*, or the word one leaves to family at death — *“do not die except as Muslims”* (3:102). Al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, and al-Ṭabrisī distribute these. The strongest first sense is truth as the revealed real, God and what He said, against the lies of *takāthur* and *kanūd*. Urging to truth is the social form of *īmān*. It includes correction, teaching, and refusing to let a brother live inside a false score. It is not a licence for harshness without *ṣabr*. The two *tawāṣī*s are a pair like faith and work: know and do; speak truth and bear the cost.


*“O believers! Be patient, persevere, and stand on guard”* (3:200); *“and they encourage one another to perseverance”* (90:17). Patience here is staying with practice under opposition, time's squeeze, and the delay of results. Urging to *ṣabr* is how a community survives an afternoon that feels like it will not end. Without it, truth-talkers burn out or turn cruel. Without truth, the patient become patient in error. Al-Rāzī's line that patience is keeping the religion when it is hard is the right practical sense.

**The Whole Message in Three Verses**

Al-Shāfiʿī's “it would suffice” is this structure: time is witness; man is in loss; except a people of faith, work, truth-counsel, patience-counsel. Nothing in the later fiqh and ʿaqīdah contradicts that axis. Everything else is how. A reciter who finishes *al-ʿAṣr* and returns to *takāthur* has not excepted himself. The exception is not a label you stick on a sect. It is four living verbs. The sūrah is small so that no one can claim he had no time to learn the way out of loss. Time itself was the first word, and the way out was the last.
