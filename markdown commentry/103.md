# Sūrah al-ʿAṣr (Chapter 103) — Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Time, Loss, and the Way Out**

Al-ʿAṣr, "The Declining Day" or "Time," is the one-hundred-and-third chapter: three verses revealed in Makkah. It is one of the shortest chapters in the Qur'an, and al-Shāfiʿī is famously reported to have said that if people reflected on this sūrah alone it would be enough for them. Al-Ṭabarānī recorded of the Companions that when two of them met, they would not part until one had recited al-ʿAṣr to the other, after which they exchanged greetings of peace. The sūrah swears an oath by time, declares that humanity is in loss, and then states the four conditions that rescue a person from it: faith, righteous action, counselling one another to truth, and counselling one another to patience.

**Time as Melting Capital**

*Al-ʿAṣr* can mean the late afternoon — the hour when the sun drops quickly toward the horizon and the day's market closes — as well as a long epoch, or time itself. Its root means to squeeze or press, so the word carries the sense of time being pressed toward its end. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī passed on the image of the ice-seller of Baghdad calling out in the heat, "Have mercy on a man whose capital is melting!" Time is that capital. Every hour spent is gone beyond recovery, and verse 2 states the verdict that follows: humanity is in loss. Wealth, fame, and youth are ice as well. What the sūrah offers is not a way to slow the melting but a way to spend what remains on something that does not melt.

**Four Conditions, Not Alternatives**

Verse 3 names four things, and they are not four options. A person may believe and do nothing with his belief; he may do good privately and stay silent when truth is at stake; he may speak for the truth and collapse when it costs him. The sūrah treats each of these as still in loss. Faith turns into action, action turns into public counsel, and counsel survives only with patience. Two of the four are personal — *āmanū*, they believed, and *ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt*, they did righteous deeds — and two are reciprocal, *tawāṣaw*, counselling one another, because living the first two in a world of loss is not a private project.

## Sūrah al-ʿAṣr 103:1

> By the ˹passage of˺ time

**An Oath by a Shrinking Resource**

The sūrah opens with an oath, and the thing sworn by is not a mountain or a star but time. Commentators read *al-ʿaṣr* in several ways: the late afternoon, the ʿaṣr prayer, a long epoch, or time in general. Al-Zamakhsharī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Bayḍāwī are among those who favour the prayer, which many identify with *"the middle prayer"* of *"Be mindful of the prayers—especially the middle prayer"* (2:238), though that identification is itself disputed. Others prefer the era of the Prophet ﷺ, on the grounds that it carried the return of prophethood; others take it as time as such. The afternoon reading has the sharpest image, since everyone has watched a day fail — the light going, the work unfinished, the market shutting.

**God Swears by What People Waste**

Oaths in the Qur'an usually call created things to witness: the dawn, the fig, the wind, the charging horses. Swearing by time makes the measure itself a witness, and no one owns the thing he is measured by. The verse is only two words in Arabic, and its work in the sūrah is to put the clock in the witness box before the accused is named. The next verse will say that humanity is in loss; the oath already explains why, because a resource being squeezed means that standing still is falling behind.

## Sūrah al-ʿAṣr 103:2

> humanity is truly in ˹great˺ loss

**The Default Condition**

*Khusr* belongs to the language of trade: a loss that eats into capital. The Qur'an's stronger form of the word, *al-khusrān al-mubīn*, the clear loss, is used of those who will lose themselves and their families on the Day of Judgment (39:15). The verse does not say that people are in danger of loss, or that many of them are; it says that *al-insān*, humanity as a kind, is in it. That is a statement about the default. Time is spent by everyone, and the only question is what it was spent on. The same sequence — an oath, a verdict on humanity, then an exception — shapes Sūrah al-Tīn, which swears by the fig, the olive, Mount Sinai, and a secure city, and then excepts those who believe and do righteous deeds (95:1–6). The parallel is a reason to read *al-insān* here as the human kind generally rather than as the people of Makkah alone.

**"Loss" Without an Adjective**

The Arabic says simply *khusr*, with no adjective attached; "great" or "grave" in English translations is an addition of emphasis, matching the Qur'an's stronger phrase used elsewhere. The emphasis is warranted by what is being lost. A small loss can be made up, but the loss of a whole self and a whole span of time cannot be made up at death. Verse 3 is the only way out that the sūrah states.

## Sūrah al-ʿAṣr 103:3

> except those who have faith, do good, and urge each other to the truth, and urge each other to perseverance

**Four Conditions Treated as One**

The exception is written in four parts, while the Qur'an's most frequent description of the saved is only the first two: *alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāt*, those who believe and do righteous deeds. This sūrah adds two more, and both take the reciprocal form *tawāṣaw* — they counsel one another. Faith alone is therefore not the exception, and neither is faith joined to private good works. The two additions make the rescue a shared one: those who are saved keep one another from sliding back into loss.

**Counselling One Another to the Truth**

The first reciprocal act is *tawāṣī bi-l-ḥaqq*, urging one another to the truth. Commentators such as al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, and al-Ṭabrisī read *al-ḥaqq* here as the revealed truth — God and what He sent down — set against the illusions of rivalry and ingratitude that the neighbouring sūrahs describe. The Qur'an uses the same word of the instruction a believer leaves with his family at death: *"do not die except as Muslims"* (3:102). In practice, urging the truth covers teaching, correction, and refusing to let a companion go on living inside a false accounting of himself. It is faith in its social form, and the verse pairs it with patience because truth spoken without patience turns harsh and then collapses.

**Counselling One Another to Patience**

The second reciprocal act is *tawāṣī bi-l-ṣabr*. Two other passages use the same expression: *"O believers! Be patient, persevere, and stand on guard"* (3:200), and, in Sūrah al-Balad, *"and they encourage one another to perseverance"* (90:17). Patience in this sense is not passivity; al-Rāzī defined it as holding on to the religion when holding on is hard. It belongs in the exception because the other three conditions carry a cost. A community that urges truth without urging patience produces people who burn out or turn cruel; a community that urges patience without truth produces people who endure quietly in the wrong direction.

**Why the Sūrah Is So Short**

Al-Shāfiʿī's remark that the sūrah would suffice people describes what three lines manage to do: name the witness, state the verdict, and give the way out. Nothing in the later law or creed contradicts that axis; everything else in the religion explains how to live it. The brevity is part of the argument. The way out of loss was given in a sūrah short enough that no one can plead he had no time to learn it — and the first word of that sūrah was time itself.
