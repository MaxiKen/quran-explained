# Sūrah at-Taghābun (Chapter 64) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:1

> Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth ˹constantly˺ glorifies Allah. The kingdom is His, and all praise is for Him. For He is Most Capable of everything

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Whole Creation Speaks First**

Before the sūrah says anything about human beings, it states what everything else is doing. The heavens and the earth and what is in them glorify Allah, *yusabbiḥu*, and the verb is in the imperfect, describing an action in progress rather than a completed event. The claim is total: nothing in the two realms is exempt, and nothing in the verse suggests that the glorification is a human activity that the rest of creation merely accompanies. This is the opening move of a group of sūrahs that begin the same way — al-Ḥadīd, al-Ḥashr, aṣ-Ṣaff, al-Jumuʿah, and this one — and the group has a purpose. A chapter that will divide humanity into believers and disbelievers starts by placing humanity inside a much larger company, most of which is already doing what the disbelievers refuse to do.

**The Tense Is Part of the Argument**

The comparison between the openings of these sūrahs is not idle, because the Qur'an shifts the verb deliberately. “Whatever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies Allah” (57:1) and the parallel openings at 59:1 and 61:1 use the past tense, *sabbaḥa*. This verse, along with “Whatever is in the heavens and the earth ˹constantly˺ glorifies Him” (59:24) and the opening of al-Jumuʿah (62:1), uses the present. The imperative appears in a third place: “Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High” (87:1). Al-Rāzī's explanation of the pattern is that the past tense covers glorification from the beginning of time to now, the present covers it from now forward, since Arabic uses the imperfect for what is continuing and coming, and the imperative covers the present moment. The effect is that glorification is described as uninterrupted across all three tenses.

**Kingdom and Praise, Stated as One Fact**

The second sentence joins two claims that the Qur'an usually keeps close together: sovereignty and praise. “The kingdom is His” establishes that there is no rival jurisdiction anywhere in what was just described, and “all praise is for Him” establishes that the recognition due to the sovereign is not divided either. The pairing answers an objection before it is raised. A ruler may hold power without deserving praise, and a person may deserve praise without holding power; the verse asserts both at once, and the glorification described in the first sentence is the evidence for the second. The same two ideas appear in the opening of al-Jumuʿah, where the sūrah calls Him “the King, the Most Holy, the Almighty, the All-Wise” (62:1). Praise here is not flattery offered by subjects who can withhold it. It is the accurate description of a thing.

**Power Over Everything Sets Up the Rest of the Chapter**

The closing clause — “For He is Most Capable of everything” (64:1) — is doing structural work rather than decoration. The disputes the sūrah will address are about resurrection and about a day of gathering, and both turn on the question of whether God can do what the disbelievers find impossible. Placing the statement of universal power in the opening verse supplies the premise in advance. Everything later follows from it: the gathering of every generation on one plain, the return of what has rotted, the reckoning of every act. A reader who accepts the clause has already conceded the argument that verses 7 and 9 will make explicit, and a reader who rejects it has identified the exact point at which he parts company with the sūrah.

**How Does a Mountain Glorify?**

The commentators ask what kind of speech is involved when a stone or a bird is said to glorify God, and they give two answers with different consequences. The first holds that every created thing glorifies by its very constitution: its order, its limit, and its dependence declare a maker, so the glorification is the thing's own nature read correctly. The second holds that the glorification is real speech, inaudible to us but genuine. The Qur'an supports the second reading more directly than the first: “There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification” (17:44), which describes an inaudibility rather than a metaphor, and “Each ˹instinctively˺ knows their manner of prayer and glorification” (24:41), which speaks of a manner rather than a resemblance. The two readings need not be separated by a wall: what a thing is made to be and what it says about its maker are not competing accounts. The reader's own position is the same either way, since the verse is not asking him to hear the glorification of stones but to notice that his is the only voice in the two realms that can stay silent by choice.

**Why a Chapter About Two Groups Begins Here**

The frame matters for everything that follows. The sūrah's subject is a division inside humanity, and its opening verse describes a creation in which that division does not exist: everything in the heavens and the earth is on one side. Human beings are the only creatures who can withhold the glorification that the rest of creation offers without effort, and the rest of the chapter treats that capacity as both a dignity and a risk. The kingdom is His whether or not it is acknowledged, and the praise is His whether or not it is given. What changes is the position of the one who withholds it. The sūrah will describe that position in verse 10, and it begins here by showing how unusual it is.

**A Frame the Rest of the Chapter Keeps Returning To**

The opening verse is not a preface that can be skipped, because the chapter refers back to it at the end. The final verse names God as “the Knower of the seen and unseen—the Almighty, All-Wise” (64:18), and the middle verses build a case on power and knowledge. Between the two stands everything the sūrah argues about resurrection, gathering, and mutual loss. A reader who grants the opening statement has granted the capacity that the argument requires; a reader who denies it has to explain how a creation that glorifies its maker without being asked is compatible with the claim that this maker cannot restore what He made.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:2

> He is the One Who created you, yet some of you are disbelievers while some are believers. And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Act of Creation, Two Outcomes**

The verse puts a single fact and a single division side by side, and the conjunction between them is the point. He created you — all of you, from one act, without distinction — *and yet* some of you disbelieve while some believe. The Arabic *fa-minhum kāfirun wa-minhum muʾmin* is not a causal statement; it does not say that the creation produced the division. It records that the division exists inside a made and identical group, which is the puzzle the verse leaves standing. The same maker, the same material, the same provisions, and then two opposite responses. By refusing to explain the difference, the verse keeps responsibility with the responders, and it makes the reader look at the difference as a fact about people rather than as a fact about how they were made.

**Only God Can See Which Group You Are In**

The verse names categories that are partly hidden. A community can observe worship, hear profession, and watch conduct, and it must act on what it can observe; the sūrah before this one is largely about the gap between appearance and reality. What no observer can see is the settled state of a heart. The clause “And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do” (64:2) closes the verse on that point: the assessment belongs to a watcher who does not work from appearances. The practical consequence for the community is restraint. It cannot sort people into these two groups with confidence, and it is not asked to. It is asked to keep its rules and to be honest about what it does not know, which is precisely the discipline the hypocrites of Madina exploited.

**The Report About Endings**

The Prophet ﷺ described a scene that makes this division harder to read from the outside. A man fought with the Muslims more bravely than anyone on a campaign, and the Prophet ﷺ said that whoever wished to look at a man of the Fire should look at him. The man was wounded and then killed himself, and a Companion who had doubted the Prophet's words came running to testify that he was indeed God's Messenger. The Prophet ﷺ then said: “A man may do the deeds of the people of the Fire while in fact he is one of the people of Paradise, and he may do the deeds of the people of Paradise while in fact he belongs to the people of Fire, and verily, (the rewards of) the deeds are decided by the last actions (deeds)” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6607). The report is not about predestination in the abstract; it is a warning against reading the two categories off a person's current performance.

**The Categories Are Described in the Present**

“Some of you are disbelievers while some are believers” (64:2) is a statement about a current condition, not a permanent classification stamped at birth. The same verse affirms that God created everyone, and it affirms the division without fixing it for ever. The Qur'an elsewhere describes people who believed and then disbelieved, and the reverse movement is the whole point of calling people to faith. That mobility is what makes the verse a warning rather than a report. A person described today as a believer is described as holding something that can be lost, and a person described today as a disbeliever is addressed by a book that expects him to change. The sūrah's later verses issue exactly that call, telling the reader to believe in God, His Messenger, and the light that has been sent down.

**What “All-Seeing” Adds to “All-Knowing”**

The verse could have closed on knowledge, and the sūrah does close its next two verses on knowledge. It closes this one on sight. *Baṣīr* is the one who sees, and the object named is what you do — acts, in the open, performed in front of other people. The emphasis is deliberate. The hidden states of the heart will be handled in verse 4; here the concern is conduct, the part of a person's religion that other people see and rely on. Acts are seen by others too, but they are seen partly and for a while, and the witness of other people can be manipulated by a man who manages his appearances. A witness who sees every act, including the ones nobody watched, is what makes the two categories real rather than reputational.

**What the Verse Asks of Its Reader**

Read from inside the group being described, the verse is uncomfortable in a specific way. It tells the reader that he is one of two things, that he may not know which, and that he cannot settle the question by looking at his own record of good behaviour. The proper response is not anxiety about classification but attention to direction, since the categories describe a direction of travel that is still under way. The sūrah supplies the test it wants applied: belief in God and His Messenger, obedience, spending, and trust. These are acts, and acts are visible to the one doing them. The verse ends on the seeing of God rather than on the seeing of the community, which is a mercy for the person whose outward life looks better than his inward one and a warning to the person for whom it looks worse. Neither of them can appeal to the judgement of the room he is standing in. The two groups named at the start of the verse are therefore open, and the reader who belongs to the better of them today belongs to it by conduct that can be continued or abandoned tomorrow.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:3

> He created the heavens and the earth for a purpose. He shaped you ˹in the womb˺, perfecting your form. And to Him is the final return

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation With a Reason Attached**

The phrase *bi-l-ḥaqq* can be rendered “in truth” or “for a purpose,” and the second rendering makes the verse's function clearer. The heavens and the earth were not made idly. The Qur'an states the negative version of the same claim twice in identical words: “We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything in between for sport” (21:16; 44:38). A creation that is not sport is a creation with an intention behind it, and an intention implies an accounting. That is why the sūrah can move from this clause to the final return without a pause: a thing made for a purpose is a thing that will be assessed against it. The exegetes note the same connection when they read the phrase as “for truth,” meaning that the doer of good and the doer of evil will each be requited according to what he did.

**The Camera Moves From the Cosmos to the Womb**

The verse begins with the two largest objects in existence and ends its middle clause with the human body in the process of being formed. The shift to the second person — “He shaped you” — is what carries the intimacy of the verse. The reader is not being shown a general truth about biology; he is being told something about his own making, in a sentence that began with the heavens. This rhetorical movement is common in the Qur'an and always does the same work: it prevents the grandeur of the cosmos from becoming an abstraction by tying it to the person hearing it. The one who arranged the stars is the one who arranged your limbs, and the second fact is known to you with more certainty than the first.

**The Form That Was Perfected**

*Fa-aḥsana ṣuwarakum*, then He perfected your forms. Three readings of the perfection are recorded and each has support in the text. It may mean the human body, its proportions and capacities, which is the plainest sense. It may mean the inner reality of the person, on the model of “Indeed, We created humans in the best form” (95:4), which many commentators take to include the intellect and the capacity for God. Or it may extend to the forms of all things, since the same verb is used of creation generally: “Who has perfected everything He created” (32:7), and again of human beings in “He shaped you ˹in the womb˺, perfecting your form” (40:64). The widest reading is the most theologically consistent, because the Qur'an nowhere treats beauty as a human privilege; the narrowest is the most immediate, because the reader can inspect it in his own hands.

**Form Is an Argument, Not a Compliment**

The verse is not merely admiring the human body. In the Qur'an the perfection of the first creation is standard evidence for the possibility of the second. The argument is put bluntly elsewhere: “They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being” (36:79), and “He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him” (30:27). Read in that light, the mention of the shaped form is not ornament; it is the premise of an inference the sūrah will draw two verses later, when the disbelievers' denial of resurrection is answered with an oath. A maker who gave a body its precise arrangement has not lost the capacity to restore it, and the verse that describes the arrangement is quietly assembling the case. The objection about crumbled bones is answered here in advance, without being named, by pointing at the arrangement nobody disputes.

**“And to Him Is the Final Return”**

The last clause is short and it changes the meaning of everything before it. *Wa-ilayhi-l-maṣīr*, and to Him is the place of return — the same phrase the exegetes gloss as the journey's end. Created for a purpose and made with care, a person is nonetheless moving, and the destination is fixed. The clause answers the natural objection to a universe made for a purpose, which is that purpose without an endpoint is merely aesthetics. The return supplies the endpoint, and it is a return rather than an arrival somewhere new: the one who made you is the one you go back to. Verse 9 will describe that return in detail, naming the day on which everyone is gathered, and this clause is the first mention of it in the sūrah.

**Why These Three Facts Belong in One Verse**

Taken together the three clauses form a complete case in miniature. The cosmos is made with an intention, so the world is not an accident; the human being is made with particular care, so he is not incidental to the world; and everything returns to the maker, so the intention will be pursued to its conclusion. Each clause on its own could be granted by someone who rejected the rest — a person may accept design and deny judgment, or accept judgment and doubt design. Placed in this order, they interlock, and the denial of any one of them weakens the others. The verse is therefore an efficient piece of argument, and it prepares the reader for the sūrah's central dispute, which is not about whether God exists but about whether the return is real. That dispute is stated plainly two verses later, and by the time it arrives the reader has already been shown the two premises it depends on: a maker who acts with intention, and a made thing that is on its way back to Him.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:4

> He knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth. And He knows whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal. For Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Ranges, Narrowing to a Single Organ**

The verse states knowledge in three widening-then-narrowing steps. First the whole of what exists: “He knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth” (64:4). Then the personal interior and exterior of the person addressed: what you conceal and what you reveal. Then the narrowest point of all, the heart itself, described with a phrase that becomes a refrain in the Qur'an. The movement from cosmos to chest is deliberate. A reader might grant the first statement easily — of course a maker knows what He made — and still assume that his private thoughts sit outside the field. The verse closes that gap in the second clause and then removes the ground under it entirely in the third. By the end, there is no interior space left that is not already known.

**“Knows Best What Is Hidden in the Heart”**

The phrase *ʿalīm bi-dhāti ṣ-ṣudūr*, knowing of what the chests contain, is one of the most repeated descriptions of God in the Qur'an, appearing in verses as different as 5:7, 11:5, and 67:13. Its form is worth noting: the chests are described as owners of their contents, *dhāt*, as though the heart were a container holding something that could be shown or kept back. The Qur'an elsewhere describes people trying to do exactly that: “Indeed, they enfold ˹what is in˺ their hearts, ˹trying˺ to hide it from Him! But even when they cover themselves with their clothes, He knows what they conceal and what they reveal” (11:5). The image of a man folding himself inside his garment to hide a thought is one of the sharpest in the Book, and it is answered with the same clause this verse uses.

**The Interior Is Not a Private Room**

The consequence is stated in a verse that many find severe: “Whether you reveal what is in your hearts or conceal it, Allah will call you to account for it” (2:284). A companion reading puts the same point in the mouth of the Prophet ﷺ to be delivered: “Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah” (3:29). Knowledge of the heart is not presented as surveillance for its own sake but as the condition of a fair reckoning, since the part of an act that other people cannot see is often the part that determines its worth. A charity given for display and the same sum given unseen are identical to every witness but one, and the difference between them is the whole of their value.

**Why This Follows the Verse About Form**

The previous verse described the shaping of the human body and its perfection. This verse describes the reading of the human heart. The pairing is not accidental: the maker who arranged the organs also knows the contents of the one that governs them. Creation and knowledge are two aspects of a single relationship, and the sūrah places them together so that neither is taken alone. A person may admire the design of his body and remain indifferent to the one who knows him; he may feel watched and forget that the watcher made him. Read in sequence, the two verses make it difficult to hold either idea without the other, and the sūrah's argument about resurrection depends on both.

**Sincerity as the Only Workable Response**

What does a person do with a knower of hearts? The verse implies the answer by the way it is phrased. Since concealment is impossible, the only remaining use of the interior is honesty — to intend what one claims to intend. This is the exact failure that the sūrah before it spent eight verses exposing: men whose words said one thing and whose hearts said another, protected by oaths that a court had to accept. Their protection worked on people and failed here. The verse therefore does not create a new demand so much as remove an illusion. The believer's task is not to manage the gap between appearance and reality, since there is no one to manage it for, but to close it.

**What This Means for Judging Other People**

There is a second, gentler consequence. If the heart is knowable only by God, then no human assessment of another person's faith can be final, and the community's rules must be built on what can be observed. That is not scepticism about people; it is accuracy about evidence. The same principle protects the sincere believer who cannot prove his sincerity and the struggling believer who looks worse than he is. It also removes a temptation available to every religious community, which is to appoint itself the inspector of intentions. The verse places that office with God and leaves the believers with something more useful: an accurate account of themselves.

**The Same Knowledge Is Also a Promise**

The verse would be bleak if knowledge of the heart worked only against the person who owns it. It does not. The same awareness that makes concealment useless makes unobtrusive goodness legible. The charity no one saw, the harm a person declined to return, the repentance made silently at three in the morning, the intention formed and never spoken — all of it is inside the range described here, and none of it depends on a witness to be counted. The verse that states the accounting of hidden things closes with the same range in the other direction: “He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills” (2:284). A believer who cannot demonstrate his sincerity to any human court therefore has exactly the audience he needs. This is why the sūrah can move from this verse to a call to believe and spend without any transition: the reader has just been told that his inner life is fully visible to the one he is being asked to trust.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:5

> Have the stories of those who disbelieved before not reached you ˹pagans˺? They tasted the evil consequences of their doings, and they will suffer a painful punishment

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Expects an Answer**

The verse opens with an interrogative, and the form is not a real inquiry. The news had reached them. Arabian audiences knew the names of destroyed peoples from their own poets, from the ruins they passed on trade routes, and from the accounts the Qur'an itself had already repeated. The question therefore functions as an appeal to what the hearer already holds: you have heard this before, so the pattern is available to you, and the only question left is whether you will apply it to yourself. The same device opens a longer version of the reminder elsewhere, where the destroyed communities are listed by name: “Have they not received the stories of those ˹destroyed˺ before them: the people of Noah, ’Âd, and Thamûd, the people of Abraham, the residents of Midian, and the overturned cities ˹of Lot˺?” (9:70).

**Two Punishments, One in Each World**

The verse divides the consequence into two clauses with different tenses, and the division is the verse's structure. They *tasted* — past tense, completed, in this life — the evil result of their affair. They *will suffer* — future, incomplete — a painful punishment. Nothing is said here about the relationship between the two, but the pairing answers an objection that disbelievers have always raised: many communities rejected their messengers and flourished afterwards, so where is the reckoning? The verse grants the observation by naming a punishment already delivered, in the destruction of those communities, and then insists that the delivered punishment is not the whole account. The first part is evidence; the second is what the evidence points to.

**The Word Is “Tasted”**

The verb *dhāqū* is sensory. Tasting is immediate, undebatable, and known by the one who does it in a way that reports cannot match. The Qur'an uses the same verb for punishment elsewhere, as in the account of communities destroyed for rejecting their messengers: “So they tasted the evil consequences of their doings, and the outcome of their doings was ˹total˺ loss” (65:9). The choice of verb matters because it removes the possibility of treating the destruction as a matter of interpretation. A people whose city was levelled did not have an opinion about what had happened to them. The word also carries a hint of proportion: what they tasted was the flavour of their own doing, drawn from it and matched to it, rather than an unrelated calamity sent from outside.

**History Used as Evidence Rather Than as Story**

The Qur'an's repeated telling of earlier communities is a method, and the method is stated in the sūrah of Yūsuf: “In their stories there is truly a lesson for people of reason” (12:111). The stories are not ornament and they are not national myth; they are data arranged in a pattern. Each case has the same elements — a messenger, clear proofs, a refusal, a delay, an end — and the repetition is what turns a series of events into a rule. That is why the verse addresses a people who were not present at any of them. The hearer is being asked to treat the past as a laboratory in which the same experiment was run many times with the same result, and then to notice that he is standing in the laboratory with the apparatus already switched on.

**The Pattern Is Shared, So the Excuse Is Gone**

The communities named in the parallel passages are separated by centuries, geography, language, and level of power. Some were builders of monuments, some were traders, some were pastoral. Their messengers differed in temperament and in the size of their following. What is identical in every case is the shape of the refusal and the shape of the end. The sūrah's audience could not argue that the earlier peoples were punished for local reasons or that their situation was structurally different, because the cases are too varied for that explanation to work. The argument is statistical in spirit: with this many instances and this consistent an outcome, the variable that predicts the end is the refusal rather than anything about the place or the period.

**Why the Reminder Comes to This Audience**

The verse is addressed to people living in the Arabian peninsula in the seventh century, and its force depends on their position in the pattern rather than on their distance from it. They had a messenger among them with clear proofs, which places them at the same point in the sequence as the people of Noah and the people of Thamūd at the moment of decision. The reminder is therefore not antiquarian. It tells the hearer that the story he has heard so often is not finished, that his own community is inside it, and that the part he is living is the part in which the earlier peoples made their choice. What follows in the sūrah is the choice itself, stated as the objection the earlier communities also made.

**The Delay Is Part of the Pattern, Not Evidence Against It**

One feature of the earlier cases is easy to misread. The destroyed peoples were not struck on the day they refused. A period passed in which they traded, built, and argued, and to an outside observer that period looked like acquittal. The Qur'an insists that it was not: the respite belonged to the sequence, and the sequence always ended the same way. This is the difficulty the sūrah's audience was facing, since they too were in the interval. Their prosperity had not been interrupted, their caravans still ran, and the messenger among them had not been vindicated by any visible catastrophe. The verse answers that observation by placing it inside the pattern rather than outside it. An interval in which nothing happens is not a counter-example to the rule; it is the part of the rule in which the choice is still open.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:6

> That was because their messengers used to come to them with clear proofs, but they said ˹mockingly˺, “How can humans be our guides?” So they persisted in disbelief and turned away. And Allah was not in need ˹of their faith˺. For Allah is Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Objection Is Not an Answer to the Evidence**

The verse states what the messengers brought and what the hearers said in response, and the mismatch between the two is the point. Clear proofs arrived — signs, arguments, and the standing miracle of each messenger's message — and the reply did not engage with any of them. It was a complaint about the messenger's species: “How can humans be our guides?” (64:6). No counter-sign was demanded on evidential grounds, no flaw was found in the proof, no alternative account of the world was offered. The hearers changed the subject from the message to the messenger's body, which is the standard move of someone who has no answer and prefers to discuss the person speaking.

**A Constant Barrier, Named in Many Places**

The Qur'an treats this objection as one of the recurring obstacles to belief rather than a local complaint. It states the mechanism openly: “And nothing has prevented people from believing when guidance comes to them except their protest: ‘Has Allah sent a human as a messenger?’” (17:94). The phrasing there is remarkable, because it names the protest itself as the thing that prevented belief — not the weakness of the evidence, but the hearer's refusal to accept the channel. The same protest appears in the mouths of the communities addressed by Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, and Shuʿayb, and in the mouths of the Arabs addressed by this sūrah. Its persistence across such different audiences is what makes the Qur'an treat it as a general feature of human pride rather than a historical accident.

**The Same Complaint, Aimed at the Prophet ﷺ**

The version directed at the Prophet ﷺ is recorded with its sarcasm intact: “What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺? If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner” (25:7). The two objections inside the verse are worth separating. The first says that a man with ordinary needs cannot be a messenger; the second says that a messenger's credibility requires a visible heavenly escort. Both assume that God's choice of instrument must meet human standards of impressiveness. The reply the Qur'an gives is not that Muhammad ﷺ lacked needs but that God chooses His messengers from among the people He is sending, so that they can be understood, followed, and imitated. A messenger who did not eat or trade would be a wonder rather than a guide.

**Two Verbs: They Disbelieved, Then They Turned**

The verse uses two verbs for one refusal, and the order is meaningful. They disbelieved and they turned away. The first describes the interior position, the withdrawal of assent; the second describes the movement that follows, the turning of the body and the back. The pairing appears repeatedly in the Qur'an's accounts of rejected messengers, and it describes a refusal that is completed rather than merely entertained. Disbelief that does not turn away is still in the room with the message, still arguing, still capable of being answered. Disbelief that has turned is finished with the conversation, which is why the verse moves immediately to what God did not do: He did not compel them.

**“Allah Was Not in Need”**

The verb *istaghnā* means that God declared or showed His independence of them. The clause is short but it does a great deal of theological work. God's desire for human belief is not a need, and a need unmet would diminish the one who has it. Because there is no need, the refusal of a community costs God nothing, and because there is no need, the refusal was not prevented by force. The commentators draw exactly this conclusion: God had the power to compel belief and did not use it, which is what makes the trial a trial. A creature coerced into assent proves nothing about itself, and a messenger sent without the power to compel leaves the outcome in the hearer's hands. The independence named here is the condition of the freedom the disbelievers misused.

**“Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy”**

The two names are joined in a pairing that recurs across the Qur'an: “Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy” (22:64), and “It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy” (35:15), and the same pair closing a verse about obedience and disobedience (4:131). The joining is the point. A being who is self-sufficient could be indifferent, and a being who is praised could be dependent on the praise. Neither applies: the sufficiency is not coldness, and the praise is not a supply God requires. He is praised because He is worthy, whether or not anyone says so, which is the claim the opening verse of this sūrah made in different words when it described the heavens and the earth glorifying Him without being asked.

**What the Verse Leaves Standing**

Two facts are left in place at the end of the verse, and the reader is between them. God does not need the belief of anyone, so nothing He has said can be dismissed as an appeal for support. The messenger does not compel, so no one can claim that his refusal was forced out of him by superior power. What remains is a choice made in freedom, with the evidence delivered and the outcome unannounced until the day described later in the sūrah. This is the position the Qur'an consistently leaves people in, and it is the position the disbelievers in this verse refused to occupy. They wanted a messenger impressive enough to remove the need for a decision, and the answer tells them that no such messenger will come, because a decision that cannot be avoided is the only kind worth having.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:7

> The disbelievers claim they will not be resurrected. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Yes, by my Lord, you will surely be resurrected, then you will certainly be informed of what you have done. And that is easy for Allah.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb for Their Claim Is a Loaded One**

The verse reports the denial with a verb chosen for the job. *Zaʿama* is not the neutral word for saying or thinking; in Qur'anic usage it marks a claim that is asserted without knowledge, and it frequently introduces statements the Book then dismantles. The polytheists' argument that nothing they did could have happened against God's will is introduced the same way, and the reply there is blunt: “Do you have any knowledge that you can produce for us? Surely you follow nothing but ˹false˺ assumptions and you do nothing but lie” (6:148). Using the same verb here characterises the denial of resurrection before it is answered. The verse is not reporting a reasoned scepticism that deserves a reasoned rebuttal; it is reporting a confident assertion with nothing behind it, and the reader is meant to hear the difference.

**An Oath Inside the Answer**

The response is not an argument but a sworn statement: “Yes, by my Lord” (64:7). The Prophet ﷺ is instructed to stake his Lord on the fact, and this is not an isolated rhetorical flourish. The same command appears in two other places, and in both the subject is the same. When the disbelievers ask whether the threat is real, the answer is “Yes, by my Lord! Most certainly it is true! And you will have no escape” (10:53). When they say the Hour will never come, the answer is “Yes—by my Lord, the Knower of the unseen—it will certainly come to you!” (34:3). Three times the Qur'an places an oath on this one point and nowhere else. The pattern tells the reader how the Book wants the dispute weighed: not as an open question awaiting evidence, but as a certainty the messenger is prepared to swear to.

**Raised First, Then Informed**

The answer contains two promises in sequence, and the sequence matters. You will be resurrected, and then you will be informed of what you did. The first is the return of the person; the second is the content of the meeting. Being told what one did is treated in the Qur'an as the substance of judgment rather than a preliminary to it: “All will then be informed of what they have sent forth and left behind” (75:13). The emphasis on information explains why the verse does not mention punishment or reward at this point. The confrontation is with a record, and the record belongs to the person confronted. A reader may escape the notice of every other witness, including himself, since people routinely forget what they did and re-describe what they meant. The verse states that this will not be possible then.

**“That Is Easy for Allah”**

The closing clause answers the objection the disbelievers actually made, which was not about God's justice but about the mechanics of return. Their repeated question was how bones that have crumbled and bodies that have scattered can be reassembled, a question the Qur'an quotes at length and in mockery. The reply is the one already given in the sūrah's third verse, where the shaping of the human form was described: the one who made it the first time is not struggling to make it again. “He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him” (30:27), and “They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time” (36:79). The clause is therefore a conclusion, not a reassurance, and it assumes the earlier verses of this sūrah have been read.

**Why the Prophet ﷺ Swears Rather Than Argues**

There is a pastoral reason for the form of the answer. Argument had been running for years, and the disbelievers' objection was not evidential. They did not dispute that God is powerful; they found the idea of their own return absurd, and absurdity is not cured by more premises. An oath does something different. It places the speaker's own standing behind the claim and invites the hearer to consider what kind of man would stake his Lord on it. The Prophet ﷺ had no wealth to lose and no army to command, and his opponents knew his record for truthfulness better than anyone. The oath asks them to weigh that record against their confidence that the dead stay dead.

**The Sūrah's Turn From Evidence to Assertion**

Everything before this verse was preparatory. The opening described a creation that glorifies God, the second divided humanity, the third established design and the return, the fourth established knowledge of the hidden, and the fifth and sixth laid out the pattern of earlier refusals. That material is the evidence base, and the verse states the conclusion in the strongest available form. The shift is important because it marks the difference between what the sūrah is willing to demonstrate and what it simply declares. Some things can be argued from creation and history. The resurrection is asserted, sworn to, and made easy for God, and the reader who wants it demonstrated has already been shown the demonstrations.

**What the Denial Actually Rests On**

A denial of resurrection is rarely an intellectual position about matter and its rearrangement. In the Qur'an's accounts it is a refusal to face an accounting, since a return implies a reckoning and a reckoning implies that the way one lived was not neutral. The verse supports this reading by attaching the information to the resurrection: the thing being denied is not a physical event but a disclosure. The disbelievers are described as claiming they will not be raised, and the answer tells them that they will be raised and told. The pairing identifies the real objection and answers it directly, which is why the sūrah can move at once from this oath to a command to believe.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:8

> So believe in Allah and His Messenger and in the Light We have revealed. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Objects Named in a Single Command**

The verse lists what belief is to attach to, and the list is precise. Allah; His Messenger; the light that has been sent down. Each item has a distinct function and none can be dropped without losing something. Belief in God alone leaves the question of how He is to be known; belief in the Messenger alone leaves the question of who sent him; belief in a revelation alone leaves the question of who brought it. The three together form the complete structure of the Islamic claim, and the Qur'an states them in this order whenever it summarises what is required. The conjunction is *fa*, “so,” which makes the command a consequence of what has just been said rather than a fresh topic.

**The Light Is the Revelation**

The third item is described with an image rather than a name, and the exegetes agree on its primary reference: the Qur'an itself, with a wider sense covering divine guidance in general. The image is not decorative. The Qur'an uses light for revelation in a way that specifies what revelation does — it discloses, it distinguishes, and it makes the road visible: “But We have made it a light, by which We guide whoever We will of Our servants” (42:52), and “He is the One Who sends down clear revelations to His servant to bring you out of darkness and into light” (57:9). The metaphor also explains why the verse puts the light after the Messenger: the light is what he carries, and the two are inseparable in the way a lamp is inseparable from the one who lifts it.

**Why Faith in the Messenger Is Named Separately**

A reader might think that belief in God and belief in His revelation would be sufficient, and that the Messenger is only the delivery mechanism. The Qur'an does not treat him that way, and the verse reflects that: the Messenger is an object of faith, not a courier. The reason is given elsewhere in a single sentence: “Whoever obeys the Messenger has truly obeyed Allah” (4:80). Obedience to the one sent is obedience to the sender, because the sent one carries authority and not merely text. This is why the sūrah's later command is phrased as a double obedience, and why the hypocrites described in the preceding sūrah failed at precisely this point — they accepted the fact of the prophethood in words while refusing the man who carried it in practice.

**The Attempt to Put the Light Out**

The image of light invites a question the Qur'an answers twice, in nearly identical words. “They wish to extinguish Allah’s light with their mouths, but Allah will ˹certainly˺ perfect His light, even to the dismay of the disbelievers” (61:8; 9:32). The attempt is described as made with mouths — with speech, propaganda, and mockery — and the futility of it is stated in the same breath. The image works because light is not the sort of thing that can be argued out of existence; it can be denied, obscured, or ignored by the person standing in it, and none of those acts reduces it. Read against the previous verses, the image explains the position of the disbelievers: they are not in the dark because the light failed, but because they closed their eyes.

**Awareness Closes the Command**

The verse ends where the second verse of the sūrah ended: with God aware of what people do. The repetition frames the argument of the chapter. Between the two statements the reader has been given the case for resurrection and the command to believe, and the frame says that the response will be seen. It also does something subtler, since the awareness named here covers the act of believing itself. Faith is largely interior, and a person can profess it without holding it — the failure the preceding sūrah spent its opening verses exposing. The closing clause tells the reader that the profession and the holding are both in view, and that the second is the one that counts.

**From Answer to Instruction**

The verse follows an oath, and the connection between them is causal. The messenger has sworn that the return is real; therefore, believe. A denial answered by an oath leaves the hearer with a decision, and the verse states the decision in the plainest possible terms. This is the ordinary movement of Qur'anic discourse: a claim is contested, the contest is resolved by assertion from God, and the reader is then told what to do with the resolution. The command is not a threat and it is not a consolation. It is the practical form of the argument, and it is addressed to people who are still able to comply. The oath in the verse before it supplied the certainty; this verse supplies the direction the certainty is meant to produce, and without the second the first would remain an item of information rather than a summons.

**Belief Described as a Direction, Not a Feeling**

The verb *fa-āminū* is imperative and plural, addressed to a community and expecting a response that can be observed in conduct. The Qur'an consistently treats belief as something that produces action rather than as a private sentiment: belief in God, in the Messenger, and in the light is followed throughout this sūrah by obedience, spending, and trust. That is why the verse can be spoken to people whose feelings are uncertain. What is being asked is not a state of emotional certainty but a commitment with a direction, made by people who know that they are seen while making it. The verse ends there, and the verses that follow describe the day on which the commitment is settled.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:9

> ˹Consider˺ the Day He will gather you ˹all˺ for the Day of Gathering—that will be the Day of mutual loss and gain. So whoever believes in Allah and does good, He will absolve them of their sins and admit them into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there for ever and ever. That is the ultimate triumph

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Name the Qur'an Uses Only Here**

The day is called *yawm at-taghābun*, and the name occurs nowhere else in the Book, which is why the sūrah takes its title from this verse. The word comes from the root *gh-b-n*, whose sense is to shortchange or outdo someone in a transaction, and the derived form implies that the parties are doing it to one another. A market word has been applied to the end of the world, and the application is exact rather than decorative. The Qur'an describes belief as a purchase and a sale at several points, and the day is named for the settlement of those deals. Every reader of the verse is being told that he has already made a transaction, whether or not he noticed, and that the day will show which side of it he was on.

**Gathering, Described**

The verse names the day twice in one clause, and the first name is the plainer: the Day of Gathering. The Qur'an describes that gathering concretely — all generations on one open plain, each community behind its leader: “˹Beware of˺ the Day We will summon every people with their leader” (17:71), and the warning “of the Day of Gathering—about which there is no doubt—˹when˺ a group will be in Paradise and another in the Blaze” (42:7). The gathering is also the answer to the objection raised two verses earlier, since it is the resurrection made visible: bones recollected, generations assembled, nobody absent. The repetition of the two names in a single sentence is deliberate. One describes the event, the other its meaning, and the verse needs both to make its point.

**The Bargain Inside the Word**

The commercial language is not a loose metaphor, because the Qur'an describes the believer's position in precisely those terms. “Allah has indeed purchased from the believers their lives and wealth in exchange for Paradise” (9:111), and there are those “who would dedicate their lives to Allah’s pleasure” (2:207). On the other side, the disbelievers are described as having made the opposite trade: “They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided” (2:16). The word *taghābun* names the moment these trades are settled. What makes the day a mutual outdoing is that the settlement reveals a difference in price that the parties could not see while the deals were being made.

**Two Abodes, and an Inheritance**

The Prophet ﷺ gave the concept a concrete form. “There is no one among you who does not have two abodes: an abode in Paradise and an abode in Hell. If he dies and enters Hell, the people of Paradise inherit his abode. This is what Allah says: ‘These are indeed the inheritors’” (Sunan Ibn Mājah 4341, graded ṣaḥīḥ by Dārussalām). The report explains the mutuality in the name. The gain of one group is not merely alongside the loss of the other; it is made out of it, since what is forfeited is taken up by someone else. That is a hard idea, and it is why the day is not called simply a day of reward and punishment. The Qur'an wants the reader to understand that the settlement is zero-sum in the only respect that matters.

**Absolution Comes Before Admission**

The order of the two gifts in the verse is not accidental. God will absolve them of their sins, and then admit them to Gardens under which rivers flow. The sequence is the same as the one the Qur'an puts in the mouths of believers asking for it: “Turn to Allah in sincere repentance, so your Lord may absolve you of your sins and admit you into Gardens, under which rivers flow” (66:8). Forgiveness is not a bonus added to entry; it is the condition of it, and the verse states it first because the reader's real obstacle is not eligibility for a garden but the weight he is carrying. The mention of rivers beneath the Gardens is the standard Qur'anic shorthand for the place itself, and its function here is to make the reward concrete in a verse otherwise full of abstractions.

**“That Is the Ultimate Triumph”**

The closing phrase, *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm*, is a set formula in the Qur'an, used in more than a dozen places for the attainment of the Garden. Its appearance here defines success in the terms the verse has just supplied: not survival, not vindication in argument, not a favourable reputation, but absolution followed by a permanent home. The word *fawz* carries the sense of coming through safely, of escaping with the thing you set out for. Set against the name of the day, it completes the commercial frame. In a settlement where one party gains what another forfeits, the triumph of the believer consists in having bought well, and the verse states the purchase price in two items: belief in God and doing good.

**Why the Verse Is So Long**

A single verse here contains the gathering, the naming of the day, the condition of belief, the two acts of God, and the verdict on the whole transaction. The length is a function of what the verse is doing, which is to compress an entire eschatology into a sentence a person can hold. Nothing in it is speculative. The gathering is stated as fact, the condition as a plain requirement, and the outcome as a promise. The reader is given the terms of the deal before the deal closes, which is the only way the naming of the day could serve as anything other than a threat.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:10

> As for those who disbelieve and reject Our revelations, they will be the residents of the Fire, staying there forever. What an evil destination

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Acts Named, and They Are Not the Same**

The verse describes a group by two things they did, joined by a conjunction: they disbelieved, and they rejected the revelations. The first is the interior refusal, the withdrawal of assent from God and His Messenger. The second is directed at the *āyāt*, a word that covers both the signs and the verses, so the rejection has two possible objects and both fit the context. The disbelievers of this sūrah rejected the message as a text and the proofs as evidence. Naming the two acts separately is not redundancy. It distinguishes the man who never received guidance from the man who received it clearly and turned it down, and the second is the one the sūrah has been describing since the oath in verse 7.

**Residents, Not Visitors**

The phrase used for the people of the Fire is *aṣḥāb an-nār*, companions or residents of the Fire, and the word choice is deliberate. *Ṣāḥib* describes a settled relation between a person and a place or thing — the companion of the garden, the people of the cave, the residents of a town. It is not the language of a temporary stay. The verse then removes any doubt by adding that they stay there for ever, using the same phrase the previous verse used of the Gardens. The symmetry is exact and it is the verse's argument: two permanent destinations, one for each of the two groups named in the second verse of the sūrah, and no third possibility mentioned.

**The Verse Answering the Verse Before**

Read in sequence, verses 9 and 10 form a single balance. The believer is absolved and admitted, and that is the ultimate triumph. The disbeliever is a resident of the Fire, and that is an evil destination. Nothing in the second verse softens the first, and nothing in the first makes the second unnecessary. The Qur'an's descriptions of the two outcomes are usually paired, and the pairing has a purpose beyond symmetry: it establishes that the difference between the groups is not one of degree. Both settlements are final, both are described in the same vocabulary of permanence, and the reader is being asked to notice that the choice made in this life selects between two things that do not resemble each other.

**“What an Evil Destination”**

The closing exclamation uses *biʾsa-l-maṣīr*, and the noun is the same one the sūrah used seven verses earlier: “And to Him is the final return” (64:3). There the *maṣīr* was neutral, the endpoint of every created thing, described without judgement. Here it is qualified as evil. The repetition turns the earlier verse into a warning by retro-action. Every reader already knew that the return was certain, since verse 3 stated it as a fact about creation; the word now supplied is what the return will be like for a particular group. The exclamation is not a curse pronounced by the text on someone else. It is an evaluation offered to the reader for adoption, an invitation to say the same thing about the destination and therefore to act accordingly.

**Rejection of the Signs as the Stated Cause**

The verse does not describe the people of the Fire as the unfortunate or the ignorant. It names an act of rejection, and the act has an object: Our revelations. The possessive matters. What was turned down was not a human opinion or a tribal custom but something belonging to God, delivered by a messenger with clear proofs, in a context where the proofs had been seen. The same combination appears in the account of earlier communities: “That was because their messengers used to come to them with clear proofs, but they persisted in disbelief” (40:22). The cause named in both places is persistence after clarity, which is why the verse is placed immediately after the sūrah's account of the earlier peoples and their objection to human messengers.

**Why This Verse Is So Short**

The verse that precedes it is the longest in the sūrah. It contains the gathering, the naming of the day, the condition of belief, the two acts of God, and the verdict of triumph. This verse contains a group, a place, a duration, and an exclamation. The contrast in length is part of the meaning. The path to the good outcome has stages, conditions, and a promise, and it takes a full verse to describe it. The other path is described in a sentence, because there is nothing to describe except the result. The reader is given more to work with on one side than on the other, and the imbalance is not accidental. One destination requires conditions to be met and can therefore be described; the other requires nothing except that the conditions were not met, and so it is described in a line.

**The Group Is Described, and the Reader Is Not in It Yet**

Nothing in the verse names individuals, and nothing in it closes the case of anyone still alive. The people described are those who disbelieved and rejected, and both verbs describe settled positions rather than passing states. The sūrah has already told the reader that the categories are open and that belief is commanded in the present tense, so the verse cannot be read as a verdict on a particular living person. Its function is to state the end of a road while the reader is still standing somewhere along it. The exclamation at the end is addressed to whoever is reading, and its purpose is to make the destination undesirable before it becomes unavoidable.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:11

> No calamity befalls ˹anyone˺ except by Allah’s Will. And whoever has faith in Allah, He will ˹rightly˺ guide their hearts ˹through adversity˺. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence About Every Loss**

The word *muṣība* covers what befalls a person against his will: a death, a defeat, a loss of property, an illness, an injury. The verse states that nothing of this kind reaches anyone except by God's leave, and it makes no exception for the believer. The scope is total, and the sūrah places this statement between the description of the Fire and the command to obey, which suggests that its audience includes people who have already been hit. Madina in these years had lost men at Uḥud and suffered the grief of families whose fathers did not come back. A verse about calamity in a sūrah revealed to that community is not an abstraction, and it is not offering an explanation of why the losses happened.

**“By Allah's Leave”**

The phrase *bi-idhni Llāh* is precise, and the precision is theologically necessary. Leave is not authorship of evil. The Qur'an consistently distinguishes between what God wills in the sense of permitting and what He commands, and the distinction is what keeps the verse from making God the author of the wrong that people do. A calamity permitted is still within a governance; it does not happen outside God's knowledge or beyond His ability to prevent it. The verse's purpose is not to solve the problem of suffering but to remove the most corrosive idea available to a sufferer, which is that what happened to him happened at random, or that it happened because God was absent or indifferent.

**The Promise Is Guidance, Not Relief**

The second clause does not promise the removal of the calamity. It promises something else: whoever believes in God, He guides his heart. The exegetes read the guidance here in a specific direction — the heart is guided to acceptance, to the knowledge that what befell him was never going to miss him, and to patience rather than protest. The promise is about the interior of the person, and it is remarkable for that reason. A religion that promised deliverance from every loss would be offering a different thing altogether; this verse offers a heart that can carry the loss without losing its orientation. The believer is not told that the pain is unreal or that it will end soon. He is told that his heart will be guided through it.

**What Guidance of the Heart Means in Practice**

The Qur'an treats the heart as the organ that grasps, and the preceding sūrah described what happens when it is closed: the hypocrites' hearts were sealed so that they did not comprehend. Guidance of the heart is therefore not an emotional uplift but a change in what the person can hold. A heart guided through adversity can keep three things in view at once — that the loss is real, that it was permitted, and that the one who permitted it is the one he trusts. Without that capacity, grief turns into accusation and loss turns into apostasy, which is the pattern the Qur'an observes in people who worship on the edge: firm in good fortune and overturned by the first reversal. The guidance promised here is what prevents that overturning.

**Why This Verse Sits Between the Fire and the Command**

The placement is exact. Verse 10 states the end of those who reject. Verse 12 commands obedience. Between them stands a verse about suffering, and the arrangement answers a difficulty that would otherwise block the command. A person asked to obey after a loss may feel that the request is unreasonable, or that the loss has shown something about the reliability of the one who asks. The verse removes the block by stating that the loss happened within a governance, that the heart of the believer will be guided through it, and that the one who permits it knows everything. The command that follows is therefore not issued to a person who has been told to ignore his pain.

**The Verse Closes on Knowledge, for the Third Time**

“Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things” (64:11) is the third such closure in this short sūrah. The first closed the verse that described the division of humanity into believers and disbelievers; the second closed the verse that described the interior of the heart; this one closes the verse about calamity. The repetition is doing the same work each time: the situation the reader is in is fully known to the one who governs it. Applied to suffering, the statement answers the loneliest part of the experience, which is the sense that nobody sees what it cost. The same clause, repeated at intervals through the sūrah, works the same way on each subject it closes. The verse asserts that it is seen, and that the seeing extends to everything rather than to the parts a person can describe.

**This Is Not an Argument for Doing Nothing**

A statement that nothing befalls except by God's leave could be used to justify passivity, and the Qur'an closes that reading elsewhere in the same sūrah. The verses that follow command mindfulness, hearing, obedience, and spending, and they are addressed to people who already know that outcomes are not in their hands. The combination is deliberate. Effort is required and results are not guaranteed, and both facts are held together without one cancelling the other. The believer who understands this verse does not stop acting; he stops treating the outcome as the measure of whether his action was right. That is the disposition the rest of the sūrah assumes.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:12

> Obey Allah and obey the Messenger! But if you turn away, then Our Messenger’s duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Obediences, Stated Twice**

The verse repeats the verb rather than combining its objects, and the repetition is standard in the Qur'an: “Obey Allah and obey the Messenger and beware!” (5:92), and the fuller version in the sūrah of an-Nisāʾ, which adds those in authority and then directs disagreement back to God and His Messenger (4:59). The doubling could be read as emphasis, but it is doing something more precise. Two commands are distinguished because two authorities are distinguished, and the second is not a restatement of the first. The same verse that commands the double obedience explains the relationship: whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed God (4:80). The distinction preserves the source of authority while making the practical route to obeying it concrete and available.

**Why the Second Obedience Is Not Redundant**

If the Messenger merely transmitted a text, obedience to him would collapse into obedience to the text and the second command would add nothing. The Qur'an does not present him that way. He is the one who applies the revelation to situations the text does not enumerate, who decides, who leads, and whose judgement the community is told to accept. That is why the verse can be spoken to people who already possess the revelation and still need to be told to obey the man who brought it. It also explains the failure of the hypocrites in the preceding sūrah, who accepted the fact of the prophethood in words while declining the practical authority it carried. Belief in the Messenger, named as a separate object of faith two verses earlier, is realised in this command rather than in a further declaration. A person who accepts the text and declines the man has accepted less than what was sent, and the double imperative exists to close that gap.

**The Conditional, and the Verb Chosen for It**

“if you turn away” uses the verb *tawallā*, the same verb the sūrah used of the earlier communities who disbelieved and turned away after their messengers came with clear proofs. The echo places the hearer in the same position as those communities, at the same point in the sequence, with the same two directions available. The conditional is not a prediction that the audience will turn; it is the acknowledgement that they can, which is what makes the command a command rather than a description. A reader who could not turn away would not need to be told to obey, and the verse's grammar assumes freedom on both sides of the sentence.

**The Messenger's Duty Is Delivery**

The second half of the verse limits what the Messenger is responsible for, and the limit is stated in a formula the Qur'an repeats. “Is not the messengers’ duty only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly?” (16:35), and again: “The Messenger’s duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly” (24:54). The adverb matters: clearly, *mubīn*, in a way that can be understood and cannot be pleaded against. The messenger's task ends at understanding. He is not answerable for the outcome, and the verse says so in terms that release him from a burden no human being could carry. The same limit is why the sūrah can command obedience without threatening the disobedient in the same sentence.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

There is no curse here, no description of the Fire, and no announcement of what happens to those who turn. The sūrah supplied that material two verses earlier, and it does not repeat it. What the verse offers instead is a division of responsibility: the Messenger carries the delivery and the hearer carries the response. The arrangement is a mercy for both. The messenger is not destroyed by refusal, and the hearer cannot claim that he was carried along by someone else's conviction or misled by someone else's failure. Each party holds exactly one item, and the verse is precise about which.

**The Comfort in the Limit**

For the Prophet ﷺ this limit was not a technicality. He spent years watching people he loved refuse him, and the Qur'an speaks to that grief in direct terms elsewhere, telling him not to grieve himself to death over those who will not believe. The statement here is the same comfort in legal form. He has done what he was sent to do when the message has been delivered clearly, and nothing further is required of him. The same principle governs everyone who calls people to the religion after him: the duty is clarity and persistence in delivery, and the result belongs to God. It is a demanding standard and a relieving one at the same time, and the two halves belong together: the demand is total on the side of delivery, and the relief is total on the side of results.

**Obedience as the Shape of Belief**

The verse stands between the command to believe and the command to be mindful of God, and its position defines what belief looks like when it is lived. Faith is not a state that remains complete on its own; it expresses itself in hearing and obeying, and the sūrah's remaining verses set out that expression in particulars — spending, trust, and a loan made to God Himself. The imperative here is short and unhedged, which suits its function. Everything the sūrah has argued is now finished, and what is left is a direction. The reader who has followed the argument to this point has been given no further ground to stand on except the act itself. The verse therefore ends where a summons should end, on an imperative and on the name of the one who sent it, leaving the rest to be shown in what the person does next.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:13

> Allah—there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. So in Allah let the believers put their trust

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Whole Case in One Clause**

After twelve verses of argument about resurrection, judgment, and the fate of earlier communities, the sūrah states its foundation in the shortest form available. Allah — there is no god worthy of worship except Him. The formula is the *shahāda* in its first half, and it is placed here rather than at the beginning because the argument has just been completed. The reader who has followed the sūrah's case about a maker who shapes, knows, gathers, and settles accounts has arrived at the point where a rival god is not merely forbidden but unintelligible. The verse is therefore a conclusion stated as a premise, and the second half of it — the command to trust — is what that conclusion requires of a person.

**Trust Is a Named Act, Not a Mood**

*Tawakkul*, the word the verse commands, describes a settled reliance on God in the outcome of one's affairs. The Qur'an treats it as an act with content rather than a feeling of calm, and it consistently places it after effort rather than instead of effort. The instruction to the Prophet ﷺ makes the sequence explicit: consult, decide, and then rely. “So pardon them, ask Allah’s forgiveness for them, and consult with them in ˹conducting˺ matters. Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah” (3:159). The trust comes at the end of the sequence, when the available means have been used and the result is still unknown. That is the only position in which trust is meaningful; a person who has done nothing and calls his idleness reliance has not performed the act.

**Why Trust Follows Monotheism**

The two halves of the verse are connected by *fa*, “so,” and the connection is logical. If there is one god and no other, then there is one source of outcomes, and a person who relies on anything else is relying on something with no power behind it. Trust is therefore not an addition to monotheism but its practical form. A man may recite that there is no god but Allah and still place his confidence in his savings, his patron, or his own cleverness, and the verse identifies that as an inconsistency rather than a weakness. The exegetes draw the same conclusion from the pairing: reliance belongs to God because divinity belongs to Him, and any reliance placed elsewhere is a quiet claim that the relied-upon thing has some share in the governance of events.

**A Formula the Messengers Themselves Used**

The phrase “in Allah let the believers put their trust” recurs in the Qur'an, and its repetitions are instructive. Two groups among the believers were about to lose heart, and the verse records that God reassured them, ending on the same command (3:122). The messengers addressing hostile peoples said it to them: “And in Allah let the believers put their trust” (14:11), and again in the face of threats, “Indeed, we will patiently endure whatever harm you may cause us. And in Allah let the faithful put their trust” (14:12). The Prophet ﷺ is told to say it on behalf of the community: “Nothing will ever befall us except what Allah has destined for us. He is our Protector. So in Allah let the believers put their trust” (9:51). The formula is what believers say when the situation is beyond them.

**Trust Is Not a Refusal to Act**

The Qur'an's model of reliance includes work, and 3:159 shows the three steps in order. The Prophet ﷺ was told to consult his Companions, which is an act of gathering information; then to resolve, which is an act of decision; and only then to place the outcome with God. A person who omits the first two steps and names the third is not practising reliance but avoiding responsibility. The distinction matters in this sūrah, which immediately goes on to command hearing, obeying, and spending. The believer is not being invited into a state of passive confidence; he is being told that the results of his spending and his obedience are not his to control, and that this is a relief rather than a handicap.

**What Trust Looks Like in This Chapter**

The verse arrives directly after a verse about calamity and a verse about obedience, and it answers both. A person who has been struck by a loss is told that his heart will be guided if he believes; trust is the name for that guided heart in action. A person commanded to obey is told that the outcome of obedience is not in his hands; trust is what makes obedience possible when the consequences are unclear. The sūrah's remaining verses will ask for spending and for a loan made to God, and both requests are unintelligible without the confidence this verse commands. Trust is therefore not a devotional extra attached to the end of the argument. It is the condition of doing what comes next.

**The Verse Resets the Chapter's Register**

Twelve verses of polemic, warning, and description give way to two short statements: who God is, and what the believer does about it. The change of register is characteristic of Qur'anic discourse, which moves between argument and affirmation without transition. The effect on the reader is a kind of rest. The disputes with the disbelievers are finished and do not need to be re-litigated; what remains is the relation between a person and the one he has acknowledged. The verse also prepares the closing of the sūrah, which will end on God's knowledge of the unseen and the seen and on His might and wisdom. Between the acknowledgement here and the description there, the sūrah asks for a life.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:14

> O believers! Indeed, some of your spouses and children are enemies to you, so beware of them. But if you pardon, overlook, and forgive ˹their faults˺, then Allah is truly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word “Enemy” Applied Inside the House**

The verse uses *ʿaduww*, the ordinary word for an enemy, and applies it to spouses and children. The shock of the word is intentional, and it is qualified in the same breath by *min*, “some of.” Not every family member is an enemy; some are, and the verse refuses to let the reader assume he is exempt. An enemy in the ordinary sense is someone who wants your harm, and the harm described here is not physical. It is the harm of being turned away from God, which the Qur'an treats as the only harm that finally matters. The verse therefore does not accuse families of violence. It identifies a category of person who, out of affection, fear, or interest, works against the religious good of someone he loves.

**The Occasion Reported by the Commentators**

The exegetes connect the verse to a specific group: men who accepted Islam and were then prevented by their wives and children from migrating to Madina. Some delayed, some gave up the migration altogether, and when they eventually arrived they found that those who had migrated earlier had learned the religion and settled into it. The account explains both halves of the verse. The warning answers a real situation in which family attachment cost people their religion, and the command to pardon answers the resentment those men felt towards the families that had held them back. The verse is therefore not a general statement about difficult relatives; it addresses people who had already been damaged by the situation and were in danger of responding to it with harshness.

**How a Loved One Becomes an Enemy**

The mechanism is not hostility. A wife who does not want her husband to leave for a dangerous journey, a child who needs his father's income, a family that fears what conversion will cost its standing — none of these is acting from malice. The enmity lies in the effect rather than the intention, and that is what makes the category so difficult to recognise. The person being pulled away is being pulled by people he loves, for reasons he finds sympathetic, and the pull operates through affection rather than through argument. This is why the verse tells the believer to beware rather than to confront. There is usually nothing to confront, only a steady pressure that feels like love and produces the same result as opposition.

**Three Verbs, Arranged in Order**

The response commanded is described with three verbs, and their order is a progression. *Taʿfū* — pardon: give up the right you have to exact something. *Taṣfaḥū* — overlook: turn the page, which is a movement of the face away from the offence, so that it is no longer in view. *Taghfirū* — forgive: cover the fault, which is the strongest of the three, since it implies that the record is not merely unpaid but concealed. The gradation describes the internal work of letting go: first the claim, then the memory, then the record. A person who gives up his claim but keeps rehearsing the injury has done the first step only, and the verse names all three because the first is not enough on its own.

**Why the Command to Forgive Comes Immediately**

A warning about enemies inside the family could easily produce a colder household, and the verse anticipates that. The conditional is direct: *if* you pardon, overlook, and forgive. The reader who has just been told that his family may be working against his religion is told in the same verse that his response should be forbearance rather than retaliation. The two commands together describe a difficult balance — keep your religion, keep your temper — and the balance is what the situation requires. A man who was held back from migration by his family has a genuine grievance, and the verse acknowledges it by naming the enmity before asking him to let it go.

**The Closing Names Are the Reason**

The verse ends with *Ghafūr Raḥīm*, All-Forgiving, Most Merciful, and the placement of those two names is the argument for the command. The believer who is asked to pardon is himself a person who needs pardon, and the verse reminds him of that at the moment he is most tempted to insist on his rights. The pattern is familiar from the Qur'an's general instruction to forgive as one hopes to be forgiven, and it works here with particular force because the offences in question are domestic and repeated. A stranger's injury can be forgotten; a family member's is lived with daily, and only a person who expects forgiveness for himself will manage to extend it. The two names are also a reminder that the offences in a household are rarely single events. They accumulate, and an accumulated grievance needs a standing disposition rather than a fresh decision each time.

**The Verse Inside the Chapter's Wider Warning**

Two neighbouring passages belong with this one. The sūrah before it warned believers that wealth and children must not divert them from the remembrance of God, and the next verse of this sūrah states that both are a test. The three verses form a single argument about the household: the people closest to a believer are the ones most able to cost him his religion, not because they are wicked but because they are loved. The verse does not tell the believer to abandon his family or to treat them as suspects. It tells him to be awake, and then it tells him to be generous. Both instructions are needed, and neither works without the other.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:15

> Your wealth and children are only a test, but Allah ˹alone˺ has a great reward

**Expanded Commentary**

**“Only a Test” — The Restriction Does the Work**

The word *innamā*, “only,” restricts what follows. Wealth and children are described as nothing more than a test, and the restriction is aimed at the way people naturally regard them. They are usually treated as achievements, as evidence of a life gone well, as the substance of a person's security. The verse strips them to a single function: they are a means of examination. The same statement appears earlier in a verse addressed to the believers: “And know that your wealth and your children are only a test and that with Allah is a great reward” (8:28). Nothing here says that the test is a punishment or that the goods are bad. A test is neutral in itself and reveals something about the person taking it.

**Why Wealth and Children Test Differently**

The two items are joined in the Qur'an constantly, but they examine different things. Wealth tests through attachment and through the fear of loss: a person's relation to what he holds shows whether he holds it or it holds him. Children test through love and through fear for someone else, which is a stronger lever, since a man will risk principles for a child that he would not risk for money. Both tests operate through legitimate affections, and that is what makes them effective. A test built on temptation to obvious evil would be easy; a test built on the desire to protect one's family and preserve one's estate is passed or failed in small, unremarkable decisions that nobody else sees.

**The Slave of the Dīnār**

The Prophet ﷺ named the failure this verse describes. “Perish the slave of Dinar, Dirham, Qatifa (thick soft cloth), and Khamisa (a garment), for if he is given, he is pleased; otherwise he is dissatisfied” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6435). The report identifies a specific person: not the man who owns money, but the man whose satisfaction is determined by it. His pleasure rises and falls with what arrives, and his contentment is therefore outside his own control and outside the control of anything he can rely on. The word “slave” is the point, since the test described in this verse is a test of who is master. Wealth held by a person is a possession; a person held by wealth is the possession, and the verse's restriction to “a test” is a reminder of which arrangement is which.

**The Second Clause Turns the Reader Elsewhere**

“but Allah ˹alone˺ has a great reward” (64:15) is not a consolation tacked onto a warning. It completes the comparison the verse is making. The believer's attention is distributed between several objects — property, family, standing — and the verse states that only one of those objects holds anything worth having in the end. The word for reward, *ajr*, is the payment for work, and the adjective “great” is the same one the sūrah used for the triumph of verse 9. The comparison is therefore between a test and a reward, and the two are related: the test is administered through the goods, and the reward is with the one who administers it. A person who understands the arrangement stops treating the test as the prize.

**The Test Is Not a Punishment, and Poverty Is Not a Pass**

A reader without wealth or children might conclude that the verse does not apply to him, and that would be a misreading. The test in the Qur'an runs in both directions. Having is a trial because attachment grows with possession; not having is a trial because the want can produce resentment, and the Qur'an describes people whose discontent is determined by what they are given. What the verse removes is the assumption that the goods themselves are the measure. A poor man who is consumed by the desire for wealth has failed the same test as a rich man consumed by the fear of losing it, and the verse's restriction applies to both, since in both cases the goods are functioning as something more than a test.

**What Passing Looks Like**

The verses that follow supply the answer, and it is not renunciation. The believer is commanded to be mindful of God as far as he is able, to hear and obey, and to spend. The test is passed by using the goods in the way the one who gave them intends, which includes providing for the family that the previous verse described as a possible enemy. Nothing in the sūrah asks the believer to abandon his household or to treat property as contamination. It asks him to hold both lightly enough that neither can cost him his religion, and generously enough that both become the means of his reward. The loan described two verses later is the same instruction in financial terms.

**The Pair With the Previous Sūrah**

This verse closes a theme the sūrah before it opened. Al-Munāfiqūn warned the believers not to let wealth or children divert them from the remembrance of God, and its final verses pressed the point with the image of a dying man asking for a short delay in order to give in charity. At-Taghābun ends the same way, with a warning about family, a statement that both are a test, and a command to spend before the opportunity ends. The two sūrahs are read together in the Friday prayer for a reason: one exposes hypocrisy in the community and the other exposes its beginnings in the private life of a believer. Neither chapter treats property and children as the problem. Both treat distraction as the problem, and both name the cure in the same word.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:16

> So be mindful of Allah to the best of your ability, hear and obey, and spend in charity—that will be best for you. And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command With a Limit Built Into It**

The verse commands mindfulness of God and immediately qualifies the command: *mā-staṭaʿtum*, as far as you are able. The qualification is not a concession to weakness but a statement about how obligation works. The same principle appears in the Prophet's ﷺ own formulation of it: “So, if I forbid you to do something, then keep away from it. And if I order you to do something, then do of it as much as you can” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7288). Prohibitions are absolute because avoiding something costs only restraint; commands are graduated because doing something costs capacity, and capacity differs between people and within the same person over time. The verse therefore does not lower the demand. It directs the effort to the whole of what a person actually has, which is more exacting than a fixed amount that he can meet comfortably.

**The Relation to “As He Deserves to Be Feared”**

An older command reads differently: “O believers! Be mindful of Allah in the way He deserves, and do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission ˹to Him˺” (3:102). Some of the exegetes held that this verse abrogates that one, replacing an unmeasurable standard with a measurable one. Others held that the two are complementary, since the demand to fear God as He deserves sets the direction and the demand to fear Him as far as one is able sets the measure. The second reading is stronger, and it is the reading that makes both verses usable. A standard nobody can reach produces despair or evasion; a standard set by capacity produces effort, and effort is what the verse is asking for rather than an achievement it can measure. Together they say that the direction is fixed by God's worth and the distance by the person's ability, and neither half can be dropped.

**Hearing Comes Before Obeying**

Two imperatives are joined: hear, and obey. The order is not incidental. Hearing in this usage means receiving the revelation with attention — listening in order to understand, not in order to reply. Obeying follows, and covers both restraint from what is forbidden and performance of what is required. The sequence matters because obedience without hearing is compliance without understanding, which is unstable, and hearing without obedience is a hobby. The Qur'an describes the opposite of both in the preceding sūrah, where people attended the recitation and then asked each other mockingly what had just been said. The pair here is the shortest possible description of a working religious life: pay attention, then act on what you attended to.

**Why Spending Comes Third**

The third imperative is the one that costs something measurable. Mindfulness is interior, hearing is receptive, and obedience can be largely negative — the avoidance of prohibitions. Spending requires a person to release a portion of what he holds, and it is therefore the clearest available evidence that the first two have happened. That is why the sūrah ends its instructions with it, and why the previous verses built toward it: family as a possible enemy, wealth and children as a test, and now the command to give. The verse does not specify an amount or a recipient, which keeps the command applicable across circumstances and makes the test one of disposition rather than of arithmetic.

**“That Will Be Best for You”**

The phrase is comparative rather than absolute, and the comparison is deliberate. Spending is not described as a loss compensated later; it is described as the better of the available options, better than holding. The reader is being addressed as someone with a choice between two uses of the same money, and the verse states which serves him. This is the same reasoning that runs through the sūrah's commercial language: a transaction is being evaluated, and the evaluation is offered before the deal closes. The claim is that generosity is in the giver's interest in a way that hoarding is not, and the reason is given in the closing clause.

**Saved From the Greed of His Own Soul**

The final sentence describes success as a rescue. *Shuḥḥ* is a stronger word than ordinary stinginess: it is a grasping that holds back what should be given and wants what should be left alone, a condition of the soul rather than a habit of the hand. The same phrase appears in the sūrah of al-Ḥashr, describing the Helpers who gave the Emigrants preference over themselves even in their own need: “And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful” (59:9). The passive form is important. A person is *saved from* his own greed, which implies that the greed is a danger he cannot remove unaided, and the sūrah's answer to it is the act of giving rather than the resolution to be generous.

**Success Defined by the Sūrah's Own Terms**

The word *mufliḥ*, successful, closes a chapter that used the language of triumph in verse 9 and the language of loss in its title. The sūrah has described the day of settlement as a mutual outdoing, and it now states who wins it: the person delivered from his own grasping. The definition is narrower and more practical than a description of Paradise. Success is not wealth, survival, or reputation; it is the condition of a soul that can let go. Everything the chapter has commanded — mindfulness, hearing, obedience, spending — is a means to that condition, and the verse ends by naming the condition rather than the reward, which is how the sūrah leaves the matter with the reader.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:17

> If you lend to Allah a good loan, He will multiply it for you and forgive you. For Allah is Most Appreciative, Most Forbearing

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Loan Made to God**

The image is deliberately strange. God, who owns the treasuries of the heavens and the earth, is described as borrowing. The same image appears earlier in the Qur'an in the form of a question: “Who will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply many times over? It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases and increases ˹wealth˺” (2:245), and again as an invitation with a reward attached: “Who is it that will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply ˹many times over˺ for them, and they will have an honourable reward?” (57:11). The oddity is the point. A loan implies a debtor who needs and a creditor who can refuse, and neither applies here. What the image does is raise the dignity of the giver: the person who gives charity is placed in the position of one who has done God a service, which reverses the ordinary feeling of the transaction.

**What Makes the Loan “Good”**

The adjective *ḥasan* qualifies the loan, and the exegetes read it as a set of conditions rather than a compliment. The money must be lawfully acquired, since a lender cannot offer what is not his to give. It must be given willingly, since a loan extracted is not a loan. And it must be given without reproach or injury to the recipient, which the Qur'an elsewhere identifies as the thing that destroys charity. On this reading the verse is not inviting people to give more but to give better, and the qualification explains why the same amount can be worth different things. A sum given to be seen, or given with a remark attached, fails the adjective, and the promise of multiplication is addressed to the loan that carries it.

**The Multiplication, Stated in Numbers**

The Qur'an describes the multiplication in a parable that makes the scale concrete: “The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills” (2:261). Seven hundred, and then an open-ended increase beyond it. The parable is agricultural rather than commercial, which suits the subject: the giver plants and does not control the yield. The same logic answers an objection the verse does not raise but anticipates, which is that the giver is diminished by what he gives. On the arithmetic the verse supplies, he is the only party to the transaction who ends with more than he started with.

**Forgiveness Added to the Multiplication**

Two returns are promised, and the second is not financial. He will multiply it for you, and He will forgive you. The pairing is significant because it identifies what the loan actually purchases. The multiplication answers the giver's material concern and removes the fear of loss; the forgiveness answers the concern that matters more, since the giver's problem is not poverty but a record. A person who gives in order to be forgiven has understood the transaction correctly, and the verse attaches forgiveness to the act without requiring that the giver ask for it explicitly. The same combination appears in the invitation to lending in al-Muzzammil: give the loan, and “Whatever good you send forth for yourselves, you will find it with Allah far better and more rewarding” (73:20).

**“Most Appreciative”**

*Shakūr* is one of the names of God, and its application here is remarkable. The word describes one who acknowledges a service and returns more than its worth, and the Qur'an uses it of God's response to human effort: “so that He will reward them in full and increase them out of His grace. He is truly All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative” (35:30). God is described as thanking a servant who lends Him what was His to begin with, and the gratitude exceeds the loan. This is the theological core of the image. A person who gives to God is not performing a favour that will be forgotten or balanced; he is placing something small into a relationship in which the small thing is received with gratitude and returned with increase.

**“Most Forbearing”**

The second name is *ḥalīm*, one who does not hasten to punish. Its presence at the end of a verse about charity is not decoration. The verse has just described a transaction in which the human party gives something and God returns more; the closing name explains the position of the party who has not given. God's forbearance is the space in which the invitation can still be accepted, and it is the same forbearance the sūrah described when it said that no soul is delayed once its term arrives. The pairing of appreciation and forbearance covers both readers of the verse: the one who gives is met with gratitude, and the one who has not yet given is met with patience rather than with a closed door.

**The Verse Settles the Chapter's Arithmetic**

At-Taghābun takes its name from a day of mutual loss and gain, and this verse is the practical answer to that name. The day will show which side of a transaction each person was on, and the transaction is available now, in the ordinary act of giving something away. The sūrah has moved from the glorification of the cosmos to the greed of the human soul, and it ends its instructions with a proposal: lend to the one who owns everything, and receive back more than the loan together with the forgiveness the loan cannot buy. The reader who accepts the proposal has settled his account before the day on which accounts are compared, and the remaining verse of the sūrah describes the one with whom the settlement is made.

## Sūrah at-Taghābun 64:18

> ˹He is the˺ Knower of the seen and unseen—the Almighty, All-Wise

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Ends on Three Names**

Eighteen verses of argument, warning, and instruction close with a description of God and nothing else. There is no command, no address to the believers, and no final warning. The sūrah that began with the heavens and the earth glorifying their maker ends by naming Him, and the effect is to leave the reader looking at the same object the creation is looking at. The form is characteristic of the sūrahs that open with glorification: al-Ḥadīd, al-Ḥashr, aṣ-Ṣaff, and al-Jumuʿah all end on names or attributes rather than on narrative or law. The last of the group follows the pattern, and the pattern says something about how these chapters understand themselves — as descriptions of God before they are anything else. A chapter that spends its length telling people what to do ends by telling them who the doing is for, and the order is not accidental.

**The Seen and the Unseen**

*ʿĀlim al-ghayb wa-sh-shahāda* covers two domains in one phrase. The unseen is everything outside human perception: what has not happened, what is hidden, what happens in the heart, and what will happen after death. The seen is the world a person can observe and act in. The phrase is repeated across the Qur'an — “He is the Knower of all—seen or unseen” (6:73) — and its function is to close the gap between what a person knows and what is true. Every decision in this sūrah has turned on that gap: the hypocrites' hidden disbelief, the heart that God reads, the return that disbelievers deny, the day that has not arrived. Naming God as the knower of both domains is the shortest way of saying that the reader's information is not the measure of reality.

**Why Knowledge Is Named Before Power**

The order is deliberate. If the verse had begun with might, the description would read as a threat. Beginning with knowledge makes the power that follows intelligible, since power without knowledge acts indiscriminately and power with knowledge acts precisely. The same order appears in the sūrah's own opening movement: it began with glorification and kingdom, and moved to “He is Most Capable of everything” (64:1). Here the sequence is knowledge, then might, then wisdom, and each term qualifies the next. The reader is told first that nothing is hidden, then that the one who sees it is not weak, and finally that what He does with both is not arbitrary.

**Might Without Wisdom Would Be Frightening**

*Al-ʿazīz al-ḥakīm*, the Almighty, the All-Wise, is one of the most common pairs of names in the Qur'an, and its recurrence is not a formula of convenience. Might describes an ability that nothing can resist; wisdom describes the placement of things where they belong. Joined, they answer the two fears a finite creature has about an unlimited one: that He cannot act, and that He acts without reason. Every verse in this sūrah that could otherwise be read as severe depends on the second name. The gathering is not a demonstration of force but a settlement; the Fire is not an outburst but a destination; the refusal of a delay at death is not cruelty but the closing of a term set with knowledge.

**The Names Answer the Chapter's Disputes**

Each of the three names answers one of the sūrah's arguments. The knower of the unseen answers the denial of resurrection, since a return requires that nothing has been lost from the record. The Almighty answers the question of capacity, which the disbelievers raised about bones and dust. The All-Wise answers the question of fairness, which arises whenever a day of settlement is described. The verse therefore functions as a summary of the case in three words. A reader who grants the three names has granted the resurrection, the gathering, and the justice of the outcome, and the sūrah needs no further argument.

**A Sentence With No Verb**

The Arabic is a nominal sentence: knower of the unseen and the seen, the Almighty, the Wise. There is no verb and therefore no time. A verbal sentence would describe something God does; a nominal sentence describes what He is. The choice suits the position of the verse, which is the last word of a chapter about a day that has not yet come. Events arrive and pass, terms expire, communities are destroyed and replaced, and the description at the end is not of anything that happens but of a state that does not change. The reader is left with the one thing in the sūrah that has no date attached to it. Everything else in the chapter is measured against it: the term that will arrive, the gathering that has not happened, the loan that is still available, and the heart that is being read while the reading goes on.

**What the Ending Asks of the Reader**

The sūrah began with a creation that glorifies without being asked and ends with the one who is glorified. Between the two stands the reader, who has been told that he is in one of two groups, that his heart is read, that his family may cost him his religion, that his wealth is a test, and that a loan is available to him. Nothing in the closing verse repeats those instructions, and the omission is meaningful. The chapter's final statement is not about the reader at all; it is about the one the reader has been told to trust, obey, and lend to. The last thing the sūrah gives him is not a task but an accurate description of who is holding the account, and it leaves him to respond. The description is the same one the creation has been giving without words since the first verse, and the sūrah ends where it began.
