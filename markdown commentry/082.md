# Sūrah al-Infiṭār (Chapter 82) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:1

> When the sky splits open

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Name Taken From a Splitting**

*Infaṭarat* is the seventh form of *f-ṭ-r*, to split, cleave, or break open, and the form is intransitive: the sky splits, of itself, from within. The sūrah takes its name from this word — al-Infiṭār, the Splitting — and the naming fixes the image as the chapter's defining one. A sky that splits is not a sky that dissolves or fades; it is a solid or continuous surface failing along a line. The verb gives no cause, no agent, and no direction, and the absence is consistent with the whole passage, which describes four events in four verses without explaining any of them. What the reader receives is the fact of a rupture in the roof of the world, stated in a single word, and the word is the same root from which the Qur'an derives its account of creation itself.

**A Root That Means Both to Split and to Originate**

*Faṭara* is used in the Qur'an for the act of originating: God is *Fāṭir*, the Originator, and the human being's primordial disposition is *fiṭrah*, the state in which he was originated. *“All praise is for Allah, the Originator of the heavens and the earth”* (35:1) uses the active participle as a divine name. The double sense of the root — to split open and to bring into being — is not a coincidence in a language that thinks of creation as a separation: light split from darkness, water from land, one thing distinguished from another. The same root therefore names the beginning of the sky and its end. The sūrah that takes its title from the splitting of the sky is describing the reversal of the act that made it, and the reversal is carried by the verb rather than stated in a sentence.

**The Splitting Described in Four Other Sūrahs**

The event recurs with a regularity that marks it as a fixed element of the Qur'an's eschatology. *“˹Watch for˺ the Day the heavens will burst with clouds, and the angels will be sent down in successive ranks”* (25:25). *“˹How horrible will it be˺ when the heavens will split apart, becoming rose-red like ˹burnt˺ oil!”* (55:37). *“When the sky bursts open”* (84:1) opens the sūrah that follows this one's neighbours. *“The sky will be ˹split˺ open, becoming ˹many˺ gates”* (78:19). *“It will ˹even˺ cause the sky to split apart. His promise ˹of judgment˺ must be fulfilled”* (73:18). Five passages describe the same rupture with verbs from the same root or from roots of breaking, and the variety within the repetition is characteristic of the Makkan descriptions: the fact is fixed, and the picture changes.

**Four Conditions and One Answer**

The sūrah's structure is minimal and exact. Four verses beginning with *idhā*, when, describe four events; the fifth states what follows from all of them. Nothing else in the chapter is conditional. The compression is striking when set beside Sūrat al-Takwīr, the sūrah immediately before it in the received order, which builds thirteen conditions before reaching its answer. Al-Infiṭār reaches its answer in five verses, and the answer is a single sentence about knowledge. The two chapters are therefore variants of one form — a list of cosmic events followed by a statement about the human person — and the shorter one achieves in five verses what the longer achieves in fourteen. The reader who encounters them in sequence hears the same argument at two speeds.

**The First Thing Named Is the Roof**

The order of the four events moves from above downward: the sky, the stars, the seas, the graves. The highest is named first and the lowest last, and the descent ends at the place where human beings are. The arrangement is not merely spatial; it is the order in which a person looking up would encounter them, and then the order in which the events arrive at him. The sky is the visible limit of everything, and its splitting removes the limit before anything else happens. A reader who hears this verse first is placed under an open sky for the rest of the sūrah, and the openness is what the following verses fill — with scattered stars, bursting seas, and emptied graves. Nothing in the description offers shelter, because the shelter has been removed in the opening line.

**A Rupture That Opens Rather Than Destroys**

Splitting is not annihilation. A thing that splits has parts, and the parts remain. The Qur'an's description of the sky's end consistently preserves this feature: it is rolled up like a scroll (21:104), torn until it is frail (69:16), opened as gates (78:19), stripped away (81:11). In every case the sky becomes something else rather than nothing. The implication for the argument of this sūrah is that what is removed is not the created order but its closure. The sky has been a barrier, and a barrier keeps two things apart. Its splitting brings the two into contact, and what comes through it is described elsewhere as the angels descending in ranks (25:25). The rupture is therefore an opening in the strict sense, and the sūrah's title is accurate to it.

**The Word That Opens the Sūrah**

Placing this event first is a structural decision with consequences for everything that follows. The remaining three conditions all presuppose it, since scattered stars require an open sky, bursting seas require a world whose limits have already failed, and inverted graves belong to an earth that has stopped holding. The reader who hears the opening verse is told, before any detail, that the arrangement has been broken at its highest point, and each subsequent verse describes a lower layer of the same break. Nothing in the sūrah explains why the sky splits, who splits it, or what happens to the pieces, and the withholding is deliberate. A description that explained its own events would invite the reader to evaluate the explanation; a description that only names them leaves him with the naming, and the naming is the point of a warning. What is broken at the top cannot be repaired from below, and the first line of the chapter establishes that irreversibility without arguing for it.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:2

> and when the stars fall away

**Expanded Commentary**

**Scattered Like Something Poured Out**

*Intatharat* is the seventh form of *n-th-r*, to scatter, strew, or disperse — the verb used of pearls thrown from a hand, of seed flung across a field, of anything held together and then let go. The form is intransitive, so the stars scatter without an expressed agent and without a stated cause. The image is of many small things losing their arrangement at once, and the loss is of arrangement rather than of existence. A scattered thing still exists; it no longer holds a pattern. That is what the verse describes: the stars are still there, and their configuration — the thing that made them signs, guides, and decoration — has come apart. The verb is chosen for that precise loss, and the translation's "fall away" carries the falling but not the scattering.

**Three Sūrahs, Three Verbs**

The neighbouring sūrahs describe the same event with three different verbs, and the differences are meaningful. Sūrat al-Takwīr says the stars went murky and fell, using a verb of darkening and pouring (81:2). Sūrat al-Mursalāt says they were put out: *“So when the stars are put out”* (77:8). This sūrah says they were scattered. One describes a loss of brightness, one a loss of light altogether, one a loss of position. The three together give the complete picture, and the fact that they are distributed across three Makkan chapters suggests that the audience heard the event described repeatedly from different angles until no aspect of it was left unstated. None of the three verbs contradicts the others, and none is explained. The repetition is the method.

**The Adornment Coming Down**

The Qur'an assigns the stars two functions, and both end with the scattering. *“Indeed, We have adorned the lowest heaven with ˹stars like˺ lamps, and made them ˹as missiles˺ for stoning ˹eavesdropping˺ devils”* (67:5). *“Indeed, We have adorned the lowest heaven with the stars for decoration”* (37:6). Adornment and protection: beauty on one side and guard on the other. A sky whose stars have scattered is neither beautiful nor guarded, and the two losses are stated in this verse without either being named. The scattering therefore does more than remove light. It removes the decoration that made the sky a thing to look at, and it removes the defence that made the heavens inaccessible. What arrives through the split sky of the previous verse arrives unopposed.

**Scattered Rather Than Extinguished**

The choice of a verb of scattering distinguishes this verse from the extinguishing of Sūrat al-Mursalāt and the darkening of Sūrat al-Takwīr. Something scattered has been let go from a position it held, and the letting go implies that the position was maintained by a hand. The Qur'an states the maintenance elsewhere in the language of holding: God holds the heavens and the earth from ceasing, and the bodies travel in courses of their own because they have been set in them. The scattering is the withdrawal of that holding. Nothing in this verse argues the point or names the holder, and the naming is unnecessary, because the verse's function inside the list is to add one more feature of the ending — that the arrangement, not only the light, is gone.

**Pearls and Stars**

The same root is used in the Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise for a scattering that is not a loss: *“They will be waited on by eternal youths. If you saw them, you would think they were scattered pearls”* (76:19). The scattered pearls are an image of beauty and abundance, and the word for scattered is from the same family as the word used of the falling stars. The overlap is a reminder that scattering is not inherently destructive; it depends on what is scattered and what held it together. Stars held in their courses are a sign and a guide; stars scattered are the end of both. The verse takes the second case and states it in a word that elsewhere carries delight, and the contrast between the two uses is one of the Qur'an's quieter ways of marking the difference between this world and the next.

**The Second Removal**

The list proceeds from the container to the contents. The sky splits, and then what was in it falls away. The order is the order of a roof failing and its fittings dropping, and it is stated without comment. After this verse there is no sky and no light in the sky, and the remaining two conditions — the seas and the graves — belong to the earth. The sūrah has therefore completed its account of the heavens in two verses and moves downward for the rest of the list. The downward movement is what brings the description to the reader's own ground, and the arrival there is the point of the whole opening: a person standing on an earth whose seas have burst and whose graves have emptied, under a sky that has split and lost its stars.

**The End of Navigation and of Guidance**

The stars serve the traveller as well as the observer. The Qur'an mentions guidance by them explicitly: *“And He is the One Who has made the stars as your guide through the darkness of land and sea”* (6:97). Navigation is the practical form of that guidance, and it ends with the scattering. A person whose stars have fallen away cannot determine where he is, which direction lies ahead, or when the year has turned. The loss is therefore not only visual but orienting, and it belongs to a Day on which every means of finding one's way has been withdrawn. The Qur'an describes that withdrawal elsewhere in terms of speech and hearing — voices hushed before the Most Compassionate, only whispers heard, no intercession of benefit except by permission (20:108–109). Here it is described in terms of sight: the lights that ordered the night are gone, and what remains is a darkness in which no traveller can take a bearing. The verse names one event, and the event removes two kinds of guidance at once.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:3

> and when the seas burst forth

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Root of Breaking Open**

*Fujjirat* is the passive of the second form of *f-j-r*, to cleave, to break open, to cause to gush. The root gives *fajr*, the dawn — the breaking of light across the horizon — and *fujūr*, wickedness, the breaking open of what should stay closed. The same root appears at the end of this sūrah in the word for the wicked, and the recurrence is one of those verbal threads that run through a short chapter without being marked. The verb here is intensive: the seas are not merely opened but broken open, made to burst. The form describes a contained thing losing its containment by force, and it is applied to the one feature of the earth whose defining characteristic is that it is contained.

**The Barrier That Held**

The Qur'an describes the seas as held by a limit. *“He merges the two bodies of ˹fresh and salt˺ water, yet between them is a barrier they never cross”* (55:19–20). *“And He is the One Who merges the two bodies of water: one fresh and palatable and the other salty and bitter, placing between them a barrier they cannot cross”* (25:53). The barrier is named among the signs, and Sūrat Fāṭir adds the benefits that depend on the separation — food from both, ornaments from both, ships ploughing through both (35:12). The bursting of this verse is the failure of that arrangement. What never crossed now crosses; what was separated is mixed; what was held is released. The verse states the failure in one word and leaves the reader to supply the consequences from the descriptions he has already heard.

**The Same Event in the Sūrah Before**

Sūrat al-Takwīr describes the seas with a different verb: they are swollen or set on fire (81:6). The two descriptions are not alternatives; they are two aspects of one event. Swelling is the rising of the water past its edge, and bursting is the breaking of the edge itself. A sea that swells is a sea approaching its limit; a sea that bursts is a sea through it. The two sūrahs are adjacent in the received order and both belong to the same Makkan phase, and the correspondence indicates a settled picture of the end in which the waters play a specific part. Nothing in either verse describes the effect on the land, and the omission is filled by the general Qur'anic account of the Day, in which the earth is levelled, emptied, and changed into a different earth.

**Bursting as the Loss of a Limit**

The sea is the Qur'an's standard image of the boundless thing that is nevertheless bounded. It covers most of the earth, it cannot be crossed without permission, and it drowns whoever enters it unprepared; yet it stays in its place. The bursting removes the one feature that made the sea a sign of order rather than of chaos. A sea in its place is a limit; a sea out of its place is the end of limits. The verse therefore does more than describe a physical event. It describes the withdrawal of the principle by which the created order is held together, and the principle is named throughout the Qur'an as measure — everything created according to a measure, and the measure held. When the seas burst, the measure has gone, and nothing else in the description needs to be added.

**Water as the Element of Life**

The Qur'an states that every living thing was made from water, and it makes water the instrument of every revival: rain sent down, dead land stirred, crops brought out. The bursting of the seas is the reversal of that function. The element that gives life becomes the agent of the ending, and it does so not by drying up but by exceeding its place. Nothing in the verse describes destruction by water, and the reader supplies it from the image. A world whose seas have burst is a world in which the ground is gone, and the ground is what the following verse needs in order to give up its dead. The two conditions are adjacent in the list because they are adjacent in fact: the earth is emptied after it has been covered.

**The Third Condition and the Descent of the List**

Three verses have moved from the sky to the stars to the seas, and the movement is downward and inward. The sky is above everyone; the stars are in the sky; the seas are on the earth and are the part of the earth no one owns. The fourth condition will be the graves, which are under the ground and are the most personal item in the list. The descent is therefore not only spatial but relational: each item is closer to the reader than the one before. By the time the list reaches the graves it has arrived at the reader's own body, and the arrival is what makes the answer in verse 5 possible. A list of cosmic events produces a statement about a single soul only because the list has been walking towards him.

**The Root Returns at the End of the Sūrah**

The same root that describes the bursting of the seas later names a class of people. Verse 14 of this chapter states that the wicked will burn in a Blazing Fire, and the word used for them, *al-fujjār*, is the intensive plural of the same root *f-j-r* that produced *fujjirat* here. The Qur'an is not indifferent to such echoes. A root meaning to break open gives, on one side, water released past its limit and, on the other, a person who has broken past the limit set for him. The connection is semantic rather than merely phonetic: both cases describe a contained thing exceeding its containment, and in both the exceeding is the whole of the problem. The seas do not cease to exist; they leave their place. The wicked are not annihilated; they leave the bounds that were set for conduct. Placing the two words in one short chapter lets the reader hear the physical event as an image of the moral one, and the hearing is available without any explanation being offered.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:4

> and when the graves spill out

**Expanded Commentary**

**Turned Inside Out**

*Buʿthirat* is from *b-ʿ-th-r*, a root meaning to stir up, scatter, and turn over — used of digging into something and throwing out its contents, and of a thing being inverted so that its inside becomes its outside. The commentators render it as the graves being turned inside out, which is the strongest sense available in English. The verb is rare and it appears in one other place in the Qur'an, where the question is asked whether people do not know that the contents of the graves will be spilled out (100:9). The two passages use the same word for the same event, and both treat it as something known — a fact the audience is expected to hold. The rarity of the verb is matched by the specificity of the image: not opened, not emptied, but inverted.

**Graves as Containers**

The Qur'an's vocabulary for the grave is consistent in treating it as a place that holds. The dead are its people; the resurrection is a bringing out; the earth is described as receiving and then returning. A container has an inside and an outside, and the normal relation between the two is fixed: what goes in stays in. The inversion described here reverses the relation, and the reversal is what makes the verse an eschatological statement rather than a physical one. A grave that opens could be closed again. A grave that is turned inside out has ceased to be a container, and the cessation is permanent. The verse describes the end of burial as an institution, and burial is one of the two acts — with eating — that the Qur'an names as belonging to the human being's dignity.

**What Comes Out**

The descriptions elsewhere are specific and they agree on the manner. *“With eyes downcast, they will come forth from the graves as if they were swarming locusts”* (54:7). *“The Trumpet will be blown ˹a second time˺, then—behold!— they will rush from the graves to their Lord”* (36:51). *“the Day they will come forth from the graves swiftly, as if racing to an idol ˹for a blessing˺”* (70:43). Three images — locusts swarming, people rushing, a crowd racing — and all three describe speed, multitude, and the absence of order. The downcast eyes in the first of them supply the affect. Nothing in this verse describes the emerging, and the silence leaves the inversion to stand alone: the graves spill out, and what spills is not described because the reader already knows.

**Earth Throwing Out Its Burdens**

Sūrat al-Zalzalah describes the same event from the side of the earth: *“When the earth is shaken ˹in˺ its ultimate quaking, and when the earth throws out ˹all˺ its contents”* (99:1–2). The throwing out is attributed to the earth there and to the graves here, and the two attributions describe one action at two scales. The earth shakes and gives up what it holds; the graves invert and release what they contain. Sūrat al-ʿĀdiyāt asks the question in the same terms — do they not know, when the contents of the graves are spilled out (100:9) — and answers it with the statement that their Lord is fully aware of them on that Day (100:11). The awareness is the point of the spilling: what comes out comes out into the presence of a Knower, and the emptiness of the graves is the condition of the accounting.

**Last of the Human Structures**

Everything human beings build is left behind at death — houses, fields, wealth, monuments. The grave is the only structure that goes with them, and it is the one structure built to be entered and never left. This verse describes that structure failing, and the failure is different in kind from the failure of the sky or the seas. The sky and the seas are creations; the grave is a human work performed on a creation. Its inversion is therefore the end of the last thing a person does for himself. After this verse there is nothing left in the world that a human being made, and the reader who has followed the list from the splitting sky arrives at the emptiest possible position: no roof, no stars, no shores, and no grave to be left in.

**The Fourth Condition and the Answer**

This is the last of the four *idhā* clauses, and the next verse supplies the consequence that all four have been waiting for. The proximity matters: the answer to the splitting of the sky, the scattering of the stars, the bursting of the seas, and the spilling of the graves is a sentence about what each soul comes to know. Four cosmic events and one act of knowledge. The disproportion is the sūrah's argument, and it is stated without a word of comment. The world ends, and the stated result is not the destruction of the world but the information of the person. Nothing in the list of conditions has been about the human being; the answer is entirely about him.

**Emptied Graves as the Condition of the Account**

An account requires the appearance of the one who is accounted for, and appearance requires release from the place of concealment. The Qur'an states the concealment plainly: the earth holds its dead, and what is hidden in it is known to God alone. The spilling of the graves is the end of that concealment, and the end is what makes the knowledge of verse 5 possible for every soul without exception. Nothing is left buried, nothing is left uncounted, and no person is absent. The three conditions of a complete reckoning — presence, disclosure, and universality — are all produced by this single event, and the verse states the event without drawing any of the conclusions from it. The conclusions are drawn elsewhere, where the record is described as leaving out nothing small or great (18:49), and they can be drawn there only because the graves have been emptied here.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:5

> ˹then˺ each soul will know what it has sent forth or left behind

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to Four Conditions**

Every verse before this one began with *idhā*, when, and none carried a consequence. This verse carries the consequence for all of them. The grammatical structure is a single sentence spread across five verses, and the sentence says: when the sky splits, the stars scatter, the seas burst, and the graves spill out, then each soul will know. The knowledge is the only stated result of the end of the world. No verse in the list describes what the events do to people — nobody is crushed, drowned, or terrified in the description — and the sole outcome named is an act of knowing. The sūrah has therefore defined the Day by its epistemic effect, and the definition is the reason the four cosmic conditions were needed: only a total ending of the ordinary world could produce a knowledge of that completeness.

**Sent Forward and Left Behind**

The two verbs are *qaddamat*, what it sent ahead, and *akhkharat*, what it put off or left behind. The first is the causative of frontness: to make a thing precede, to dispatch it in advance. The second is the causative of laterness: to delay, to leave for later. The pair is a metaphor taken from travel and from wealth — a person sends his goods ahead of him so that they arrive before he does, and he leaves other things behind to be dealt with later. Applied to deeds the metaphor produces the Qur'an's standard account of a life: what a person did has gone ahead of him and is waiting, and what he did not do remains behind him and is also counted. The verse names both, and the naming of the second is what makes the knowledge complete.

**Three Readings of What Is Left Behind**

The commentators differ on the second term. Some take it as commanded deeds that were left undone — the good a person could have done and did not. Others take it broadly as all the good and evil a person did, together with all he could have done and did not. Al-Ṭabarī objects to the first reading on the ground that a deed cannot be said to be left behind unless it was performed, and he offers a third reading: what a person left behind is a practice he established, such that whoever performs it after him carries the same reward or the same burden. The three readings differ in scope and each is supported. The strongest is the one that fits the metaphor, since sending ahead and leaving behind are both acts, and a practice established is an act that continues after the actor. The verse is general enough to carry all three.

**The Same Statement in the Resurrection Sūrah**

Sūrat al-Qiyāmah states the identical fact: *“All will then be informed of what they have sent forth and left behind”* (75:13). The wording is nearly the same and the position is the same — a statement of universal knowledge following a description of the Day. Two Makkan sūrahs separated by several chapters carry the same sentence, which marks it as a fixed element of the early preaching. The agreement also confirms the reading of the two terms, since al-Qiyāmah's formulation leaves no room for a narrow interpretation: what has been sent forward and what has been left behind covers the whole of a person's relation to his own deeds, performed and unperformed, and continues after him.

**Sending Ahead as the Qur'an's Standing Image**

The metaphor is used as an instruction, not only as a description. *“O believers! Be mindful of Allah and let every soul look to what ˹deeds˺ it has sent forth for tomorrow”* (59:18). *“Establish prayer, and pay alms-tax. Whatever good you send forth for yourselves, you will ˹certainly˺ find ˹its reward˺ with Allah”* (2:110). In both cases the believer is told to look at what he has dispatched, and the looking is commanded in this life rather than reserved for the next. This verse describes the moment when the looking becomes unavoidable. Nothing new happens to the deeds; they were sent ahead already and are already there. What happens is that the person arrives, and the arrival is what produces the knowledge the verse names.

**Two Sūrahs, Two Verbs of Knowing**

The sūrah immediately before this one in the received order closes its own list of conditions with a parallel sentence: each soul will know what it has brought along (81:14). The verb of knowing is the same, and the object differs — what a person brought, what a person sent ahead and left behind. The two formulations cover the same ground from different directions: al-Takwīr describes the deeds as possessions carried to the Day, and al-Infiṭār describes them as shipments dispatched in advance. Both images deny that the deeds stay in the past, and both make the arrival at the Day a meeting rather than a judgement of a distant record. A reader who encounters the two chapters in sequence receives the same statement twice in two metaphors, and the doubling removes any suggestion that the knowledge described is partial.

**Knowledge Without Intermediaries**

The verse says the soul will know; it does not say it will be told, shown, or reminded. The distinction matters, because a person who is told something receives it from another and can contest the telling. A person who knows something in the sense used here has the fact present to him directly. The Qur'an describes the record that accompanies this presence — a book laid open, a scroll given in the hand, nothing omitted (18:49) — but the record is a medium of the knowledge rather than its cause, and the verse under discussion bypasses the medium entirely. What the soul comes to know is what it did, and the knowing is immediate. The absence of intermediaries is what makes the state described here final: there is no one to argue with, nothing to dispute, and no gap between the deed and its recognition. The four cosmic events have cleared away the world in which such gaps were possible.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:6

> O humanity! What has emboldened you against your Lord, the Most Generous

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Address to the Species**

*Yā ayyuhā al-insān* — O human being. The vocative is addressed to the species rather than to a group, a city, or a believer, and it appears in the Qur'an at moments when the argument requires the widest possible addressee. Nothing in the address restricts it: not the Quraysh, not the Arabs, not the disbelievers. Every reader is inside it, and the inclusion is what gives the question that follows its force. The sūrah has just described the end of the world in four verses and the knowledge of every soul in the fifth; it now turns and speaks to everyone. The turn is unannounced, and the unannounced turn is characteristic of the short Makkan chapters, which move from description to address without a connective and expect the reader to feel the change of direction.

**What Deceived You**

*Mā gharraka* — what deluded you, what deceived you, what made you bold. The root *gh-r-r* denotes deception through appearances: a thing looks one way and is another, and the person who trusts the look is *maghrūr*. The noun from the same root, *ghurūr*, is used of the life of this world as a deceptive enjoyment, and *al-gharrūr*, the Deluder, is a title of Satan. The Qur'an names both deceivers in a single warning: *“So do not let the life of this world deceive you, nor let the Chief Deceiver deceive you about Allah”* (31:33), repeated in nearly the same words in Sūrat Fāṭir (35:5). The question in this verse asks which of the two has done the work, and it asks in a form that assumes one of them has. No human being is bold against his Lord without having been deceived, and the deception is the thing to be identified.

**The Most Generous**

The divine name chosen for the rebuke is *al-Karīm* — the Most Generous, the Noble, the One who gives without being asked and does not exact. The choice is remarkable. A rebuke could have been framed by a name of power — the Almighty, the Compeller, the Possessor of the Throne — and the sūrah that precedes this one uses exactly those names in its description of the angelic messenger. This verse uses generosity, and the use changes the character of the accusation. Boldness against a powerful lord is folly; boldness against a generous one is something worse, because it takes the generosity as the ground of the boldness. The person deceived by this world has been deceived by gifts that came from the one he is defying, and the verse names the giver by the attribute that produced them.

**Why Generosity Makes the Question Harder**

A person who rebels against a severe master can claim provocation. A person who rebels against a generous one cannot, and the impossibility is the content of the question. The verses that follow list the gifts — creation, fashioning, proportioning, the composition of the form — and the list is offered not as evidence of power but as evidence of generosity. Every item in it is a benefit conferred on the person being addressed, conferred before he asked and before he existed to ask. The rebuke therefore has the form of an inquiry into a specific failure: what convinced you that you could act against the one who made you and shaped you and chose your form. The question has no answer, and the absence of an answer is what makes it a question rather than an accusation.

**The Same Name in the First Revelation**

*Al-Karīm* appears in the earliest verses revealed, in the command to read: *“Read! And your Lord is the Most Generous, Who taught by the pen— taught humanity what they knew not”* (96:3–5). The name is attached there to teaching, and the teaching is described as a gift to a species that did not know. The recurrence in this sūrah attaches the same name to a rebuke, and the attachment is consistent: the generosity that taught is the generosity that has been defied. A reader who knows the first revelation hears this verse with that name's history in it, and the history makes the question sharper. The one who taught by the pen is the one against whom the taught has become bold, and the boldness is described in a verse that names the teacher by his generosity.

**A Question With No Answer Expected**

The interrogative *mā* — what — asks for a thing, not a reason, and the question does not invite a response. Nothing follows it in the form of an answer from the addressee; the sūrah supplies the facts instead, in three verses describing what the Lord did. The structure is that of a rhetorical question used to open a case, and the case is then made without reference back to it. The technique places the burden on the listener: he must supply the answer himself, and every available answer is a version of deception. This world deceived him, or the Deluder did, or his own appetite did. The verse does not name the deceiver because naming it would allow the listener to treat it as an external cause, and the question is designed to make him look for the cause inside his own choices.

**Deception as the Qur'an's Diagnosis of Boldness**

The verse does not accuse human beings of strength, intelligence, or open rebellion. It accuses them of having been fooled, and the accusation is more serious than the alternatives, because it implies that the person acted on a false appearance while believing himself to be acting on a true one. The Qur'an names the appearance: the life of this world, which is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children (57:20). Each feature is real and each is misleading, and the misleading consists in the assumption that what is present is what matters. The boldness complained of here is the behaviour of someone who has made that assumption, and the assumption is not a philosophical position but a habit of attention. The question therefore reaches people who have never formulated a denial, since the denial is embedded in how they spend their days rather than in what they claim to believe.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:7

> Who created you, fashioned you, and perfected your design

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Verbs of Making**

The verse answers the question of the previous one with a relative clause containing three verbs: *khalaqaka*, He created you; *sawwāka*, He fashioned you; *ʿadalaka*, He proportioned you. The three are not synonyms and they are not redundant. Creation is the bringing into being; fashioning is the making sound and complete; proportioning is the setting of measure and balance. Each verb covers a different stage and a different aspect of the same work, and the accumulation is what gives the rebuke its weight. A person who has been created, fashioned, and proportioned has received three distinct acts of attention, and the question of the previous verse asks what convinced him that he could act boldly against the one who performed them.

**Fashioning as Making Sound**

*Sawwā* comes from a root meaning level, even, straight, and it is used of making a thing complete and free of defect. The commentators explain it here as the making of all the human parts and faculties sound and functional — every organ in place, every faculty operative. The word appears in the Qur'an's account of the first creation: *“So when I have fashioned him and had a spirit of My Own ˹creation˺ breathed into him, fall down in prostration to him”* (15:29), where the fashioning precedes the breathing of the spirit and is the ground of the command to the angels. The same sequence appears in Sūrat al-Sajdah, which adds what was given with the fashioning: *“then He fashioned them and had a spirit of His Own ˹creation˺ breathed into them. And He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect. ˹Yet˺ you hardly give any thanks”* (32:9). The addition of the faculties in that verse is exactly what *sawwāka* denotes here.

**Proportioned or Turned**

The third verb was read in two ways by the transmitted recitations. With a shaddah on the *ayn*, *ʿaddalaka* means He proportioned you, He adjusted your parts into balance. Without the shaddah, *ʿadalaka* means He turned you, directed you — which the commentators explain as God turning people in whatever direction He wills. Both readings are canonical and both are coherent. The first fits the sequence, since proportioning completes the making; the second introduces a new idea, that the direction of a person's life is also given. The grammarians prefer the first when verse 8 is read as a continuation of verse 7, because the composition of the form follows naturally from the proportioning of the parts. The second reading survives because it is transmitted and because it opens a meaning the context can bear: the one who fashioned the body also turns the person.

**The Same Triad Elsewhere**

The combination of creation, fashioning, and proportioning recurs as a settled formula. *“It is Allah Who made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy. He shaped you ˹in the womb˺, perfecting your form. And He has provided you with what is good and lawful. That is Allah—your Lord. So Blessed is Allah, Lord of all worlds”* (40:64). *“He created the heavens and the earth for a purpose. He shaped you ˹in the womb˺, perfecting your form. And to Him is the final return”* (64:3). Sūrat al-Aʿlā gives the pair in its shortest form: *“Who created and ˹perfectly˺ fashioned ˹all˺, and Who ordained precisely and inspired accordingly”* (87:2–3). The formula's recurrence shows that it was a recognized way of stating the argument from the human body, and its use here places the rebuke inside a standard demonstration rather than a new one.

**The Argument From the Body**

The verse makes its case from the reader's own form, and the choice of evidence is deliberate. The sky splitting, the stars scattering, the seas bursting, and the graves spilling are events the reader has not seen. His body is present, and its making is described in three verbs he can test against his own experience: he exists, he is complete, he is proportioned. The argument therefore moves from the cosmic to the intimate in two verses, and the intimacy is what makes the rebuke unavoidable. A person can doubt a description of the end of the world. He cannot doubt that he was made, that his parts are sound, and that his form has a measure. The verse places the undeniable where the accusation needs it, immediately after the question about boldness.

**Creation as the Ground of Obligation**

The Qur'an consistently derives obligation from the fact of being created. *“O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women”* (4:1). The command to be mindful is attached directly to the description of the creating, and the attachment is causal rather than decorative: the one who made you has a claim on you. This verse makes the same attachment in the form of a rebuke. The boldness complained of in verse 6 is boldness against a Lord, and the word Lord is defined here by three acts. Nothing in the verse states the obligation explicitly, and none needs to be stated, because a maker's relation to what he has made carries it.

**The Faculties That Go With the Fashioning**

The parallel passages add what the body alone does not account for. *“He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect. ˹Yet˺ you hardly give any thanks”* (32:9). Sūrat al-Muʾminūn states the same list and adds that thanks is hardly given (23:78), and Sūrat al-Mulk puts the list in the Prophet's mouth as a proclamation: He brought you into being and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect (67:23). Three sūrahs name the same three faculties in the same order and attach the same complaint to them: they are given freely and thanked for rarely. The list in this verse stops at the body — created, fashioned, proportioned — and the omission of the faculties makes the next verse's turn to the form all the more pointed. A person composed in a chosen shape is also a person given the means to know who composed him, and the means are the reason the question in verse 6 can be asked at all.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:8

> moulding you in whatever form He willed

**Expanded Commentary**

**Composed and Put Together**

*Rakkabaka* is from *r-k-b*, the root of mounting, riding, and composing — a thing placed upon or into another so that the two become one arrangement. The verb is used of mounting an animal, of riding a ship, and of the composition of a structure from parts. Applied to the human being it describes the assembly: the parts fashioned in the previous verse are put together into a single working form. The image is of composition rather than of manufacture, and composition implies a prior arrangement of separate elements. Nothing in the verse names the elements, and the naming is unnecessary — the reader is himself the composed thing, and the composition is what he experiences every time he moves, sees, or speaks. The verse ends the list of acts with the act that produces the person as a whole.

**Whatever Form He Willed**

*Fī ayyi ṣūratin shāʾ* — in whatever form He willed. The phrase is general and it is attached to the willing rather than to a specification. The commentators read it as a statement about the variety of human forms: tall and short, fair and dark, beautiful and plain, each composed in the form its Maker chose. The reading is supported by the grammar, since *ayyi* with the indefinite sense covers every possibility without selecting one. The emphasis therefore falls not on any particular form but on the fact that the form was chosen. A person did not arrange his own composition, did not select his features, and did not decide his proportions. The verse places all three inside a willing that preceded him, and the placing is the answer to the question in verse 6.

**An Alternative Reading: Transformation**

Some exegetes took the verse to mean that God is able to compose human beings in the forms of other creatures, as a punishment, and they cited the transformation of the Sabbath-breakers: *“We said to them, ‘Be disgraced apes!’”* (2:65). The reading makes the verse a warning rather than a description of the human form, and it has the advantage of connecting the passage to a known Qur'anic precedent. It has the disadvantage of importing a punitive sense into a list that has been entirely beneficent — created, fashioned, proportioned, composed — and of making the closing question a threat where the preceding verses are gifts. The majority reading, that the verse describes the composition of each person in the form willed for him, fits the sequence better and does not require the reader to supply an unmentioned punishment.

**An Inner Reading: States Rather Than Shapes**

A third strand of interpretation takes the form as inward rather than outward. On this reading the composition refers to the states of the soul — faith or disobedience, felicity or wretchedness — and the verse says that God composes each person in whatever state He wills. The support for the reading is the Qur'an's use of the same vocabulary for the two outcomes, as in the description of the Day when some are wretched and some are joyful (11:105). The reading is coherent but it strains the word *ṣūrah*, which denotes form and shape throughout the Qur'an and is used of the human body in the verses immediately before this one. The outer reading remains primary; the inner one survives as a reminder that the composition described here is not merely physical, since the same hand that shaped the body gave the hearing, the sight, and the intellect.

**The Will as the Last Word of the Rebuke**

The sequence of four acts ends with a willing, and the placement is significant. Creation, fashioning, proportioning, and composition are all descriptions of what was done; the willing is the ground of the doing. Ending on the ground rather than on the act removes any possibility of the reader treating his form as an accident or as his own achievement. Nothing about him was chosen by him, and everything about him was chosen. The rebuke of verse 6 asked what had made him bold, and the answer supplied across three verses is that he is a made thing whose making was willed at every stage. Boldness in a made thing against its maker is not only ungrateful but unintelligible, and the unintelligibility is what the question of verse 6 was asking about.

**One Sentence Across Three Verses**

Verses 6, 7, and 8 form a single interrogative sentence, and the question mark falls only at the end of verse 8. The structure requires the reader to hold the question open through the whole list of gifts: what has made you bold against your Lord, the Most Generous, who created you, who fashioned you, who proportioned you, who composed you in whatever form He willed? The suspension is the rhetorical device, and its effect is to make each gift arrive inside a question that has not been closed. A list of benefits read as a statement would produce gratitude in the abstract; the same list read as the middle of an accusation produces something sharper. The reader reaches the question mark with the whole inventory in his mind and no answer in his mouth.

**From Description to Response**

The three verses of gift are followed immediately by a single particle of refusal in verse 9, and the sequence tells the reader what the gifts were for. A description of benefits ends, in the Qur'an's Makkan style, either in gratitude or in denial, and this passage presents the denial as the unexpected outcome. The unexpectedness is what makes the particle necessary: *kallā* marks a rejection of something that has just been established, and what has been established is that the reader is a composed, proportioned, willed creation of a generous Lord. The denial named in the following verse is a denial of the Day of Judgement, and the connection between the two is that a person who refuses to be a made thing will refuse to be a summoned one. Nothing in verse 8 states this link, and none needs to be stated. The composition of the form was itself the argument, and the argument's failure is what the next verse records.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:9

> But no! In fact, you deny the ˹final˺ Judgment

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Particle of Refusal**

*Kallā* is a particle with no exact equivalent in English, and the Qur'an uses it thirty-three times, always in Makkan sūrahs and almost always in the second half of them. It refuses, rebukes, and contradicts, and it does all three at once. Rendered as "but no", "certainly not", or "no indeed", it signals that something has just been said or assumed that cannot stand. What it refuses here is not stated in a preceding proposition; the refusal is directed at the condition of the person addressed in verses 6 to 8. A made thing that acts boldly against its maker has been operating on an assumption, and *kallā* withdraws the assumption. The particle's placement at the head of a verse is characteristic: it opens the second movement of the sūrah and separates the argument about the human body from what follows.

**al-Dīn: Judgement and Religion**

The word denied is *al-dīn*, which carries two related senses throughout the Qur'an. It means a judgement, a reckoning, a requital, and it means a religion, a way of life, an obedience. The two senses are joined in the phrase *yawm al-dīn*, the Day of Judgement, which Sūrat al-Fātiḥah names as God's dominion: *“Master of the Day of Judgment”* (1:4). The translation used here selects the first sense, and the selection is sound, because the sūrah's argument has been leading to a reckoning from the opening verse. But the second sense is not excluded. To deny *al-dīn* is to deny that there is a reckoning and to deny that there is an obedience owed. Both refusals are contained in one word, and the word's doubleness is why the Qur'an can use it as the name of the Day and as the name of the religion without distinguishing them.

**A Denial in the Present Tense**

*Tukadhdhibūna* is an imperfect verb, and the imperfect in Arabic denotes an action ongoing or repeated rather than completed. The accusation is therefore not that the addressee denied something once, in the past, on a particular occasion. It is that he is engaged in a continuing practice of denial. The intensive form of the root *k-dh-b*, reduplicated in the middle, adds repetition and effort: not merely to call something false but to keep calling it false. A present-tense intensive verb of denial describes a settled disposition, and the disposition is what makes the boldness of verse 6 intelligible. The person is bold because he is, continuously and with effort, refusing to accept that he will be brought to account.

**Why the Denial Is Named Here**

The placement is deliberate. Three verses have described creation, fashioning, proportioning, and composition, all of them gifts from a Lord named the Most Generous. The natural conclusion of such a list would be gratitude, and the Qur'an's parallel lists do draw that conclusion: *He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect; yet thanks is hardly given* (32:9). This sūrah draws a harsher conclusion. After the list of gifts it states that the addressee denies the Judgement, and the statement functions as a diagnosis. The boldness complained of in verse 6 is not primarily a moral failure of ingratitude; it is an error about what will happen. A person who believed that a reckoning was coming would not behave as this one behaves, and the verse identifies the missing belief as the source of the behaviour.

**The Same Refusal in the Sūrah That Follows**

Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn, the next chapter in the received order, makes the identical move with the identical particle. It rebukes those who cheat in measure, then says of the deniers: *“those who deny Judgment Day!”* (83:11), and adds that *“None would deny it except every evildoing transgressor”* (83:12). The correspondence is close enough to suggest a settled pattern of argument in the Makkan preaching: a rebuke about conduct, then the naming of the denial behind the conduct, then a description of its consequence. Here the rebuke is about boldness rather than cheating, but the structure holds. The denial is not treated as an intellectual position arrived at by weighing evidence; it is treated as a symptom of a way of living, and the two chapters agree on that treatment.

**Denial as the Ground of the Surveillance**

The verses that follow this one describe watchers over every soul, noble angels who record everything and know whatever people do. The description is a direct answer to the denial, and the answer is not an argument but a fact. Nothing in verses 10 to 12 attempts to demonstrate that the Judgement will occur; the demonstration offered is that the person who denies it is already being observed by beings who are writing down what he does. A denial of the reckoning is shown to be incompatible with the actual conditions of the denier's life. The technique is characteristic of the short Makkan chapters: rather than refuting the objection, they describe the situation in which the objection is being raised, and the description does the refuting without being offered as one.

**Two Senses of Denial in One Sentence**

The verse can be read as an accusation of unbelief or as an accusation of living as though there were no reckoning, and the two readings are not alternatives. A person who denies the Judgement as a proposition will also deny it as a constraint on his conduct, and the second denial persists even in those who accept the first. The intensive present-tense verb covers both, which is why the accusation can be addressed to humanity as a whole rather than to a particular group of disbelievers. The address of verse 6 was universal — O humanity — and this verse continues it. Whatever a person claims to believe about the end, the manner of his days either affirms the claim or denies it, and the denial performed by conduct is the one this verse names. That is why the particle of refusal is addressed to humanity at large: the delusion is not the property of a faction, and the reckoning that is refused by the manner of living is refused by almost everyone at almost every hour.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:10

> while you are certainly observed by vigilant

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence Deliberately Left Open**

The Arabic verse ends on an adjective: *la-fī ḥāfiẓīn* — you are certainly under watchers. No noun follows, and the description is completed only in the next verse, where the watchers are named as noble angels who record. The suspension is a feature of the text rather than an accident of translation, and it produces a specific effect. For the length of one verse the reader holds an unspecified surveillance: something is watching, it is plural, and it is of a kind denoted by a word meaning to guard. Only in the following verse does the surveillance acquire a nature. The technique is common in the short Makkan chapters, which frequently split a single description across a verse boundary to make each half land separately. Here the first half establishes that the watching exists; the second establishes who is doing it.

**Ḥāfiẓīn: Guards and Keepers**

The word is the active plural participle of *ḥ-f-ẓ*, a root whose range covers guarding, protecting, preserving, and memorizing. The same root gives *ḥāfiẓ* for one who guards a thing from loss and *ḥafīẓ* for one who preserves it faithfully, and it is used of God as *al-Ḥafīẓ*, the Preserver. Applied to the angels it carries both senses at once: they guard what happens to the person, and they preserve it from being lost. The root's association with memorization is not incidental. A record kept by a guard is a record that survives, and the survival of the record is what makes the reckoning possible. The choice of this root rather than a word meaning merely to see or to observe fixes the character of the surveillance as custodial rather than inquisitorial.

**Over Every Soul a Watcher**

Sūrat al-Ṭāriq states the same fact in a verse of comparable brevity: *“There is no soul without a vigilant angel ˹recording everything˺”* (86:4). The formulation there is universal and negative — no soul exists without one — which excludes exceptions rather than describing a general rule. The two passages agree in placing the watcher over the individual soul rather than over a community, a city, or a generation. The individuality is essential to the argument of this sūrah, which has moved from cosmic events to a single person's knowledge of his own deeds. A surveillance distributed across groups would not produce the knowledge described in verse 5, since that knowledge is of what each soul sent forward. The watcher is plural in form and singular in assignment, and the plural here denotes a class rather than a crowd.

**The Two Receivers**

Sūrat Qāf describes the arrangement in more detail: *“As the two recording-angels— ˹one˺ sitting to the right, and ˹the other to˺ the left—note ˹everything˺”* (50:17). The pairing of right and left is the standard description, and the two are distinguished in the later tradition by the kind of deed each records. The verse adds the immediacy of the work: *“not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺”* (50:18). The word used for vigilant there is from the same root as the word used here, which links the two descriptions firmly. Nothing in this verse names the number of watchers or their positions, and the omission allows it to stand as the general statement of which Sūrat Qāf gives the particular arrangement.

**Observed While Believing Himself Unobserved**

The contrast with verse 6 is the point of the sequence. A person emboldened against his Lord has acted as though nothing were watching. The verse states that he is watched, and the statement is not a threat of future exposure but a description of a present condition. He has been observed throughout the boldness. The deception complained of in verse 6 — what deluded you — is identified here in its mechanism: the delusion consisted in the appearance of privacy. Nothing in the verse says the person was unaware of the watchers, and the commentators do not require that he was; the delusion is the assumption that what is unseen is unrecorded. The correction offered is that the unseen is precisely where the recording happens, and that the record has been accumulating during every act the person took to be his alone.

**The Emphasis Particle Inside the Statement**

The construction begins with *inna*, rendered here as "certainly", and the emphasis is not decorative. A bare statement of surveillance could be treated as a claim open to dispute; the particle marks it as an assertion the speaker is committed to. The same particle opens verse 13, where the virtuous are declared to be in bliss, and its use in both places ties the surveillance to the outcome: the watchers exist because a judgement is coming, and the judgement is coming because the watching has been done. The certainty of the one is the certainty of the other. Nothing in the sūrah argues for the existence of the watchers, and the emphatic particle is what stands in place of an argument — a declaration rather than a demonstration.

**Watching as a Form of Honour**

The next verse names the watchers as noble, and the nobility changes the character of the surveillance described here. A record kept by contemptible agents would be a threat; a record kept by noble ones is a form of attention. The Qur'an's language of honour for the angels appears again in Sūrat al-Takwīr, where the messenger of revelation is described as noble and powerful, obeyed and trusted (81:19–21). The honour of the recording angels belongs to the same order. Their work is not a policing of a suspect but the keeping of a document by beings of high rank, and the rank is what guarantees the document's integrity. The verse under discussion names only the guarding; the honour arrives in the next verse and reinterprets it. A person watched by noble beings is a person whose affairs are being treated as significant, and significance is the opposite of the neglect the deluded person has assumed.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:11

> honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Noble, and the Name That Answered**

The adjective is *kirām*, plural of *karīm* — noble, generous, honoured — and it is the same word from the divine name used in verse 6, *al-Karīm*, the Most Generous. The recurrence is short and unmistakable within a chapter of nineteen verses. The Lord against whom humanity has become bold is the Most Generous, and the angels he has set over humanity are noble. The echo is not merely verbal; it fixes the character of the surveillance. What is being kept over a person's deeds is kept by beings whose defining attribute is honour, and the honour derives from the one who is Generous. A reader who notices the echo understands the recording as an act of dignified attention rather than of suspicion, and the understanding is confirmed by the second adjective that follows immediately.

**Writers**

*Kātibīn* is the plural of *kātib*, writer, from *k-t-b*, the root of writing, ordaining, and prescribing. The participle denotes an ongoing activity rather than a completed one: they are writing, continuously, and the writing is their function. The verb appears in the same context in Sūrat Qāf, where the two recording angels are described as noting everything and no word escapes them (50:17–18). What is written is not specified in this verse, and the unspecified object is what makes the surveillance complete. A writer whose task is defined by a list would leave gaps; a writer whose task is the person's deeds leaves none. The Qur'an's language of writing for divine matters is broader than records — decrees are written, and the Book itself is written — but here the writing is of human acts and its product is a document the person will later receive.

**The Record Laid Open**

The product of this writing appears on the Day, and its appearance is described in detail elsewhere. *“And on the Day of Judgment We will bring forth to each ˹person˺ a record which they will find laid open”* (17:13). The record is individual, produced, and open, and the person is addressed directly: *“Read your record. You ˹alone˺ are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself”* (17:14). Sūrat al-Kahf adds its completeness: the wicked see in it nothing omitted, small or great (18:49). The three descriptions agree that the document is the person's own and that its contents are exhaustive. This verse supplies the origin of that document without describing its delivery. The writing is happening now; the handing over happens on the Day; the connection between the two is the reason the surveillance described in the previous verse matters.

**Right Hand and Left**

The manner of delivery is given in the neighbouring sūrahs. *“As for those who are given their record in their right hand, they will have an easy reckoning”* (84:7–8). *“And as for those who are given their record ˹in their left hand˺ from behind their backs … and will burn in the blazing Fire”* (84:10–12). Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah gives the same division from the two sides, with the one who receives his record in his right hand crying out for everyone to read it (69:19). The division into two classes corresponds exactly to the division in verses 13 and 14 of this sūrah, the virtuous in bliss and the wicked in Hell. The record is therefore not a neutral document; the hand that receives it is already an indication of the outcome, and the outcome is determined by what was written.

**Not a Word Escapes**

The completeness of the recording is stated in its strongest form in Sūrat Qāf: *“not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺”* (50:18). Speech is singled out there because it is the act most easily forgotten by its speaker — words pass, and the speaker assumes they pass away. The verse denies the assumption. If words are written, deeds are written, and if the smallest utterance is captured, nothing larger escapes. This verse's phrase *recording everything* carries the same scope, and the translation supplies the object in brackets because the Arabic leaves it unexpressed. The unexpressed object is broader than any specification would be: whatever falls under the heading of what a person does is written down, and the writing is done by beings who cannot forget, cannot be bribed, and cannot be deceived.

**Why Angels Write What God Already Knows**

The recording is not for God's information. The Qur'an is emphatic that nothing is hidden from Him, and this sūrah's own closing verse places all authority with Him on the Day. The writing is for the person. A record kept by another is a record the person can be shown, and the showing is what makes the reckoning something he participates in rather than something done to him. The command to read your own record (17:14) presupposes a document produced by someone else and handed over. The noble angels are therefore not informants but witnesses, and their nobility is what qualifies them: a testimony of weight requires a witness of weight. The writing described here creates the evidence by which the person will be convinced of his own case.

**The Sentence Completed**

Verses 10 and 11 form one statement, and the completion is what supplies the subject of the watching. Read together: you are certainly under watchers — noble angels who record. The first verse established that a surveillance exists over every person; the second establishes its agents, its rank, and its activity. Nothing further is added about the angels in this sūrah. Their number, their names, their manner of writing, and their position relative to the person are all left unstated, and the unstating keeps the emphasis where the sūrah wants it: on the fact of being recorded. The verse that follows reduces the description to its shortest form — they know whatever you do — and the reduction confirms that the point of the whole passage is knowledge of the person's deeds, held by others, preserved, and awaiting delivery.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:12

> They know whatever you do

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shortest Verse in the Passage**

Four words in Arabic: *yaʿlamūna mā tafʿalūn* — they know what you do. The verse is the shortest in this section and among the shortest in the sūrah, and its brevity is functional. After two verses establishing watchers and naming them as noble recording angels, this verse removes the apparatus and states the result. What remains is knowledge. No instrument is mentioned, no document, no method. The recording of the previous verse is not contradicted but subsumed: writing is a form of keeping, and keeping presupposes knowing. The verse's placement as the last of the three means that the reader arrives at knowledge as the conclusion of the surveillance, and the conclusion is stated with the least possible elaboration. Nothing needs to be added, because the addition would be about the angels rather than about the person, and the person is the subject of the whole passage.

**Knowing the Deed**

The object is *mā tafʿalūn*, what you do — from *f-ʿ-l*, the root of action, deed, and doing. The root is the most general available in Arabic for human conduct; it covers acts of the body, acts of speech, and acts of giving or withholding. The verse does not restrict it to what is visible, to what is serious, or to what is remembered. Sūrat Qāf extends the same coverage to speech, and no word a person utters escapes the observer ready to write it down (50:18). If words are included, acts are included, and if the smallest utterance is known, the largest deed is known. The generality of the root is what makes the knowledge complete, and the completeness is what the reckoning described at the end of this sūrah requires. A partial knowledge would produce a partial account, and the Qur'an's account of the Day admits no gaps.

**Knowledge Beyond Writing**

Writing could in principle be mechanical: a mark set down for each event. Knowing is not mechanical. The verse moves from the record to the awareness behind it, and the movement raises the status of the watchers. They are not instruments but knowers, and their knowing is what makes their testimony weighty. A document produced by something that did not understand what it recorded could be contested; a document produced by beings who know what the person did cannot be. The order of the three verses — watching, writing, knowing — is therefore an ascent rather than a list. Each verse attributes more to the watchers than the one before, and the last attributes the most while saying the least. The compression is deliberate: the highest claim is made in the shortest sentence.

**The Witness Who Cannot Be Contradicted**

The knowledge described here produces the document that the person will be told to read. *“Read your record. You ˹alone˺ are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself”* (17:14). The command places the reckoning in the person's own hands, and the placement is possible only because the record was made by others who knew what they were recording. A person cannot dispute a document he is competent to read and whose contents he recognizes. The verse's statement that they know whatever you do is the ground of that recognition: what is in the book is what you did, and you know it because they knew it first. The Qur'an's account of the Day consistently presents the person as convinced by his own case rather than as overruled, and the convincing depends on the knowledge stated in this verse.

**What the Watchers Do Not Know**

The verse limits the object to deeds. It does not say the watchers know the heart, and the Qur'an reserves knowledge of what is in the breasts to God: He knows what is hidden and what the hearts contain, and no one knows what is in the soul except its Maker. The limitation is not a deficiency in the angels but a marking of a boundary. The record is of acts, and the assessment of acts belongs to the one who knows their inward dimension. Nothing in this verse claims more, and the restraint is what allows the sūrah's closing verse to place all authority with God. The watchers know what was done; the judge knows why it was done, what it was worth, and what followed from it. The division of knowledge is the division of the passage.

**A Continuous Present**

*Tafʿalūn* is imperfect, denoting action ongoing or repeated rather than completed. The knowledge is therefore not of a finished life reviewed afterwards but of a life being lived and known as it is lived. The tense matters for the argument of the sūrah, which has been addressing a person in the middle of his boldness. He is being bold now, and the doing is being known now. The verse denies him the interval he has assumed — the space between an act and its assessment in which the act seems to belong to him alone. There is no such interval. What he is doing is being known while he does it, and the knowing is continuous with the doing. The present tense is what makes the surveillance a fact about this moment rather than a warning about a later one.

**The Hinge to the Two Destinies**

Verse 13 opens a new section with *inna*, indeed, and announces that the virtuous will be in bliss. The connection between the knowledge stated here and the division announced there is not spelled out, but it is the hinge of the sūrah's second half. What was known will be reckoned; what was reckoned determines the class to which a person belongs; and the classes are two. The three verses of surveillance have prepared the two verses of destiny, and the preparation consists entirely in establishing that nothing has been lost. A person might hope that his deeds have passed away with the moments in which they occurred. This verse removes the hope by stating that others have known them throughout. What follows is therefore not an accusation but a consequence, and the consequence is already in motion.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:13

> Indeed, the virtuous will be in bliss

**Expanded Commentary**

**al-Abrār**

The word is the plural of *barr*, from *b-r-r*, a root whose field covers goodness, kindness, piety, and truthfulness. The noun *barr* denotes dry land as opposed to sea, and the same root gives the adjective for one who is good in the widest sense — upright, generous, faithful to his word. The plural *abrār* is used in the Qur'an exclusively of the righteous, and it appears in descriptions of Paradise rather than of worldly conduct. Nothing in the verse defines the term, and the definition has to be gathered from elsewhere. The gathering produces a consistent picture: the *barr* is the one whose goodness extends outward to others and is grounded in belief, and the two features are inseparable in the Qur'an's usage.

**Righteousness Defined at Length**

Sūrat al-Baqarah supplies the definition in its most extended form: *“Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west. Rather, the righteous are those who believe in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets; who give charity out of their cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives; who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make; and who are patient in times of suffering, adversity, and in ˹the heat of˺ battle”* (2:177). The list combines belief, giving, worship, honesty, and endurance, and it opens by rejecting a formal criterion in favour of a substantive one. The *abrār* of this verse are the people described there. Sūrat Āl ʿImrān adds the cost of the giving: righteousness is not attained until a person donates something he cherishes (3:92).

**Bliss as a State**

*Naʿīm* denotes delight, ease, and the condition of being well provided for; the root is used of soft living and of the enjoyment that follows sufficiency. The verse places the virtuous *in* bliss — *la-fī naʿīm* — using the preposition of containment. They are not given bliss as a possession but situated inside it as an environment. The construction is significant: a possession can be lost, an environment cannot be avoided. The same construction is used for the opposite class in the next verse, where the wicked are *in* Hell, and the symmetry is exact. Both classes are described as enclosed by their condition. Sūrat al-Takāthur attaches a warning to the same word: on that Day you will be questioned about the pleasures you enjoyed (102:8), which indicates that *naʿīm* in this world is a loan and in the next a dwelling.

**The Same Statement in the Following Sūrah**

Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn repeats this verse nearly word for word: *“Surely the virtuous will be in bliss”* (83:22). The two chapters are adjacent in the received order and both are Makkan, and the correspondence shows that the statement was a fixed formula rather than an incidental phrase. Al-Muṭaffifīn then expands what this sūrah leaves unstated — couches, a sealed wine, a musk-scented drink, companions, and the observation that this is the reward of the doers of good (83:23–34). The expansion confirms that the brevity of this verse is a matter of scope rather than of content. It names the class and the condition and stops, leaving the description of the condition to the chapters that take it up at greater length. The reader who moves from this sūrah to the next finds the same statement with its details filled in.

**The Virtuous Named First**

The order of the two classes is not arbitrary. The sūrah has spent four verses describing the end of the world, one verse describing knowledge, four verses describing boldness and denial and surveillance, and now it turns to the outcome. The first outcome named is the good one. The placement is consistent with the Qur'an's general practice of naming Paradise before Hell in paired statements, and it has a rhetorical effect: the reader encounters the possibility of bliss while still holding the accusation of verses 6 to 9. The accusation has not been withdrawn, and the bliss has not been assigned to anyone in particular. Both classes are open, and the openness is what makes the sequence of the sūrah a summons rather than a verdict. The name of the Lord in verse 6 was the Most Generous, and the first outcome described is the generous one.

**Birr and the Generous Lord**

The connection between the class named here and the divine name in verse 6 is closer than it appears. *Karīm* and *barr* both belong to the language of generous goodness, and the Qur'an describes the act that defines the *abrār* in terms that answer the generosity: *“and give food—despite their desire for it—to the poor, the orphan, and the captive, ˹saying to themselves,˺ ‘We feed you only for the sake of Allah, seeking neither reward nor thanks from you’”* (76:8–9). Giving without expectation of return is the human counterpart of giving without being asked, and the counterpart is what makes the virtuous a class defined by relation to God rather than by a list of qualities. The boldness of verse 6 was refusal of that relation; the birr of this verse is its acceptance.

**Bliss Named Without Description**

The verse contains no image. No gardens, no rivers, no couches, no companions — nothing that the later sūrahs supply in abundance. What it contains is a name for a condition and a statement that a class of people will be inside it. The absence of imagery is what gives the verse its weight in this position, because a description of Paradise would divert attention from the division itself. The sūrah is concerned with the fact that humanity falls into two classes and that the classes have different surroundings. Everything about the surroundings is deferred. The deferral is also consistent with the chapter's method throughout: it states facts in the shortest available form and leaves the elaboration to the passages that share its vocabulary. What remains here is the division, and the division is the argument.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:14

> and the wicked will be in Hell

**Expanded Commentary**

**al-Fujjār**

The word is the intensive plural of *fājir*, from *f-j-r*, the root of breaking open, bursting, and gushing forth. A *fājir* is one who breaks through a limit, and the breaking is not a single act but a disposition — hence the intensive form, which denotes a person characterized by it. The same root appears earlier in this very sūrah, where the seas are described as bursting open in verse 3. The echo is one of the Qur'an's quietest and most exact: the verb of a contained thing exceeding its containment, and the noun of a person exceeding the containment set for his conduct. Nothing in either verse draws the connection, and none needs to be drawn. The word carries it, and the reader who hears both in one short chapter hears a physical event and a moral category named by the same root.

**Jaḥīm**

*Jaḥīm* denotes an intensely blazing fire, and the root *j-ḥ-m* conveys fierce heat and the glare of it. The Qur'an uses the word as one of the names of the Fire, alongside *nār*, *saʿīr*, *jaḥannam*, *laẓā*, *saqar*, and *ḥuṭamah*. Each name carries a different emphasis: *saʿīr* a raging blaze, *laẓā* a fire that strips, *ḥuṭamah* a crusher, *saqar* a scorcher. *Jaḥīm* specifies intensity, and the specification is what the following verse develops when it describes the wicked as burning in it on the Day of Judgement. The translation's "Hell" is adequate but general; the Arabic word is particular. It names a fire of a specific character, and the naming is the first of three verses that describe what happens to this class.

**An Exact Mirror of the Previous Verse**

The grammar of verses 13 and 14 is identical, and the identity is deliberate. Both begin with *inna*; both place their subject inside a condition using the preposition of containment; both name the class and then the state. The virtuous in bliss, the wicked in Hell. Nothing distinguishes the two sentences except their content, and the perfect parallelism makes the difference between the contents unmistakable. The technique removes any suggestion that the two outcomes are of different kinds or degrees — both are enclosures, both are certain, both follow from the same reckoning. What differs is what encloses. A reader who hears the two verses in sequence hears a single structure applied twice, and the structure's symmetry is what makes the choice of class the only variable left to the person addressed.

**The Same Statement in the Following Sūrah**

Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn describes the same class with the same root and adds the mechanism: *“But no! In fact, their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit!”* (83:14), followed by the sealing off from their Lord (83:15) and the burning in Hell (83:16). The staining is the inward counterpart of the *fujūr* named here — a breaking open that has left a mark. Al-Muṭaffifīn also identifies the deniers as those who deny the Day of Judgement, none of whom denies it except every evildoing transgressor (83:11–12), which connects the class to the accusation in verse 9 of this sūrah. The two chapters therefore describe the same people from two directions: this one names them by their character, that one by what their character has done to their hearts.

**Two Classes and Nothing Between**

The division presented in these two verses is exhaustive. Every soul described in verse 5 comes to know what it sent forward, and every such soul belongs to one of the two classes named here. The Qur'an does admit intermediate language elsewhere — the people of the heights, the mixing of a good deed with a bad, the forgiven among the sinful — but this sūrah does not use it. Its method throughout is the shortest statement of the largest fact, and the largest fact here is that there are two surroundings and that everyone will be inside one of them. The absence of intermediate categories is not a denial that they exist; it is a refusal to elaborate. The chapter's nineteen verses do not have room for qualification, and the qualification would weaken the summons that the division is meant to produce.

**Fujūr as the Breaking of Bounds**

The moral sense of the root is worth stating precisely, because it explains why the class is named as it is. *Fujūr* in the Qur'an's usage is not primarily a catalogue of specific sins; it is the condition of a person who has broken the limit set for him. Sūrat al-Shams places the two possibilities before the soul in a single line, describing God as inspiring the soul with both its wrongdoing and its piety — *fujūr* and *taqwā* in the Arabic (91:8). The pairing is exact: *taqwā* is keeping within bounds out of awareness, and *fujūr* is going beyond them. This sūrah's division of humanity therefore rests on the same two terms, and the verse under discussion names the class that chose the second. The choice was available to both classes, and the availability is what makes the outcome a consequence rather than a sentence imposed from outside.

**Named, Then Described**

This verse names the class and its enclosure and stops. The two verses that follow supply what this one withholds: the burning on the Day of Judgement, and the absence of any escape. The sequence is the same as the one used for the virtuous class, which is named here and described at length in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn. The method is consistent — state the division first, then fill in one side. What makes the difference is that the description of the wicked class is given in this chapter while the description of the virtuous is given in the next. The arrangement means that a reader of this sūrah receives the full account of the punishment and only the name of the reward, and the asymmetry is appropriate to a chapter whose purpose is to wake a person who has been deluded by the appearance of this world.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:15

> burning in it on Judgment Day

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb of Entering Fire**

*Yaṣlawnahā* comes from *ṣ-l-y*, a root used of exposing something to fire, of being scorched, and of entering a fire in order to experience it. The verb does not merely place the subject in the vicinity of the flame; it describes contact and the effect of contact. The same root is used elsewhere in the Qur'an for the entering of the Fire as a punishment and for the roasting of a thing in it. The form here is active, which is worth noting: the wicked are said to burn, not to be burned, and the active construction makes the experience their own. Nothing in the verse describes an agent applying the fire to them. They enter it and they undergo it, and the undergoing is attributed to them in the same grammatical voice in which they earlier acted boldly against their Lord.

**The Day Named a Third Time**

*Yawm al-dīn* appears in verse 9 as the thing being denied and appears again here as the time of the burning. Between them the word *dīn* has carried the whole weight of the sūrah's second half: the denial, the surveillance, the two classes, and now the date. The recurrence is not a repetition of vocabulary but a return to the point at issue. The person addressed in verse 9 denies that there is a Day of Judgement; verse 15 states that the wicked will burn in the Fire on that very Day. The two verses are joined by the same phrase, and the joining is the argument. What is denied in one is asserted in the other, and the assertion is given in the form of an event with a time rather than a proposition to be debated.

**A Time Added to an Enclosure**

Verse 14 placed the wicked inside Hell. This verse adds when they will be inside it. The addition is significant because it converts a state into an appointment. A condition described without a time can be treated as general or metaphorical; a condition attached to a named day is a schedule. The sūrah has therefore moved from classification to calendar, and the calendar is the same one whose denial was diagnosed in verse 9. Nothing in the verse describes how the wicked arrive, what precedes the Day, or what else happens on it. Only the burning and the date are given. The economy is consistent with the chapter's method: state the fact in the fewest words, and let the fact's relation to the other stated facts do the work.

**The Same Statement in the Following Sūrah**

Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn says of the wicked that they will surely burn in Hell (83:16), and adds what follows the burning: they will be told that this is what they used to deny (83:17). The addition supplies the link that this verse leaves implicit. The burning is not an arbitrary penalty but the arrival of the denied thing, and the announcement makes the connection explicit to the person undergoing it. The two chapters use the same vocabulary — *fujjār*, *jaḥīm*, *yawm al-dīn*, *takdhīb* — within a few verses of one another, and the correspondence shows a settled account of the end being delivered in slightly different arrangements. What al-Muṭaffifīn states in three verses this sūrah states in two, and neither version explains itself.

**No Date Given for Bliss**

The asymmetry between the two classes is worth observing. Verse 13 places the virtuous in bliss without attaching a time to it; verse 15 places the wicked in the Fire on the Day of Judgement. The virtuous are described as inside a condition; the wicked are described as arriving at an appointment. One reading takes the difference as an indication that bliss is already prepared and continuously available, while the burning belongs to a specific moment of reckoning. Another takes it as a matter of emphasis, the sūrah specifying the time for the class whose time is being denied. Neither reading requires the other to be false. The effect on the reader is that the punishment is dated and the reward is not, and the dating is what makes the denial of verse 9 a denial of something in particular.

**What Burning Adds to Being In**

Two prepositions of containment have already been used — the virtuous *in* bliss, the wicked *in* Hell. This verse changes the relation from position to action. Being inside a fire is not the same as burning in it, and the difference is the difference between a location and an experience. The Qur'an's descriptions of the Fire consistently supply the experience: a fire that climbs over the hearts, that strips and burns, whose fuel is people and stones, whose people will call for water and be answered with something like molten metal. None of that is given here. A single verb of burning carries it, and the carrying is possible because the audience has heard the descriptions before. The verse names the experience and relies on the rest of the revelation for its content.

**Arrival at What Was Already Prepared**

The burning on the Day is not the beginning of the matter. The verses before it described watchers over every soul, writers recording everything, and knowers aware of whatever people do. The record was being made throughout, and the Day is when the record is delivered. Sūrat al-Isrāʾ describes the delivery: every person's destiny bound to his neck, and a record brought forth and laid open (17:13). This verse describes what the record leads to for one class. The sequence is therefore continuous rather than abrupt — the writing, the knowing, the classification, the burning — and the continuity is what makes the Day a consequence instead of an interruption. Nothing happens on it that was not already in motion before it, and the verse's specification of the date marks the point at which what was in motion arrives.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:16

> and they will have no escape from it

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word for Absence**

The Arabic phrase is *wa-mā hum ʿanhā bi-ghāʾibīn* — and they will not be absent from it. The operative word is *ghāʾibīn*, the plural participle of *gh-y-b*, the root of absence, concealment, and the unseen. The same root gives *ghayb*, that which is hidden from perception, and *ghāʾib*, one who is not present. The verse therefore states its claim in negative terms: not that they will be held, guarded, or prevented from leaving, but that they will not be absent. The negative is stronger than a positive prohibition would be, because it excludes the possibility itself rather than forbidding the act. A person prevented from leaving is still someone who might have left; a person of whom it is said that he will not be absent has no such alternative in the description. The phrasing removes the option by removing its grammatical ground.

**Escape as the Translation's Reading**

The English renders the phrase as having no escape, which conveys the practical consequence rather than the literal statement. The two are equivalent in effect, and the choice of the idiomatic English is sound: absence from a fire one is burning in is escape from it. But the literal Arabic retains something the idiom loses — the notion of concealment. To be absent is not only to be elsewhere but to be out of reach of perception. The verse denies both. The wicked will neither be elsewhere nor hidden. Nothing in the description says they will be watched; the watchers of verses 10 to 12 have already been described, and this verse describes the outcome of that watching in terms of presence. What was recorded will be present with the recorder's subject, and the subject will be present with the fire.

**The Cry for Escape**

Sūrat al-Qiyāmah gives the scene that this verse describes the outcome of. The Day arrives, the moon is eclipsed, the sun and moon are joined, and the person cries out: *“Where is the escape?”* (75:10). The answer is given immediately and in the same particle of refusal that opened verse 9 of this sūrah: *“But no! There will be no refuge”* (75:11). The correspondence is exact — a question about escape answered by a denial of refuge — and this verse states the same denial in the form of a fact about a class of people. The cry in al-Qiyāmah is universal, the denial there is absolute, and the specification here attaches both to the *fujjār*. Nothing is added by the specification except the identification of who will be crying.

**No Refuge, No Helper, No Ransom**

The Qur'an closes off the exits one by one. Al-Qiyāmah denies refuge (75:11). Sūrat al-Baqarah denies the three remaining possibilities in a single verse: *“Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another. No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given”* (2:48). The same three denials are repeated in almost the same words later in the sūrah (2:123). Sūrat al-Dukhān denies the aid of kinship: no kith or kin will benefit another, nor will they be helped (44:41). Taken together these passages enumerate everything a person might rely on — flight, refuge, another soul, an intercessor, a payment, a relative — and deny each. This verse contributes the denial of flight and of concealment, and it contributes it in the shortest form available.

**Permanence Rather Than Duration**

The verse says nothing about how long. It states that absence will not occur, and the statement is compatible with any duration that lacks absence. The Qur'an's explicit statements about permanence are given elsewhere and in their own terms; this verse does not make one. The omission is characteristic of the chapter, which consistently states the fact and withholds the qualification. A reader looking here for a doctrine of the Fire's duration will not find it, and will not find its opposite either. What he will find is the removal of the expectation on which people ordinarily rely — that a painful situation can be exited, hidden from, or survived by absence. The verse removes that expectation and leaves the duration to the passages that address it.

**Concealment as the Delusion's Last Resource**

Verse 6 asked what had made humanity bold against its Lord, and the answer given across the following verses was deception — an appearance taken for a reality. The most basic appearance is privacy: what nobody sees cannot be counted. Verses 10 to 12 dismantled that assumption by describing watchers, writers, and knowers. This verse dismantles its last form. Even if the record were somehow incomplete, the person himself will not be absent from the fire, and absence is the only privacy left to hope for. The delusion is therefore refuted at both ends — the deed cannot escape the record, and the doer cannot escape the place. The refutation is carried by a negative statement about a root meaning to be hidden, and the choice of that root rather than one meaning to flee ties the verse back to the whole argument about what is unseen.

**The Closing of the Pair**

Verses 15 and 16 complete the description of the wicked class, and the completion is what allows the sūrah to turn. Verse 14 named the class and its enclosure, verse 15 gave the experience and the date, and this verse removes the exit. Three verses, and after them nothing further is said about the Fire in this chapter. The turn that follows is a turn to the reader: two questions about the Day of Judgement, and then a single sentence describing what the Day will be. The structure of the second half is therefore description followed by interrogation, and the interrogation is possible only because the description is finished. A chapter that went on describing would never reach the question, and the question is what carries the summons that the whole sūrah has been preparing.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:17

> What will make you realize what Judgment Day is

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Idiom of Realization**

*Mā adrāka* — literally, what has made you know, what has informed you, what has caused you to reach. The verb is from *d-r-y*, to know, and the construction is interrogative with an implied addressee who does not possess the knowledge. The idiom is not a request for information; it is a statement that the information is beyond the addressee's reach. Rendered here as "what will make you realize", it preserves the causative sense of the Arabic: something would have to be done to you in order for you to know. The construction appears throughout the Makkan sūrahs in a consistent pattern — a thing is named, a question is asked about it using this idiom, and the answer is either given or withheld. The pattern is one of the most recognizable features of the early revelation's style, and its appearance here signals a shift in the chapter's register from description to address.

**Where the Idiom Appears**

The same formula opens and structures several short sūrahs. Sūrat al-Qāriʿah asks what will make one realize what the Striking Disaster is (101:3), and returns to the idiom a second time later in the chapter (101:10). Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah puts the same question about the Inevitable Hour (69:3); Sūrat al-Muddaththir about Hell (74:27); Sūrat al-Ṭāriq about the nightly star that appears in its opening oath (86:2); and Sūrat al-Humazah about the Crusher (104:5). In every case the object of the question is something vast — the Hour, the Fire, a celestial sign, a disaster — and in every case the question is asked of a person who cannot answer it. The recurrence establishes the idiom as the Qur'an's way of marking a subject as exceeding ordinary comprehension, and its use in this sūrah places the Day of Judgement in the same category as those five.

**The Shift to the Singular**

Verses 6 and 9 addressed humanity in the plural: what has emboldened you, you deny. This verse addresses a single person. The Arabic second-person singular is unambiguous, and the change of number changes the character of the passage. A plural address indicts a group; a singular address speaks to one listener and, through him, to whoever hears. The commentators differ on whether the singular here is the Prophet or a generic individual, and the difference is not decisive for the meaning. The idiom is used of the Prophet elsewhere in contexts where he is clearly not the one who knows the answer, and it is used in ways that include every hearer. What matters is that the question has stopped being a charge laid against a crowd and has become a direct address to one person standing under it.

**A Question That Expects No Answer**

The rhetorical question in this sūrah has already appeared once, in verse 6, where the addressee was asked what had deluded him. That question also expected no answer, but it implied that an answer existed and could be found. This question is different. It does not imply that the addressee can reach the knowledge; it states that reaching it requires an agency outside him. What will make you realize — the making is not yours. The distinction is important for what follows, because the next verse repeats the question without advancing it, and the repetition confirms that no answer is coming from the addressee. The knowledge of the Day is not something to be acquired by inquiry. It is something to be given, and the giving is what the final verse of the chapter partially performs.

**Withholding as the Method**

No description follows this verse. The next verse repeats the question, and the verse after that supplies one feature of the Day — that no soul will avail another — without describing the Day itself. The withholding is deliberate and consistent with the idiom's function. A full description would answer the question and thereby imply that the question could have been answered by the listener's own effort. By withholding, the sūrah maintains the gap between the person addressed and the thing addressed. The technique is used in Sūrat al-Qāriʿah, where the doubled question about the Striking Disaster is answered by a single image of mountains like carded wool and people like scattered moths (101:4–5), and in Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah, where the question about the Inevitable Hour is answered by naming the peoples who denied it and were destroyed (69:4–8). The answer is always partial, and the partiality is the point.

**The Day Named Again**

*Yawm al-dīn* occurs in verse 9, in verse 15, and twice in the two questions that close the chapter. Four occurrences in nineteen verses, and the chapter's name for its subject is the same phrase each time. The consistency means that the reader cannot mistake what is being discussed: the Day whose denial was diagnosed, the Day on which the wicked burn, the Day that no one can be made to realize by his own effort. The phrase carries the whole argument, and its repetition in the final movement converts the description into a summons. Nothing in these verses introduces a new term or a new image. The chapter ends with the word it has been using throughout, asked about twice, and answered once in the shortest possible form.

**The Function of the Suspension**

The effect of the doubled question on a listener is attention without content. He is told that something is coming, that he does not know what it is, and that knowing it is not within his power. The state produced is not curiosity, which expects satisfaction, but exposure, which does not. The Qur'an's short Makkan chapters work on the listener through states of this kind, and the idiom of realization is their chief instrument. A description can be evaluated and set aside; a question that denies the listener the capacity to answer it cannot. He is left holding an acknowledgment that there is something he does not know and cannot reach, and the acknowledgment is what the following verse's single sentence then fills with one fact — that no soul will be of any use to another, and that all authority on that Day belongs to God.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:18

> Again, what will make you realize what Judgment Day is

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Particle of Sequence**

*Thumma* — then, again, moreover. The particle denotes sequence with a sense of interval or of ascent, and it is used in the Qur'an to mark a step beyond what has just been said rather than a mere continuation. Its presence here signals that the second question is not a restatement for emphasis alone but a further move. The first question asked what would make the addressee realize the Day; the second asks the same thing again after an interval marked by *thumma*. The interval contains nothing — no answer, no description, no new information — and the emptiness of the interval is the significance of the particle. It sets up an expectation of advance and then delivers repetition, and the delivered repetition tells the listener that there is no advance to be had.

**Repetition as Intensification**

The words of the two verses are identical apart from the opening particle. Nothing is added, nothing is withdrawn, and nothing is explained. Repetition of this kind in the Qur'an's Makkan style functions as intensification rather than as amplification: the same statement made twice carries more weight, not more content. The technique is familiar from the refrain of Sūrat al-Raḥmān, where a single question is repeated thirty-one times, and from Sūrat al-Mursalāt, where a warning is repeated at intervals through the chapter. In both cases the repetition produces a state in the listener rather than an argument. Here the doubled question produces the state appropriate to a thing that cannot be described: an acknowledgment, twice required, that the listener does not and cannot know.

**Doubled Questions Elsewhere**

Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah names its subject and then asks about it twice in successive verses: the Inevitable Hour, what is the Inevitable Hour, and what will make you realize what the Inevitable Hour is (69:1–3). Sūrat al-Qāriʿah does the same with the Striking Disaster (101:1–3). The pattern is a name, a question about the name, and a question using the idiom of realization, and it appears in three short Makkan chapters. This sūrah uses the last element of the pattern twice and omits the first two, which is a variation rather than a departure. The doubled *mā adrāka* is stronger than the single one, and the strength is needed here because the subject — the Day of Judgement — has already been named and denied earlier in the same chapter. What requires doubling is not the naming but the acknowledgment.

**What the Doubling Denies**

A single question might be answered. Two questions, the second marked as a step beyond the first and containing no step, deny the possibility of an answer. The denial is not of the Day but of the listener's access to it. Nothing in these two verses suggests that the Day is uncertain or that its occurrence is in doubt; the preceding verses have already stated that the wicked will burn in the Fire on it and that they will not escape. What the doubling denies is comprehension. The listener is being told, twice, that he cannot reach an understanding of this thing by asking, and the telling is itself the instruction. A person who has been asked twice what will make him realize something, and has received no information either time, has been placed in a position of dependence rather than of inquiry.

**The Singular Address Held**

The second-person singular introduced in the previous verse continues here. Both questions are addressed to one listener, and the holding of the singular across the pair makes the address personal rather than general. The chapter began its second movement with a plural accusation — O humanity, what has emboldened you, you deny — and now speaks to an individual. The movement from crowd to person is the movement the whole sūrah has been making, from the splitting of the sky down to the knowledge of a single soul about its own deeds. The doubled question is the point at which the descent reaches the listener directly. He is not being told what his people have done; he is being asked what would make him know what is coming to him, and the question is asked twice because the first asking has not been answered.

**The Partial Answer That Follows**

Verse 19 supplies a response, and the response is limited to two clauses: no soul will avail another anything, and all authority on that Day belongs to God. Neither clause describes the Day's events, its appearance, its duration, or its procedures. What they describe is the condition of persons within it — isolation and sovereignty. The limitation is consistent with the doubled question. A full description would have made the question answerable, and the question was not asked in order to be answered. The two clauses give what can be given without dissolving the unknowability: enough for the listener to understand his own position, not enough for him to comprehend the thing itself. The proportion between two questions and one short answer is the chapter's final structural statement, and it says that the matter is grave and that the knowledge of it is small.

**The Last Move Before the Close**

Nineteen verses, and the chapter ends on this pair and its answer. There is no concluding exhortation, no call to faith, no promise, no threat beyond what has already been stated. The sūrah simply stops after naming the sovereignty of God on the Day. The stopping is a compositional choice with an effect: the listener is left with the doubled question ringing and a single sentence of answer. Nothing resolves the tension between them, and nothing is offered to fill the gap. The chapter's final movement therefore leaves the reader in the state its method has been producing throughout — aware that the world's structure will fail, that his deeds are known, that humanity divides into two classes, and that the Day he cannot comprehend is the day on which no one will be able to help him.

## Sūrah al-Infiṭār 82:19

> ˹It is˺ the Day no soul will be of ˹any˺ benefit to another whatsoever, for all authority on that Day belongs to Allah ˹entirely˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to Two Questions**

The previous two verses asked, twice, what would make the addressee realize the Day of Judgement. This verse is the answer, and it answers with one sentence containing two clauses. The sentence is the longest in the chapter and the last, and its length is proportional to the weight of the doubled question. Nothing in it describes the Day's appearance or its events; both clauses describe its order — the relation of souls to one another, and the location of authority. The choice of what to say is therefore a choice about what matters. A person who wants to know what the Day will look like is told instead what his position in it will be, and the telling is the answer to the question he was asked rather than to the question he wanted to ask.

**No Soul Avails Another**

*Lā taghnī nafsun ʿan nafsin shayʾan* — no soul will avail another anything. The verb is from *gh-n-y*, a root meaning to suffice, to be enough, to render independent. A thing that *yughnī* removes a need. The negation is total: not merely that help will be difficult or costly, but that no soul will remove any need of any other soul. The word *shayʾan*, anything, is the object of the negation and is indefinite, which in a negative construction produces universality — not the slightest thing. The verse therefore denies availing in every degree, from the smallest intercession to the largest rescue. The two souls named are generic; nothing restricts them to relatives, friends, or allies. Any soul and any other soul, and between them no sufficiency of any amount.

**The Same Denial in al-Baqarah**

Sūrat al-Baqarah states the denial twice and adds the three alternatives that are also closed. *“Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another. No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given”* (2:48). The same three denials appear again at 2:123 in nearly identical words. The addition is systematic: after denying that one soul can help another, the verse denies the three means by which help is ordinarily obtained — a mediator, a payment, an ally. All three are unavailable. This sūrah's formulation is shorter and does not enumerate them, but the enumeration is implied by the universality of the negation. A person seeking help on that Day has no route, and the absence of routes is what makes the sovereignty stated in the second clause absolute.

**Kinship Dissolved**

The Qur'an describes the dissolution of the strongest human ties on that Day. *“O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord, and beware of a Day when no parent will be of any benefit to their child, nor will a child be of any benefit to their parent”* (31:33). *“the Day no kith or kin will be of benefit to another whatsoever, nor will they be helped”* (44:41). Sūrat ʿAbasa goes further and describes flight rather than mere inability: on that Day every person will flee from his siblings, his mother and father, his spouse and children, because everyone will have enough concern of his own (80:34–37). The three passages agree that the tie which organizes human life — kinship — ceases to function, and ʿAbasa adds that the cessation is not imposed from outside but arises from each person's own preoccupation.

**All Authority Belongs to God**

*Wa-l-amru yawmaʾidhin li-Llāh* — and the command, the affair, the authority on that day is God's. The word *amr* covers command, matter, affair, and authority, and its range is what makes the clause comprehensive. Not one domain of the Day is allocated to anyone else. The same claim is made in other terms elsewhere: the believers are told that all matters are destined by God (3:154), and the Day is described as belonging to God as Master in the opening of the Qur'an (1:4). The clause is joined to the first by *wa-lām*, translated here as "for", which makes the second clause the ground of the first. No soul avails another because the authority is entirely God's. The causality is stated rather than argued, and the statement is the chapter's final word.

**The Closing Returns to Sovereignty**

The chapter opened with the sky splitting, the stars scattering, the seas bursting, and the graves spilling out — four failures of the created order. It closes with the authority of God intact. The frame is complete and its symmetry is exact: everything that was held together comes apart, and the one who held it is not diminished. Nothing in the closing clause refers back to the opening images, and the absence of reference is what makes the return effective. The reader who has followed the chapter from the first verse arrives at the last with the ruin of the world in mind, and the final sentence tells him what remains. The world's structure was a delegation; the delegation ends; the authority returns to being undivided and unshared.

**Nineteen Verses and Their Shape**

The chapter divides into four movements of unequal length. Four verses describe the end of the world; one verse states what each soul comes to know; eight verses address humanity with a question about its boldness, three acts of making, a denial, and a triple description of surveillance; and six verses give the two classes, their two outcomes, and the doubled question about the Day with its single-sentence answer. The proportion is striking: the largest movement is the address to the person, and the smallest is the description of the cosmic events that opened it. The chapter's title comes from its first word and its argument comes from its middle. What it is about is not the splitting of the sky but the delusion of a made thing that has forgotten its Maker, and the last verse states where that delusion ends — in a Day when no soul will suffice for another and all authority will be God's.
