# Sūrah Ṭā-Hā (Chapter 20) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Conversion of ʿUmar and the Transformation of History**

In the annals of early Islam, Sūrah Ṭā-Hā is immortalized as the catalyst that triggered the conversion of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb—an event that fundamentally altered the political destiny of the prophetic mission. In the sixth year of prophethood, ʿUmar, then a fierce and implacable persecutor of the faith, set out with a drawn sword through the streets of Makkah, determined to assassinate the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and end the division of his tribe. Intercepted by a secret convert who informed him that his own sister Fāṭimah and brother-in-law Saʿīd had embraced the new creed, ʿUmar stormed into their home, finding them listening to a parchment being recited by the Companion Khabbāb ibn al-Aratt. In a fit of fury, ʿUmar assaulted his brother-in-law and struck his sister across the face, drawing blood. Shamed by the sight of her blood and moved by her unshakeable defiance, ʿUmar demanded to read the parchment. When ʿUmar unrolled the scroll and read the opening verses of Sūrah Ṭā-Hā—*“Indeed, I am Allah. There is no deity except Me, so worship Me and establish prayer for My remembrance”* (20:14)—his heart melted, his intellectual arrogance shattered, and he exclaimed: *“How noble and sublime is this speech! Guide me to Muḥammad!”* He marched to the house of al-Arqam, where his public declaration of faith struck terror into the Quraysh and allowed the believers to pray openly beside the Kaʿbah for the first time.

**The Epic Narrative of Moses and Aaron**

The architecture of Sūrah Ṭā-Hā moves from celestial reassurance to the dramatic mountain encounter, and from the palace confrontation to the psychological collapse of Adam in the Garden. The opening movement (1–8) establishes that the Qur'an was not revealed to cause distress or exhaustion, affirming that the Most Compassionate has established Himself upon the Throne (*al-Raḥmānu ʿalā al-ʿarshi stawā*), owning all that is in the heavens, earth, and beneath the subterranean soil. It records Moses' famous, deeply vulnerable supplication: *“My Lord, expand for me my breast, and ease for me my task, and untie the knot from my tongue that they may understand my speech, and appoint for me a minister from my family, Aaron my brother”* (20:25–30). The narrative proceeds through the historic liberation struggle: the confrontation with Pharaoh, the dramatic contest against the imperial magicians whose optical illusions are swallowed by Moses' living staff, the instantaneous conversion of the magicians who choose crucifixion over apostasy (*“So decree whatever you will decree; you can only decree for this worldly life,”* 20:72), the nocturnal flight through the parted sea, the drowning of Pharaoh's hosts, and the subsequent tragedy of the golden calf sculpted by the demagogue al-Sāmirī while Moses was communing on the mountain. The third movement (99–114) warns against turning away from the Reminder (*al-Dhikr*), describing the Day of Judgment when the mountains will be blasted into level dust and all voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate (*wa-khashaʿati al-aṣwātu li-l-Raḥmān*), followed by the divine command to the Prophet: *“And do not hasten with the Qur'an before its revelation is completed to you, and say, 'My Lord, increase me in knowledge'”* (20:114). The fourth movement (115–127) recounts the primordial fall of Adam: forgetting the covenant, deceived by Satan's whisper of the tree of eternity, stripped of his garments, repenting to receive divine election. Descending to earth under the promise: *“Whoever follows My guidance will neither go astray nor suffer, but whoever turns away from My reminder will have a depressed, constricted life (*maʿīshatan ḍankā*).”* The sūrah concludes (128–135) with the command to endure the deniers patiently, to glorify Allah before the rising of the sun and before its setting, and to bid one's family to prayer and be steadfast in it.

The opening lines of the sūrah contain one of the most sublime statements about the psychological and spiritual purpose of divine revelation: *“Ṭā-Hā. We have not sent down to you the Qur'an that you should be distressed (*li-tashqā*), but only as a reminder for those who fear [Allah]”* (20:1–3).

## Verse 20:1

> Ṭâ-Hâ

**Direct Address to the Prophet Muhammad**

A prominent stream of classical commentary identifies *Ṭā-Hā* not merely as cryptic letters, but as a noble form of direct address to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. Major philologists and early commentators, including Ibn ʿAbbās and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, recorded that in certain ancient dialects of Arabia, such as Nabataean or Syriac-influenced Arabic registers, the phrase *ṭā-hā* carried the vocative sense of "O man!" (*yā rajul* or *yā insān*). In this reading, the Creator addresses His Messenger with personal intimacy at the outset of a sūrah dedicated to comfort, spiritual strength, and prophetic endurance. The designation establishes a compassionate relationship between the Divine Speaker and the human recipient before addressing the heavy responsibilities of prophethood. This personal address mirrors the opening of other prophetic chapters, anchoring the Messenger's heart before recalling the trials faced by earlier messengers such as Mūsā (20:9). The term conveys dignity, honour, and affectionate proximity between God and His chosen Messenger.

**Symbolic Polarities of Paradise and Punishment**

In addition to linguistic and titular interpretations, esoteric commentary explored symbolic polarities within the phonetic structure of *ṭāʾ* and *hāʾ*. The classical commentator Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī records an interpretation in which *ṭāʾ* serves as an allusion to *Ṭūbā*, the blessed tree planted in Paradise for the righteous (13:29), while *hāʾ* points toward *al-Hāwiyah*, the deep abyss prepared for the defiant (101:9). Under this symbolic reading, the opening letters form a grand spiritual oath by the ultimate destinations of human existence: the Garden of ultimate delight and the Fire of severe reckoning. The contrast of these two realities at the very start of the sūrah mirrors the overarching theme of the chapter, which contrasts the ultimate success of those who accept divine guidance with the ruin of those whose arrogance leads them to destruction. The entire spectrum of human destiny is so contained within two simple sounds, reminding human beings that every choice carries eternal weight.

**Historical Context of Revelation**

The revelation of Sūrah Ṭā-Hā occurred during a period of intense pressure and persecution against the nascent Muslim community in Mecca. The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ bore immense grief over the rejection of his people and spent long hours of the night standing in prayer until his feet swelled from exhaustion. Reports recorded in *Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī* mention that the idolaters mocked his devotion, claiming that the Qur'an had brought him nothing but suffering and hardship. The opening letters of Sūrah Ṭā-Hā arrived as a divine gentle reassurance, breaking the silence with an authoritative call that restored peace to the Prophet's heart and affirmed that divine revelation is a mercy, not a burden. This historical backdrop illuminates the emotional depth of the sūrah, showing how divine speech restored hope to a struggling community.

## Verse 20:2

> We have not revealed the Quran to you ˹O Prophet˺ to cause you distress

**Divine Purpose Behind Revelation**

The explicit declaration that the Qur'an was not revealed to cause distress or misery (*li-tashqā*) establishes the essential character of divine guidance as an act of unearned mercy rather than a burdensome imposition upon humanity. The root verb *shaqiya* means hardship, spiritual exhaustion, and ultimate misfortune, contrasting directly with *saʿāda*, which means true spiritual felicity and eternal well-being. The commentators such as al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī record that when the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ spent long nights in devout prayer until his feet swelled from standing, unbelievers among the Quraysh mocked his physical devotion, claiming that his new faith had brought him nothing but pain and social alienation. This verse directly refutes their taunts, asserting that spiritual effort and sincere devotion are not sources of wretchedness. The revelation came to liberate human souls from the tyranny of falsehood and spiritual blindness, restoring them to their natural state of harmony with the Creator. It promises that living by divine light brings peace to the heart regardless of outward circumstances, providing an inner sanctuary against worldly storms.

**The Meaning of Prophetic Hardship and Shaqāwah**

The term *tashqā* carries both worldly and Last-Day dimensions that classical scholars analyzed with great analytical nuance. In the worldly sphere, the Prophet's intense grief over his people's stubborn rejection caused him profound emotional pain, prompting repeated divine reassurances across several sūrahs (18:6; 26:3). The Qur'an clarifies that a messenger is responsible only for delivering the message clearly, not for compelling belief or bearing the spiritual guilt of those who choose disbelief. On the Last-Day level, *shaqāwah* refers to the eternal misery of those who reject divine truth, as seen in later passages describing the companion of the Fire (20:123). By clarifying that the Qur'an does not cause *shaqāwah*, the verse affirms that following divine revelation is the sole path to true peace, protecting human beings from both worldly despair and ultimate ruin. The burdens of prophethood are noble trials that lead to eternal elevation rather than tragic destruction, refining the soul for divine presence.

**Prolonged Night Worship and Prophetic Concern**

A prominent secondary narrative about this verse concerns the Prophet's voluntary night prayers (*qiyām al-layl*). Following the early command in Sūrah al-Muzzammil (73:1-4), the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ stood in worship for two-thirds of the night, resting on alternate feet to manage his extreme physical fatigue. Seeing his dedication, God revealed this verse as a gentle relaxation of physical strain, reminding His Messenger that divine law does not demand self-destruction or intolerable physical suffering. Al-Qurṭubī notes that this principle became foundational in Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*), establishing the legal maxim that hardship brings ease (*al-mashaqqah tajlib al-taysīr*). The religion is built upon moderation and sustainability, ensuring that spiritual practice enriches human life rather than draining its vital strength or breaking human endurance over time.

## Verse 20:3

> but as a reminder to those in awe ˹of Allah˺

**Revelation as a Universal Reminder**

The exception introduced by the particle *illā* designates the true purpose of revelation as a *tadhkirah*—a spiritual reminder and awakening—for those who have reverent awe (*yakhshā*). The word *tadhkirah* derives from the Arabic root *dh-k-r*, which implies calling back to consciousness a truth already embedded within the human soul rather than imposing an alien ideology. The commentators such as Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī explain that the human soul is created with an innate primordial disposition (*fiṭrah*) that inherently acknowledges the Creator's oneness and majesty. However, worldly distractions, desires, and social conditioning cast a veil of heedlessness (*ghaflah*) over this innate knowledge. The Qur'an acts as an external catalyst that clears away these spiritual veils, awakening the dormant awareness within the heart. It does not force compliance upon an unwilling mind, but rather restores the soul to its original clarity, orientation, and divine alignment, allowing human beings to fulfill their cosmic purpose.

**The Nature of Tadhkirah in Qur'anic Discourse**

The term *tadhkirah* appears frequently across the Qur'anic canon, serving as an important stylistic and religious marker. In Sūrah al-Muddaththir, the revelation is described as 'a reminder; so whoever wills may remember it' (74:54-55), while in Sūrah Abasa it is called 'a noble reminder' (80:11). Exegetes point out that calling the revelation a reminder implies that human beings will be held accountable on the Day of Resurrection based on knowledge that was made accessible to them. The reminder leaves no excuse for spiritual negligence. It addresses human reason, emotional capacity, and moral intuition simultaneously, providing a balanced call that resonates across all levels of human consciousness. The Qur'an does not demand blind credulity, but invites rigorous reflection upon the signs scattered across creation, history, and human experience.

**Moral Responsibility and Spiritual Perception**

The relationship between divine awe (*khashyah*) and knowledge (*ʿilm*) is central to Qur'anic account of knowledge. As stated in Sūrah Fāṭir, 'Only those of His servants who have knowledge truly fear Allah' (35:28). True knowledge is not only the accumulation of factual data or linguistic skill, but the deep realization of divine presence and moral responsibility. When a person cultivates this inner awareness, every verse of the Qur'an becomes a powerful catalyst for spiritual growth and moral transformation. Conversely, without *khashyah*, intellectual knowledge remains superficial, failing to alter a person's character or choices. The verse reminds believers to continuously cultivate awe in their hearts so that they remain susceptible to the ongoing guidance of the Book throughout life's complex journey.

## Verse 20:4

> ˹It is˺ a revelation from the One Who created the earth and the high heavens—

**The Verbal Power of Tanzīlan**

The use of the absolute verbal noun (*masḍar*) *tanzīlan* stresses the majestic, step-by-step descent of the Qur'an from the highest spiritual realm into the earthly domain. In Arabic rhetoric (*balāghah*), employing the verbal noun in this manner stresses both the absolute truth of the action and the immense grandeur of the source. Classical commentators such as Ibn ʿĀshūr note that *tanzīl* specifies a gradual, deliberate sending down over twenty-three years, tailored to the evolving needs, trials, and questions of the fledgling Muslim community. This gradual descent allowed the believers to absorb divine teachings deeply, integrating them into their daily character and communal structures. The verbal noun reassures the recipient that every word of the text carries the direct authority and power of the Creator, standing firm against all earthly opposition, human skepticism, and philosophical challenges. It confirms that the message is divine, immutable, and preserved from human distortion throughout history.

## Verse 20:5

> the Most Compassionate, ˹Who is˺ established on the Throne

**The Contrast of Infinite Mercy and Supreme Majesty**

The divine declaration *al-Raḥmānu ʿalā l-ʿarshi stawā* synthesizes two foundational dimensions of Islamic belief: infinite, all-encompassing mercy (*al-Raḥmān*) and supreme, unchallengeable cosmic sovereignty (*al-ʿarsh*). By introducing the Sovereign upon the Throne specifically through the name *al-Raḥmān* rather than titles of wrath or vengeance, the verse establishes that divine mercy is the supreme governing principle of the cosmos. Classical commentators such as Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī highlight that the Throne (*al-ʿarsh*) represents the immense roof of creation and the seat of divine decree. Governing this vast universe through mercy implies that every divine law, decree, and act of creation is rooted in ultimate compassion and benefit for creatures. Mercy is not a secondary attribute, but the primary expression of divine governance that sustains all existence across space and time, offering hope to all created beings.

**The Throne as the Symbol of Universal Dominion**

In the Qur'anic picture of the universe, the Throne (*al-ʿarsh*) is described as the greatest created entity, encompassing the heavens and the earth. In Sūrah al-Aʿrāf (7:54) and Sūrah Yūnus (10:3), *istiwā'* upon the Throne is linked to the creation of the heavens and earth and the continuous management of cosmic affairs (*yudabbiru l-amr*). The verse assures believers that the universe is not an abandoned clockwork left to chance or chaotic forces, but a perfectly governed realm subject to divine wisdom. Every event, from the movement of celestial bodies to the trials of human existence, unfolds under the direct authority of the Most Compassionate Sovereign. This realization instills deep peace in the hearts of believers during times of worldly turmoil and uncertainty, reminding them that God controls all outcomes with wisdom and justice. No earthly tyrant or natural disaster operates outside divine sovereign control. The physical grandeur of the Throne serves as a cognitive stepping stone preparing human minds to appreciate uncreated divine majesty in all its manifestations. Contemplating this cosmic order reinforces human trust in divine providence.

## Verse 20:6

> To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth and whatever is in between and whatever is underground

**Everything Belongs to Him**
*Lahū mā fī al-samāwāti wa-mā fī al-arḍ* — to Him belongs all that is in the heavens and all that is on the earth. The grammarians point out that *lahū*, "to Him", is placed first for a reason: it puts ownership before the list, so the list reads as belonging and not merely as existing. God owns; we hold. What a person calls his property is a loan handed to him for a while, with the return date already set.
The verse then sweeps the whole of space in four phrases: what is in the heavens, what is on the earth, what lies between the two, and what is beneath the moist soil — *mā taḥta al-tharā*. Nothing is left outside the sentence, not the visible world and not what grows under the ground. Human pride over possessions rests on forgetting that arrangement.
**Why the Verse Ends Under the Soil**
The last word, *tharā*, points back to what God said of the human being earlier in the sūrah: from the earth He created you, and to it He will return you, and from it He will bring you out again (20:55). So the verse ends where a person will end. The food on the table comes out of that soil; the minerals in the body came out of it; and the grave is dug into it. Whoever holds that in mind cannot strut over what he holds, because he does not even hold himself. The wealth that survives the ground is the nearness of the One who owns it.

## Verse 20:7

> Whether you speak openly ˹or not˺, He certainly knows what is secret and what is even more hidden

**He Knows the Secret and the Hidden**

The clause *wa-in tajhar bi-l-qawli fa-innahū yaʿlamu s-sirra wa-akhfā* establishes the absolute, unrestricted nature of divine knowledge across all registers of human expression. Whether a person proclaims speech openly (*tajhar bi-l-qawli*) in public gatherings or keeps it completely concealed within the private sanctuary of the mind, divine omniscience encompasses it effortlessly. Classical commentators such as al-Ṭabarī note that human communication relies on physical sound waves and social transmission, limiting human perception to audible words. Divine knowledge, by contrast, perceives the unspoken thoughts, subtle inclinations, and hidden motives of every heart simultaneously. The contrast between open speech and subterranean secret thoughts highlights that privacy is an illusion before the Creator, calling human beings to internal sincerity, moral integrity, and pure intentions.

**Psychological and Moral Implications for the Believer**

Realizing that God knows both open speech and what is even more hidden transforms a believer's moral consciousness, cultivating the virtue of spiritual excellence (*iḥsān*). As defined in authentic prophetic traditions, *iḥsān* is to worship God as if you see Him, for even if you do not see Him, He certainly sees you (*Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* 50). When an individual recognizes that divine knowledge monitors every unspoken thought and fleeting impulse, hypocritical pretense becomes impossible. Outer religious observance must align with inner purity of intention. This awareness inspires continuous self-reckoning (*muḥāsabah*), encouraging believers to cleanse their hearts of envy, pride, malice, and insincerity, aligning internal reality with outward profession in every aspect of life. Cultivating *iḥsān* protects the soul from secret sins that corrode spiritual vitality. True devotion blossoms when private life mirrors public worship, producing genuine peace, integrity, and spiritual resilience. The believer walks through the world with an abiding sense of sacred accountability before the Omnipresent Lord.

**The Absolute Transparency of All Existence to the Creator**

The verse concludes a four-verse religious sequence (20:4-7) establishing divine majesty, cosmic creation, supreme throne-establishment, total ownership, and complete omniscience. This sequence presents a comprehensive portrait of God that demands absolute monotheism (*tawḥīd*). The God who created the heavens and earth, governs the Throne with mercy, owns every particle from celestial heights to subterranean depths, and knows the unspoken secrets of every heart is uniquely worthy of worship. Human idols, worldly rulers, and material causes have none of these attributes.

## Verse 20:8

> Allah—there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. He has the Most Beautiful Names

**The Names That Belong to Him**
The verse closes the sūrah's opening — the letters *Ṭā Hā* (20:1), the praise, and the declaration of mercy — with a sentence the Muslim reader hears many times a day: *lahu al-asmāʾ al-ḥusnā* — to Him belong the most beautiful names. The word for beautiful here is *ḥusnā*, the superlative, and the point of it is not decoration. Each name describes something real about God, and each one is perfect in what it names: the Most Compassionate is not compassionate sometimes, the All-Knowing does not learn, the Holy is holy without a flaw anywhere in the quality. Al-Ghazālī devoted a book to the subject, *al-Maqṣad al-Asnā*, and Ibn al-Qayyim returned to it again and again; both treat the names not as a list of titles but as the vocabulary of knowing God.
**The Names as Doors**
A reader who takes the names one by one finds that they open onto different things. *Al-Raḥmān* speaks to need and mercy, *al-ʿAlīm* to a knowledge that misses nothing, *al-Qaddūs* to a purity no creature shares. The Book asks people to consider them and to call on Him by them. What the names produce in a heart that works through them is the point of the verse: love for the source of every good, and reverence for the One whose majesty does not bend. The Prophet spoke of the number ninety-nine, saying that God has ninety-nine names and that whoever *enumerates* them enters the Garden, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6410. The scholars explained that enumerating (*iḥṣāʾ*) means more than running through a list: it is learning them, understanding what they mean, living by what they require, and calling on God through them — which is why the tradition treats them as a discipline of the character and not a devotional decoration.
**The Place in the Sūrah**
Set at the head of the sūrah, the names do a job in the ordering of the story that follows. The same God whose mercy and knowledge and majesty have just been named will be the one who speaks to Moses at the fire in the next words of the sūrah (20:9), sends him to the tyrant, and follows the children of Israel through the sea. The reader learns the names first so that the story that comes next reads as their working out in history, and so that the answer to Pharaoh, in chapter after chapter of the sūrah, is a name and not merely a wonder.

## Verse 20:9

> Has the story of Moses reached you ˹O Prophet˺

**What the Question Is Asking**
*Wa-hal atāka ḥadīthu Mūsā* — and has the news of Moses come to you? The words are a question put to the Prophet, and the answer arrives in the verses that follow rather than in the sentence itself. The choice of the verb *atāka* is worth noticing: the story is described as something that comes to a person, the way news travels the road into a town. The Book uses the same opening for other accounts, and it uses it here for the longest narrative in the sūrah, which will run from the burning bush to the sea and end with a lesson drawn out loud (20:99).
The chapter has just named itself and its purpose in its opening address (20:1-8). 
The chapter was given in Makkah, at a point when the Prophet's people had heard every argument and were waiting for him to give up. The story it opens with a question is about a messenger who had been through far worse. Moses was born into a death sentence, raised in the household of the man hunting him, made a fugitive for a killing he had not intended, and sent back to the same court years later with nothing but a staff. The sūrah tells the Prophet to look at the road before him through the story of the one who walked it first. This is the Book's way of comforting its messenger, and it explains why the account is given in such detail.
**Favours Given Before the Call**

The sūrah will later remind Moses of what was done for him before any of this: he was cast into the river, taken into the palace, and raised under the eye of the One who had arranged it (20:37-39). The man hearing this story did not know, during those years, that he was being prepared, and the question in 20:9 is holding that fact for the Prophet as well. The years that look wasted are often the years being used.

**Where It Goes**
The verses immediately after it take Moses to the fire he saw from a distance and told his family to wait for (20:10). The rest of the chapter holds the whole story: the voice at the valley of Ṭuwā, the signs of the staff and the hand, the request for Aaron, the confrontation with Pharaoh's magicians, the crossing of the sea, and the calf that the people made while Moses was on the mountain. The sūrah then closes the account by telling the Prophet that the outcome is not in doubt (20:99). The question in this verse is the door of that whole passage, and the passage is the sūrah's main answer to its own audience.

## Verse 20:10

> When he saw a fire, he said to his family, “Wait here, ˹for˺ I have spotted a fire. Perhaps I can bring you a torch from it, or find some guidance at the fire.”

**The Desert Journey and Physical Vulnerability**

The narrative opens in the dark, cold wilderness of Midian and Sinai, where Mūsā traveled with his family at night, losing his way amidst bitter cold and obscurity. Seeing a distant flame, Mūsā exclaimed to his family: *ānastu nāran* ('I have spotted a fire'). Classical lexicographers note that the verb *ānasa* implies perceiving something with relief, warmth, and hope after a period of isolation or fear. Mūsā was seeking physical warmth (*qabas*) or a traveler's guide (*hudā*) to cross the dangerous desert night. This initial setting highlights human vulnerability: Mūsā approached the fire as a cold, disoriented traveler seeking practical earthly aid, completely unaware that he was stepping toward the supreme spiritual encounter of his life. Human beings often encounter profound spiritual awakenings while seeking solutions to ordinary physical difficulties. Divine wisdom orchestrates mundane circumstances to draw human souls toward transcendent purpose, proving that divine guidance reaches people in their moments of greatest earthly need. Mūsā's physical vulnerability became the threshold of eternal prophecy, demonstrating that divine light breaks through human darkness when least expected.

**Spiritual Epiphany Disguised as Human Need**

Mūsā's encounter at the fire illustrates how divine providence guides human destiny through seemingly casual occurrences. A lost traveler seeking warmth in a dark desert becomes the recipient of divine law and the liberator of an enslaved people. What appeared to be a minor detour caused by cold weather was in reality the destined convergence of time and place (*ʿalā qadarin yā mūsā*, 20:40). Believers learn from this narrative that no circumstance in life is meaningless. Earthly hardships, unexpected detours, and physical needs are frequently the veiled instruments through which God leads His servants toward their true spiritual calling. Divine decree shapes human life through quiet, ordinary moments. Trusting in divine providence transforms our understanding of worldly trials, enabling us to recognize hidden divine blessings behind unexpected detours.

## Verse 20:11

> But when he approached it, he was called, “O Moses

**The Divine Call in the Wilderness**

When Prophet Mūsā reached the location of the light, he encountered a sight that defied physical law: a luminous fire glowing brightly within a green desert bush without consuming its branches. As he stood mesmerized by this miraculous spectacle, an authoritative voice resonated through the valley, calling him directly by his personal name: *nūdiya yā mūsā* ('he was called: O Moses!'). The commentators such as al-Ṭabarī and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī note that hearing his own name spoken in an uninhabited wilderness filled Mūsā with profound awe and reverent fear. The divine call stripped away his anonymity, singling him out from all humanity to receive direct revelation and divine commission. Hearing the divine call in solitude creates an indelible spiritual awakening. The personal address *yā mūsā* reassured the trembling traveler that his Lord was intimately aware of his presence, his name, and his heart.

**Direct Address and Prophetic Intimacy**

The personal vocative *yā mūsā* represents an extraordinary level of divine favor and spiritual intimacy (*munājāt*). Among all the messengers, Mūsā was granted the unique title *Kalīm Allāh* (The One with Whom God Spoke directly), as affirmed in Sūrah al-Nisā': 'And to Moses Allah spoke directly' (4:164). Being addressed by name established an immediate, personal relationship between the Creator and His chosen servant before any command or law was issued. This direct address reassured Mūsā in his state of disorientation, affirming that the Supreme Sovereign knew him individually, oversaw his past trials, and was calling him toward a glorious prophetic destiny. Direct divine communication validates the infinite dignity of the human soul.

## Verse 20:12

> It is truly I. I am your Lord! So take off your sandals, for you are in the sacred valley of Ṭuwa

**The Absolute Declaration of Divine Lordship**

The verse opens with the solemn declaration *innī anā rabbuk* ('I am indeed your Lord'), establishing divine identity with paramount authority and intimate proximity. The emphatic particle *inna* paired with the independent pronoun *anā* leaves no ambiguity about the identity of the Speaker. God introduces Himself to Mūsā not through abstract statement about realitys, but through the relational title *Rabb*—the Sustainer, Nurturer, and Sovereign Lord who raised Mūsā through infancy, protected him in Pharaoh's palace, guided his exile in Midian, and now called him to prophecy. This divine introduction reassured Mūsā in his state of overwhelming awe, reminding him that the One addressing him was the very Guardian who had guided every step of his life. Hearing *innī anā rabbuk* filled Mūsā's trembling heart with absolute security and profound peace. Divine lordship is introduced as a protective relationship that encompasses all human needs. The personal address dissolved Mūsā's apprehension, replacing worldly fear with unshakeable spiritual grounding. Recognizing divine lordship establishes the foundation for eternal trust and unwavering obedience in the face of all worldly trials.

**Historical Continuity of Sacred Precincts**

The designation of Ṭuwā as a sacred valley (*al-wādī al-muqaddas*) connects Mūsā's experience to a broader Qur'anic theology of sacred geography. Throughout sacred history, specific locations—such as the Kaʿbah in Mecca, Mount Sinai, and Jerusalem (*al-Masjid al-Aqṣā*)—were designated by divine decree as sanctuaries of spiritual illumination and worship. Sūrah al-Nāziʿāt recalls this exact moment: 'When his Lord called him in the sacred valley of Ṭuwā' (79:16). Sacred spaces serve as enduring reminders of divine encounters, inspiring believers across generations to approach divine worship with reverent awe and purified intention. Sacred geography links human history to transcendent divine revelation.

## Verse 20:13

> I have chosen you, so listen to what is revealed

**The Sublime Reality of Divine Election**

The statement *wa-anā khtartuk* ('I have chosen you') designates Prophet Mūsā's official divine appointment to prophethood (*al-iṣṭifā'*). The verb *ikhtāra* means selecting an individual out of pure divine grace, wisdom, and foreknowledge, independent of human merit, social standing, or worldly power. ## Verse 20:32

> and let him share my task

**A Share in the Task**
*Wa-ashrikhu fī amrī* — and make him a partner in my affair. The verb comes from *shirkah*, partnership, the sharing of a thing between two. Moses had already asked for a helper, then for strength through him, and now asks that his brother be a sharer in the mission itself. Not an assistant who carries the bags; a partner in what Moses was sent to do, with the same work and the same exposure in front of the same king.
**What the Request Says About the Man Making It**
The prayer is often read as a lesson in delegation, and it is more than that. A leader who has been chosen, spoken to, and given the signs is asking God to put a second man at his side, and the man he names is the one with the better tongue (28:34) - the same brother Moses had asked to be his support before he left for the mountain (7:142). That is the humility in the verse: Moses measured himself accurately and asked for what he lacked. The rest of the prayer says why he wanted a partner at all — that we may glorify You much and remember You much (20:33-34) — so the object of the request was worship rather than authority. Nobody is asked to bless a division of power here; the thing being multiplied is the praise.
**The Prayer Answered in Full**
The next two verses give the answer in the same register: *you have been granted your request, O Moses* (20:36). The whole list is granted — the expanding of the chest, the easing of the tongue, the loosening of the knot, the partner, the strength through him, the glorifying and the remembering — and the sūrah records the answer without trimming anything the servant had asked for. The scene is the sūrah's portrait of what asking God for help looks like: specific, modest about its own strength, and aimed at the worship that comes out of the mission rather than at the mission's rewards.

## Verse 20:40

> ˹Remember˺ when your sister came along and proposed, ‘Shall I direct you to someone who will nurse him?’ So We reunited you with your mother so that her heart would be put at ease, and she would not grieve. ˹Later˺ you killed a man ˹by mistake˺, but We saved you from sorrow, as well as other tests We put you through. Then you stayed for a number of years among the people of Midian. Then you came here as pre-destined, O Moses

**The Sister's Intervention and Maternal Reunion**
God recounted the strategic role of Mūsā's sister (Miriam), who followed the chest along the Nile and approached Pharaoh's palace court: *idh tamshī ukhtuka fa-taqūlu hal adullukum ʿalā man yakfuluhu* ('Remember when your sister came along and proposed: Shall I direct you to someone who will nurse him?'). Through her clever diplomatic proposal, baby Mūsā rejected all Egyptian wet nurses, prompting the royal court to accept her recommendation and hire Mūsā's own biological mother as his paid royal nurse. Strategic human initiative guided by divine wisdom achieves miraculous outcomes. Mūsā's sister acted with remarkable courage, intelligence, and poise in the royal presence. Reuniting infant Mūsā with his biological mother fulfilled divine promise (*wa-da-nāhū ilā ummih*). God restored the child to his mother's arms, ensuring he was raised with true monotheistic faith.
The account in Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ fills in what this verse states briefly: the mother put the child in the river, the sister followed him, and the family refused the wet-nurses of the palace until the sister named her own mother (28:7–13). So the verse is the third telling of the same event in the Book, and here the point is the promise stated at the child's birth — that God would return him to his mother, make him a messenger, and make him one of the messengers (28:7). The sūrah mentions the reunion in passing because the reader is expected to recognise the story in the flow of Moses' life being summarised.

**The Sister and the River**
The verse collects several moments from Moses's early life into one sentence. His sister walked along and offered to show the palace a woman who could nurse him, which returned him to his mother, and the Book names the purpose: so that her eye might be comforted and she might not grieve. Then the verse moves to the killing of the Egyptian and the years of hiding, and it describes the whole stretch as the care of God: We saved you from sorrow, and We tried you with trials. The Sūrah of the Story tells the same events at length, with the mother's fear and the sister's errand (28:7-13).
**Why the Sūrah Gathers It Here**
The verses are part of the reminder given to Moses at the burning bush, where God lists what was done for him before he was anyone. The list includes the river, the palace, the return to his mother, the flight, and the years in Midian (20:37-40), and it ends with the sentence that explains all of it: I have made you for Myself (20:41). The man being sent back to face the king is told that the years he thought were lost were the years of preparation. The sūrah then gives the Prophet the same account, in the same chapter, as the model for his own long road.

**The Years in Midian and the Road Back**
The verse passes over more than a decade in one clause: then you stayed for a number of years among the people of Midian. The sūrah of the Story tells what those years contained — the flight from Egypt after the killing of the Egyptian, the arrival at the well of Midian, the watering of the flocks for the two women, and the old man's offer of marriage with a wage of eight years' service, which he agreed to complete to ten (28:22-28). The Book presents the whole stretch as the making of the man who would be sent back: the shepherd's years spent with his father-in-law's flocks were the training for a man who would lead a people out of a king's country. The verse's ending reads the same way: then you came here as predestined, O Moses. The appointment at the burning bush was the end of a road whose stages he had not been shown, and the verses before it name the providences of the earlier road — the casting into the river, the love cast on him, the rearing under God's eye (20:37-40). The sūrah hands the account to the Prophet as the model for his own long preparation, and it ends the list of favours with the sentence that explains them all: I have made you for Myself (20:41).

**The Killing, the Flight, and the Rescue From Sorrow**
The verse also mentions the killing in a way that repays attention: you killed a man, but We saved you from sorrow. The sūrah of the Story gives the scene in full — Moses struck the Egyptian, killed him by accident, and the next day found himself in a quarrel where the man he had helped the day before called out his secret, so that he fled the city in fear, praying for forgiveness and asking his Lord to save him from the people who wronged (28:15-21). The Book calls the act a mistake and answers the prayer, and it calls the rescue a deliverance from sorrow, which covers both the fear of arrest in Egypt and the years of exile. The reader who has just been told that the man was made for God is shown that the making began with the worst day of his life. The Book's use of the account is stated in the sūrah's own words about revelation: it tells these stories so that the heart may be steadied (11:120).

Islamic belief distinguishes between general divine presence (*maʿiyyah ʿāmmah*) and special divine presence (*maʿiyyah khāṣṣah*). Mūsā and Aaron received special *maʿiyyah*. Special divine presence (*maʿiyyah khāṣṣah*) guarantees active divine protection and victory. Armed imperial guards inside Pharaoh's palace were powerless against messengers backed by divine presence. Experiencing special divine presence fills the soul with transcendent peace (*sakīnah*). External danger loses its terrifying power when the heart rests in divine proximity. Understanding divine presence elevates human consciousness, encouraging believers to seek divine companionship above all worldly alliances and political favors.

## Verse 20:47

> So go to him and say, ‘Indeed we are both messengers from your Lord, so let the Children of Israel go with us, and do not oppress them. We have come to you with a sign from your Lord. And salvation will be for whoever follows the ˹right˺ guidance

Mūsā and Aaron entered Pharaoh's palace and expressed their opening diplomatic decree: *fa'tiyāhu fa-qūlā innā rasūlā rabbika* ('So go to him and say: Indeed we are both messengers from your Lord'). By identifying themselves as 'messengers from your Lord,' they immediately challenged Pharaoh's self-proclaimed divinity. They informed Pharaoh that he was not the supreme lord of Egypt, but a created subject subordinate to the Creator who had dispatched two ambassadors to his court. Addressing a tyrant with 'your Lord' (*rabbik*) served as a gentle yet uncompromising reminder of Pharaoh's creaturely dependence upon God. Mūsā and Aaron spoke as a cohesive dual delegation, representing the supreme divine court before the Egyptian throne. The primary political and human rights demand was expressed clearly: *fa-arsil maʿanā banī isrā'īla wa-lā tuʿadhibhum* ('so let the Children of Israel go with us, and do not oppress them').

The Book records the same opening address in Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ, where the two brothers are told to say that they are messengers of the Lord of all worlds and are told to let the Children of Israel go with them (26:16–17). It also records the ground of the demand elsewhere: the Children of Israel were enslaved by Pharaoh's decree, their infant sons killed and their women kept alive (28:4; 2:49). So the request is not a favour asked of a king but the release of a people whose treatment had already been placed before him, and the sūrah states that the evidence of God's favour toward Moses is standing in front of him throughout his life.

**What the Verse Gives the Two Men to Say**
The verse reports the instruction to Moses and Aaron: go to Pharaoh and say that they are the messengers of his Lord, that he should send the children of Israel with them, and that he should not punish them, and then the statement that they have come with a sign from their Lord, and the sentence about peace upon whoever follows the guidance. The Book gives the men a script, and it is notable for what it does not contain. There is no demand for the throne, no case about the years of slavery, and no request for compensation. The demand is the release of a people and the end of their torment.
**The Two Words That Carry the Message**
The verse's sentence about peace on the one who follows the guidance is the Book's way of putting the choice in front of the king, and the sūrah repeats it in the passage where the magicians make their decision (20:47 and 20:69-70). The Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān gives the same sentence in the words of the believers with the People of the Book (3:20), and the Sūrah of the Bee gives it as the greeting the community is to use. The passage in front of us is the first contact between the revelation and an empire, and the Book records the message rather than the exchange of courtesies.
**Where the Account Goes**
The verses after this one record Pharaoh's answer, which is a question about their Lord, and then his threat and the contest with the magicians. The sūrah gives the whole account with the arguments and the outcomes, and the verse in front of us is the beginning of the confrontation. The Book tells the story to the Prophet in Makkah, where a man with no army was sending messages to a court, and the arrangement of the passage shows what the first approach looked like.

## Verse 20:48

> It has indeed been revealed to us that the punishment will be upon whoever denies ˹the truth˺ and turns away.’”

**The Explicit Warning of Divine Punishment for Denial**

Mūsā and Aaron delivered the second part of their divine mandate: *innā qad ūḥiya ilaynā anna al-ʿadhāba ʿalā man kadhhaba wa-tawallā* ('It has indeed been revealed to us that the punishment will be upon whoever denies the truth and turns away'). Having offered peace to those who follow guidance in verse 47, they now expressed the inescapable divine consequence for persistent denial and arrogance: severe divine punishment (*ʿadhāb*). Expressing clear consequences for moral rejection completes prophetic warning. Divine justice mandates that persistent denial of truth incurs inevitable spiritual and physical retribution. Grounding warnings in direct divine revelation (*ūḥiya ilaynā*) confirms that messengers speak with divine authorization rather than personal malice or political hostility. Balancing promises of peace (*salām*) with warnings of punishment (*ʿadhāb*) maintains religious integrity. True guidance presents both divine mercy and divine justice with absolute clarity. Delivering solemn warnings establishes full legal accountability.

## Verse 20:49

> Pharaoh asked, “Who then is the Lord of you two, O Moses?”

**Pharaoh's Arrogant Interrogative Challenge**

Pharaoh responded to Mūsā and Aaron's message with a direct, challenging inquiry: *fa-qāla fa-man rabbukumā yā Mūsā* ('Pharaoh asked: Who then is the Lord of you two, O Moses?'). In Egyptian royal theology, Pharaoh considered himself the living incarnation of the sun-god Ra and supreme master of Egypt. Confronted by two former subjects claiming allegiance to a higher 'Lord' (*rabb*), Pharaoh demanded to know the identity and nature of this unseen deity who dared challenge his supreme authority. Interrogating prophetic authority reflects the arrogant defensiveness of earthly tyrants. Pharaoh tried to dismiss their message by questioning the reality and identity of their God. Addressing Mūsā individually (*yā Mūsā*) ignored Aaron's presence, attempting to divide the prophetic team. Tyrants frequently try to isolate primary leaders during high-stakes public debates. The term *rabb* (Sustainer, Master, Lord) struck at the core of Pharaonic political legitimacy.

**The Shift from Political Demand to Theological Foundation**

Verse 49 marks a pivotal transition in the confrontation. Mūsā and Aaron began with a specific political demand: release the Children of Israel (20:47). Pharaoh deflected the political demand for human rights by shifting the dialogue to abstract theology: 'Who is your Lord?' Mūsā seized this shift, recognizing that establishing true divine theology (*tawḥīd*) is the ultimate foundation for human liberation. Anchoring human rights and political liberation in true theology (*tawḥīd*) provides an unshakeable moral foundation. Human freedom flows directly from divine lordship. Deflecting political demands through ideological questioning is a common tactic of tyrannical powers.

## Verse 20:50

> He answered, “Our Lord is the One Who has given everything its ˹distinctive˺ form, then guided ˹it˺.”

**The Masterpiece Definition of Divine Lordship**

Mūsā delivered a breathtaking, comprehensive definition of God: *qāla rabbunā al-ladhī aʿṭā kulla shay'in khalqahu thumma hadā* ('He answered: Our Lord is the One Who has given everything its distinctive form, then guided it'). In a single sentence of supreme eloquence, Mūsā defined divine lordship (*rubūbiyyah*) through two cosmic dimensions: *khalq* (creating and fashioning every entity with its unique physical form and capacity) and *hidāyah* (guiding every creature to its natural function, survival, and ultimate purpose). Defining divine lordship through creation (*khalq*) and guidance (*hidāyah*) represents a religious masterpiece. God is both the Master Architect and the Active Sustainer of the cosmos. Bestowing distinctive form (*aʿṭā kulla shay'in khalqahu*) highlights divine precision, beauty, and wisdom. Every atom, plant, animal, and human is crafted with specialized features suited to its existence. Universal guidance (*thumma hadā*) proves that God does not abandon creation after fashioning it.

## Verse 20:51

> Pharaoh asked, “And what about previous peoples?”

**Pharaoh Changes Ground**
Moses had just given Pharaoh a definition of God: the One who gave everything its nature and then guided it (20:50). Pharaoh had no answer to that. So he asked a different question: *fa-mā bālu al-qurūni al-ūlā* — and what about the earlier generations? It sounds like a sincere question about the fate of the dead. It was a trap. If Moses said those earlier generations were lost, he would be condemning the ancestors of every Egyptian standing in the hall, and the court would turn on him as an enemy of the nation's past. The move is old and still common: when the case in front of you cannot be answered, raise the ancestors.
**The Answer That Refuses the Trap**
Moses does not take the bait, and he does not dodge. The next verse gives his reply: the knowledge of them is with my Lord, in a record; my Lord neither makes mistakes nor forgets (20:52). He declines to pronounce on individuals he was not sent to judge, and he leaves the whole matter where it belongs — with the One who keeps the record and loses nothing. What he delivers is a principle, not an opinion, and the trap closes on nothing.
**Why It Matters**
The pattern repeats in every generation. The Qur'an records the argument in the mouths of the Meccans: we found our fathers on a path and we follow their footsteps (43:22). Ancestral tradition is the oldest defence of a comfortable position — not because it is a good argument, but because it turns a question of truth into a question of loyalty to family. The scene shows the way through: leave the past to God's record, and answer for what you are doing now, with the guidance that has reached you.

## Verse 20:52

> He replied, “That knowledge is with my Lord in a Record. My Lord neither falters nor forgets ˹anything˺.”

**The Masterful Prophetic Response on Divine Record**

Mūsā delivered an infinitely wise, divinely guided response that completely dismantled Pharaoh's trap: *qāla ʿilmuhā ʿinda rabbī fī kitābin lā yaḍillu rabbī wa-lā yansā* ('He replied: That knowledge is with my Lord in a Record. My Lord neither falters nor forgets'). Mūsā placed the ultimate judgment of past generations completely in God's hands, affirming that their full historical record is preserved in a celestial Book (*Kitāb*) governed by flawless divine omniscience and perfect memory. Entrusting the fate of past generations to divine judgment is the epitome of religious wisdom. Mūsā disarmed Pharaoh's trap by refusing to issue unauthorized human decrees. Affirming divine record (*fī kitāb*) establishes absolute historical accountability. Every human action, past and present, is closely preserved in divine records. Refusing to speculate on the eternal destiny of deceased individuals models profound spiritual humility. Final judgment belongs exclusively to the All-Knowing God.

## Verse 20:53

> ˹He is the One˺ Who has laid out the earth for ˹all of˺ you, and set in it pathways for you, and sends down rain from the sky, causing various types of plants to grow

**The Cradle and the Roads**
*Alladhī jaʿala lakumu al-arḍa mahdan wa-salaka lakum fīhā subulan* — the One who made the earth a cradle for you and threaded roads through it for you. The word *mahd* is the bed a mother prepares for a child, and the verse uses it for the whole planet, which is a picture of provision rather than a description of the surface. Then comes the second gift: roads. The Book treats the paths through mountains and valleys as signs in their own right, saying in the Sūrah of the Bee that God placed mountains so the earth would not shake and made rivers and roads so that people could find their way (16:15). A person who has walked a road to another town has used a sign without noticing it.
**The Water and the Pairs of Plants**
The verse continues: and He sent down water from the sky, and with it We brought out pairs of plants of every kind. The word *azwāj* means pairs, and the Book uses it for the whole of creation, saying in the Sūrah of Yā Sīn that everything is made in pairs (36:36). The plants come out in kinds and in twos, and the verse offers them as evidence of a Maker whose provision is arranged rather than random. The rain and the plants have been the Book's standard proof for the raising of the dead since the earliest sūrahs, and the verse places them in the middle of a speech Moses is making to Pharaoh.
**Who Is Listening**
The verses that follow state the conclusion, telling the people to eat and graze their animals and pointing out that in this there are signs for people of understanding (20:54). Moses is speaking to a king who called himself a god, and the argument he offers is the ground under that king's feet. The sūrah then uses the same passage again at the end of the account, where the Prophet is told that the story has been given so that hearts may be steadied (20:113). The cradle, the roads, the rain and the plants are all placed in the mouth of a man standing in a palace, and the point is the same one the sūrah is making to Makkah.

## Verse 20:54

> ˹so˺ eat and graze your cattle. Surely in this are signs for people of sound judgment

**Eat, and Graze Your Cattle**

Mūsā is told, "eat and graze your cattle" — *kulū wa-irʿaw anʿāmakum*. The two words cover the human and the animal side of God's provision: food for people, pasture for the herds that carry and feed them. The verse then says that in this there is a sign for people of sound judgment.

The commentators take the sign to be the ordinary evidence of a world arranged for life: rain, growth, green fields where the soil had been bare, and a creation that produces what living creatures need. The command to eat is permission, spoken in the middle of an account of God's care — the earth spread out, roads laid in it, water sent down from the sky. What the verse asks the reader to notice is not an unusual event but the ordinary one, which is why it addresses people of sound judgment: those who look at a harvest and see provision rather than mere nature.

## Verse 20:55

> From the earth We created you, and into it We will return you, and from it We will bring you back again

**The Threefold Biological and Last-Day Journey of Humanity**

Mūsā expressed a profound, humbling summary of human physical existence: *minhā khalaqnākum wa-fīhā nuʿīdukum wa-minhā nukhrijukum tāratan ukhrā* ('From the earth We created you, and into it We will return you, and from it We will bring you back again'). This single verse encompasses the entire human journey across three distinct stages: original physical creation from earth (*khalaqnākum*), physical burial into earth upon death (*nuʿīdukum*), and bodily resurrection out of earth on Judgment Day (*nukhrijukum*). The three-stage cycle of creation, death, and resurrection defines human physical destiny. Physical matter from the earth forms the temporary vessel of the human soul. Originating from soil (*minhā khalaqnākum*) establishes ultimate biological equality among all human beings. Kings and slaves share identical earthly origin. Returning to earth in death (*wa-fīhā nuʿīdukum*) reminds humanity of inescapable physical mortality. Earthly palaces and imperial crowns dissolve into dust.

The Book repeats the same three-part cycle in Sūrah Nūḥ, where God is said to have created people from the earth, to return them into it. To bring them out again (71:17–18), and it states the principle in the argument for resurrection: the God who brings the dead land to life is the God who will bring out what is in the graves (22:5–7; 30:19). Moses says this to Pharaoh as part of the argument he was commanded to make, so the verse is not a meditation but a premise in a debate: the king who called himself a god is being reminded of what he is made of and where he is going.

## Verse 20:56

> And We certainly showed Pharaoh all of Our signs, but he denied them and refused ˹to believe˺

**All the Signs, and the Answer He Gave**
*Wa-laqad araynāhu āyātinā kullahā fa-kadhhaba wa-abā* — and We showed him all of Our signs, and he called them lies and refused. The verse is a summary placed after the account of the contest, and it names two acts rather than one. *Kadhdhaba* is the denial in the mind: the signs were called magic, the magicians were called traitors, the whole business was filed under trickery. *Abā* is the refusal in the will: even with the account settled, the man would not bend. The Book gives the same pair for the devil at the command to prostrate, where he refused and was arrogant (2:34), and the pairing is the point — a person can lose an argument and still be free to submit, and it is the second step that is refused here.
**What "All of Our Signs" Means**
The reference is to the nine, the same set the sūrah of the Night Journey names (17:101) and the sūrah of the Heights spells out: the staff, the hand, and the years of drought followed by the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs and the blood (7:130-133). A man watched the water of his own river turn, watched the harvest fail, watched his magicians lose in front of his court, and the verse says he saw the lot. That is the measure the Book is giving the reader: the evidence was complete, and what failed was not the evidence.
**Why the Verse Is Placed Here**
The scene has just finished the confrontation — the magicians prostrating, Pharaoh threatening them, Moses leaving with his people — and this verse steps back to state what the whole contest was. The sūrah is preparing the ground for the crossing of the sea, which is the last sign and the one the king did not survive. The sentence that says he saw everything and refused makes the drowning read as something other than a tragedy. It is the end of a case that had been argued to the limit, in public, in his own court, with his own magicians as the first converts.

## Verse 20:57

> He said, “Have you come to drive us out of our land with your magic, O Moses

**The Accusation**
Pharaoh had just been shown the signs, and he had nothing to say about them. So the verse gives his reply: *have you come to drive us out of our land with your magic, O Moses?* Two charges are packed into one sentence. The first is the familiar label — what Moses brought is *siḥr*, magic, a trick performed on an audience. The second is political: this man and his message are a foreign plot to take the country and push its people out. The first charge denies that anything supernatural happened; the second turns a call to worship God into a threat against the nation.
**Why the Two Go Together**
The two accusations work as a pair, and the pairing is worth seeing clearly. If a man can dismiss the sign as a trick, he can call the man who performed it a deceiver; if he can call the man a foreign agent, he does not have to answer what the man said. Moses' demand in Egypt was religious — let my people go and worship their Lord — and the verse never lets the reader forget that Pharaoh heard it and called it a coup. The next verse shows what he did with the accusation: he sent for every skilled magician in the land to match the trick with a better one (20:58-60). A man who believed his own charge would not have needed the contest.
**The Pattern the Sūrah Repeats**
The move is not a one-off. In Makkah the same men who refused to answer the Qur'an called it magic and its bearer a sorcerer and a poet, and the sūrahs keep recording the labels because the labels are all the opposition ever produced. The scene with Pharaoh is the pattern shown at full size, in a country with an army, a treasury and a priesthood of its own, and the outcome of the contest is written into the same narrative.

## Verse 20:58

> We can surely meet you with similar magic. So set for us an appointment that neither of us will fail to keep, in a central place.”

**Pharaoh's Proposal for a Public Magical Duel**

Confident in Egypt's imperial sorcerers, Pharaoh issued a formal challenge: *fa-la-na'tiyannaka bi-siḥrin mithlihī fa-ijʿal baynanā wa-baynaka mawʿidan lā nukhlifuhū naḥnu wa-lā anta makānan suwan* ('We can surely meet you with similar magic. So set for us an appointment that neither of us will fail to keep, in a central place'). Pharaoh proposed a national public duel between Mūsā and Egypt's master magicians, demanding an unbreakable appointment (*mawʿid*) at a central, accessible location (*makānan suwan*). Proposing a public duel reflected Pharaoh's supreme confidence in Egyptian sorcery and state power. Pharaoh sought to crush Mūsā's movement through public humiliation. Demanding an unbreakable appointment (*mawʿid*) bound both parties to public accountability. Pharaoh intended to showcase state supremacy before the entire Egyptian empire. Selecting a central, accessible location (*makānan suwan*) ensured maximum public attendance. Pharaoh wanted all social classes to witness Mūsā's defeat. Imperial overconfidence frequently creates the venue for political downfall. Pharaoh asked for a public appointment at a central place, and the verse after this one records Moses accepting it and fixing the day of the festival, promising that the people would be gathered in the morning (20:59 gives both the day and the gathering). The sūrah then reports the contest itself, and it records the end of it: the men who had come to beat Moses were the ones who submitted (20:70 reports their submission, and 20:72 through 20:73 give their answer to Pharaoh's threat).

## Verse 20:59

> Moses said, “Your appointment is on the Day of the Festival, and let the people be gathered mid-morning.”

**Mūsā's Confident Selection of the Day of the Festival**

Mūsā accepted Pharaoh's challenge instantly, selecting the precise timing for the public duel: *qāla mawʿidukum yawmu al-zīnati wa-a yuḥshara al-nāsu ḍuḥā* ('Moses said: Your appointment is on the Day of the Festival, and let the people be gathered mid-morning'). Mūsā specified two strategic conditions: the 'Day of the Festival' (*Yawm al-Zīnah*), Egypt's primary national holiday, and 'mid-morning' (*ḍuḥā*), when sunlight was brightest and crowds were fully rested. Selecting the national holiday (*Yawm al-Zīnah*) maximized public attendance. Mūsā wanted the entire population—royable elites, courtiers, and common citizens—to witness divine truth. Specifying mid-morning (*ḍuḥā*) ensured maximum visibility and clear natural light. Sunlight eliminated any possibility of Pharaoh claiming Mūsā used dark, shadowy optical tricks. Accepting the duel without hesitation showed Mūsā's absolute confidence in divine backing. True faith steps fearlessly onto the grandest public stage. Commandeering a pagan national holiday for a monotheistic demonstration turned state celebrations into a divine sanctuary of truth. Strategic selection of time and venue optimizes public advocacy, ensuring that divine signs are delivered under conditions of absolute clarity and maximum impact.

## Verse 20:60

> Pharaoh then withdrew, orchestrated his scheme, then returned

**Pharaoh Withdraws to Plan**
The previous verse had Moses answer that the meeting would be on the day of the festival, at mid-morning, and that the people should gather. Then: *Pharaoh turned away, gathered his scheme, and came*. *Tawallā* is a turning back, and the commentators read it as a withdrawal from the conversation into the machinery he had: the men around him, the messengers he could send, and the treasury he could open. He had no answer for the discussion, so he left it and went to work.
**The Summoning of the Magicians**
*Jamaʿa kaydahū* — he collected his plan — is told in full in another sūrah. The sūrah of the Heights reports that Pharaoh's officers were sent out to gather the skilled magicians of the land, and that the men were offered a price: a place near the king, favour and rank, if they won (7:112, with 7:113-114). The commentators describe delegations going out to the cities where such men were known, and a promise of reward that would have moved a man in any trade. The regime knew exactly which professional class it needed, and it bought it.
**What the Scene Says About Power**
A king with an empire summons the best of a craft and promises them what kings can promise — and the Book uses the same word for the council that sat in Makkah to arrange the Prophet's imprisonment or death (8:30). Nothing in the passage presents the contest as a fair test of anything; the point of the scene is what the day revealed about the two sides. On one side, an army of trained men paid to produce an illusion in front of a crowd; on the other, two men with a staff. The sūrah lets the whole apparatus be assembled and displayed before the staff is thrown, because the story is about what happens to the apparatus, not about who had more of it.

## Verse 20:61

> Moses warned the magicians, “Woe to you! Do not fabricate a lie against Allah, or He will wipe you out with a torment. Whoever fabricates ˹lies˺ is bound to fail.”

Stepping into the festival arena before the assembled crowds and state sorcerers, Mūsā delivered a solemn moral warning: *qāla lahum Mūsā waylakum lā taftarū ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban fa-yusḥitakum bi-ʿadhābin wa-qad khāba mani iftarā* ('Moses warned the magicians: Woe to you! Do not fabricate a lie against Allah, or He will wipe you out with a torment. Whoever fabricates lies is bound to fail'). Mūsā addressed the magicians' conscience directly before a single staff was thrown, warning that misrepresenting divine reality through deceptive occult tricks constituted a grave sin against God. Mūsā offered the magicians a chance to repent before stepping into divine judgment.

**The Psychological Impact of Mūsā's Warning on the Magicians**

Mūsā's warning produced an immediate psychological shock wave among the assembled magicians. They expected an arrogant political opponent seeking royal power; instead, they encountered a serene prophet speaking with terrifying moral authority about God, judgment, and eternal failure. This moral warning introduced internal division into their ranks before the duel even began, as recorded in verse 62. Moral authority disarms hostile opponents before physical or intellectual confrontation begins. Mūsā's sincere warning shaken the magicians' professional confidence. Introducing moral doubt into oppressive systems weakens their operational cohesion. The magicians began questioning whether they were serving a tyrant against God. Authentic spiritual power speaks directly to the soul, bypassing external political spectacles. Mūsā's words resonated with the magicians' innate moral intuition. Preparing the ground for spiritual conversion requires courage and clarity. Mūsā's warning softened the magicians' hearts, enabling them to recognize divine truth later. Demonstrating spiritual authority in public discourse inspires searching minds, proving that divine truth commands respect even from institutional opponents.

The warning is delivered before the contest, and its content is stated plainly: whoever fabricates a lie against God will be destroyed, and whoever does so will fail. The same warning is made to the sorcerers in the sūrah of the Poets, where Moses tells them what they have been practising will not bring them success (26:44–46), and the fuller account in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf records the moment the magicians realised the difference between their craft and the proof shown them (7:119–122). The verse's place in the sūrah is the sūrah's own method: the argument is put to the conscience before the demonstration is made, so that the proof arrives to a decision already half-taken.

## Verse 20:62

> So the magicians disputed the matter among themselves, conversing privately

**The Magicians Divide**
Moses had warned them in the previous verse: woe to you, do not invent a lie against God or He will destroy you. The warning landed, and the verse records the effect on the men who had been brought to defeat him: *fa-tanāzaʿū amrahum baynahum wa-asarrū al-najwā* — they disputed the matter among themselves and kept their whispering quiet. A delegation that had arrived with one purpose and one confidence now had something to argue about, and the argument had to be conducted in whispers because the court was watching.
**What the Whispering Says**
The next verse reports what they were saying: this man and his brother are plainly two sorcerers, and they mean to drive you out of your land with their magic, and to take away your way of life (20:63) — so hold to your plan and come forward in one line (20:64). Their conclusion was professional. They put the quarrel aside and told each other to stand together, because two magicians beating one was a thing they could understand; what they could not afford was that the crowd should see them agreeing with what Moses had said. The scene is the first crack in Pharaoh's instrument, and it appears before a single staff is thrown. By the end of the contest, the men whispering here will be lying face down in prostration, declaring their faith in the God of Moses and Aaron.

## Verse 20:63

> They concluded, “These two are only magicians who want to drive you out of your land with their magic, and do away with your most cherished traditions

**Re-alignment with Pharaonic State Propaganda**

Overcoming their internal doubts, the dominant faction of state sorcerers re-aligned with Pharaonic propaganda, declaring publicly: *in hādhāni la-sāḥirāni yurīdāni a yukhrijākum min arḍikum bi-siḥrihimā wa-yadh-habā bi-ṭarīqatikumu al-muthlā* ('They concluded: These two are only magicians who want to drive you out of your land with their magic, and do away with your most cherished traditions'). To justify their upcoming duel, they adopted Pharaoh's exact rhetoric, labeling Mūsā and Aaron as rival magicians plotting political expulsion and cultural destruction. Regressing to state propaganda reflects the triumph of fear and professional ambition over short-lived moral doubt. The magicians echoed Pharaoh's exact accusations. Labeling prophets as mere magicians (*la-sāḥirān*) tried to strip Mūsā and Aaron of divine authority, reducing sacred revelation to competitive sorcery. AccUSING messengers of seeking territorial expulsion (*a yukhrijākum min arḍikum*) weaponized nationalistic panic to unite Egyptian citizens against Israel.

The Book tells the same confrontation in two other sūrahs. Both keep the charge of magic and the fear of expulsion: in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf the magicians warn that Moses will drive the people out of the land (7:109–110), and in Sūrah al-Shuʿarāʾ they call him a magician and say he wants to expel them by his magic (26:34–35). The repetition across three accounts shows how the objection worked: it turned a claim about God into a contest between two techniques and a dispute over territory. Pharaoh's court has already said the same thing in private, and here the magicians say it in public.

## Verse 20:64

> So orchestrate your plan, then come forward in ˹perfect˺ ranks. And whoever prevails today will certainly be successful.”

**The Advice the Magicians Gave Each Other**
The verse is part of a scene inside the enemy camp. The magicians had been brought from across Egypt, and before the contest they argued among themselves about what to do and kept their voices down (20:62). Then they settled on the plan in this verse: get your act in order, and come forward in a single line. Whoever wins today will be the one who succeeds. The words are a speech, and the sūrah preserves it, which is what makes the verse interesting. The men who delivered the strongest case against Moses on that day were the ones who had decided in advance what success meant.
**The Word They Used**
They chose *aflaḥa* — the word for real success, the same word the Book reserves for salvation. Sūrah al-Aʿlā announces that the one who purifies himself succeeds (87:14), and Sūrah al-Muʾminūn opens by describing the believers as the ones who have succeeded (23:1). The magicians used the word for a victory in a contest in front of a king, and they were certain of it. The sūrah then shows them losing the contest and winning something else entirely, when the same men fall to the ground in worship and ask their Lord's forgiveness (20:70; 20:73). A few verses later the sūrah states what is kept for the people who come to their Lord as believers (20:75), and the men who lost the contest are the first in the story to be promised it.
**The Line and the Ranks**
Their plan was to come as one body, and the sūrah of the Ranks uses the picture of a solid line for the believers who fight together (61:4). The irony is that the magicians got what they were aiming for in the wrong place. They came in ranks against the truth, and the Book records their names among the ones it honours, because they saw the sign at close range and accepted it at the moment it cost them their lives.

Offering the first initiative (*immā a tulqiya*) displayed professional courtly etiquette before the massive festival audience. Strategic arrogance often disguises itself as polite generosity. Directing the opening move to Mūsā gave Mūsā absolute control over the psychological tempo of the confrontation. Prophetic poise accepts public choices calmly.

Moses' answer in this sūrah is a decisive line: throw first (20:66), and the Book reports that the sight of their sorcery made him fear something within himself (20:67), until God told him to throw and promised that their work would come to nothing (20:69). The courtesy of the magicians turns out to be a test of nerve as much as of power, and it is answered by a prophet who is told what the outcome will be before he moves.

## Verse 20:67

> So Moses concealed fear within himself

**Moses Felt Fear — and Kept Still**
Moses answered the magicians by letting them go first, and when they threw, their ropes and staffs appeared to him to be moving, by their magic (20:66). Then comes this very human line: *fa-awjasa fī nafsihī khīfatan Mūsā* — Moses felt a fear inside himself. *Awjasa* is not the word for a shout or a stumble; it describes a movement that stays inside, a tightening of the chest that does not reach the face. He was afraid, and no one in the arena could tell.
**Nothing Was Wrong With the Fear**
The fear was not a hole in his prophethood; it was a correct reading of the moment. What he saw was an army of serpents, and what stood opposite him was a king with magicians, soldiers and a crowd. The verse records the feeling without a word of blame, and the next verse records the reply: *Do not fear — it is you who will prevail* (20:68). God steadies him rather than scolding him, and that is itself a lesson. Emotion is not disobedience. What a man does with it decides the matter.
**Why the Stillness Mattered**
The crowd was watching, and so was Israel. Had Moses flinched, the contest would have been over before the staff was thrown, and the people already frightened of Pharaoh would have lost heart. He kept his bearing and handed the outcome to God, and the next verses show what happened to the illusion: the staff swallowed it and the magicians dropped to the ground in prostration.

## Verse 20:69

> Cast what is in your right hand, and it will swallow up what they have made, for what they have made is no more than a magic trick. And magicians can never succeed wherever they go.”

**The Command to Cast the Staff and the Miracle of Devouring**

God issued the decisive operational command to Mūsā: *wa-alqi mā fī yamīnika talqaf mā ṣanaʿū innamā ṣanaʿū kaydu sāḥirin wa-lā yufliḥu al-sāḥiru ḥaythu atā* ('Cast what is in your right hand, and it will swallow up what they have made, for what they have made is no more than a magic trick. And magicians can never succeed wherever they go'). Mūsā threw his wooden staff (*mā fī yamīnik*), which instantly transformed by divine creative power into a massive, living serpent that devoured (*talqaf*) every single rope and staff thrown by the state sorcerers. Commanding Mūsā to cast 'what is in your right hand' (*mā fī yamīnik*) highlighted the humble physical instrument—a simple wooden shepherd's staff—used by God to dismantle Egypt's imperial sorcery. The miracle of devouring (*talqaf*) was physical and biological. Mūsā's serpent actually swallowed the physical wood and ropes of the magicians, leaving the arena completely clear.

---

The command and the reason are given together: the staff will swallow what they have made, and what they have made is only the trick of a magician, and the magician does not prosper. The same event is narrated in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf, where the staff swallows their falsehoods and snares (7:117–119), and in the sūrah of the Poets with the magicians' prostration following (26:45–48). The distinction the verse draws is between what is made and what is given: their craft is a production, and what Moses carries is a creation under God's command, which is why the swallowing is not a contest of skill but a demonstration of what the two things are.

## Verse 20:70

> So the magicians fell down in prostration, declaring, “We believe in the Lord of Aaron and Moses.”

**Instantaneous Prostration of the Master Magicians**

The moment Mūsā's living serpent devoured their ropes and staffs, the state sorcerers experienced an instantaneous spiritual revolution: *fa-ulqiya al-saḥaratu sujjadan qālū āmannā bi-rabbi Hārūna wa-Mūsā* ('So the magicians fell down in prostration, declaring: We believe in the Lord of Aaron and Moses'). The passive verb *ulqiya* ('they were cast down') means an overwhelming, involuntary physical response; the sheer indisputable reality of divine creative power struck their expert minds so profoundly that they collapsed directly onto their faces in prostration (*sujjad*). Instantaneous prostration (*ulqiya sujjadan*) represents the ultimate surrender of intellect and heart before clear divine proof. Expert sorcerers recognized that Mūsā's staff was authentic divine creation. The passive verb *ulqiya* highlights the irresistible weight of divine truth. Truth cast them onto their faces before they even consciously processed the physical movement. Expertise in illusion enabled the magicians to recognize genuine divine miracles instantly.

---

The magicians' prostration is the sūrah's turning point, and it comes before the threat, so that the prostration is a decision rather than a collapse. The fuller account in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf adds the exchange that follows: Pharaoh threatens crucifixion and they answer that they will not prefer him to what has come to them, and ask for patience (7:124–126). The sūrah of the Poets records their prayer in the same terms, that they ask to die as those who submit (26:51). Their conversion is the proof the sūrah has been building toward: the craftsmen who made the spectacle recognised the maker of the world at the moment their craft was exposed.

The Book records the threat and the acceptance in three sentences, and it lets the reader hear both sides.

The Book tells the same conversation in four sūrahs with the same details, which is its way of fixing a moment in the community's memory. The men who were promised a reward for their sorcery asked for the reward of patience instead.
**Where the Passage Is Going**
The verses that follow carry Moses out of the country with his people, and the sūrah describes the pursuit and the sea in the words the whole community knew. Pharaoh's threat in front of us is the last act of his authority before his army is drowned, and the Book puts the two scenes next to each other for that reason: the king who promised to make an example of his own men, and the water that closed over the cavalry behind them.

## Verse 20:74

> Whoever comes to their Lord as an evildoer will certainly have Hell, where they can neither live nor die

The verse describes the horrifying condition of souls in Hell: *lā yamūtu fīhā wa-lā yaḥyā* ('in which he neither dies nor lives'). This paradox depicts an agonizing state of perpetual existential suspension—denied the relief of total physical death (*lā yamūtu*) to end suffering, yet denied true, wholesome life (*lā yaḥyā*), experiencing continuous physical, psychological, and spiritual torment. The horrific paradox *lā yamūtu wa-lā yaḥyā* conveys the infinite severity of eternal divine punishment for unrepentant tyranny and rejection of truth. Denying the relief of death (*lā yamūtu*) means that physical destruction is impossible in Hell; the soul endures perpetual conscious awareness of its ruin. Denying true life (*lā yaḥyā*) highlights that existence in Hell is a living death filled with regret, agony, and total alienation from divine mercy. Contrasting fleeting earthly physical pain with eternal existential torment in Hell exposed the utter absurdity of Pharaoh's torture threats.

The condition the verse describes is the punishment of a person who has no way out and no way to end the suffering, which is what makes it a fitting answer to Pharaoh's threat of death. The men he is threatening have just told him that his torture and their life in this world are both short, and that the punishment of God is more lasting (20:72).

## Verse 20:75

> But whoever comes to Him as a believer, having done good, they will have the highest ranks

## Verse 20:76

> the Gardens of Eternity, under which rivers flow, where they will stay forever. That is the reward of those who purify themselves

**Gardens of Eden**
*Jannātu ʿadnin tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — gardens of Eden with rivers running beneath them. The name *ʿadn* is used in the Book for the settled home of the people of Paradise, and the rivers running under the trees are the Book's standing description of a garden that never needs to be watered by hand. The verse then adds the two words that separate this from anything a listener could have seen in his own life: *khālidīna fīhā*, staying there forever. The gardens of this world pass to an heir, and the verse is describing a garden that has no heir.
**The Last Three Words**
*Wa-dhālika jazāʾu man tazakkā* — and that is the reward of whoever purifies himself. *Tazakkā* comes from the same root as *zakāh*, and the sense runs through the whole family of words: to grow, to be clean, to have the surplus taken out. A person purifies himself when he clears out what the Book calls the filth of the heart — the idolatry first, then the pride, the showing-off, and the love of what is passing. Note what the verse does not say: it does not describe a reward handed out in proportion to a person's performance, but the outcome that belongs to the work itself, since the one who cleans a thing is the one who ends up with it clean. The verse before this one names the same people from another angle: the believers whose good deeds are met with more than they did, and the men who came asking for forgiveness at dawn (20:75).
**Where the Verse Sits**
This is the answer to a prayer Moses made several verses earlier, where he asked God to write for the people the good of this world and the next (20:73-74 is about the magicians and their defiance). The point the sūrah is making by its order is worth noticing: the magicians who threw down their ropes and accepted the consequences end the chapter with the promise of paradise stated plainly, and the king who threatened them is left in his palace with nothing said about him at all. The reward is named last so that it is what the reader carries out of the scene.

## Verse 20:77

> And We surely inspired Moses, ˹saying,˺ “Leave with My servants ˹at night˺ and strike a dry passage for them across the sea. Have no fear of being overtaken, nor be concerned ˹of drowning˺.”

Following years of confrontation and plagues in Egypt, God issued the operational command for liberation: *wa-laqad awḥaynā ilā Mūsā an asri bi-ʿibādī fa-iḍrib lahum ṭarīqan fī al-baḥri yabasan lā takhāfu darakan wa-lā takhshā* ('And We surely inspired Moses, saying: Leave with My servants at night and strike a dry passage for them across the sea. Have no fear of being overtaken, nor be concerned of drowning'). God commanded Mūsā to lead the enslaved Israelites out of Egypt under cover of night (*an asri bi-ʿibādī*) and proceed directly toward the Red Sea. Commanding Mūsā to strike a dry passage (*ṭarīqan fī al-baḥri yabasan*) foretold the miraculous parting of the Red Sea into dry ground.

**The Miracle of the Dry Passage Across the Red Sea**

The command *fa-iḍrib lahum ṭarīqan fī al-baḥri yabasan* ('strike for them a dry path in the sea') prepared Mūsā for one of the greatest physical miracles in human history. When Mūsā struck the Red Sea with his staff (26:63), the sea split into twelve dry corridors flanked by massive walls of water. The seabed was transformed instantly into bone-dry soil (*yabas*), allowing millions of men, women, children, and livestock to walk safely across.

**Eliminating the Dual Fears of Pursuit and Drowning**

God issued a double spiritual command: *lā takhāfu darakan wa-lā takhshā* ('Have no fear of being overtaken, nor be concerned of drowning'). Trapped between Pharaoh's approaching chariots behind them and the roaring sea before them, the Israelites panicked (26:61). God's explicit command eliminated both terror sources: divine power would neutralize Pharaoh's pursuit (*darak*) and prevent sea drowning (*khashyah*). Addressing specific human anxieties shows exquisite divine compassion. God reassured fleeing refugees about every physical threat. Eliminating fear of pursuit (*darak*) promised that Pharaonic military chariots would not slaughter or re-enslave the liberated population. Eliminating fear of drowning (*khashyah*) promised that the parted walls of water would remain stable until every single Israelite crossed safely. Divine promises restore emotional composure during terrifying national crises, empowering refugees to march forward with resolute faith. Trusting divine protection during geopolitical transitions enables oppressed communities to step fearlessly into unknown futures. Dissolving terror through divine pledges transforms desperate refugees into a confident, liberated nation marching under divine guidance.

**The Vain Deathbed Repentance of Pharaoh**

As elaborated in Sūrah Yūnus (10:90-92), when the crushing waters of the Red Sea closed over Pharaoh's head, he tried a frantic deathbed conversion: 'I believe that there is no god except Him in Whom the Children of Israel believe!' God rejected his forced deathbed repentance (*āl-'āna wa-qad ʿaṣayta qabl*), preserving his drowned physical corpse as an eternal mummy to serve as a physical sign for future generations. Deathbed repentance (*al-tawbah ʿinda al-ya's*) offered under imminent physical destruction is spiritually invalid before divine law. Delaying repentance until physical death is inescapable reveals spiritual insincerity; true repentance must be offered voluntarily during life. Preserving Pharaoh's physical corpse (*numanjīka bi-badanik*) serves as a tangible, empirical sign for all subsequent human generations. Exposing the tragic failure of forced deathbed conversion warns humanity against procrastinating spiritual repentance. Divine justice rejects hypocritical deathbed pleas from unrepentant tyrants who spent lifetimes brutalizing innocent human beings. Inspiring immediate, sincere repentance during active life protects souls from experiencing Pharaonic regret when death arrives unexpectedly.

---

## Verse 20:79

> And ˹so˺ Pharaoh led his people astray, and did not guide ˹them rightly˺

**The Verdict on a Reign**
*Wa-aḍalla Firʿawnu qawmahū wa-mā hadā* — and Pharaoh led his people astray, and did not guide them. The sentence is short and it covers a whole career. The man had pyramids, titles, armies and a court, and the Book takes all of it and enters one line against it. The verse is placed at the end of the account of the rescue of the children of Israel, so the reader has just watched a nation walk out of the country and an empire follow it into the water.
**What He Claimed and What He Did**
Earlier in the sūrah he tells his people that he only shows them what he sees and guides them only to the right path (40:29). The verse here answers that claim with a single word: he did not guide them. The Book gives the same verdict in the Sūrah of Hūd, where the people who followed a leader into destruction are told that he will lead them to the Fire (11:98), and in the Sūrah of the Ornament it explains how he managed it: he made his people small in their own eyes, and they obeyed him (43:54). A nation follows a tyrant because he first makes it feel small. Pharaoh's system worked by keeping the people afraid and flattered at the same time, and the Book names that as the mechanism of being led astray.
**The Lesson for the Prophet's Own City**
The sūrah is addressing Makkah, where the same arrangement held the same population in place. The men who ran the city had the money, the temple and the ancestors' names, and the verses put their situation and their fate on the same page. The next verse describes the children of Israel inheriting the land and the palaces, and the one after it describes them being overtaken by the sea, which is the sūrah's way of showing where the line about guidance actually lands.

## Verse 20:80

> O Children of Israel! We saved you from your enemy, and made an appointment with you on the right side of Mount Ṭûr, and sent down to you manna and quails

Following the Red Sea victory, God addressed the newly liberated nation directly: *yā banī isrā'īla qad anjaynākum min ʿaduwwikum wa-wāʿadnākum jāniba al-ṭūri al-aymana wa-nazzalnā ʿalaykumu al-manna wa-al-salwā* ('O Children of Israel! We saved you from your enemy, and made an appointment with you on the right side of Mount Ṭūr, and sent down to you manna and quails'). God reminded Israel of three massive divine favors: physical rescue from Pharaonic slavery, a sacred spiritual covenant at Mount Sinai (*Ṭūr*), and miraculous daily sustenance in the desert. Establishing a spiritual appointment (*wāʿadnākum*) at Mount Sinai elevated Israel from traumatized refugees into a holy nation governed by divine law.

**Out of Egypt and Into Freedom**

Verse 80 illustrates the complete social and spiritual transition executed by divine liberation. In Egypt, Israel served Pharaoh (*ʿabīd Firʿawn*), working as forced laborers under lethal coercion. Through divine intervention, they became servants of God (*ʿibād Allāh*), nourished by heavenly food and guided by divine revelation. Shifting allegiance from a human tyrant to the Lord cosmic defines true human elevation. Shifting allegiance from human tyrants to the Almighty converts debasing slavery into noble spiritual servant-hood (*ʿubūdiyyah*). Serving God exclusively liberates human beings from all forms of human oppression, fear, and economic exploitation. Nourishing body (*manna and salwā*) and soul (*Sinai revelation*) simultaneously shows the holistic nature of divine guidance. Rebuilding an enslaved population into a holy nation demands intense pastoral care, lawgiving, and continuous spiritual education. Exalting the formerly oppressed models divine justice, proving that God elevates the weak to confound the arrogant. Inspiring humanity to seek spiritual servant-hood under divine law secures true freedom, human dignity, and eternal divine favor.

## Verse 20:81

> ˹saying,˺ “Eat from the good things We have provided for you, but do not transgress in them, or My wrath will befall you. And whoever My wrath befalls is certainly doomed

**Prohibition of Economic Transgression and Hoarding**

The clause *wa-lā taṭghaw fīhi* ('do not transgress in them') directly addressed Israelite behavioral misconduct about *manna* and *salwā*. Some individuals, driven by greed and lack of faith, hoarded food overnight. The hoarded food rotted instantly (2:57), demonstrating that economic transgression transforms divine blessings into putrid waste. Economic transgression (*ṭughyān*) in food includes hoarding, monopolization, artificial scarcity, and ungrateful wastefulness. Hoarding provisions reflects a lack of faith in divine providence, driven by selfish panic rather than trust in God's continuous sustenance. Rotting hoarded food served as an immediate empirical sign that economic greed destroys divine blessing (*barakah*). Distributing economic resources equitably ensures that every member of the community enjoys enough daily nourishment without fear. Prohibiting greed in food systems models sustainable economics, teaching humanity that resources must be shared rather than hoarded by elites. Cultivating trust in divine daily sustenance frees human hearts from consumerist anxiety, building compassionate, sharing communities.

---

The command to eat from the good things given and the prohibition on transgressing in them are read by the commentators with the account of the manna and the quail, and with the reports that some of the people stored what they were given rather than trusting the day's provision. The warning that follows — whoever earns God's wrath falls — is the same sentence the sūrah of the Heifer gives to the same people when they tired of the food and asked for vegetables (2:61). The verse therefore joins gratitude for provision to the discipline of using it: the gift is given for the day, and the transgression named is the habit of treating the gift as an entitlement.

## Verse 20:82

> But I am truly Most Forgiving to whoever repents, believes, and does good, then persists on ˹true˺ guidance.”

**Fourfold Path of Divine Forgiveness and Salvation**

Immediately following the solemn warning of divine wrath in verse 81, God opened the wide door of divine mercy: *wa-innī la-ghaffārun li-man tāba wa-āmana wa-ʿamila ṣāliḥan thumma ihtadā* ('But I am truly Most Forgiving to whoever repents, believes, and does good, then persists on true guidance'). God revealed His divine attribute *al-Ghaffār* (the Intensely, Repeatedly Forgiving), outlining the four-step spiritual journey to total absolution: repentance (*tāba*), faith (*āmana*), righteous action (*ʿamila ṣāliḥan*), and steadfast perseverance on guidance (*thumma ihtadā*). Proclaiming the divine attribute *al-Ghaffār* assures human souls that divine mercy is infinitely vast, repeatedly forgiving every repenting sinner. Repentance (*tāba*) is the essential first step, requiring sincere regret, cessation of sin, and turning back to God with humble submission. Faith (*āmana*) re-anchors the repenting soul in pure monotheism, renewing spiritual commitment to divine lordship and revelation.

The Book pairs the same terms in the same order elsewhere: the one who repents, believes, does right, then follows guidance — for those, God will replace their misdeeds with good ones. He is Forgiving and Merciful (25:70), and Sūrah al-Nūr states the same hope for the confessing and believing and righteous (24:5, 24:31). The closing word *ihtadā*, guided, names what the whole petition of the sūrah has been about: the guidance Moses asked for himself and his brother (20:10, 20:47). The verse is God's statement to Moses about his own people after the warning in the previous verse, and it puts the door of return in front of the very people who made the calf.

---

## Verse 20:83

> ˹Allah asked,˺ “Why have you come with such haste ahead of your people, O Moses?”

**The Question on the Mountain**
*Wa-mā aʿjalaka ʿan qawmika yā Mūsā* — and what made you hurry ahead of your people, Moses? The scene is the appointment of forty nights that Moses was keeping on the mountain, and the Sūrah of the Heights sets out the full arrangement, with Moses leaving Aaron in charge of the people while he was away (7:142). The question sounds like a reproach and is answered in the next verse with Moses's own words: they are following in my tracks, and I hurried to You, my Lord, so that You might be pleased (20:84). The exchange is brief, and its purpose is to place the man on the mountain at the moment the calf was being made below.
**The Eagerness the Book Praises**
The Book does not treat Moses's haste as a fault in itself. The Sūrah of the Prophets praises the servants of God for the opposite of sluggishness, saying that they used to hasten to good works and call on their Lord with hope and fear (21:90). What the verse does is set the eagerness inside the appointment. Moses had left a people he had just led through the sea, and the Book records that they fell into idolatry within the weeks of his absence. The question is asked so that the reader sees both scenes at once: the man climbing for a meeting with God, and the crowd at the foot of the mountain melting their gold.
**What Follows the Question**
The chapter then turns to the news Moses receives on his way down: your people have been tried after you left, and the Sāmirī has led them astray (20:85). The reader who has heard the question already knows what is coming, and the sūrah is arranged that way. Moses's hurry did not cause the people's fall, and the Book is careful not to say it did. What it shows is that the man who was climbing to meet God had left a community that needed him, and that both facts were true at the same time.

## Verse 20:84

> He replied, “They are close on my tracks. And I have hastened to You, my Lord, so You will be pleased.”

**Mūsā's Sincere Defense and Declaration of Longing**

Mūsā answered God's inquiry with transparent sincerity and passionate love: *qāla hum ulā'i ʿalā atharī wa-ʿajiltu ilayka rabbi li-tarḍā* ('He replied: They are close on my tracks. And I have hastened to You, my Lord, so You will be pleased'). Mūsā explained that the seventy elders were following closely behind him (*ʿalā atharī*), and that his personal haste (*ʿajiltu ilayka*) was driven by a single supreme objective: seeking divine pleasure (*li-tarḍā*). Declaring *ʿajiltu ilayka rabbi li-tarḍā* ('I hastened to You, my Lord, so You will be pleased') represents one of the most beautiful expressions of divine love in sacred history. Reassuring God that his people were following closely (*ʿalā atharī*) reflected Mūsā's belief that his delegation was safely marching toward Sinai. Focusing exclusively upon divine pleasure (*li-tarḍā*) confirmed that Mūsā's haste was free from personal ambition, pride, or worldly self-interest.

**The Supreme Motive: Seeking Divine Pleasure (Li-Tarḍā)**

The phrase *li-tarḍā* ('so that You may be pleased') defines the ultimate spiritual motive of all prophetic action and genuine Islamic worship. Mūsā did not hasten to Sinai for personal status, intellectual curiosity, or heavenly rewards; he sprinted up the mountain solely to earn divine satisfaction (*riḍwān Allāh*). Earning divine pleasure is the highest spiritual station accessible to created beings. Seeking divine pleasure (*li-tarḍā*) is the supreme objective of human existence, transcending all desire for worldly recognition or physical rewards. Divine satisfaction (*riḍwān Allāh*) constitutes the ultimate spiritual bliss in both this world and the Hereafter (9:72).

## Verse 20:85

> Allah responded, “We have indeed tested your people in your absence, and the Sâmiri has led them astray.”

**The News From Below**
*Fa-innā qad fatannā qawmaka min baʿdika wa-aḍallahumu al-Sāmiriyy* — We have tried your people after you left, and the Sāmirī has led them astray. The answer to Moses's hurry is the report of what the hurry cost, and the Book delivers it in one sentence. The word *fatannā* means put to the test, and the trial was the calf, which the Sāmirī made from the gold the people had borrowed from the Egyptians and which the sūrah describes as a body that lowed (20:88). A people that had walked through the split sea weeks earlier were singing to a metal shape, and the verse names both the one who made it and the people who followed him.
**Who the Sāmirī Was**
The Book mentions the Sāmirī only in this passage, and it gives him no history. The name is read as a reference to a place or a group in Egypt, and the commentators gathered reports about him without agreeing on the details. What the sūrah gives is precise: he took dust from the track of the messenger's mount, threw it in, and made the calf, and when Moses asked him why, he answered with his own reasoning (20:96). The Book leaves him with that answer and moves on. The lesson it draws is not about the man but about the crowd, because the crowd is the thing the reader can see himself in.
**What the Passage Is For**
The chapter is placed alongside the account of the Prophet's own people, and the calf story is the sūrah's warning about a community led astray in the absence of its guide. Aaron's protest and weakness, Moses's anger, and the words the people finally use are all recorded in the verses that follow (20:90-94). The Book gives the account in full detail, including the excuses, because the question of who leads the community after the messenger has gone is the question that every generation after this one will face.

## Verse 20:86

> So Moses returned to his people, furious and sorrowful. He said, “O my people! Had your Lord not made you a good promise? Has my absence been too long for you? Or have you wished for wrath from your Lord to befall you, so you broke your promise to me?”

**Mūsā's Return Filled with Prophetic Wrath and Grief**

Armed with the Tablets of the Law and burdened by divine news, Mūsā descended Mount Sinai and returned to his camp: *fa-rajaʿa Mūsū ilā qawmihī ghaḍbāna asifā* ('So Moses returned to his people, furious and sorrowful'). The two adjectival states *ghaḍbān* (intense, righteous moral wrath) and *asif* (profound, heartbreaking sorrow) describe Mūsā's emotional state. His wrath was directed against the blasphemous insult to God, while his sorrow was felt for his people's tragic spiritual self-destruction. Combining righteous wrath (*ghaḍbān*) with heartbreaking sorrow (*asif*) reflects the authentic emotional depth of prophetic character. Righteous anger (*ghaḍab lillāh*) is a noble spiritual response when divine boundaries are violated and oppression or idolatry is committed. Heartbreaking sorrow (*asaf*) shows Mūsā's deep pastoral love for his people; he grieved over their self-inflicted spiritual ruin. Returning directly to the camp (*fa-rajaʿa*) highlights Mūsā's immediate, fearless engagement to confront communal corruption face-to-face.

---

Moses' return with the tablets and his anger are given at greater length in the sūrah of al-Aʿrāf, where he throws down the tablets when he finds his people worshipping the calf, and then takes his brother by the head (7:150). The sūrah's account is compressed here, and what it keeps is the accusation: he holds his people to the promise their Lord made and asks whether his absence was long enough for them to abandon it. The verses that follow give the exchange with Sāmirī and the burning of the calf, so the anger is shown to be the beginning of a correction rather than the end of the mission.

**What Moses Said When He Came Back**
The verse records his return and his words: O my people, did your Lord not promise you a good promise? Did the time seem long to you, or did you wish that the anger of your Lord should fall on you, that you broke your promise to me? The Book gives the account with his fury and his grief in the same speech, and the verses that follow record the answer of the people, who blamed the Sāmirī and produced the gold. The passage is one of the Book's full accounts of a community that failed while its guide was away, and it does not spare the detail.
**What the Tablets Were**
The verse mentions the tablets in the words Moses used when he threw them down, and the sūrah of the Heights gives the sequel, where Moses takes up the tablets again and in their writing is guidance and mercy for those who fear their Lord (7:154). The Book's arrangement gives the anger and then the mercy, and it does not present the messenger as a man without temper. The Sūrah of the Heights also records the words of Aaron, who had told the people not to follow the calf and had been ignored (7:150), and the two accounts together give the reader the whole scene.
**What the Return Revealed**
The sūrah of Ṭā Hā records the exchange between the two brothers after the return, where Moses takes his brother by the head and Aaron explains himself (20:92-94). The Book gives the community both failures: the people who made the calf and the leader who could not stop them, and it lets each speak in his own defence. The passage in front of us is the moment the guide sees what has happened, and the Book's honesty about his reaction is part of what the community is meant to learn about the work of leading people.

## Verse 20:87

> They argued, “We did not break our promise to you of our own free will, but we were made to carry the burden of the people’s ˹golden˺ jewellery, then we threw it ˹into the fire˺, and so did the Sâmiri.”

**The Excuse They Offered**
Moses came down the mountain and asked the people why they had broken the appointment and worshipped the calf, and this verse gives their answer: *we did not break the promise to you by our own choice; we were made to carry the ornaments of the people, and we threw them in — and so the Sāmiri threw*. The sentence does two things at once. It admits they threw the gold, and it plants the responsibility somewhere else, on the man who shaped the calf and on the weight of what they were carrying.
**What the Gold Was**
*Awzāran min zīnati al-qawm* — burdens of the people's ornaments. The gold was Egyptian: the jewelry the Israelites had taken with them when they left, which the commentators connect with the wealth they asked their neighbours to lend them on the night of the departure (12:35 uses the same idea of a load in Joseph's story), and the word *awzār* is the plural of *wizr*, the word for a load that weighs a person down. The verse lets the Israelites describe the gold in the language of guilt, which is a rare thing for a guilty man to do, and it says something about what they knew even as they carried it. Gold taken from Egypt became the material of a god, which is the whole shape of the loss: they left the country and carried its habits out with them.
**The Sāmiri and the Fire**
The last clause reports what the man did with the same material: *and thus the Sāmiri threw*. The verse that follows has him telling the people what came out of the fire and calling it their god, and Moses puts the question to him directly later in the scene: what was your case, O Sāmiri? (20:96). The verse's point is not the mechanics of the casting; it is the pattern of responsibility at work in the camp. The crowd blames the weight and the man; the man blames his vision; and the Book records all of it without letting any of them off, because each of them had a hand on the metal and a voice that could have said no.

## Verse 20:88

> Then he moulded for them an idol of a calf that made a lowing sound. They said, “This is your god and the god of Moses, but Moses forgot ˹where it was˺!”

**Fashioning of the Golden Calf Idol**

Verse 88 describes the exact climax of the Sāmiri's subversion: *fa-akhraja lahum ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwārun fa-qālū hādhā ilāhukum wa-ilāhu Mūsā fa-nasī* ('Then he moulded for them an idol of a calf that made a lowing sound. They said: This is your god and the god of Moses, but Moses forgot!'). The Sāmiri cast a golden calf sculpture (*ʿijlan jasadan*) with hollow internal vents that produced a hollow, wind-driven lowing acoustic sound (*khuwār*), claiming this statue was the physical manifestation of God. Moulding a physical golden calf (*ʿijlan jasadan*) represented a direct violation of pure monotheism and the second commandment. Engineered wind-driven acoustics (*khuwār*) provided a pseudo-miraculous spectacle that mesmerized an impressionable, traumatized crowd. Claiming 'this is your god and the god of Moses' executed a syncretic lie, attempting to combine Pharaonic cow-worship with Mūsā's faith.

The Book tells the story of the calf in several sūrahs and names the same culprit: the Sāmiri (20:85, 20:95), who took a handful of dust from the footprints of the messenger and cast it into the idol (20:96). Sūrah al-Aʿrāf says the people took a calf in his absence and were wrongdoers (7:148). The lowing sound was produced by that mixture, and the Book describes the result with the word *khuwār* — a sound, not a speech, and not a miracle. What follows the verse is Moses' return and the burning of the idol and its scattering into the sea (20:97), which is the answer to the claim that this was anybody's god.

---

## Verse 20:89

> Did they not see that it did not respond to them, nor could it protect or benefit them

God exposed the absolute intellectual absurdity of the golden calf through a devastating rational inquiry: *a-fa-lā yarawna allā yarjiʿu ilayhim qawlan wa-lā yamliku lahum ḍarran wa-lā nafʿā* ('Did they not see that it did not respond to them, nor could it protect or benefit them?'). The Quran applied three criteria of divine reality: the ability to hear and speak (*yarjiʿu qawlan*), the power to avert harm (*ḍarr*), and the power to bestow benefit (*nafʿ*). Powerlessness to protect from harm (*lā yamliku ḍarran*) or bestow benefit (*lā nafʿan*) exposed the idol as utterly useless in human affairs.

**Attribute of Divine Speech vs Mute Idols**

The critique *allā yarjiʿu ilayhim qawlan* ('it cannot return a word to them') highlights the paramount importance of divine speech (*Kalām Allāh*). The True God is *al-Mutakallim*—He speaks to His prophets, reveals scripture, answers prayers, and guides human conscience. In contrast, the golden calf was a mute statue that could only produce a mindless, wind-driven lowing noise (*khuwār*). Worshipping a mute object reflects tragic spiritual degradation. Divine speech (*Kalām*) is a fundamental attribute of the living God, who communicates clear moral guidance through revelation. A mute idol that cannot speak (*lā yarjiʿu qawlan*) can offer zero moral guidance, zero law, and zero spiritual wisdom to its worshippers. Trading the living speech of God at Mount Sinai for the mute wind-noise of a metal calf represented total intellectual and spiritual bankruptcy. Communicating with God through prayer (*duʿā'*) requires a living, All-Hearing Sustainer who understands and responds to human petitions.

## Verse 20:90

> Aaron had already warned them beforehand, “O my people! You are only being tested by this, for indeed your ˹one true˺ Lord is the Most Compassionate. So follow me and obey my orders.”

**Aaron's Proactive Warning and Pastoral Intervention**

Verse 90 reveals that Aaron did not remain passive during the golden calf crisis; he executed proactive, courageous pastoral intervention: *wa-laqad qāla lahum Hārūnu min qablu yā qawmi innamā futintum bihī wa-inna rabbakumu al-Raḥmānu fa-ittabiʿūnī wa-aṭīʿū amrī* ('Aaron had already warned them beforehand: O my people! You are only being tested by this, for indeed your one true Lord is the Most Compassionate. So follow me and obey my orders'). Aaron confronted the crowd before Mūsā's return, warning them that the calf was a divine test (*fitnah*). Reminding them that their true Lord is the Most Compassionate (*al-Raḥmān*) countered the Sāmiri's false claim that God had abandoned them. Vindicating Aaron's moral character, verse 90 proves that Aaron fought courageously to preserve monotheism during Mūsā's absence.

**Aaron's Assertion of Legitimate Prophetic Leadership**

Aaron explicitly commanded the rebellious crowd: *fa-ittabiʿūnī wa-aṭīʿū amrī* ('So follow me and obey my orders'). Mūsā had explicitly called Aaron his deputy leader, commanding him: 'Take my place among my people, maintain righteousness, and do not follow the path of corruptors' (7:142). Aaron exerted his full prophetic authority to halt the idolatrous movement, demanding that Israel remain loyal to the divine appointment. Asserting legitimate prophetic authority (*fa-ittabiʿūnī*) is a duty when internal subverters try to mislead the community. Demanding obedience to divine law (*aṭīʿū amrī*) protects religious movements from chaotic fragmentation and ideological drift. Aaron's command established clear legal lines: following Aaron meant remaining faithful to God; following the Sāmiri meant apostasy. Legitimate leadership derives authority from divine appointment and alignment with revealed law rather than popular sentiment. Standing firm against popular rebellion requires immense moral courage, as Aaron faced death threats from the armed crowd (7:150). Exposing the illegitimacy of subverters protects communities, ensuring that public loyalty remains anchored in righteous leadership.

**The Limits of Aaron's Power and Prevention of Civil War**

Despite Aaron's passionate warnings, the majority of the crowd rejected his leadership, threatening to kill him (7:150) and declaring: 'We will not cease worshipping it until Moses returns' (20:91). Aaron faced an agonizing choice: start a bloody civil war among the Israelites that could destroy the nation, or maintain a peaceful holding action while preserving the faithful minority until Mūsā's return. Aaron chose pastoral preservation over armed conflict. Pastoral wisdom sometimes requires avoiding catastrophic civil war while maintaining uncompromising personal fidelity to truth. Facing life threats from an armed, rebellious crowd, Aaron preserved the faithful core while awaiting Mūsā's authoritative return. Preventing communal fragmentation (*a taqūla farraqta bayna banī isrā'īl*, 20:94) was a strategic pastoral priority for Aaron. Executing duty despite public rejection vindicates Aaron's moral integrity before God and sacred history. Balancing firm moral warning with strategic conflict avoidance shows the profound complexity of prophetic leadership. Vindicating Aaron against false biblical accusations of complicity, the Quran establishes Aaron as a steadfast, noble prophet of monotheism.

## Verse 20:91

> They replied, “We will not cease to worship it until Moses returns to us.”

**The Answer Aaron Received**
The crowd had heard the warning already. Aaron had said to them: O my people, you are only being tested by it, and your Lord is the Most Compassionate, so follow me and obey my command (20:90). What the verse here records is their reply: *we will not stop being devoted to it until Moses comes back to us*. They knew the calf was a test — their words concede nothing about it being a god — and they chose to keep it anyway, and they hung their obedience on the return of the man who was up the mountain.
**The Excuse and What It Hides**
The sentence sounds like loyalty and works like a refusal. The prophet standing in front of them was Moses' brother and the man Moses had placed over them before he left: take my place among my people, he had said, and do not follow the way of those who spread corruption (7:142). Turning obedience into a waiting game made the deputy's authority depend on the absent man's presence, which is how authority is dissolved without a word being said against it. And the object at the centre of the dispute was a calf made of gold and shaped with their own hands, which the man who made it had told them was their god and the god of Moses (20:88). They chose to wait at its feet.
**What Comes Next**
The verse's last words set up the return: the crowd would not move until Moses came back, and when he did, he came back with the Tablets in his hands and the news of what they had done. The scene that follows — his question to his brother, and his brother's defence — is the first thing to happen at the foot of the mountain, before anything is done about the calf itself.

## Verse 20:92

> Moses scolded ˹his brother˺, “O Aaron! What prevented you, when you saw them going astray

**Moses Turns on His Brother First**
When Moses came back and saw what the camp had become, his first question was not put to the crowd. It went to the man he had left in charge: *O Aaron, what kept you, when you saw them going astray?* The form of the question is an inventory: not "why did they do it" but "what stopped you". Moses had appointed his brother over the people and had told him to take his place and not follow the corrupters (7:142), and the return brought the accounting with it, because a leader who is given charge of a people is answerable for how he held that charge.
**Aaron's Answer**
The next verse gives his defence, and it is not a denial: *O son of my mother, do not seize me by my beard or my head; I feared you would say, you have caused division among the children of Israel, and you did not wait for my word* (20:94). He had been left with a crowd that had already turned, an armed festival on the flat ground, and no way to fight it without breaking the community apart in Moses' absence. So he chose to hold what could be held and wait for the man who had authority over them. Both brothers wanted the same thing; they read the moment differently, and Moses' temper met his brother's patience and then — the next verse reports it — Moses asked forgiveness for them both and confessed his own anger.
**What the Scene Teaches**
The picture is not of one right man and one wrong one. Moses was given the law on the mountain and came down burning with zeal for God's honour; Aaron was given the people and measured what he could hold together. The Book records the exchange without smoothing it, which is why it has always been read as a lesson for anyone who has to lead a divided people: the damage done by bad counsel is real, the fear of breaking the community is real, and a disagreement between two men who are both serving God is still a disagreement to be settled with forgiveness, not with blame.

## Verse 20:93

> from following after me? How could you disobey my orders?”

**The Question Put to Aaron**
Moses' question to his brother has two parts: *why did you not follow me?* and *have you then disobeyed my command?* The command in question is the one he had given before climbing the mountain: take my place among my people, do right, and do not follow the way of those who spread corruption (7:142). *Allā tattabiʿani* asks why Aaron did not come after him to report what was happening, and *a-fa-ʿaṣayta amrī* asks whether the restraint he chose amounted to setting the order aside.
**What Moses Was Not Asking**
The interrogation is not the questioning of a man who thinks his brother joined the calf-worshippers. Moses left the camp knowing Aaron's character, and the verse puts the two of them in the same frame: one man burning with zeal for God's honour, the other holding a frightened crowd together. What Moses wanted to know was why he had been left to find out about the calf from his own eyes rather than from his deputy's word. Leadership under a prophet is not a licence to make one's own judgement about which instructions still apply, and the question is the one every deputy has to be able to answer.
**The Answer the Sūrah Gives Next**
Aaron's reply comes in the following verse, and it is heard before Moses has to ask for it twice: he had feared that Moses would say to him, you have caused a division among the children of Israel, and you did not wait for my word (20:94). He had chosen the harm he could see — the calf standing in front of him — over the harm he could only imagine, a community splitting into armed factions while its prophet was away. The sūrah then records Moses' own reaction, which is the other half of the lesson: in the sūrah of the Heights he cries out, Lord, forgive me and my brother, and admit us into Your mercy (7:151), and he takes up the Tablets again after his anger has quieted (7:154), so that the reader is left with two men who were both trying to serve God and had to work out, in the middle of a crisis, which duty came first.

## Verse 20:94

> Aaron pleaded, “O son of my mother! Do not seize me by my beard or ˹the hair of˺ my head. I really feared that you would say, ‘You have caused division among the Children of Israel, and did not observe my word.’”

**Aaron's Tender Emotional Appeal to Mūsā**

Faced with Mūsā's intense physical and verbal confrontation, Aaron offered a tender, disarming plea: *qāla yā ibna umma lā ta'khudh bi-liḥyatī wa-lā bi-ra'sī innī khashītu a taqūla farraqta bayna banī isrā'īla wa-lam tarqub qawlī* ('Aaron pleaded: O son of my mother! Do not seize me by my beard or the hair of my head. I really feared that you would say: You have caused division among the Children of Israel, and did not observe my word'). Aaron addressed Mūsā as 'son of my mother' (*yā ibna umm*), invoking their shared maternal womb to soften Mūsā's anger and appeal to their deep emotional bond. Addressing Mūsā as 'son of my mother' (*yā ibna umm*) invoked maternal tenderness, softening Mūsā's righteous indignation through shared familial intimacy. Pleading against physical grasping (*lā ta'khudh bi-liḥyatī*) revealed Mūsā's intense emotional distress upon returning to find idolatry in his camp.

**Observing Mūsā's Word and Delegated Authority (Wa-Lam Tarqub Qawlī)**

Aaron concluded his defense: *wa-lam tarqub qawlī* ('and you would say: You did not observe my word/instruction'). Mūsā's original instruction to Aaron before ascending Mount Sinai was: 'Take my place among my people, maintain righteousness, and do not follow the path of corruptors' (7:142). Aaron argued that he observed Mūsā's core mandate by keeping the community intact and righteousness maintained among the faithful, awaiting Mūsā's authoritative word. Observing leadership instructions (*tarqub qawlī*) guided Aaron's conduct throughout Mūsā's forty-night retreat on Mount Sinai. Aaron maintained righteousness among the faithful minority while constantly warning the rebellious crowd (20:90). Fulfilling delegated responsibility requires executing instructions faithfully while adapting tactics to unpredictable crisis situations on the ground. Mūsā accepted Aaron's explanation completely, immediately shifting his anger away from Aaron to confront the true subverter, the Sāmiri, in verse 95. Mūsā prayed for divine forgiveness for both himself and Aaron (7:151), confirming their complete mutual love, forgiveness, and prophetic unity. Vindicating Aaron's operational fidelity reinforces faith, proving that both prophets acted with absolute moral sincerity for God's glory.

## Verse 20:95

> Moses then asked, “What did you think you were doing, O Sâmiri?”

**The Question to the Maker of the Calf**
After clearing Aaron, Moses turned to the man who had made the calf: *fa-mā khaṭbuka yā Sāmiriyy* — what is your case, O Sāmirī? The word *khaṭb* is used of a grave affair, a serious matter; this is not small talk but a summons to give an account. Earlier in the scene Moses had spoken to his brother with pain and patience, asking why he had not stopped the people (20:92–94). His words here are of a different kind. He asks the cause.
**The Account and the Sentence**
The Sāmiri answers in the next verse: I saw what they did not see, so I took a handful from the track of the messenger and threw it in (20:96). Whatever that sight was, he claims knowledge the others lacked — which is precisely the claim every maker of a false religion makes. He does not say God told him. He says he saw something no one else saw, and on that private vision he built a god for a whole people. Moses' answer comes in verse 97: go away, and in this life you will say "do not touch me", and your god will be burned and scattered over the sea.
**What the Scene Teaches**
The contrast is the lesson. A man misled by a leader's trick is dealt with as a brother who was caught, and his excuse is heard. A man who invents the misguidance and sets it before the people is removed from among them, because the harm does not stay with him — it grows as long as he is nearby. Prophetic leadership is not one tone applied to everyone; it is a clear eye for the difference between the deceived and the deceiver.

## Verse 20:96

> He said, “I saw what they did not see, so I took a handful ˹of dust˺ from the hoof-prints of ˹the horse of˺ the messenger-angel ˹Gabriel˺ then cast it ˹on the moulded calf˺. This is what my lower-self tempted me into.”

The Sāmiri offered his confession, revealing his hidden psychological and mystical process: *qāla baṣurtu bi-mā lam yabṣurū bihī fa-qabaḍtu qabḍatan min athari al-rasūli fa-nabadhtuhā wa-kadhālika sawwalat lī nafsī* ('He said: I saw what they did not see, so I took a handful of dust from the hoof-prints of the messenger-angel Gabriel then cast it on the moulded calf. This is what my lower-self tempted me into'). He admitted that his action was driven by egoistic arrogance (*baṣurtu bi-mā lam yabṣurū*) and internal lower-self temptation (*sawwalat lī nafsī*).

**What He Took From the Messenger's Track**

Classical commentators like Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Ṭabarī explain that the Sāmiri witnessed the angel Gabriel (*al-Rasūl*) riding a celestial steed during the Red Sea crossing. Recognizing the life-giving spiritual energy in dust touched by the angel's horse, the Sāmiri preserved a handful of that sacred dust. In his subversion, he misapplied this sacred trace, throwing it into the golden calf furnace to manufacture a living acoustic effect. Misapplying sacred spiritual traces (*athari al-rasūl*) to empower idolatrous innovations represents supreme spiritual perversion. Using sacred symbols to justify un-Islamic innovations is a classic tactic deployed by religious deceivers. The Sāmiri combined real spiritual phenomenon (angelic dust) with Egyptian paganism (golden calf) to manufacture a syncretic illusion. Spiritual tools divorced from obedience to divine revelation become instruments of destruction and misguidance. Protecting sacred symbols from co-optation by corrupt manipulators preserves the integrity of religious traditions. Establishing that spiritual power must always serve divine law prevents believers from being mesmerized by pseudo-sacred displays.

## Verse 20:97

> Moses said, “Go away then! And for ˹the rest of your˺ life you will surely be crying, ‘Do not touch ˹me˺!’ Then you will certainly have a fate that you cannot escape. Now look at your god to which you have been devoted: we will burn it up, then scatter it in the sea completely.”

Having heard the Sāmiri's confession, Mūsā pronounced immediate, irrevocable judgment: *qāla fa-idh-hab fa-inna laka fī al-ḥayāti a taqūla lā misāsa wa-inna laka mawʿidan lan tukhlafahū* ('Moses said: Go away then! And for the rest of your life you will surely be crying: Do not touch me! Then you will certainly have a fate that you cannot escape'). Mūsā imposed a twofold punishment: immediate social expulsion accompanied by a miraculous physical/psychological affliction (*lā misās*—'no touch'), followed by inevitable Last-Day punishment in the Hereafter. Executing swift social expulsion (*fa-idh-hab*) isolated the unrepentant subverter, protecting community health from further ideological contagion.

**The Total Destruction and Ocean Scattering of the Golden Calf**

Mūsā proceeded to execute physical judgment upon the golden calf itself: *wa-unẓur ilā ilāhika al-ladhī ẓalta ʿalayhi ʿākifan la-nuḥarriqannahu thumma la-nansifannahu fī al-yammi nasfā* ('Now look at your god to which you have been devoted: we will burn it up, then scatter it in the sea completely'). Mūsā ordered the golden calf to be melted down in fire (*la-nuḥarriqannahu*), ground into fine gold powder, and scattered completely across the ocean waters (*la-nansifannahu fī al-yamm*), eradicating every physical trace of the idol. Eradicating every physical trace of the idol (*la-nuḥarriqannahu*) showed the absolute helplessness of false gods. Melting gold into powder and scattering it into the ocean (*fī al-yamm*) made it physically impossible for anyone to reassemble the idol. Mocking the idol's helplessness ('look at your god!'), Mūsā proved to the crowd that their 'god' could not even defend itself against fire.

**The Sentence and Its Parts**
Moses gives the Sāmirī two punishments, and the first is put in the sentence he is to utter for the rest of his life: do not touch me, a phrase that the commentators read as a social isolation rather than a skin condition, though the older reports describe both. The second is the appointment, a date that he will not escape. The Book then has Moses name the object he had made and tell him to burn it and scatter it in the sea. That final act is a picture of the whole passage, since a god made of gold is not argued with but destroyed.
**What the Sāmirī Had Done**
The verses before this one record his own account: he saw something, he took a handful of dust from the track of the messenger, and he threw it into the shape, and the calf lowed (20:96). The commentators gathered reports about the man, his people and his craft, and the sūrah itself records the judgement of Moses and the verdict of God in one line: he has made a thing that will bring harm (20:95). The Book does not preserve his biography. It preserves the confession and the sentence, and it moves on to the people who followed him.
**The Warning the Passage Carries**
The account is placed in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā beside the story of Adam and the refusal of Iblīs, and the three belong together: a command, a figure made, and a fall. The Prophet's own report in Bukhārī about the calf-makers of the community, and the warning that a person should beware of being separated from the community at the end, is the community's own application. The Book's lesson in this passage is that the maker is punished and the crowd is asked why it followed, and the sūrah puts the question to the people in front of the mountain before it turns back to the Prophet's own city.

## Verse 20:98

> ˹Then Moses addressed his people,˺ “Your only god is Allah, there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. He encompasses everything in ˹His˺ knowledge.”

**Supreme Declaration of Pure Monotheism (Tawḥīd)**

Having destroyed the golden calf and expelled the Sāmiri, Mūsā delivered the ultimate religious conclusion to his people: *innamā ilāhukumu Allāhu al-ladhī lā ilāha illā huwa wasiʿa kulla shay'in ʿilmā* ('Then Moses addressed his people: Your only god is Allah, there is no god worthy of worship except Him. He encompasses everything in His knowledge'). Mūsā restored pristine monotheism (*Tawḥīd*), declaring that divine worship belongs exclusively to Allāh—the Unseen, Infinite God whose knowledge encompasses all creation. Declaring *innamā ilāhukumu Allāh* ('Your only God is Allāh') re-established absolute religious boundaries after the golden calf crisis. Affirming the Kalimah (*lā ilāha illā huwa*) purged every lingering trace of Egyptian polytheism from the community's consciousness. Restoring *Tawḥīd* completed the pastoral rehabilitation of Israel, shifting their devotion from a dead metal calf to the Living God. Declaring that God's knowledge encompasses everything (*wasiʿa kulla shay'in ʿilmā*) re-asserted divine omniscience over human affairs.

**Final Disarming of the Sāmiri's Deception**

Mūsā's sermon in verse 98 executed the final intellectual and spiritual disarming of the Sāmiri's deception. The Sāmiri had claimed the golden calf was 'your god and the god of Moses' (20:88). Mūsā demolished this lie, declaring that neither he nor his people had any god except Allāh. By re-establishing *Tawḥīd*, Mūsā re-united the community under divine law, ending the ideological crisis completely. Dismantling syncretic lies requires explicit, uncompromising restatement of foundational monotheistic principles. Mūsā's declaration restored intellectual sanity to the community, proving that the calf was a worthless piece of melted metal.

## Verse 20:99

## Verse 20:100

> Whoever turns away from it will surely bear the burden ˹of sin˺ on the Day of Judgment

**Gravity of Turning Away from the Divine Reminder**

God expressed the catastrophic Last-Day consequence awaiting those who reject revelation: *man aʿraḍa ʿanhu fa-innahu yaḥmilu yawma al-qiyāmati wizrā* ('Whoever turns away from it will surely bear the burden of sin on the Day of Judgment'). The verb *aʿraḍa* means deliberate, arrogant turning away from divine guidance (*Dhikr*), whether through intellectual skepticism, moral apathy, or political rebellion. Those who turn away carry a crushing physical and spiritual burden of sin (*wizr*) on Resurrection Day. Deliberate aversion (*aʿrāḍ*) toward Quranic guidance represents a major spiritual crime against the Creator. The term *wizr* means a heavy, agonizing spiritual and physical weight of sin that bows down the sinner on Judgment Day.

**Symbolism of Carrying Sins as a Physical Weight (Wizr)**

The phrase *yaḥmilu yawma al-qiyāmati wizrā* ('shall bear on the Day of Judgment a heavy burden') presents a striking Last-Day picture. In Quranic imagery, abstract moral sins committed during earthly life will materialize on Judgment Day as literal, suffocating physical weights loaded upon the sinner's back. The arrogant denier who walked proud in this world will stumble across the Judgment plain bowed beneath the unbearable weight of his own transgressions. Materializing abstract sins into physical weights (*wizr*) illustrates the inescapable reality of moral responsibility. Carrying personal sins on Resurrection Day stresses that no soul can transfer its moral burdens onto others (35:18). The physical crushing weight of *wizr* reflects the psychological guilt and spiritual shame suffocating the unrepentant sinner. Contrasting the light, elevated state of righteous believers with the crushed, bowed state of deniers highlights Last-Day justice. Exposing the literal weight of sin deters human beings from accumulating spiritual debts through arrogance and oppression. Incurring *wizr* is the natural consequence of rejecting divine guidance, proving that sinners fashion their own Last-Day punishment. Purifying the soul through sincere repentance in this life lifts the heavy burden of *wizr* before Resurrection Day arrives.

Categorizing the wicked as *mujrimīn* (unrepentant criminals against divine law) highlights that Last-Day terror targets persistent moral rebellion. **Contrast Between Believer Peace and Evildoer Terror**

Verse 102 invites a profound contrast between the terror of the wicked (*zurqā*) and the serene peace granted to righteous believers. Sūrah al-Anbiya' promises about believers: 'The Great Terror will not grieve them, and the angels will meet them saying: This is your Day which you were promised' (21:103). ## Verse 20:103

> They will whisper among themselves, “You stayed no more than ten days ˹on the earth˺.”

Frantic, hushed whispering (*yatakhāfatūn*) reflects the loss of arrogant public posturing; tyrants who shouted threats on earth speak in terrified whispers. The whisper is the sound of the first real measurement a person ever makes of his life, and it is short. ## Verse 20:105

> And ˹if˺ they ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the mountains, ˹then˺ say, “My Lord will wipe them out completely

**The Question and the Answer**
*Wa-yasʾalūnaka ʿani al-jibāl* — and they ask you about the mountains. The questioners were the men of Makkah, and the question was the kind that comes from a person who has understood the claim well enough to look for a weak point. The mountains were the most solid thing they knew, so a day on which the world is flattened could not be imagined, and the Prophet is being invited to explain the mechanics. The answer he is given leaves the mechanics alone: my Lord will scatter them into dust. The following verse completes the picture, saying that the earth will be left a level plain (20:106).
**The Word That Does the Work**
*Nansifuhā nasfā* is total pulverizing, the crushing of a structure until it is powder, and the Book uses the mountains elsewhere in both roles. The Sūrah of the Tidings calls them pegs driven into the earth (78:7), and the same Book takes them away on the Day with the casualness of a person clearing a table. The argument is not lost on a reader in a town built on bare rock: the things that seemed permanent are the ones with a date attached, and their removal is not a problem for the One who set them there.
**Where the Passage Goes**
From the mountains the sūrah moves to the call on that day, and to the people walking to it with voices subdued before the Merciful and no sound but a footfall (20:108). Then it turns to the intercession, which will be allowed only for those whom the Merciful permits (20:109). The answer to the mockers is therefore not a lecture about physics. It is a summary of the Day itself, delivered in the order it will happen, and the last verse of the passage leaves the questioners with nothing to check and everything to answer for.

## Verse 20:106

> leaving the earth level and bare

**The Word That Describes the Result**
*Fa-yadharuhā qāʿan ṣafṣafā* — and He will leave it a level, empty plain. The word *ṣafṣafā* is what remains after everything has been knocked flat: bare, dust-blown and without a feature to point at. The verse answers the question from the one before it about the mountains (20:105), and it is the picture of a world with the landmarks gone. Sūrah al-Kahf states the same: the day We set the mountains in motion and you see the earth laid bare (18:47), and the Sūrah of the Tidings says the mountains will be moved and become a mirage (78:20).
**What the Verse Adds**
The answer to the questioners is not only that the mountains go; it is that nothing takes their place. A person could imagine a collapse and then a new landscape, and the verse says that what is left will not be a landscape. The next verse seals it, saying no crookedness and no curve will be seen in it (20:107). The world that was full of landmarks — the mountain that marked a town, the stone they swore by, the ridge a man used to find his way — will be a surface with nothing on it. The men who asked the question were being told what would happen to a landscape they had counted on.
**Where the Scene Is Going**
The sūrah then moves straight to the call and the people walking to it with voices lowered before the Merciful (20:108). No one speaks except in a whisper, and the intercession is named as something that will not happen except by His permission. The flat plain belongs to the setting of that crowd. After the gathering, the whole world of landmarks is replaced by the two records: the one that is read out and the one that is not missed, and the verses that follow state both in the same passage (20:124-127).

## Verse 20:107

> with neither depressions nor elevations to be seen.”

**Absolute Absence of Curvature and Elevation (Lā Tarā Fīhā ʿIwajan)**

Elaborating upon the flattened plain of verse 106, God describes its absolute, unbroken uniformity: *lā tarā fīhā ʿiwajan wa-lā amta* ('in which you will see no depression or elevation'). The noun *ʿiwaj* means crookedness, dips, or low valleys, while *amt* refers to hills, mounds, or high elevations. The entire terrestrial globe is rendered completely flat, smooth, and featureless, leaving zero physical unevenness across its surface. Eliminating all dips (*ʿiwaj*) and elevations (*amt*) creates an utterly smooth plain for cosmic judgment. Unbroken physical uniformity ensures that no resurrected person is obscured from view or separated by terrain. Describing total flatness stresses the absolute precision of divine creation and planetary transformation. Stripping the earth of valleys and hills mirrors the removal of moral crookedness and arrogance from creation. Awakening human reflection through vivid negative description (*lā tarā*) highlights the drastic shift from earthly geography.

## Verse 20:108

> On that Day all will follow the caller ˹for assembly˺, ˹and˺ none will dare to deviate. All voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate. Only whispers will be heard

God depicts the immediate, disciplined response of resurrected humanity on the level plain: *yawma'idhin yattabiʿūna al-dāʿiya lā ʿiwaja lahu wa-khashaʿati al-aṣwātu li-l-raḥmāni fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsā* ('On that Day people will follow the caller directly, with no crookedness. Voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate, and you will hear nothing but a faint rustling'). The caller (*al-dāʿī*), usually identified as Angel Isrāfīl sounding the trumpet, issues an irresistible divine summons. Following the caller directly (*yattabiʿūna al-dāʿiy*) illustrates the total, irresistible authority of divine command on Resurrection Day.

**Universal Framework for Preparing for the Quiet Day**

Verse 108 offers a timeless spiritual discipline for every believer. Reflect upon the Day when all human voices will be hushed to a faint rustling (*fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsā*). Discipline your speech today by abandoning arrogant boasting, lies, and idle chatter. Cultivate reverential awe before the Most Compassionate (*al-Raḥmān*), walk straight upon the divine path, and prepare to meet your Creator in peaceful submission.

---

The scene of the Day is completed by the verses that follow: the faces will be downcast before the Ever-Living, the self-sustaining, and whoever carries wrongdoing will have failed (20:111). The Book describes the same discipline of the crowd in the sūrah of the Cessation, where the summoner calls to a hard day and the people rise as if they were locusts scattered (54:7–8), and in the sūrah of the Bee, where the voice of the caller is followed without deviation (16:84, 78:18). What the verse stresses is the absence of the noise that defines human gatherings: at the call to the plain, the hubbub of argument and objection has no place.

## Verse 20:109

> On that Day no intercession will be of any benefit, except by those granted permission by the Most Compassionate and whose words are agreeable to Him

**No Intercession Without Leave**
The verse sets out what will and will not work on that Day: *no intercession will be of any benefit except from one to whom the Most Compassionate has given permission, and whose word He accepts*. Intercession — one person speaking for another before God — is a hope every religion makes room for, and the verse does not abolish it. What it does is move the whole matter out of the hands of the intercessor. Permission is God's to give, and the word spoken must be one He accepts. Nobody earns the right to speak for someone else in that court; nobody can buy it, and nobody will be asked to pay.
**The Hopes the Verse Corrects**
The idolaters of Makkah had a ready answer when they were asked why they worshipped stones and images: these are our intercessors with God (10:18). The verse ends that arrangement with a sentence that names the whole chain of the argument: the idols cannot speak, cannot approach, and were never granted anything, because a lifeless object has no standing to ask for. The same applies to the men who set themselves up as brokers, whether in this life or the next. The verse leaves the believer with a hope that is real and a list of sponsors that is empty — which is what the sūrah has been saying from its first page: who else is there that could help?

## Verse 20:110

> He ˹fully˺ knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them, but they cannot encompass Him in ˹their˺ knowledge

**The Two Directions of Knowledge**
*Yaʿlamu mā bayna aydīhim wa-mā khalfahum* — He knows what is before them and what is behind them. The words describe the whole span of a person's life from where God stands: the deeds already done and the deeds not yet done. The Book gives the same description of the Throne verse, where God's knowledge is stated and then the limit is set: they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills (2:255). The verse here has the same pair of clauses, and they are placed in the middle of the sūrah's picture of the Day because everything that happens on it depends on what was known beforehand.
**The Limit on Knowledge**
The second clause states what the created being can hold: nothing, unless God permits. This is not a denial that people know things. It is a statement about the reach of the knowledge, and the Book uses the same formula for the intercession it has just described: no one speaks on that day except by permission, and what is said will be right (20:109). A creature that knows only what it is given cannot stand in front of the One who gave it as an equal, and the verse draws that line in the middle of the scene rather than in a lesson at the end.
**Where the Verse Stands**
The passage around it describes the Day in four movements: the voices are lowered (20:108), intercession is permitted (20:109), knowledge is bounded (20:110), and the faces are humbled before the Living, the Self-Subsisting (20:111). The sūrah is teaching the reader how to picture the court, and the verse in the middle is what keeps the picture from becoming a study of thrones and ranks. It says the judge knows everything, and the parties know what they were permitted to know, and nothing else.

## Verse 20:111

> And all faces will be humbled before the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining. And those burdened with wrongdoing will be in loss

**Universal Humbling of Faces Before the Living, Self-Sustaining God**

Verse 111 depicts the climactic submission of all created beings before the divine court: *wa-ʿanati al-wujūhu li-l-ḥayyi al-qayyūmi wa-qad khāba man ḥamala ẓulmā* ('All faces will be humbled before the Living, the Self-Sustaining. And those weighed down with injustice will be truly ruined'). The verb *ʿanat* means extreme, helpless submission and downcast bowing of faces (*al-wujūh*). All created beings—kings, emperors, scholars, and subjects—bow down in absolute helplessness before Allāh, described by His supreme names *al-Ḥayy* (the Ever-Living) and *al-Qayyūm* (the Self-Sustaining Sustainer of all existence). Helpless bowing of faces (*ʿanati al-wujūh*) means the ultimate, universal surrender of human pride on Resurrection Day. Invoking *al-Ḥayy* (the Ever-Living) stresses that while all earthly kings died and decomposed, God remains eternally Living. Invoking *al-Qayyūm* (the Self-Sustaining) highlights that God alone sustains all existence, while created beings depend completely upon Him.

**The Theological Depth of Al-Ḥayy and Al-Qayyūm**

The divine names *al-Ḥayy* and *al-Qayyūm* represent the core attributes of divine perfection in Islamic belief. *Al-Ḥayy* indicates eternal, uncreated life unaffected by sleep, fatigue, sickness, or death. *Al-Qayyūm* indicates absolute self-sufficiency and continuous sustenance of the entire cosmos. Eternal uncreated life (*al-Ḥayy*) distinguishes the True Creator from dying human leaders and lifeless physical idols. Combining *al-Ḥayy* and *al-Qayyūm* forms the Greatest Name of Allāh (*al-Ism al-Aʿẓam*) through which supplications are answered. Bowing before the Ever-Living Sustainer aligns human existence with the fundamental truth of cosmic reality. Polytheistic *shirk* represents the greatest form of injustice (*innal-shirka la-ẓulmun ʿaẓīm*, 31:13), severing the soul from divine grace.

## Verse 20:112

> But whoever does good and is a believer will have no fear of being wronged or denied ˹their reward˺

**Unshakeable Security for Believing Doers of Good**

Following the stern warning to oppressors in verse 111, God presents the radiant promise granted to righteous believers: *wa-man yaʿmal min al-ṣāliḥāti wa-huwa mu'minun fa-lā yakhāfu ẓulman wa-lā haḍmā* ('But whoever does good—while believing—will have no fear of injustice or deprivation'). God establishes that true Last-Day security requires two intertwined pillars: righteous action (*yaʿmal min al-ṣāliḥāt*) and authentic monotheistic faith (*wa-huwa mu'min*). Those who unite faith and righteous works are granted complete immunity from unfair punishment (*ẓulm*) and reduction of earned rewards (*haḍm*). Contrasting the ruin of oppressors (verse 111) with the security of believers (verse 112) highlights flawless divine justice. Reassuring righteous souls, verse 112 removes Last-Day anxiety, filling the heart with tranquil confidence in divine grace.

**Linguistic Distinction Between Ẓulm and Haḍm**

Classical Quranic commentators highlight the subtle, profound distinction between *ẓulm* and *haḍm*. In Last-Day law, *ẓulm* refers to adding uncommitted sins to a person's record or punishing an innocent person, while *haḍm* refers to diminishing, discounting, or erasing earned good deeds. God promises that neither form of injustice will ever occur in His divine court; every believer receives full credit for every good deed and zero false accusations. Negating *ẓulm* guarantees that no false sins will ever be added to a believer's record on Judgment Day. Negating *haḍm* guarantees that no righteous deed—even the weight of an atom (*mithqāla dharratin*)—will be discounted or diminished. Flawless judicial accuracy in divine accounting refutes human fears of bureaucratic error or judicial bias. Magnifying divine generosity, God promises that good deeds will be multiplied tenfold or many hundredfold (6:160). Encouraging meticulous moral tracking, awareness that no deed is lost inspires believers to perform small daily acts of kindness. Demonstrating absolute judicial fairness elevates human love and gratitude toward the Sovereign King. Validating every hidden sacrifice, divine promise assures believers that silent suffering for truth is fully noticed.

**The Magnification of Believer Rewards in Divine Law**

Divine justice in Islam is uniquely weighted in favor of mercy. And, divine grace (*faḍl*) converts repented bad deeds into good deeds (25:70), ensuring that believers step onto the plain of Judgment with overwhelming spiritual assets.

## Verse 20:113

> And so We have sent it down as an Arabic Quran and varied the warnings in it, so perhaps they will shun evil or it may cause them to be mindful

**An Arabic Recitation, and Warnings Turned Over**
The verse states two facts about how the Book was given: *and so We sent it down as an Arabic Qur'an*, and *and We turned over in it the warnings, in various forms*, so that people might be mindful or that it might produce in them a recollection. The first fact is about the language. A message meant to reach a particular people in their own lifetime came in their own tongue, clear and unmediated, so that the most eloquent of them could find no complaint on the ground of language and no excuse about hearing it second-hand.
**Why the Warnings Repeat**
The second fact is about method. *Ṣarrafnā* means turning a thing over and presenting it in different ways — the same warning as a story here, a parable there, a threat of consequence in another place. The Book does not say a thing once and leave the reader to remember it. It circles a subject because people are not all reached the same way: one man is moved by a description of the Day, another by the fate of a town, another by a single phrase in a verse he has heard a hundred times. The two outcomes named are the two things that save a person — *taqwā*, the watchful fear of God, and *dhikr*, the recollection that brings a man back to himself. The verse places them at the end and leaves them there: the warning does its work when one of the two is produced.

**The Arabic and the Guarding of It**
The language of the revelation was not chosen at random. The men who were being addressed were the masters of the Arabic tongue, and a message that came to them in it left no room for the complaint that the words had passed through a translator's hands or reached them second-hand. That is also why the Arabic text matters to the Muslim community in every later century: what is recited in prayer is the Book itself, while a translation is an explanation of it. The Book contains the promise on this point — We sent down the Reminder and We are surely guarding it (15:9) — and the community's history of memorisation, of chains of transmission, and of a single agreed text is what that guarding has looked like in practice. Arabic is not a badge of superiority here; it is the means by which the words keep their first shape across fourteen centuries and every continent.

## Verse 20:114

> Exalted is Allah, the True King! Do not rush to recite ˹a revelation of˺ the Quran ˹O Prophet˺ before it is ˹properly˺ conveyed to you, and pray, “My Lord! Increase me in knowledge.”

**The Exalted Kingship of Allāh (Fa-Taʿālā Allāhu Al-Maliku Al-Ḥaqq)**

God proclaims His supreme majesty and absolute royal sovereignty: *fa-taʿālā Allāhu al-maliku al-ḥaqqu wa-lā taʿjal bi-l-qur'āni min qabli a yuqḍā ilayka waḥyuhu wa-qul rabbi zidnī ʿilmā* ('Exalted is Allah, the True King! Do not rush with the Quran before its revelation is completed to you, and say: My Lord! Increase me in knowledge'). The phrase *fa-taʿālā Allāhu* declares God infinitely exalted above all human misconceptions, polytheistic idols, and worldly tyrants. He is *al-Maliku al-Ḥaqq*—the True, Ultimate Sovereign whose kingship is eternal, absolute, and uncreated. Proclaiming *fa-taʿālā Allāhu* establishes divine supreme transcendence above all created entities and human speculations. Calling God *al-Maliku al-Ḥaqq* ('the True King') contrasts eternal divine sovereignty with perishable, unjust earthly rulers. Declaring divine kingship immediately after discussing revelation confirms that the Quran is a royal decree issued by the King of all things.

**Divine Discipline in Receiving Revelation (Wa-Lā Taʿjal Bi-l-Qur'ān)**

God addresses Prophet Muḥammad, instructing him on the proper protocol for receiving revelation: *wa-lā taʿjal bi-l-qur'ān* ('do not rush with the Quran'). Out of intense love for the divine word and fear of forgetting any part of the message, Prophet Muḥammad used to repeat the verses rapidly after Angel Jibrīl while revelation was still being recited. Instructing the Prophet to listen patiently (*wa-lā taʿjal*) shows the supreme decorum and discipline required when receiving divine speech. Reassuring the Prophet, God promised to take personal responsibility for preserving and explaining the Quran in his heart (75:16-19).

## Verse 20:115

> And indeed, We once made a covenant with Adam, but he forgot, and ˹so˺ We did not find determination in him

**The Covenant and the Forgetfulness**
*Wa-laqad ʿahidnā ilā ādama min qablu fa-nasiya* — We made a covenant with Adam before, and he forgot. The Sūrah of the Heights and the Sūrah of the Cow give the setting: Adam and his wife were told to live in the Garden and eat freely, with one tree forbidden, and warned that the enemy was hostile to them (2:35; 7:19-20). The verse here states what happened in one word. The warning was in place, the enemy was named, and the man still forgot, which is why the Book tells the story rather than describes an ideal human being.
**What "We Found in Him No Firm Resolve" Means**
The second half of the sentence explains the first: no *ʿazm*, no settled determination. The word the Book uses for the messengers who hold steady under years of opposition is the same one, when the Prophet is told to be patient as the messengers of resolve were patient (46:35). Adam's lapse was not a hardened rebellion. It was a moment of missing resolve, and the distinction matters, because the next verses show what followed. A person who falls in a weak moment is not in the same category as the man who builds his life against God, and the Book says so by naming what was missing rather than what was intended.
**How the Story Ends**
The account finishes with the mercy that the reader of the sūrah is meant to carry: Adam's Lord chose him, turned to him, and guided him (20:122), and the Sūrah of the Heights records the words the man and his wife used — our Lord, we have wronged ourselves; if You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will be among the losers (7:23). The Book rarely tells the story of a failure without ending it at the door of return. The next verses turn to the guidance that came down, and to the warning that whoever turns away from it will have a narrow life (20:124).

## Verse 20:116

> And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who refused ˹arrogantly˺

**The Command and the One Who Did Not Bow**
The scene is the making of Adam. God told the angels to prostrate before him, they all bowed, and one of those present did not: Iblīs. The sūrah of the Cow tells the same scene with the answer written in: *he refused and was arrogant, and so became one of the disbelievers* (2:34). The refusal is the subject of the verse, and the Book returns to it in several places, because what happened there is the founding case of every later refusal.
**The Argument He Made**
Al-Ḥijr, Ṣād and al-Aʿrāf all report the exchange that followed. God asked what kept him back; he answered that he was better than Adam, since he was made from fire and Adam from clay (7:12; 15:33; 38:76). Here is the whole disease in one sentence. He was not confused about the command — he had heard it plainly. He set up a comparison of his own choosing (*qiyās*), measured honour by what a person was made of, and used the measurement to overrule the One who makes. The Book also notes what he had that Adam did not: a long record of worship among the angels, and a place near the Throne. The Sūrah of the Heights records his claim and the answer together, and the Sūrah of the Cave reports his answer spoken back on the Day of the gathering (17:61-62).
**What He Could Not Bear**
The angels were not told to bow because Adam was made of better stuff, and they had no way of knowing what honour meant. When God taught Adam the names (2:31), the honour became visible, and envy (*ḥasad*) did the rest: Iblīs could not accept a creature of clay standing higher than himself. The verses about the angels at the Throne belong in the picture too. They ask forgiveness for the believers and carry out what they are told without hesitation (40:7; 66:6); the one who refused was the exception, and what made him the exception was not information but pride. Sūrah al-Ḥijr states the limit that followed: over God's sincere servants he has no authority (15:42), and the whole story is given as a warning to a person who might one day weigh his own record against God's order. The sūrah of Ṭā Hā puts it in front of the reader at the point where the account of Moses is about to begin, because the same test appears in both stories: a man with power, a command, and the choice of what to do with himself.

## Verse 20:117

> So We cautioned, “O Adam! This is surely an enemy to you and to your wife. So do not let him drive you both out of Paradise, for you ˹O Adam˺ would then suffer ˹hardship˺

God warned Adam and Hawwā' (Eve) in Paradise about the dangerous enemy threatening their happiness: *fa-qulnā yā ādamu inna hādhā ʿaduwwun laka wa-li-zawjika fa-lā yukhrijannakumā min al-jannati fa-tashqā* ('So We said: O Adam! This is truly an enemy to you and your wife. So do not let him drive you both out of Paradise, or you will be miserable'). God explicitly identified Iblīs as a declared enemy (*ʿaduww*), cautioning them that yielding to Satan's ploys would result in expulsion from heavenly bliss into earthly hardship and misery (*fa-tashqā*). Inculcating constant spiritual vigilance, verse 117 warns all human descendants against trusting the declared enemy of humanity.

**The Shared Partnership of Adam and Hawwā' in Quranic Narrative**

The Quranic phrase *laka wa-li-zawjika* ('to you and your wife') reinforces the fundamental Islamic doctrine of equal spiritual status and joint partnership between man and woman. Unlike biblical traditions that blame Eve as the sole temptress who corrupted Adam, the Quran presents Adam and Hawwā' as equal co-recipients of divine warnings, equal victims of Satan's deception, equal partners in slipping, and equal co-seekers of divine forgiveness. Establishing joint spiritual partnership, the Quran treats man and woman with identical moral dignity and accountability. RefUTING misogynistic myths blaming women for the fall of humanity, the Quran explicitly states that Satan tempted both Adam and Hawwā' together (2:36). Highlighting joint responsibility, both spouses must support each other in maintaining spiritual vigilance and family harmony. Demonstrating mutual repentance, Adam and Hawwā' prayed together: 'Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves...' (7:23). Fostering healthy marital dynamics, marriage is portrayed as a sacred partnership designed for mutual comfort, protection, and faith. Elevating the status of women, Islam restored full spiritual, legal, and human rights to women centuries ahead of other civilizations. Guarding the Muslim family against Satanic subversion requires both husband and wife to stand united against spiritual temptations.

**The Reality of Shaqā' (Spiritual and Earthly Misery)**

The term *shaqā'* encompasses all forms of human misery—physical exhaustion, psychological anxiety, spiritual alienation, and Last-Day damnation. In Paradise, Adam enjoyed complete internal peace and physical abundance. Outside Paradise, turning away from divine guidance introduces *shaqā'* into human life. Verse 117 establishes that true human happiness (*saʿādah*) is found exclusively in divine proximity, while misery (*shaqā'*) is the inevitable result of Satanic alignment. Contrasting *saʿādah* (felicity) with *shaqā'* (misery), human choices determine whether life is filled with celestial peace or earthly despair. Guiding human ambitions, true success lies in securing divine pleasure and returning safely to the eternal Gardens of Paradise. Proclaiming Quranic healing, divine revelation was sent down not to cause misery, but to lift *shaqā'* from human life (20:2).

## Verse 20:118

> Here it is guaranteed that you will never go hungry or unclothed

**The Two Needs Named**
The verse is part of the speech God makes to Adam at his beginning: here you will not go hungry and you will not be naked. Food and clothing are the first two things a human being needs, and they are the two things that were taken away from Adam and Eve when they fell. The Sūrah of the Heights describes that moment, where they began to cover themselves with the leaves of the Garden after their nakedness became visible to them (7:22), and the same sūrah states the gift that covers more than leaves: clothing of awareness of God, which is the best clothing of all (7:26).
**What the Next Verse Adds**
The verse after this one completes the promise: and you will not be thirsty in it, nor will you suffer from the heat (20:119). The list runs through the body's needs one by one — hunger, covering, drink, shelter from the sun — and it answers them all in the same place and in the same breathless way, as a statement of fact rather than a reward being negotiated. What was lost is restored, and the restoration is described in the plainest terms a hearer could picture.
**Why the Sūrah Puts It Here**
The passage sits at the point in the sūrah where Adam's story is introduced, and the sūrah will go on to show the enemy who made the promise fall apart. The first thing Adam is told after his creation is what he will not lack. The Book's descriptions of the Garden are like this throughout: shade, fruit within reach, no heat and no thirst. The reward is spoken of in the language of the needs the reader already knows, and the reader is left to understand that what is being given is far beyond the words.

## Verse 20:119

> nor will you ˹ever˺ suffer from thirst or ˹the sun’s˺ heat.”

**Total Protection from Climate Extremes in Paradise**

Completing the catalog of heavenly provisions begun in verse 118, God describes the total climate comfort enjoyed in Paradise: *wa-annaka lā taẓma'u fīhā wa-lā taḍḥā* ('nor will you suffer from thirst or the sun's heat'). God guaranteed Adam total immunity from thirst (*la taẓma'*) and environmental heat exposure (*lā taḍḥā*). In Paradise, climate conditions were perpetually temperate, soothing, and pleasant, devoid of burning desert sun, scorching heat, or suffocating dehydration. Banishing thirst (*lā taẓma'*) guaranteed continuous internal hydration and soothing physical comfort without exertion. Banishing environmental heat (*lā taḍḥā*) protected Adam from sunstroke, exhaustion, and uncomfortable climatic exposure. Completing the fourfold guarantee of basic provisions—food, clothing, water, and shelter—covered all physical human requirements. Contrasting heavenly climate with the harsh Arabian desert heat highlights the immense relief and bliss of Paradise for early readers. Demonstrating divine perfection, Paradise provides an environment where physical nature exists in perpetual, soothing harmony.

**Linguistic Nuance of Taḍḥā (The Scorching Midday Sun)**

The verb *taḍḥā* derives from *ḍuḥā* (forenoon / midday sun) and specifically means exposure to the direct, burning heat of the sun (*al-ḍaḥā'*). Saying *wa-lā taḍḥā* means Adam was never exposed to painful, burning solar radiation or oppressive heat. In Paradise, there is neither freezing cold nor scorching heat, as described in Sūrah al-Insān: 'they will see in it neither scorching sun nor freezing cold' (76:13). Denying solar exposure (*lā taḍḥā*) highlights the perfectly balanced, temperate climate (*iʿtidāl*) governing Paradise. Protecting the human body from sunburn and heat exhaustion reflects God's complete, compassionate care for Adam. Contrasting heavenly shade with the scorching heat of Judgment Day reminds believers to seek divine protection today.

## Verse 20:120

> But Satan whispered to him, saying, “O Adam! Shall I show you the Tree of Immortality and a kingdom that does not fade away?”

Verse 120 depicts the pivotal moment of temptation and deception in Paradise: *fa-waswasa ilayhi al-shayṭānu qāla yā ādamu hal adulluka ʿalā shajarati al-khuldi wa-mulkin lā yablā* ('But Satan whispered to him, saying: O Adam! Shall I show you the Tree of Immortality and a kingdom that never fades?'). The verb *waswasa* means a quiet, repetitive, seductive whisper that penetrates human thoughts unnoticed. Satan (*al-Shayṭān*) approached Adam not with open hostility, but under the guise of an affectionate advisor offering ultimate human desires: absolute immortality (*shajarat al-khuld*) and an undecaying, eternal kingdom (*mulk lā yablā*). Exposing the mechanics of spiritual deception, verse 120 provides a masterclass in recognizing Satanic strategies.

**The Nature of Waswasah (Satanic Whispering) and How to Combat It**

The Quranic concept of *waswasah* describes subtle, intrusive thoughts suggested by Satan into the human chest (*fī ṣudūri al-nās*, 114:5). *Waswasah* does not have physical force or compulsory power; it is merely an invitation. Satan cannot force anyone to sin. The human being retains full free will and moral agency to reject the whisper or yield to it. Defining *waswasah*, Satanic whispering consists of quiet intrusive suggestions that exploit human desires and spiritual heedlessness. Confirming human free will, Satan has zero compulsory power over human beings; he merely invites, and humans choose (14:22). Recognizing the subtle arrival of *waswasah* enables individuals to reject sinful thoughts before they translate into physical actions. Combating Satanic whispering requires immediate recourse to divine remembrance (*dhikr*) and seeking refuge in God (*istiʿādhah*). RefLECTING upon prophetic advice, when a Satanic whisper arrives, say: 'I seek refuge in Allāh from the accursed Satan' (*aʿūdhu billāhi min al-shayṭān al-rajīm*). Purifying the internal heart environment deprives Satanic whispers of fertile ground in which to take root. Empowering spiritual defense, continuous Quranic recitation and daily prayers build an impenetrable fortress around the human mind.

**The Contrast Between Divine Command and Satanic Promise**

Verse 120 creates a stark contrast between God's clear, loving command and Satan's deceptive, hollow promise. God had explicitly warned Adam: 'This is truly an enemy to you... Do not let him drive you out' (20:117). Satan offered a counter-narrative: 'Shall I show you the Tree of Immortality?' Adam's tragic mistake was giving weight to the word of a declared enemy over the explicit command of his Loving Creator. Contrasting divine commands with Satanic promises reveals the absolute reliability of divine truth versus the total falsehood of Satanic lies. Prioritizing explicit divine revelation over seductive human or Satanic counter-narratives is the core test of monotheistic faith. Exposing Satanic betrayal, Satan promises absolute freedom and eternal kingdoms, but delivers shame, exposure, and poverty. Learning from Adam's mistake, believers resolve never to negotiate with or entertain arguments that contradict explicit divine prohibitions. Anchoring heart-trust in Allāh prevents souls from being swept away by attractive worldly philosophies that contradict sacred law. Reinforcing prophetic guidance, true success lies in listening unconditionally to the King cosmic (*al-Maliku al-Ḥaqq*). Sustaining spiritual vigilance ensures that divine warnings remain active and prominent in daily decision-making.

God had simply commanded: 'Do not approach this tree' (2:35), without giving it a special name.

Satan exploited Adam's noble desire for permanent divine proximity, deceptively claiming that eating from the forbidden tree would transform him into an immortal angel or an eternal king (7:20).

## Verse 20:121

> So they both ate from the tree and then their nakedness was exposed to them, prompting them to cover themselves with leaves from Paradise. So Adam disobeyed his Lord, and ˹so˺ lost his way

Verse 121 relates the tragic execution of the slip and its immediate, humiliating aftermath: *fa-akalā minhā fa-badat lahumā saw'ātuhumā wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni ʿalayhimā min waraqi al-jannati wa-ʿaṣā ādamu rabbahu fa-ghawā* ('So they both ate from it, and their private parts became exposed to them, and they began to cover themselves with the leaves of Paradise. So Adam disobeyed his Lord and went astray'). Driven by Satan's seductive promise of immortality, both Adam and Hawwā' ate from the forbidden tree (*fa-akalā minhā*). Instantly, their heavenly garments dissolved, exposing their nakedness (*badat lahumā saw'ātuhumā*).

**The Innate Human Impulse Toward Modesty (Ḥayā')**

A deeply profound aspect of verse 121 is Adam and Hawwā's instinctive reaction upon becoming naked: *wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni ʿalayhimā min waraqi al-jannat* ('and they began to stitch leaves of Paradise over themselves'). God had not yet issued a formal legal command about physical modesty on earth, yet both Adam and Hawwā' instinctively felt intense shame and rushed to cover their bodies. ## Verse 20:127

The verse defines two primary spiritual crimes: exceeding divine limits (*asrafa*) and persistent disbelief in divine signs (*lam yu'min bi-āyāt*). The resulting Last-Day punishment is described as incomparably more severe (*ashadd*) and far more lasting (*abqā*) than any earthly hardship. Establishing universal divine law (*wa-kadhālika najzī*), God confirms that Last-Day retribution applies impartially across all human history. Defining primary spiritual crimes, *isrāf* (extravagance in sin and exceeding boundaries) combined with *kufr* (disbelief in signs) leads to total ruin.
**Concept of Isrāf (Exceeding Divine Limits)**
The Quranic term *isrāf* means exceeding proper boundaries, squandering divine gifts, and committing extravagance in sin. Defining *isrāf*, exceeding divine boundaries squanders the precious, non-renewable asset of human physical lifespan. Exposing the root of transgression, *isrāf* stems from spiritual heedlessness (*ghaflah*) and unchecked desire worship (*hawā*). Contrasting *isrāf* with *iqtiṣād* (moderation) and *taqwā* (moral restraint), Islam commands balanced, disciplined living. Offering hope through repentance, parallel verses declare: 'O My servants who have transgressed against themselves (*asrafū*), do not despair of divine mercy' (39:53).

The verse states the rule behind the punishment of the man who went too far and would not believe the signs of his Lord: this is how We repay the one who is extravagant. The word *musrif* is the one the sūrah has been using for Pharaoh since the beginning, where Moses is told to go to him because he has transgressed (20:24; 20:43). The same word covers the person who spends what God gave him on his own appetite and calls the direction of his life his own business. The verse then names the outcome in the next world as both severer and longer lasting, and the sūrah has already described what that life feels like: a constricted grave-like existence (20:124), and a fire where a man neither dies nor lives (20:74).
**Where the Passage Is Standing**
The verses around this one are the sūrah's consolation to the Prophet. It has just been told that God sees, and before that, that many generations were destroyed in whose dwellings the people of Makkah now walk (20:128). The verse before this one tells the Prophet to be patient with what they say, and to praise and pray at the two ends of the day (20:130). The warning and the patience are given in the same passage, and the sūrah's arrangement says what a messenger is supposed to do with the information: keep speaking and leave the repayment to the One who made the appointment.

Unlike physical miracles granted to earlier prophets—such as Mūsā's staff turning into a serpent or ʿĪsā healing the blind—which were limited in time and witnessed only by immediate onlookers, the miracle granted to Prophet Muḥammad is an eternal, intellectual miracle: the Holy Quran. Anyone across any century or continent can read, analyze, test, and experience the living miracle of the Quran directly. Defining the intellectual miracle (*al-muʿjizah al-ʿaqliyyah*), the Quran appeals directly to human intellect, reason, and heart reflection. ## Verse 20:134

> Had We destroyed them with a torment before this ˹Prophet came˺, they would have surely argued, “Our Lord! If only You had sent us a messenger, we would have followed Your revelations before being humiliated and put to shame.”

God establishes the absolute judicial necessity of sending messengers before enforcing judgment: *wa-law annā ahlaknāhum bi-ʿadhābin min qablihi la-qālū rabbanā law-lā arsalta ilaynā rasūlan fa-nattabiʿa āyātika min qabli a nadhilla wa-nakhzā* ('If We had destroyed them with a punishment before this, they would have said: Our Lord! If only You had sent us a messenger, we would have followed Your signs before we were humbled and disgraced'). Eliminating human legal excuses (*la-qālū rabbanā law-lā...*), sending messengers closes every loophole, leaving humanity fully accountable for their choices. Fulfilling the universal Quranic legal principle: 'We never punish until We have sent a messenger' (*wa-mā kunnā muʿadhdhibīna ḥattā nabʿatha rasūlā*, 17:15).

**Exposing the Hypocrisy of Post-Facto Human Excuses**

Verse 134 exposes a psychological pattern in human behavior. Before receiving guidance, people boastful of their morality claim: 'If only God sent us a guide, we would be the most obedient believers!' Yet when a prophet arrives with explicit scriptures (*āyāt*), many of those same people reject him out of arrogance, social pride, and desire worship. Their claim that they would have followed guidance is exposed as a hypothetical lie. Exposing hypothetical human excuses, people often claim they would obey guidance under different circumstances while ignoring guidance today. Fulfilling parallel Quranic observations: 'If a warner came to them, they would be more guided than any other nation; but when a warner came, it only increased them in aversion' (35:42).

**The Prophetic Mission as the Ultimate Demonstration of Divine Compassion**

The sending of Prophet Muḥammad and the revelation of the Quran represent the highest expression of divine mercy (*Raḥmatan li-l-ʿĀlamīn*, 21:107). God did not leave humanity to wander blindly in moral confusion or suffer sudden destruction. By sending a human messenger who walked among people, spoke their language, modeled sacred law, and delivered clear scriptures, God provided complete, accessible, living guidance. Defining prophetic sending as supreme mercy, messengers provide living, practical roadmaps for human flourishing. Bridging celestial truth and human life, a human prophet models how to implement divine commands in daily marriage, business, and governance. Eliminating ambiguity in moral law, revelation clarifies exact boundaries about justice, worship, family ethics, and human rights. Demonstrating divine patience, God repeatedly sends prophets and revivers (*mujaddidīn*) to awaken heedless societies. Exalting the finality of Prophet Muḥammad, his universal message preserves complete guidance for all humanity until Judgment Day. Inspiring deep gratitude, believers recognize that receiving Quranic guidance is the greatest blessing granted to their lives. Fulfilling the duty of daʿwah, Muslims must convey Quranic guidance compassionately to humanity, leaving no one without warning.

## Verse 20:135

> Say ˹to them, O Prophet˺, “Each ˹of us˺ is waiting, so keep waiting! You will soon know who is on the Straight Path and is ˹rightly˺ guided.”

**The Final Prophetic Proclamation and Last-Day Waiting**

Sūrah Ṭā-Hā concludes with a majestic, dignified prophetic proclamation to all deniers and humanity: *qul kullun mutarabbbiṣun fa-tarabbaṣū fa-sa-taʿlamūna man aṣḥābu al-ṣirāṭi al-sawiyyi wa-mani ihtadā* ('Say O Prophet: Each of us is waiting, so wait! You will come to know who are the followers of the even path and who are guided'). God commands Prophet Muḥammad to address his opponents with calm, unshakeable confidence. Both the believers and the deniers are waiting (*kullun mutarabbbiṣ*) for time to unfold and divine decrees to manifest. The deniers wait for the Prophet's movement to fail, while the Prophet waits for divine victory in this world and ultimate vindication in the Hereafter. Soon, all humanity will know with absolute certainty who walked the straight, balanced path (*al-ṣirāṭ al-sawiyy*) and achieved authentic guidance (*mani ihtadā*). Proclaiming prophetic confidence (*qul kullun mutarabbbiṣ*), verse 135 ends debate with serene, dignified trust in divine outcome. Acknowledging cosmic waiting (*fa-tarabbaṣū*), both truth and falsehood await the inescapable manifestation of divine judgment. Asserting absolute ultimate clarity (*fa-sa-taʿlamūn*), Resurrection Day and historical resolution will expose truth with undeniable certainty. Defining the straight path as *al-ṣirāṭ al-sawiyy* ('the even / balanced path'), authentic monotheism provides perfect moral and intellectual balance. Identifying true guidance (*mani ihtadā*), ultimate success belongs exclusively to those who accepted divine revelation and lived righteously. Concluding Sūrah Ṭā-Hā with majestic symmetry, the sūrah that began with comforting the Prophet (20:2-3) ends with fortifying his absolute victory. Guiding all believers across time, verse 135 imparts serene emotional resilience when facing ideological hostility and worldly opposition.

**The Ultimate Resolution on Judgment Day (Fa-Sa-Taʿlamūn)**

The divine declaration *fa-sa-taʿlamūn* ('you will come to know!') carries a solemn, unyielding weight. In earthly life, deniers indulge in philosophical debates, arrogant mocking, and media propaganda, creating an illusion that truth is subjective or unknowable. Judgment Day shatters all noise, revealing Reality with absolute, undeniable clarity. When the veil is lifted, every human being will know instantly who was guided and who was ruined. Asserting ultimate cognitive clarity (*fa-sa-taʿlamūn*), Resurrection Day ends all ideological debates and worldly propaganda. Lifting the earthly veil (*kashafnā ʿanka ghiṭā'aka*, 50:22), every soul perceives cosmic reality with absolute, undeniable sharpness. Vindicating the prophets and believers, those who were mocked on earth stand honored, radiant, and victorious before God. Confirming the tragedy of deniers, those who boasted of worldly wisdom realize too late that they walked in total blindness. Transforming modern skepticism, awareness of *fa-sa-taʿlamūn* drives wise individuals to seek truth before death closes the trial.
