# Sūrah ad-Dukhān (Chapter 44) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Architecture and Movement of the Discourse**

The structural movement of Sūrah ad-Dukhān unfolds with flawless dramatic and religious cohesion across three major movements:.

The opening movement (vv. 1–16) establishes the celestial origin of scripture, swearing by the *clear Book* sent down upon a *blessed night* in which every matter of wisdom is ordained by divine command. The text diagnoses the root pathology of pagan disbelief: not an absence of intellectual proof, but a frivolous addiction to worldly amusement and skepticism (*fī shakkin yalʿabūn*). The discourse immediately confronts this levity with the looming threat of the suffocating smoke, documenting how the proud aristocrats plead for relief only to revert shameless to their disbelief once prosperity returns. Their treachery sets the stage for the ultimate decree: the promise of *the fiercest blow* (*al-baṭshah al-kubrā*), when divine justice will extract full retribution.

The second movement (vv. 17–39) anchors this cosmic warning in the living mirror of sacred history. The text introduces the confrontation between Mūsā and the imperial regime of Pharaoh, depicting the clash between divine authority (*sulṭān mubīn*) and totalitarian hubris (*ʿulūw*).

The third movement (vv. 40–59) brings the entire cosmic argument to its decisive culmination on the *Day of Decision* (*Yawm al-Faṣl*). The text unveils the horrifying penal diet of the damned: the infernal tree of Zaqqūm, whose boiling fruit surges like molten metal within the bellies, accompanied by the violent dragging of the sinner into the midmost furnace and the sarcastic mockery of his worldly pretensions (*“Taste this! You mighty, noble one!”*). The sūrah concludes by returning to the clear Qur'an facilitated in the Arabic tongue, instructing the Prophet to maintain a posture of serene vigilance: *“Wait then! They too are certainly waiting.”*

## Verse 44:1

> Ḥâ-Mĩm

**The Fifth of the Crowned Openings**

The letters *ḥāʾ* and *mīm* open an unbroken sequence of seven consecutive sūrahs, from Sūrah Ghāfir (chapter 40) through Sūrah al-Jāthiyah (chapter 45), known throughout the commentary tradition as the *Ḥawāmīm*. While other groups of sūrahs open with disjointed letters such as *alif-lām-mīm* or *alif-lām-rā*, the *Ḥawāmīm* share an immediate, unified orientation: every single one of them follows the initial letters with an explicit oath upon, or direct celebration of, the divine descent of scripture (*tanzīl al-Kitāb*).

Ibn Masʿūd famously remarked that the *Ḥawāmīm* are the splendid adornment or silk brocade of the Qur'an (*dībājat al-Qurʾān*). They were revealed in Makkah during intense ideological friction between the Prophet ﷺ and the Quraysh leadership, functioning as decisive proclamations of divine unicity, prophetic truth, and impending accountability.

**Exegetical Interpretations of the Letters Ḥā and Mīm**

Beyond the general discourse surrounding the disjointed letters, early authorities recorded specific symbolic and linguistic readings for the pair *ḥāʾ-mīm*. Ibn ʿAbbās reported in one narration that *ḥāʾ-mīm* stands as one of the greatest names of God (*ismun min asmāʾillāh al-aʿẓam*), broken into component parts across these seven chapters, just as *al-Raḥmān* is hinted at through the distributed letters of various openings. Other early commentators, such as al-Ḍaḥḥāk and ʿIkrimah, suggested that each letter represents divine attributes: the *ḥāʾ* means His life (*ḥayātuhu*), His forbearance (*ḥilmuhu*), or His praise (*ḥamduhu*), while the *mīm* means His dominion (*mulkuhu*), His majesty (*majduhu*), or His grace (*mannuhu*).

A further interpretative tradition preserved by al-Ṭabarī links the letters to divine judgment and decree, suggesting that they swear by the divine decree that the matter is settled (*ḥumma al-amr*). This reading carries particular weight in Sūrah ad-Dukhān, which proceeds directly to discuss the night in which *“every matter of wisdom is ordained”* (44:4). The phonetic combination itself so hints at the sovereign authority that governs both the sending of scripture and the execution of cosmic decrees, establishing an immediate conceptual bridge between the opening utterance and the thematic core of the sūrah.

## Verse 44:2

> By the clear Book

**The Divine Oath by Scripture**

The sūrah opens its real address with an oath: *wa-l-Kitābi l-mubīn* — By the clear Book! In the linguistic architecture of the Qur'an, when the Creator swears by an entity, that oath serves to magnify the sworn object and elevate it before human consideration. Here, the oath is sworn not by the stars, the sun, or the dawn, but by the revealed Book itself. By placing this oath immediately after the disjointed letters *Ḥâ-Mĩm*, the text establishes the absolute primacy of scripture as the definitive criterion for human guidance and cosmic reality.

The grammatical construct uses the oath particle *wāw*, which requires a subsequent complement (*jawāb al-qasam*).

**The Contrast with Obscurity and Superstition**

Against that suffocating fog of deliberate ambiguity, the Qur'an arrives with radiant transparency. It appeals openly to human reason, conscience, and historical observation. It presents its arguments in plain daylight and invites critical scrutiny: *“Do they not then reflect upon the Quran?”* (4:82). The clarity of the Book is an emancipatory force, releasing the human mind from hereditary fear and irrational subjugation into the lucid air of divine truth.

## Verse 44:3

> Indeed, We sent it down on a blessed night, for We always warn ˹against evil˺

**The Blessed Night of Scripture's Descent**

The verse provides the response to the opening oath (*jawāb al-qasam*): *innā anzalnāhu fī laylatin mubārakah* — Indeed, We sent it down on a blessed night. The pronoun *it* (*hu*) refers directly to the clear Book sworn by in the preceding verse, demonstrating an unbroken thematic unity. The term *mubārakah* derives from *barakah*, meaning abundant, multiplying, and enduring goodness bestowed by divine grace. The night is sanctified because within it the ultimate vehicle of human guidance crossed the threshold from the celestial realm into the terrestrial sphere.

The vast majority of classical commentators, including Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, and Ibn Kathīr, identify this blessed night as *Laylat al-Qadr* (the Night of Power or Decree), situated within the sacred month of Ramadan. This identification is confirmed by comparing this verse with explicit Qur'anic declarations elsewhere: *“Indeed, We sent this ˹Quran˺ down on the Night of Glory”* (97:1), and *“Ramadan is the month in which the Quran was revealed”* (2:185). The convergence of these texts forms an airtight interpretive proof that the initial, total descent of revelation occurred during this singular nocturnal window.

**The Alternative Exegetical Opinion Regarding Shaʿbān**

A minority interpretation, famously reported from the early Successor ʿIkrimah and recorded by al-Ṭabarī and al-Qurṭubī, suggested that the blessed night mentioned here is the fifteenth night of Shaʿbān (*laylat al-niṣf min Shaʿbān*). Proponents of this view argued that divine decrees are formulated or demarcated on the middle night of Shaʿbān and later handed over to the angelic emissaries on the Night of Power in Ramadan.

However, the major interpretive tradition firmly rejected this attribution as contradictory to the definitive text of Sūrah al-Baqarah (2:185). Ibn Kathīr strictly noted that whoever designates this night as middle Shaʿbān has departed from the clear scriptural evidence, for the Qur'an itself states that its revelation took place in Ramadan. The consensus remains established: the blessed night of Sūrah ad-Dukhān is identical to the Night of Power celebrated in Sūrah al-Qadr, a night of unsurpassed spiritual luminosity and heavenly descent.

**The Mechanics of Divine Descent**

From that celestial station, the revelation was later communicated by the Angel Jibrīl to the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ in stages over twenty-three years, responding to unfolding historical events, communal questions, and spiritual milestones. This dual-stage descent stresses the transcendent perfection of scripture: it was completely established in the heavenly world before being woven into the fabric of human history to guide, heal, and transform human society.

## Verse 44:4

> On that night every matter of wisdom is ordained

**What Is Sent Down on That Night**

The discourse moves from the sanctity of the blessed night to the momentous celestial operations executed within it: *fīhā yufraqu kullu amrin ḥakīm* — On that night every matter of wisdom is ordained. The passive verb *yufraqu* means separation, discrimination, and definitive detailing. From the comprehensive divine decree recorded eternally with God, the specific events, measures, and provisions for the coming year are differentiated, apportioned, and delivered into the hands of the executive angels.

**Characteristics of the Wise Command**

The adjective *ḥakīm* qualifying *amr* means an affair characterized by absolute wisdom, flawless execution, and definitive finality. It carries the dual meaning of being inherently wise (*dhū ḥikmah*) and firmly sealed or non-negotiable (*muḥkam*). Nothing ordained on this night is subject to arbitrary fluctuation, whimsical amendment, or human obstruction. Every decree reflects the infinite knowledge of the Creator, balancing cosmic justice, mercy, and purpose with absolute precision.

**Delegation to the Angelic Hosts**

The apportionment described by *yufraqu* is intimately connected with the angelic missions detailed in Sūrah al-Qadr: *“The angels and the Spirit descend in it by permission of their Lord for every matter”* (97:4). Classical commentaries, including those of al-Baghawī and Ibn Kathīr, record that the archangels Jibrīl, Mīkā'īl, Isrāfīl, and ʿIzrā'īl receive their respective portfolios of divine execution during this night.

## Verse 44:5

> by a command from Us, for We have always sent ˹messengers˺

**The Ultimate Source of the Decree**

The ordaining of every wise matter and the descent of revelation are anchored to their singular, supreme origin: *amran min ʿindinā* — by a command from Us. The accusative noun *amran* functions grammatically either as a circumstantial qualifier (*ḥāl*) making clear the nature of the descent, or as an absolute object (*mafʿūl muṭlaq*) emphasizing the direct, authoritative source of all cosmic governance. By prefixing the phrase with *min ʿindinā* (from Our very presence), the text forcefully repudiates any notion of secondary celestial autonomy or earthly intervention.

**The Historical Reality of Divine Sending**

The second half of the verse introduces another enduring divine practice: *innā kunnā mursilīn* — for We have always sent ˹messengers˺. Just as the preceding verse emphasized that God continuously warns (*kunnā mundhirīn*), here the text stresses that He continuously sends emissaries to creation. The mission of the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ is so framed not as an isolated, erratic occurrence, but as the natural continuation of an ancient and consistent prophetic tradition.

Throughout human generations, whenever humanity wandered into moral decay and religious confusion, the divine response was the dispatching of messengers bearing guidance, evidence, and scripture. From Nūḥ, Ibrāhīm, and Mūsā to ʿĪsā and Muḥammad, peace be upon them all, the transmission of prophetic light has been a constant feature of human existence. The rejection faced by the Prophet ﷺ from his contemporaries was therefore an assault against an established divine institution that had governed human history since its inception. Prophecy is the perennial lifeline connecting earth with heavenly instruction.

## Verse 44:6

> as a mercy from your Lord. He ˹alone˺ is truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing—

**A Mercy From Your Lord**
The phrase the verse uses is "a mercy from your Lord", and it explains why God sends prophets and books. The Book does not describe the sending as a display of power or as a burden laid on people; it describes it as mercy, which is what a person receives when the danger around him is named before it catches him.

**What the Mercy Is For**
The Book says the same thing about the Prophet in another sūrah, where it tells him that he was not sent except as a mercy to the whole world (21:107 states it). The mercy is not a vague feeling; it has content. It tells people what to believe about the One who made them, it tells them how to live with one another, and it warns them about the account that follows. A society without that guidance drifts toward worshipping whatever gives it advantage and toward treating the weak as material, and the Book names the results plainly.

**Why the Verse Says "Your Lord"**
The verse uses the name that describes God as the one who brings things up and cares for them, and it attaches it to the Prophet personally. That pairing runs through the sūrahs revealed in Makkah, where the Prophet was being mocked and the community was small. The Lord who made the heavens and the earth is not a distant power in the verse; He is the one who raised the Prophet, trained him and stands behind what he carries. The wording came to a man who had lost his wife and his uncle in the same period and was being pressured to give up.

**What the Verse Tells the Reader**
The verse tells the reader that the warning he is reading is not aimed at him from a distance. The sūrah has been describing the night on which the Book was sent down, the army that drowned, and the gardens left behind by the men who thought they could not be touched. The verse in front of the reader puts the reason for all of it in two words, and the reason is the reader's own good. The sūrah goes on to tell him to watch for the day when the sky brings a visible smoke, and the same mercy is what gives him time to prepare before it.

## Verse 44:7

> the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, if only you had sure faith

**Cosmic Sovereignty Defined Across All Realms**

The verse elaborates upon the identity of the Lord whose mercy was proclaimed in the previous verse: *rabbi s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa mā baynahumā* — the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between. The grammatical reading follows either the genitive as an appositive (*badal*) to *your Lord*, or the nominative as an independent predicate, both emphasizing that the God who sent Muḥammad ﷺ is the absolute Architect and Sustainer of the cosmos. By encompassing the celestial heights, the terrestrial plane, and the intermediary atmosphere, the phrase covers the totality of physical existence.

**Conditional Summons to Genuine Conviction**

The second clause introduces a profound epistemic challenge: *in kuntum mūqinīn* — if only you had sure faith, or if you are people of conviction. The particle *in* conditions the recognition of divine lordship upon genuine certainty (*īqān*). Certainty in Qur'anic account of knowledge is not vague sentiment, inherited assumption, or verbal lip-service; it is verified conviction grounded in sound intellect, careful reflection, and spiritual lucidity.

## Verse 44:8

> There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. He ˹alone˺ gives life and causes death. ˹He is˺ your Lord, and the Lord of your forefathers

**Absolute Declaration of Divine Exclusivity**

The discourse culminates in the foundational creed of Islam: *lā ilāha illā huwa* — There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. This universal negation (*lā nāfiyah li-l-jins*) dismantles every false deity, imagined partner, and manufactured idol, while the subsequent exception (*illā*) affirms unconditioned, exclusive divinity to the Creator alone. Following the cosmic demonstration in the previous verse, this proclamation stands not as a dogmatic assertion, but as the inevitable logical conclusion of cosmic reality. If He alone created and sustains the heavens and earth, He alone has the right to be worshipped, obeyed, and loved.

**Sovereign Powers of Life and Death**

These twin verbs encompass the entire drama of earthly existence. In the Qur'an, the power to bestow life and inflict death is frequently presented as the decisive proof of God's unshared divinity, notably in the debate between the Prophet Ibrāhīm and the haughty monarch: *“My Lord is the One Who gives life and causes death”* (2:258). By invoking these inescapable realities, the verse confronts human fragility. The proudest tyrant who walks the earth was granted life by divine decree and will be stripped of that life by the same sovereign will at the destined hour.

**Transgenerational Scope of Divine Lordship**

The verse extends its address across the generations: *rabbukum wa rabbu ābāʾikumu l-awwalīn* — ˹He is˺ your Lord, and the Lord of your forefathers. This phrase directly targeted the central psychological defense mechanism of the Makkan pagans. Whenever the Quraysh were challenged to abandon idolatry, their standard retort was an appeal to ancestral tradition: *“Rather, we will follow what we found our forefathers doing”* (2:170). They treated the religion of their ancestors as an inviolable sacred authority.

## Verse 44:9

> In fact, they are in doubt, amusing themselves

Having established the majestic proofs of divine unity, cosmic creation, and transgenerational lordship in verses 6 through 8, the discourse pivots with dramatic abruptness to expose the moral condition of the deniers: *bal hum fī shakkin yalʿabūn* — In fact, they are in doubt, amusing themselves. The digressive particle *bal* (nay, rather, in fact) indicates an epistemic rupture (*iḍrāb*). It sweeps aside their pretentious philosophical objections and exposes the real psychological reality beneath their disbelief: their denial does not stem from an absence of evidence, but from willful skepticism married to frivolous hedonism.

**Tragic Trivialization of Human Existence**

In the broader moral vision of the Qur'an, human life is an arena of supreme consequence, framed by divine trust (*amānah*) and moral agency. To reduce this sacred opportunity to mere play (*laʿib*) is to squander the soul's divine inheritance. The deniers treated the warnings of eternity with the same superficial flippancy they brought to marketplace amusements and evening banter around the Kaʿbah.

## Verse 44:10

> Wait then ˹O Prophet˺ for the day ˹when˺ the sky will be veiled in haze, clearly visible

The discourse shifts from exposing pagan frivolity to an imperative of resolute patience directed to the Prophet ﷺ: *fartaqib yawma taʾtī s-samāʾu bi-dukhānin mubīn* — Wait then ˹O Prophet˺ for the day ˹when˺ the sky will be veiled in haze, clearly visible. The verb *irtaqib*, derived from the root *r-q-b*, means vigilant observation, attentive waiting, and steady anticipation from a position of moral certitude. The Prophet is instructed to cease agonizing over their stubborn mockery and instead watch for the unfolding of divine decree.

**Historical Understanding: The Makkan Drought**

Classical scholarship preserved two major interpretations about the nature and timing of this manifest smoke (*dukhān mubīn*). The first opinion, championed by the eminent Companion ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd and recorded in both *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (4774) and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2798), maintains that this verse describes a historical event that befell the Quraysh during the Prophet's Makkan ministry.

According to Ibn Masʿūd, when the Quraysh persisted in their persecution and rejected every prophetic appeal, the Prophet ﷺ supplicated against them, saying: *“O Allah, assist me against them with seven years like the seven years of Yūsuf.”* Consequently, severe drought and famine gripped Makkah. Food supplies were exhausted to such an extent that people ate carrion, leather, and bones. Due to intense hunger and extreme physical distress, when a person looked toward the sky, they saw an optical haze resembling thick smoke obscuring the firmament. In this historical reading, the smoke was a severe earthly chastisement designed to break pagan obstinacy.

**Last-Day Understanding: An End-Time Sign**

The alternative interpretation, held by prominent Companions including ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, Ibn ʿAbbās. Abū Hurayrah, and supported by later commentators such as al-Qurṭubī and Ibn Kathīr, asserts that the smoke mentioned here is an actual, physical smoke that will engulf the entire planet as one of the major signs preceding the Day of Resurrection. This view is corroborated by prophetic traditions enumerating the ten major signs of the Hour, among which the smoke (*al-dukhān*) is explicitly listed alongside the Dajjāl, the Beast, and the rising of the sun from the west (recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 2901 from Ḥudhayfah ibn Asīd).

Proponents of this Last-Day reading argue that the literal wording of the verse—specifying that the sky itself brings forth visible smoke—demands a literal, physical manifestation rather than a metaphorical hallucination caused by famine. According to this tradition, this cosmic smoke will envelop the globe for forty days: it will cause the believer to experience merely a mild nasal congestion resembling a common cold, whereas it will penetrate the ears, nostrils, and pores of the disbeliever, causing excruciating torment and distress.

**Semantic Weight of the Clear Adjective Mubīn**

Regardless of whether the smoke is understood as a historical drought or an apocalyptic sign, the text qualifies it with the adjective *mubīn* (manifest, clear, unmistakable). This word mirrors the description of the Book in verse 2 (*al-Kitābi l-mubīn*), establishing a brilliant literary and religious symmetry. The deniers who refused to acknowledge the clear Book will be compelled to face a clear punishment.

## Verse 44:11

> overwhelming the people. ˹They will cry,˺ “This is a painful torment

**The Enveloping Scope of the Affliction**

The vision of the smoke started in the preceding verse unfolds into its suffocating effect upon the populace: *yaghshā n-nās hādhā ʿadhābun alīm* — overwhelming the people. ˹They will cry,˺ “This is a painful torment.” The active participle *yaghshā*, derived from the root *gh-sh-y*, conveys the complete covering, engulfing, and blanket inundation of an object from every direction. The affliction does not strike isolated enclaves or select classes; it descends like an inescapable atmospheric shroud over the entire collective (*an-nās*), cutting off breathable air, light, and orientation across the entire territory.

**Voices of Desperation and Concession**

Alternatively, Ibn Kathīr and others suggest that these words represent the solemn pronouncement of the angels or the voice of revelation rebuking them as the punishment descends, echoing the cosmic declaration in Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr: *“This is the Fire which you used to deny!”* (52:14). In either interpretation, the function of the utterance remains identical: it is the total obliteration of skepticism. In the presence of acute suffering, philosophical games and frivolous doubts evaporate, replaced by the stark, undeniable reality of divine retribution that leaves no room for debate.

**Psychological Collapse Preceding Supplication**

The confession *“This is a painful torment”* marks the psychological turning point in the sūrah's dramatic narrative. It serves as the immediate emotional catalyst for the frantic plea recorded in the following verse. Arrogance cannot sustain its posture under persistent, overwhelming pain; the human creature eventually breaks and acknowledges its fundamental frailty.

This collapse reveals the tragic tragedy of human obstinacy. The deniers were invited to embrace truth through gentle logic, sublime literary beauty, and moral elevation; yet they chose to ignore those noble invitations. Consequently, they are brought to truth through the humiliating language of physical suffering. The verse captures that precise threshold where proud laughter dies upon pagan lips, giving way to the terrified realization that the God of Muḥammad ﷺ is an absolute, unavoidable sovereign.

## Verse 44:12

> Our Lord! Remove ˹this˺ torment from us, ˹and˺ we will certainly believe.”

**The Cry of Crisis: Invoking Divine Lordship**

In the grip of suffocating affliction, the deniers cry out in desperate supplication: *rabbanā kshif ʿannā l-ʿadhāba innā muʾminūn* — “Our Lord! Remove ˹this˺ torment from us, ˹and˺ we will certainly believe” (44:12). The immediate psychological revelation of this plea is found in their opening address: *rabbanā* (Our Lord!). In their ordinary hours of health, commercial prosperity, and tribal assemblies, these same individuals vociferously denied the undivided Lordship of God, stubbornly insisting upon the efficacy of their stone idols and ancestral intermediaries.

**Conditional Bargaining in the Presence of Agony**

The plea contains a formal covenant: **(44:12)** The grammatical structure pairs an imperative petition (*ikshif*) with an emphatic nominal sentence (*innā muʾminūn*), indicating a solemn pledge of future obedience. They promise that if the agony is lifted, their stubborn skepticism will instantly transform into faithful submission to the Prophet's message.

This pattern of crisis bargaining is a recurring motif in the Qur'anic diagnosis of human hypocrisy. In Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, Pharaoh and his court make an identical desperate pledge to Mūsā whenever a plague strikes Egypt: *“O Moses! Pray to your Lord on our behalf by virtue of the covenant He made with you. If you remove this plague from us, we will certainly believe in you and let the Children of Israel go with you”* (7:134). The psychology of the Makkan deniers mirrors precisely that of ancient pharaonic tyranny: they treat faith not as an inward spiritual devotion, but as a desperate transaction traded for physical relief.

**Historical Context of the Makkan Delegation**

Classical historians and commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, anchor this verse within the dramatic historical encounter between the Quraysh leadership and the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ during the great Makkan drought. When the famine reached its zenith and corpses were consumed, prominent chieftains—most notably Abū Sufyān ibn Ḥarb—came to the Prophet in humiliation.

**The Nature of Opportunistic Repentance**

The sincerity of repentance under duress has long occupied Islamic theologians and moral philosophers. Faith born purely out of the desire to escape physical torture is inherently suspect, because it is compelled by external terror rather than free intellectual and spiritual assent. The Qur'an repeatedly warns that when the penalty becomes visible and inescapable, declarations of belief lose their moral value: *“When they saw Our punishment, they cried, ‘We believe in Allah alone and reject what we used to associate with Him!’ But their faith could not benefit them once they had seen Our punishment”* (40:84–85).

## Verse 44:13

> How can they be reminded when a messenger has already come to them, making things clear

**The Historical Reality of the Clear Messenger**

The verse stresses the tragic absurdity of their condition by highlighting what had already been granted to them: *wa qad jāʾahum rasūlun mubīn* — a messenger has already come to them, making things clear. The particle *qad* establishes historical finality and certainty. The Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ did not appear as an obscure stranger from an alien civilization, nor did he bring ambiguous, convoluted doctrines. He was one of their own, whose entire life had been lived in their midst with unimpeachable integrity.

**The Clarity That Leaves No Excuse**

The adjective *mubīn* modifying *messenger* mirrors its usage in verse 2 with the Book. Just as the revelation has absolute clarity, the Messenger who conveys it embodies complete moral transparency. His life was an open book, free from duplicity, political maneuvering, or selfish accumulation.

When such a messenger stands before a people, their rejection cannot be excused by claims of confusion or lack of clarity. The light shone with total brilliance; it was the human eye that deliberately shut itself to avoid the illumination. Verse 13 highlights this terrible moral responsibility, establishing that divine judgment falls justly upon those who knowingly turn away from the clear light of truth.

## Verse 44:14

> then they turned away from him, saying, “A madman, taught by others!”

**The Slander of Being Taught by Mortals**

To justify their rejection, the Quraysh leadership fabricated contradictory slanders against the Prophet's character, beginning with the accusation: *muʿallam* (taught by others). They alleged that the sublime verses recited by the Prophet were not divine revelation, but information secretly dictated to him by foreign slaves, Christian blacksmiths, or Jewish scholars residing in Makkah. The Qur'an records and dismantles this specific slander elsewhere: *“And We certainly know that they say, ‘It is only a human being who teaches him.’ The tongue of the one they refer to is foreign, while this ˹Quran˺ is in clear Arabic speech”* (16:103).

**The Slander of Possession and Madness**

Simultaneously, they paired the accusation of human instruction with a radically contradictory charge: *majnūn* (had by jinn, insane, or mad). In the psychological landscape of pre-Islamic Arabia, madness was believed to be caused by demonic possession (*jinn*), leading to erratic behavior, incoherent babbling, and loss of rational control. By labeling the Prophet ﷺ as *majnūn*, the pagan elite tried to marginalize him as an unstable eccentric whose words carried no moral or legal weight.

This accusation was hurled at nearly every major prophet throughout sacred history, as noted in Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt: *“Similarly, no messenger came to those before them but they said, ‘A magician or a madman!’”* (51:52). It is the classic weapon employed by entrenched oligarchies against moral reformers. When a prophet challenges systemic idolatry, economic exploitation, and ancestral arrogance, the beneficiaries of that corrupt order instinctively dismiss the reformer as insane, attempting to pathologize moral righteousness and preserve their privileged status quo.

**The Moral Malice of Defamation**

The tragedy of their slander was intensified by the fact that the accusers knew their claims were completely false. The pagan chieftains—such as al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, Abū Jahl, and al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith—had known the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ since childhood. They knew his spotless character, his serene wisdom, and his flawless mental stability. In private councils, when al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah evaluated the Prophet's speech, he admitted: *“By God, it possesses a sweetness, and upon it is an elegance; its top is fruitful and its base is abundant; it surpasses all and cannot be surpassed.”*

## Verse 44:15

> Indeed, We will remove ˹that˺ torment for a while, and you ˹Meccans˺ will return ˹to disbelief˺

**The Omniscient Prediction of Relapse**

The second clause delivers a devastating, omniscient prophecy: *innakum ʿāʾidūn* — you will return ˹to disbelief˺. The use of the active participle *ʿāʾidūn* functions in Arabic grammar to indicate an established future certainty. The Lord of the worlds, Whose knowledge encompasses the unseen and the secrets of human hearts, declares that their pledge of faith (*innā muʾminūn*) was an illusion. The moment their bellies are filled and the sky clears, the panic that produced their pious vows will evaporate, and they will revert precisely to their entrenched idolatry, mockery, and persecution.

**The Theological Doctrine of Istidrāj**

This verse provides a vivid illustration of the Qur'anic concept of *istidrāj*—the gradual leading of unrepentant sinners toward their ultimate reckoning through the granting of worldly prosperity and reprieve. When a community experiences divine deliverance from a crisis yet responds with increased arrogance, that relief ceases to be a blessing; it transforms into a spiritual trap. In Sūrah al-Anʿām, the Qur'an expresses this terrifying psychological dynamic: *“So when they forgot that by which they had been reminded, We opened to them the doors of every ˹good˺ thing until, when they rejoiced in that which they were given, We seized them suddenly, and they were plunged into despair”* (6:44).

**Alternative Exegetical Readings of the Reversion**

In this reading, the verse echoes the terrifying reality of the damned described elsewhere: whenever they try to crawl out of the torment of the Fire, they are returned into it (*uʿīdū fīhā*), as stated in Sūrah al-Ḥajj (22:22) and Sūrah as-Sajdah (32:20). While this Last-Day dimension enriches the thematic background, the primary contextual meaning remains focused upon the immediate moral failure of the Makkans: they asked for relief, were granted relief by divine grace, and repaid that grace with shameless treachery.

---

## Verse 44:16

> ˹Then˺ on the Day We will deal ˹you˺ the fiercest blow, We will surely inflict punishment

The discourse reaches an ominous climax in the warning directed to the unrepentant deniers: *yawma nabṭishu l-baṭshata l-kubrā innā muntaqimūn* — ˹Then˺ on the Day We will deal ˹you˺ the fiercest blow, We will surely inflict punishment. The root *b-ṭ-sh* in Arabic means seizing with immense physical force, unyielding dominance, and irresistible violence. When amplified by the cognate absolute object (*al-baṭshata*) and the superlative feminine adjective (*al-kubrā*), it means a seizure of catastrophic, unmatched magnitude that brooks no resistance.

**Classical Divergence: The Day of Badr**

The commentators divided into two major interpretations about the historical realization of this *fiercest blow*. The first position, famously championed by ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, and Ibn ʿAbbās in one transmission, identifies this blow with the Battle of Badr in the second year of the Hijrah. This view is recorded in both *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (4774) and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2798).

**The Last-Day Dimension: The Day of Resurrection**

A second major interpretative tradition, maintained by al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, ʿIkrimah, and favored by later commentators including Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, asserts that the *fiercest blow* refers definitively to the Day of Resurrection. Proponents of this view argue that the absolute superlative *al-kubrā* (the greatest or fiercest) can only truly apply to the cosmic convulsions of the final Hour and the terrifying descent of the Hellfire, compared to which any worldly defeat—even Badr—is relatively minor.

In this Last-Day reading, while Badr was an undeniable manifestation of divine judgment against specific tyrants, the ultimate realization of the *fiercest blow* remains reserved for the day when all creation is assembled before the Almighty. On that Day, the heavens are rolled up, the earth is crushed, and the deniers are seized without any possibility of ransom or escape. The two interpretations need not be mutually exclusive; Badr served as an immediate historical foretaste of that supreme cosmic seizure which will encompass all transgressors on the Day of Judgment.

---

## Verse 44:17

> Indeed, before them We tested Pharaoh’s people: a noble messenger came to them

**The Historical Pivot to Sacred Precedent**

Having delivered the decisive warning of the fiercest blow to the Quraysh, the sūrah pivots across time and space to start a historical pattern: *wa-laqad fatannā qablahum qawma firʿawn* — Indeed, before them We tested Pharaoh’s people. The opening particles *wa-laqad* combine the conjunction, the emphatic oath particle *lām*, and the particle of certainty *qad*, alerting the listener to an undeniable historical fact. The narrative turns away from contemporary Makkah toward ancient imperial Egypt, confronting the Makkan deniers with the fate of an empire far mightier than their own.

**Parallels with the Makkan Crisis**

The introduction of the Pharaonic narrative was tailor-made for the psychological reality of early Islamic Makkah. The parallels between the two historical moments are deliberate and striking. In ancient Egypt, an entrenched, arrogant elite dominated and enslaved a vulnerable population, mocking an authentic prophet who called them to monotheism and justice. In seventh-century Makkah, the pagan Quraysh oligarchy similarly oppressed the poor and enslaved Muslims, heaping abuse upon the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ.

By calling Mūsā a *noble messenger*, the verse simultaneously affirms the nobility of the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ. The Makkans dismissed Muḥammad as a social disruption and an impoverished orphan; the Qur'an counters by reminding them that worldly tyrants have always looked down upon prophets with contempt. The nobility of a messenger does not derive from the approval of corrupt oligarchs, but from the divine commission that elevates him above the kings of the earth.

---

## Verse 44:18

> ˹proclaiming,˺ “Hand over the servants of Allah to me. I am truly a trustworthy messenger to you

**The Radical Demand for Liberation**

The noble messenger initiates his mission with a direct, uncompromising demand to the Egyptian tyrant: *an addū ilayya ʿibādallāh* — ˹proclaiming,˺ “Hand over the servants of Allah to me” (44:18). The imperative verb *addū*, derived from the root *ʾ-d-y*, means paying a due, rendering back a deposit, or delivering an entrusted right to its rightful recipient. Grammatically, *ʿibādallāh* (the servants of Allah) represents the object of the demand, referring to the oppressed Children of Israel who were languishing under brutal state-sponsored slavery.

**The Declaration of Incorruptible Trustworthiness**

This declaration is a standard formula used by the great prophets throughout the Qur'anic corpus. In Sūrah ash-Shuʿarā, Nūḥ, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lūṭ, and Shuʿayb all address their hostile communities with the identical statement: *“Indeed, I am to you a trustworthy messenger”* (26:107, 125, 143, 162, 178). By claiming the title of *amīn*, Mūsā assures Pharaoh's court that he does not fabricate revelations, exaggerate commands, or seek material gain. He is an authentic ambassador of heaven whose words must be received with total confidence.

**Echoes in the Makkan Setting**

The resonance of the title *al-Amīn* in the ears of the seventh-century Makkan audience was electrifying. Long before he received the first verses of revelation on the Mountain of Light, the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ was universally recognized by the Quraysh by this exact epithet: *al-Amīn* (the Trustworthy One). When the Kaʿbah was rebuilt and tribal war threatened to erupt over the placement of the Black Stone, the chieftains rejoiced when Muḥammad appeared, exclaiming: *“This is al-Amīn! We are satisfied with his judgment!”*

---

## Verse 44:19

> And do not be arrogant with Allah. I have certainly come to you with a compelling proof

**The Prohibition Against Transcendent Arrogance**

The prophetic warning escalates from a demand for justice to a direct prohibition against spiritual hubris: *wa-a lā taʿlū ʿalallāh* — And do not be arrogant with Allah, or do not exalt yourselves against God. The verb *taʿlū*, derived from the root *ʿ-l-w*, means rising high, exalting oneself, dominating with haughty pride, and displaying tyrannical arrogance. By appending the preposition *ʿalā* (against, upon) linked to the supreme name *Allāh*, the phrase identifies the most egregious form of arrogance known to creation: creaturely rebellion against the absolute Sovereign of existence.

This prohibition struck at the core of Pharaoh's pathological self-deification. In Egyptian imperial ideology, Pharaoh was not merely an earthly monarch; he was venerated as a living god, the physical embodiment of Horus and the son of Ra. He claimed ultimate legislative and of being supremacy over his subjects, famously boasting: *“I am your greatest lord!”* (79:24), and *“O chiefs! I know of no god for you other than myself”* (28:38). Mūsā’s command confronts this delusion with uncompromising boldness, commanding the crowned emperor to abandon his cosmic pretensions and submit to the true Lord of all things.

**Direct Admonition to the Makkan Elite**

The verse warned them that their tribal arrogance was identical to Pharaoh's imperial hubris, and that it would yield the exact same ruinous result. The Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ had similarly come to them with a *compelling proof* (*sulṭān mubīn*): the miraculous, inimitable Qur'an, whose literary majesty and spiritual power held them spellbound. By persisting in their defiance, the Quraysh were repeating the fatal mistake of ancient Egypt, challenging a divine authority that no mortal coalition could ever overcome.

---

## Verse 44:20

> And indeed, I seek refuge with my Lord and your Lord so you do not stone me ˹to death˺

**The Prophetic Sanctuary in Divine Protection**

Faced with the violent threats of the enraged tyrant, Mūsā proclaims his absolute spiritual sanctuary: *wa-innī ʿudhtu bi-rabbī wa-rabbikum a tarjumūn* — And indeed, I seek refuge with my Lord and your Lord so you do not stone me ˹to death˺. The verb *ʿudhtu*, derived from the root *ʿ-w-dh*, means fleeing from a terrifying danger to an unassailable stronghold, seeking sanctuary in an all-powerful protector where no enemy can reach. Mūsā does not appeal to a human tribal confederacy, an armed bodyguard, or a political alliance; he anchors his safety exclusively in the divine presence.

**Echoes in the Early Makkan Persecution**

For the early Muslims enduring intense persecution in Makkah, this verse provided immense psychological and spiritual fortitude. The Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ and his vulnerable followers—such as Khabbāb ibn al-Aratt, Bilāl ibn Rabāḥ, and Sumayyah bint Khayyāṭ—faced identical threats of physical torture and execution from the Quraysh elite. When the Prophet prayed at the Kaʿbah, Abū Jahl threatened to crush his neck with a massive stone.

In reciting the words of Mūsā, the Prophet ﷺ and his companions were taught how to navigate extreme hostility. They learned that the ultimate refuge is not found in compromise with oppressors or in yielding to fear, but in seeking sanctuary with the Lord of the heavens and earth. The assurance that God protects His messengers sustained the nascent Muslim community through their darkest hours of boycotts, assassinations, and exile.

---

## Verse 44:21

> ˹But˺ if you do not believe me, then let me be.”

**A Dignified Offer of Non-Aggression**

After delivering the divine proofs and declaring his sanctuary in God, Mūsā offers the Egyptian court a final, dignified alternative: *wa-in lam tuʾminū lī fa-ʿtazilūn* — ˹But˺ if you do not believe me, then let me be. The conditional particle *in* introduces their refusal as a recognized possibility, while the imperative *iʿtazilūn* (from *ʿ-z-l*) means stepping aside, withdrawing, keeping distance, and maintaining neutral disengagement. Mūsā essentially proposes a pact of peaceful coexistence: if your hearts are too hardened to accept faith, then at least refrain from violent interference and let me depart in peace.

**The Semantic Scope of Iʿtazilūn**

Linguists like al-Zamakhsharī and Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī noted a broader spiritual and political meaning: “If you will not join me in faith, then let there be a complete separation between our paths until God judges between us.” This echoes the fundamental Qur'anic principle of religious boundary expressed in Sūrah al-Kāfirūn: *“To you be your religion, and to me mine”* (109:6). It established that faith cannot be compelled, and that when dialogue reaches an impasse, the righteous community seeks only the freedom to worship God in peace.

---

## Verse 44:22

> Ultimately, he cried out to his Lord, “These are a wicked people!”

**The Final Cry of Exhausted Hope**

When every peaceful overture was violently rejected, the narrative reaches its decisive turning point: *fa-daʿā rabbahu anna hāʾulāʾi qawmun mujrimūn* — Ultimately, he cried out to his Lord, “These are a wicked people!” The connective conjunction *fa* indicates immediate succession following the failure of persuasion in the previous verse. The verb *daʿā* (he called upon, supplicated, cried out) means an intense, heartfelt appeal from the depths of the prophet's soul to his Sustainer.

**The Legal Form of Prophetic Testimony**

In the divine governance of human history, a destroyed nation is never annihilated without a formal prophetic testimony closing its case. The cry of the messenger functions as an official courtroom deposition before the Lord of the worlds. When the prophet testifies *these are a wicked people*, he effectively confirms that the message was delivered with absolute clarity (*mubīn*), and that the people’s rejection was an act of deliberate, incorrigible criminality.

This echoes the prayers of earlier prophets whose communities crossed the threshold of redemption. When Nūḥ realized that further centuries of preaching would yield only more corruption, he prayed: *“My Lord! Do not leave a single disbeliever upon the earth”* (71:26). Similarly, Mūsā prayed against Pharaoh’s regime in Sūrah Yūnus: *“Our Lord! Blot out their wealth and harden their hearts, so that they will not believe till they see the painful punishment”* (10:88). The cry of verse 22 is the moment the prophetic ledger closes and the execution of cosmic justice begins.

**Echoes in the Prophet’s Struggles in Makkah**

For the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ, who was reciting this verse to a small band of persecuted followers in Makkah, Mūsā’s cry carried profound personal resonance. The Quraysh were replicating Pharaoh’s exact patterns of crime: boycotting the Prophet’s family, starving vulnerable believers in the mountain passes, and plotting violence against the Messenger.

The lament attributed to the Prophet at the end of Sūrah az-Zukhruf—*“O my Lord, truly these are a people who believe not”* (43:88)—harmonizes directly with Mūsā’s cry in 44:22. The Prophet was reminded that when an arrogant society chooses persistent criminality, the messenger is not held responsible for their destruction. His duty is to deliver the message; the ultimate vindication of the oppressed and the judgment of criminals rests completely in the hands of the Almighty.

**The Transition from Mercy to Retribution**

Mūsā’s prayer marks the definitive transition in the sūrah from the dispensation of mercy to the dispensation of retribution. Throughout the opening verses, God emphasized that revelation is sent *as a mercy from your Lord* (44:6). Divine mercy is broad and patient, granting multiple respites and opportunities for repentance.

However, when a society systematically abuses that mercy and hardens its heart in crime, mercy gives way to the demands of divine justice. To allow tyrants to exploit, enslave, and murder indefinitely without consequence would be a betrayal of the oppressed. Mūsā’s appeal does not contradict divine compassion; rather, it invokes divine justice to deliver the weak from the clutches of incorrigible wickedness.

---

## Verse 44:23

> ˹Allah responded,˺ “Leave with My servants at night, for you will surely be pursued

**Leave at Night, You Will Be Followed**
The verse gives the command that set the people of Israel on the road out of Egypt: leave with My servants at night, for you will be followed. The verse answers the prayer Moses had just made, and it begins the last stage of the story in this sūrah.

**What the Command Shows**
The wording says that God is sending them out and calling them His servants. Moses had told Pharaoh to release the servants of God in the earlier part of the account, and the verse in front of the reader has God saying that they are His. That is the answer to everything the people had been told about themselves in Egypt. They had been treated as property, and now the demand for their release is God's own claim on them.

**Why the Night**
The escape was planned for the hours when it would be hardest to stop. The Book chooses the night for the great transitions of its stories: the Qur'an itself was sent down on a blessed night (44:3 states it), and the same root word describes the Prophet's own night journey from Makkah. The angels told Lot to leave with his family in a part of the night before the punishment came (11:81 states it). The pattern tells the early Muslims, who heard this sūrah in Makkah while they were being harassed, that God's way of rescuing people is often a road out under cover of darkness.

**What the Escape Meant to the First Listeners**
The first Muslims hearing this sūrah were in a position much like the people of Moses. They were a small group in a town run by men who controlled the trade, the temples and the weapons, and the pressure on them was rising. The sūrah of the Cave, revealed in the same period, speaks of a group who left their town for their religion and were given shelter. The verse in front of the reader was a promise carried into that situation: the community that is chased out of its home is not abandoned, and the pursuit by a powerful enemy is not the end of the story.

**Where the Story Goes**
The verses that follow describe the sea opening and the army drowning behind them, and the sūrah then turns to the habits of the same people after they were saved. The verse in front of the reader is the moment of departure, and the rest of the passage shows what God did for a community that had no strength left and nothing to bargain with. The Muslim community in Makkah would soon make the same journey for the same reasons, and the Book had already told them how it would end.

---

## Verse 44:24

> And leave the sea parted, for they are certainly an army bound to drown.”

**The Divine Injunction to Leave the Sea Parted**

Following the miraculous crossing of the waters, the divine command issues an extraordinary imperative to Mūsā: *wa-truki l-baḥra rahwan innahum jundun mughraqūn* — And leave the sea parted, for they are certainly an army bound to drown. The noun *rahw* in the old Arabic dictionaries means a state of stillness, calm, wide separation, and open furrowing. Here, it refers to the sea remaining parted in its miraculously split state, with dry paths opened between towering walls of water, just as described in Sūrah ṬāHā: *“Strike for them a dry path through the sea”* (20:77).

Classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Zamakhsharī, reconstruct the dramatic psychological moment behind this command. When Mūsā and the Children of Israel safely reached the opposite shore, Mūsā looked back and saw Pharaoh’s formidable cavalry galloping toward the open sea bed. Naturally anxious to protect his vulnerable followers from being overtaken, Mūsā instinctively prepared to strike the waters again with his staff so they would collapse and prevent the Egyptian army from entering the seabed. God arrested his hand with this decisive command: *leave the sea parted!*

**Parallel with the Decisive Overthrow of the Quraysh**

In the historical framework of the Qur'an’s revelation to the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ, this verse stood as a terrifying prophecy for the pagan aristocracy of Makkah. Like Pharaoh, the leaders of the Quraysh were assembling their military host, confident that their superior arms and prestige would allow them to crush the nascent Muslim community.

---

## Verse 44:25

> ˹Imagine˺ how many gardens and springs the tyrants left behind

**What the Tyrants Left Behind**
The verse asks the reader to look at what the drowning army left behind: gardens and springs, and the question form is the kind that means "how many". It is placed immediately after the account of Pharaoh and his men being swallowed by the sea, and it makes the reader turn from the scene of the water to the scene of the empty estates.

**The Wealth That Had No Owner**
The verse that follows names the rest of it: crops, fine positions, and the comfort they used to enjoy (44:26 and 44:27 list them). The point is not that the wealth was taken away from them in a slow decline; it stood there complete, with nobody left to enjoy it. The sūrah of the Embellishment gives the boast that the verse is answering, where Pharaoh told his people that the kingdom of Egypt was his and that the rivers flowed beneath him (43:51 records his words). The verse in front of the reader shows the same rivers and the same estates with their owner gone.

**The Gardens Put Beside the Gardens**
Later in the same sūrah, the Book describes the gardens promised to the people who were mindful of God: gardens and springs, where they will live at ease (44:51 and 44:52 give the two verses). The reader is meant to put the two sets of gardens beside each other. One was built with forced labour and left standing in a day; the other is given as mercy and is never lost. The verse in front of the reader is short because the contrast does the work.

**What the Verse Asks of the Reader**
The verse asks the reader to look at what he is building and what he expects to keep. The men in the story had the richest land in the world around them and no time left to use it, and the sūrah's response is not a lesson in poverty but a picture of the moment the account closes. The same sūrah will go on to describe the punishment of the men who lived that way and the reward of the men who did not. The verse is the pause in the middle of the story where the reader is invited to look at the orchards and think about who will be there to eat the fruit.

---

## Verse 44:26

> as well as ˹various˺ crops and splendid residences

**The Semantic Splendor of Maqām Karīm**

The phrase *maqāmin karīm* unites architectural magnificence with social and political prestige. The noun *maqām* means a place of standing, an assembly hall, an elevated throne, or a splendid dwelling. Modified by the adjective *karīm* (noble, splendid, honorable, precious), it designates the luxurious palaces, marble courtyards, painted pavilions, and royal council chambers where the Egyptian elite gathered in state ceremonies.

**A Direct Challenge to Makkan Materialism**

This enumeration of crops and splendid dwellings spoke directly to the ambitions of the pagan Quraysh elite. While the Quraysh did not have palaces comparable to the Pharaohs, they were intensely invested in commercial prestige, stone dwellings in Makkah, and agricultural estates in aṭ-Ṭā'if.

**The Contrast Between Worldly and Heavenly Stations**

In the broader moral architecture of the Qur'an, the term *maqām* appears in stark, deliberate contrasts. While Pharaoh’s worldly station was described as *maqām karīm*, it led directly to eternal damnation. Conversely, the Qur'an promises the righteous who fear standing before their Lord a truly enduring station: *“And for the one who feared the standing before his Lord, there are two gardens”* (55:46).

---

## Verse 44:27

> and luxuries which they fully enjoyed

**Parallels with the Warnings to the Quraysh**

The Makkan oligarchs spent their days reclining on embroidered cushions near the Kaʿbah, boasting of their wealth and mocking the impoverished companions of the Prophet ﷺ. Verse 27 placed their petty luxury in historical context. Pharaoh’s court had enjoyed luxuries far beyond the imagination of the desert merchants, reveling in palaces of gold and linen. Yet, all that revelry was extinguished in a single morning. The verse warned the Quraysh that their comfortable lifestyle was no shield against the moral law of the cosmos.

**The Lexical Nuance Between Naʿmah and Niʿmah**

Classical lexicographers and exegetes, including al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and al-Qurṭubī, emphasized the critical religious distinction between *naʿmah* and *niʿmah*. A *niʿmah* is a true blessing from God, given to inspire gratitude, worship, and ethical conduct. When received with humility and shared with the needy, a *niʿmah* elevates the human soul and becomes a source of eternal reward.

The resonance of this verse in Makkah was razor-sharp. The chieftains of the Quraysh—men who controlled the lucrative trade networks of Arabia—were similarly described in the Qur'an as *ūlī n-naʿmah* (the possessors of ease and luxury): *“And leave Me ˹O Prophet˺ with the deniers, the people of luxury, and bear with them for a little while”* (73:11).

**The Contrast Between Worldly Fākihīn and the Righteous**

A striking literary and religious parallel exists between this worldly revelry and the ultimate joy of the believers in the Hereafter. The root *f-k-h* appears again later in the Qur'an to describe the dwellers of Paradise: *“Indeed, the companions of Paradise, that Day, will be amused in joyful occupations”* (36:55).

**The Vanishing Mirage of Earthly Pleasure**

The past tense auxiliary *kānū* (they were) carries a poignant philosophical finality. All that singing, dining, laughing, and pampering of the flesh is now reduced to a historical memory. When the cold waters of the sea closed over their heads, every trace of worldly pleasure evaporated as though it had never existed.

---

## Verse 44:28

> So it was. And We awarded it ˹all˺ to another people

**The Identity of the Inheriting People**

Classical commentators investigated the precise identity of the *other people* (*qawman ākharīn*) who inherited the wealth and lands of Pharaoh. The overwhelming consensus of early authorities, including Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, and Ibn Kathīr, affirms that this refers directly to the Children of Israel. This identification is confirmed by parallel verses across the Qur'anic text: *“so it was. And We caused the Children of Israel to inherit them”* (26:59), and *“We caused the people who were considered weak to inherit the eastern parts of the land and the western parts of it, which We had blessed”* (7:137).

A secondary interpretive opinion, explored by al-Zamakhsharī and al-Qurṭubī, suggested that *another people* could also refer to subsequent non-Israelite dynasties—such as the Persians, Greeks, or other Egyptian lineages—who took possession of the Nile valley after Pharaoh's destruction. However, the primary religious lesson remains anchored in the stunning reversal between Pharaoh and the Israelites: the very slaves who had been beaten, degraded, and systematically butchered were made the inheritors of the earth, while their former masters were cast into watery oblivion.

**The Divine Law of Revolutionary Empowerment**

This dramatic transfer of property and dominion illustrates one of the most foundational revolutionary doctrines of the Qur'an: the divine empowerment of the oppressed (*istidʿāf*). In Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ, the Almighty expresses this universal cosmic law: *“And We wished to do a favor to those who were oppressed in the land, and make them leaders and make them the inheritors, and establish them in the land”* (28:5–6).

**The Moral Responsibility of the Inheritors**

Inheriting the earth carries immense spiritual responsibility. Divine favor is not an unconditional racial or tribal privilege; it is a profound moral trial (*amānah*). The Children of Israel did not inherit Egypt's bounty because of their ethnic superiority, but because God willed to liberate them from injustice and test them with freedom and covenantal obedience.

**Consolation and Prophecy for the Early Muslims**

For the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ and his companions in Makkah, verse 28 provided electrifying hope and a clear historical prophecy. In the eyes of the Quraysh elite, the Muslims were weak, impoverished outcasts who could be tortured with impunity. Men like ʿAmmār, Bilāl, and Suhayb had no tribal protection or material wealth.

---

## Verse 44:29

> Neither heaven nor earth wept over them, nor was their fate delayed

**The Exegetical Tradition: The Tears of the Cosmos**

Classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī, recorded a profound tradition transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid about why heaven and earth weep for certain individuals and not for others. When a man asked Ibn ʿAbbās: *“Does heaven and earth truly weep for anyone?”* Ibn ʿAbbās replied: *“Yes! There is no creature except that it has a gate in heaven through which its sustenance descends and another gate through which its deeds ascend.”*

**The Immediate Execution of Divine Decree**

The second clause delivers the temporal finality of their destruction: *wa-mā kānū munẓarīn* — nor was their fate delayed, or nor were they granted respite. The passive participle *munẓarīn* (from *n-ẓ-r*) means the granting of a delay, respite, or postponement of execution. When the waters of the Red Sea began to collapse, Pharaoh and his troops were not granted an additional second of reprieve.

Pharaoh tried to utter a desperate declaration of faith as the water rushed into his throat (*“I believe that there is no god except the One in whom the Children of Israel believe”*), but the decree was irreversible: his term was finished.

**The Cosmos as a Living Witness to God**

The depiction of heaven and earth weeping or withholding tears reflects the profound Qur'anic worldview that the natural cosmos is not inert, dead matter, but an active, praise-filled creation attuned to the moral order. The heavens, the mountains, the seas, and the trees all glorify their Creator according to their nature: *“The seven heavens and the earth and whatever is in them glorify Him”* (17:44).

---

## Verse 44:30

> And We certainly delivered the Children of Israel from the humiliating torment

**Saved From a Humiliating Punishment**
The verse says that God saved the people of Israel from the punishment that humiliated them. The word describing the punishment means the kind that is meant to crush a person's dignity, and the choice of the word fits what the government of Egypt had been doing to those people.

**What the Torment Consisted Of**
The teachers of the community listed the parts of it: forced labour under the whip, the daughters taken into service in the great houses, the rights of the people ignored in the courts, and the order that the newborn boys be killed so that the enslaved nation would never grow strong enough to rise (the order is named in the account of Moses being placed in the river, and the oppression is stated in the sūrah of the Heifer). The point of such treatment is not only to take labour from a people; it is to make them believe they are worth no more than the labour. That is what the word in the verse is describing.

**What the Rescue Restored**
The verse says the people were saved from that condition, and the Book grounds the rescue in what a human being is worth. It says in another sūrah that God honoured the children of Adam, carried them on land and sea, gave them good provision and favoured them above many of the creatures He made (17:70 states all of it). A government like Pharaoh's sets out to disprove that statement about a whole people, and the rescue in front of the reader is the answer. The Book tells the children of Israel to remember the favour it gave them and that they were favoured above the peoples of their time (2:47 states the command).

**The End of the Same Road for the Tyrants**
The verse's word for humiliation turns up again in the Book for the men who humiliated others. The sūrah of the Heifer says that the men who rejected the message will have a humiliating punishment (2:90 states it), and the same word is used through the Book for the punishment of the proud. The Book's habit is to show that what a tyrant does to other people is what he ends up receiving from God: the men who drowned the babies of a nation were drowned in the sea with their army.

**What the Verse Asks of the Reader**
The verse asks the reader to remember a rescue that was not earned. The people who were saved had nothing to bargain with: no army, no wealth and no standing. The Book keeps telling them to remember the favour precisely because they kept forgetting it, and the sūrahs that follow their story show them complaining, building a calf, and asking to go back. The verse in front of the reader is a reminder to the reader about his own rescue, and the Book tells him to answer it with gratitude rather than with the amnesia of the people who came before him.

---

## Verse 44:31

> of Pharaoh. He was truly a tyrant, a transgressor

**The Singular Source of Humiliation Named**

The discourse explicitly identifies the historical architect of the humiliating torment mentioned in the preceding verse: *min firʿawn innahū kāna ʿāliyan min al-musrifīn* — of Pharaoh. He was truly a tyrant, a transgressor. The prepositional phrase *min firʿawn* depends grammatically upon *the humiliating torment* (*al-ʿadhāb al-muhīn*), completing the historical sentence started in 44:30. By isolating Pharaoh’s name at the head of the verse, the text focuses total moral opprobrium upon the individual who institutionalized state terror, infanticide, and religious self-deification.

**The Semantic Depth of ʿĀliyan**

In Sūrah al-Qaṣaṣ, the text provides the diagnostic definition of this pharaonic *ʿulūw*: *“Indeed, Pharaoh exalted himself in the land and made its people into factions, oppressing a group among them, slaughtering their sons and keeping their females alive. Indeed, he was of the corruptors”* (28:4). Pharaoh’s arrogance was not a passive psychological trait; it was an aggressive, operational ideology that divided human societies, weaponized fear, and normalized atrocities to preserve imperial supremacy.

**Dismantling the Mythology of Totalitarian Power**

Imperial Egypt spent centuries constructing an aura of supernatural invincibility around the person of Pharaoh. Royal monuments depicted him as a colossal figure crushing diminutive enemies beneath his chariot wheels, beloved by the gods and master of the Nile. Official court scribes recorded his deeds in eulogies of fawning adulation.

Verse 31 deconstructs this imperial propaganda with two devastating moral words: *ʿāliyan* (a tyrant) and *min al-musrifīn* (an extremist transgressor). In the sight of the Lord of all things, all that royal splendor was merely criminal pathology.

---

## Verse 44:32

> And indeed, We chose the Israelites knowingly above the others

**The Theological Weight of "Knowingly" (ʿAlā ʿIlm)**

The circumstantial phrase *ʿalā ʿilmin* (knowingly, based upon knowledge) carries immense religious precision. It means that God chose the Children of Israel with full, perfect omniscience about their strengths, their weaknesses, their future obedience, and their recurring rebellions. The Creator was under no illusions about their human frailty; He selected them fully aware of their spiritual flaws and historical challenges.

**The Scope of "Above the Others" (ʿAlā l-ʿĀlamīn)**

This qualification is essential to prevent any misinterpretation of divine election as an unconditional, perpetual racial entitlement. In Qur'anic theology, divine favor is strictly covenantal and moral, never genetic or unconditional. When a subsequent community—the Ummah of the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ—arrived, fulfilling the requirements of faith and moral stewardship, the Qur'an addressed them with comparable election: *“You are the best nation produced for mankind: you enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong and believe in Allah”* (3:110). Selection is tied to fidelity to the message, not biological lineage.

**An Admonition to the Contemporary Audience**

Simultaneously, the verse warned them against the perils of communal complacency. If God did not spare the historical Children of Israel when they abandoned their covenant, He would certainly not spare any subsequent community that took divine favor for granted. Spiritual leadership must be continuously renewed through righteous deeds, humility, and steadfast adherence to the clear Book.

**The Universal Horizon of Divine Election**

Some classical spiritual exegetes, such as Maybudī, observed a broader mystical dimension in *ikhtarnāhum*: it reflects the ultimate honoring of the human species over the rest of the physical creation. Human beings were chosen *knowingly* to bear the sacred trust (*amānah*) of conscious moral agency, as stated in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ: *“And We have certainly honored the Children of Adam and carried them on the land and sea and provided for them of the good things and preferred them over much of what We have created”* (17:70).

---

## Verse 44:33

> And We showed them signs in which there was a clear test

**The Dual Meaning of Balāʾ: Trial and Blessing**

The core religious and linguistic pivot of the verse resides in the noun *balāʾ*. In the old Arabic dictionaries, *balāʾ* carries a dual, interconnected meaning that encompasses both severe testing through adversity and profound testing through abundance and divine blessing (*al-balāʾ bi-n-niʿmah wa-l-balāʾ bi-n-niqmah*). This duality is explicitly expressed in Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ: *“And We test you with evil and with good as a trial; and to Us you will be returned”* (21:35).

Early commentators, including Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, and Qatādah, highlighted both dimensions of *balāʾ* in this verse. On one hand, the signs were a *balāʾ* in the sense of an immense, manifest blessing (*niʿmatun ẓāhirah*): God rescued them from brutal slavery, showered them with food from heaven, and honored them with divine speech. On the other hand, those very blessings constituted an exacting moral trial (*ikhtibār*): Would they respond to these unprecedented miracles with enduring gratitude, humble worship, and unyielding obedience? Or would they grow entitled, complain about the food, and worship the golden calf the moment Mūsā climbed the mountain?

**The Transparency of the Test: Mubīn**

The qualification of the trial as *mubīn* (clear, manifest, unmistakable) mirrors the usage of this key term throughout Sūrah ad-Dukhān. The Book is *mubīn* (44:2), the smoke is *mubīn* (44:10), the Messenger is *mubīn* (44:13), Mūsā’s authority is *mubīn* (44:19), and here the trial is *mubīn*. The clarity of the trial means that the expectations placed upon the Israelites were neither obscure nor unreasonable.

**The Human Tendency Toward Spiritual Ingratitude**

Sacred history records that despite these astonishing signs, the human heart remains remarkably prone to forgetfulness and rebellion. Even as the miraculous manna and quails descended daily from the sky, some among the Israelites complained of the monotony of their diet, longing for the onions, garlic, and lentils of Egypt. When Mūsā ascended Mount Sinai for forty nights to receive the tablets of the Torah, a faction gathered around the golden calf fashioned by the Sāmirī, lapsing into the very polytheism from which they had just been rescued.

**A Mirror for the Companions of the Prophet**

In the immediate context of seventh-century Arabia, verse 33 served as a vital educational warning for the nascent Muslim community. The companions of the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ were experiencing their own continuous sequence of divine signs: the peerless recitation of the Qur'an, the protection of the Messenger against assassination, and the gradual shifting of Arabian history.

The verse reminded the early believers that every sign bestowed upon them was simultaneously a *clear test* (*balāʾun mubīn*). If God blessed them with victory, unity, and eventually the conquest of lands, those blessings were not an invitation to worldly arrogance, but an examination of their spiritual fidelity. The companions took this lesson deeply to heart, maintaining extraordinary humility, ascetic detachment, and moral vigilance even as the riches of the Persian and Roman empires flowed into Madinah.

## Verse 44:34

> Indeed, these ˹Meccans˺ say

**These People Say**
The sūrah has just finished the long account of Moses, Pharaoh, and the rescue of the children of Israel through the sea, and it turns without a bridge: *inna hāʾulāʾi la-yaqūlūn* — indeed, these people say. *Hāʾulāʾi*, "these", points at the men sitting in front of the Prophet in Makkah, and the turn is abrupt on purpose. The story of a drowned empire ends, and the argument resumes with the same sentence a wealthy merchant would say in the market: there is nothing but our first death, and we will not be raised (44:35).
**The Weight of the Particles**
*Inna* with the prefixed *lām* on the verb makes the statement emphatic: this is not a passing doubt muttered once. It is a position they repeat, and the sūrah treats it as a doctrine rather than an uncertainty — the men in that town had made a campaign out of it, debating the Prophet in public and reassuring the crowds that nothing comes after the grave. The verse gets its force from the contrast, because the audience has just been listening to what happened to a king who said the same kind of thing in the same kind of confidence.
**What the Sūrah Sets Against It**
The verses that follow answer the doctrine with a question about their own creation and the creation around them, and then with the Day itself. The refusal to believe in a reckoning is not answered here with a miracle; it is answered by pointing at the two facts the men were standing on every morning of their lives — that they were made, and that the world they were living in was made. The Book never treats the denial of the resurrection as an intellectual position requiring a long refutation. It treats it as a decision that has to be made every day against the evidence of one's own body.

## Verse 44:35

> “There is nothing beyond our first death, and we will never be resurrected

**The Materialist Creed Expressed**

The core doctrine of pre-Islamic Arabian materialism is laid bare in its raw, uncompromising simplicity: *in hiya illā mawtatunā l-ūlā wa-mā naḥnu bi-munsharīn* — “There is nothing beyond our first death, and we will never be resurrected. The syntax employs a restrictive negative construction: *in* (functioning as *mā*, there is not) followed by the exceptive particle *illā* (except, beyond). They assert that human existence is bounded strictly by the biological event of physical death; once a person dies and their bones decompose into dust, the human story is permanently closed.

This assertion represents the definitive rejection of bodily resurrection (*al-baʿth*), judgment, and eternity. In the pagan imagination, the universe was governed not by a purposeful, all-wise Creator, but by the blind, inexorable churning of physical time (*ad-dahr*). As the Qur'an records their philosophy elsewhere: *“There is nothing but our worldly life! We die and we live, and nothing destroys us except time!”* (45:24). Verse 35 encapsulates this bleak, reductionist worldview: human life is a temporary biological accident, ending in absolute, irreversible annihilation.

**The Paradox of "Our First Death"**

The commentators, including al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and al-Qurṭubī, explored the intriguing phrasing *our first death* (*mawtatunā l-ūlā*). If they believed that death was absolute and final, why did they qualify it as the *first*? Does not calling something *first* logically imply the possibility of a second?

The commentators explained that the pagans used *first death* to designate the only death they acknowledged—the physical end of their worldly existence—in contrast to the dual cycle of death and life proclaimed by the Prophet ﷺ. The Qur'an taught that human beings experience two deaths and two lives: nonexistence before conception, followed by worldly birth, followed by physical death, followed by resurrection on the Day of Judgment (as expressed in 2:28 and 40:11: *“They will say, ‘Our Lord, You made us lifeless twice and gave us life twice’”*). The pagans mockingly rejected this religious framework, insisting: there is only the single, physical death we see with our eyes, and nothing beyond it.

**The Emphatic Denial of Resurrection**

To the pagan mind, the idea that scattered, pulverized bones mixed with desert dust could ever be reconstituted, re-clothed with flesh, and re-animated with conscious souls was physically impossible and laughable. When the pagan chieftain Ubayy ibn Khalaf took a crumbling bone, pulverized it in his hands, and blew the powder into the face of the Prophet ﷺ, he sneered: *“Who will revive these bones when they are rotted?”* (36:78). Verse 35 documents this deep sensory skepticism, where human beings judge the limits of divine omnipotence strictly by their own finite, material experience.

Verse 35 records their desperate wishful thinking with devastating clarity.

## Verse 44:36

> Bring ˹back˺ our forefathers, if what you say is true.”

**The Demand They Made**
*Faʾtū bi-ābāʾinā in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — then bring back our fathers, if you are telling the truth. The demand follows the denial of the resurrection in the verse before it, and it is a challenge rather than a question: produce the evidence in public, on the spot, in the form of the fathers they had buried. The Book records the same challenge elsewhere from the same kind of audience, in the Sūrah of the Crouching, where they say: bring our fathers back if you are truthful (45:25). The argument they were making was a simple one, and they thought it was unanswerable: nobody has ever come back, so nobody ever will.
**Why It Was Not Answered How They Asked**
The Book gives its answer in the verses that follow, and the answer is a question about creation: did We create the heavens and the earth and what is between them in play? (44:38-39). The demand is met with a statement about the One being addressed, rather than with a miracle on request. The Sūrah of the Moon describes the pattern in a single line, saying that whenever a sign comes to them they turn away and call it magic, and that they follow their own desires (54:2-3). The point is not that the Book refuses to prove. It is that the proofs were already all around them and were being treated as a performance.
**What the Passage Is Doing**
The sūrah has just finished the account of Moses and Pharaoh, and it returns to the men sitting in front of the Prophet in Makkah with the same scepticism as the court of Egypt. Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī all read this passage as the response to the mockery of the Quraysh. The demand for their fathers is the last argument of a person who has decided in advance, and the sūrah answers it by naming the day when the dead will be brought back without anyone needing to ask.

## Verse 44:37

> Are they superior to the people of Tubba’ and those before them? We destroyed them ˹all˺, ˹for˺ they were truly wicked

**The People of Tubbaʿ**

The *people of Tubbaʿ* (*qawm Tubbaʿ*) refers to the mighty Ḥimyarite dynasty of southern Arabia (modern Yemen), whose kings bore the royal title *Tubbaʿ* (the follower, or one followed by legions). The Ḥimyarites ruled a sprawling empire for centuries, controlling the lucrative incense routes, constructing colossal dams and stone fortresses, and commanding massive armies that campaigned across Arabia, the Persian Gulf, and into Central Asia.

In the collective memory of the Arabian peninsula, the kings of Tubbaʿ were legends of invincible military power, monumental palaces, and boundless agricultural wealth. Classical authorities, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, record prophetic traditions about the specific Tubbaʿ mentioned in sacred history. In a well-known report recorded by al-Ḥākim and at-Tirmidhī, the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ said: *“Do not curse Tubbaʿ, for he had submitted [embraced monotheism].”* The classical consensus explains that a righteous Ḥimyarite king embraced faith, but his imperial aristocracy and populace (*qawmuhu*) persisted in polytheism, tyranny, and denial of the Resurrection. Consequently, it was the *people of Tubbaʿ* who were annihilated by divine decree.

**The Root Cause of Civilizational Ruin: Mujrimīn**

The divine rationale for their destruction is expressed in a single moral verdict: *innahum kānū mujrimīn* — for they were truly wicked, or criminals. This echoes the exact term Mūsā used to describe Pharaoh’s regime in verse 22 (*qawmun mujrimūn*). The repetition of *mujrimīn* establishes an unifying moral philosophy of history throughout Sūrah ad-Dukhān.

Empires are not destroyed because of ethnic identity, geographic location, or economic status; they fall because of entrenched moral criminality (*ijrām*). When a society normalizes idolatry, mocks divine revelation, exploits the weak, and denies cosmic accountability, it signs its own historical death warrant. The verse warns the Quraysh that persistence in their criminal rejection of the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ will inevitably trigger the identical divine law that obliterated the mighty people of Tubbaʿ.

---

## Verse 44:38

> We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything in between for sport

**The Grand Purpose-driven Demonstration**

From the historical rubble of destroyed civilizations, the discourse ascends to the highest cosmic altitude, engaging the core metaphysical question of existence: *wa-mā khalaqnā s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa wa-mā baynahumā lāʿibīn* — We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything in between for sport. The negative particle *mā* combined with the circumstantial participle *lāʿibīn* (in play, for sport, frivolously) issues an absolute, categorical denial. The Architect of the cosmos did not construct this breathtaking universe to be an arbitrary playground, an amusement, or a cosmic accident devoid of ultimate purpose.

**A Direct Rebuke to Pagan Frivolity**

The word *lāʿibīn* (for sport, in play) creates a devastating literary symmetry with the description of the deniers in verse 9: *“In fact, they are in doubt, amusing themselves [yalʿabūn]”* (44:9). The contrast is breathtaking. The mortal creature spends his brief, fragile life playing in frivolous skepticism, mocking sacred truths and treating existence like a game; while the Creator of the cosmos declares with majestic solemnity: *We did not create the universe in play!*

## Verse 44:39

> We only created them for a purpose, but most of these ˹pagans˺ do not know

**The Dimensions of Al-Ḥaqq in Creation**

The commentators, including al-Zamakhsharī, al-Baghawī, and Ibn Kathīr, identified three fundamental dimensions of *al-ḥaqq* as the purpose of cosmic creation:.

Third, moral accountability and requital: The cosmos was designed as an arena of moral testing for conscious creatures (humans and jinn), requiring a subsequent Day of Judgment where righteous deeds are rewarded with Paradise and unrepentant wickedness is punished with the Hellfire. Without the final reckoning, creation would be deficient in truth. As the Qur'an declares elsewhere: *“Allah created the heavens and the earth in truth, and so that every soul may be recompensed for what it earned, and they will not be wronged”* (45:22).

**The Diagnosis of Human Ignorance (Lā Yaʿlamūn)**

Their ignorance was of a deeper, spiritual order: an epistemic failure to perceive the metaphysical meaning behind physical phenomena. They saw the sky only as weather for their sheep, the earth only as pastures for their camels, and human life only as a biological race for wealth and sons. They were experts in the surface mechanics of worldly life while remaining completely blind to the ultimate purpose of their existence: *“They know what is apparent of the worldly life, but they, of the Hereafter, are oblivious”* (30:7).

**The Tragic Majority of Spiritual Blindness**

This reminder provided vital psychological consolation to the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ and his small band of followers in Makkah. Surrounded by an overwhelming majority of mocking polytheists, the early believers might have felt isolated and plagued by doubt: If our message is true, why do the vast majority of our esteemed elders and chieftains reject it? Verse 39 answers: because *most of them do not know*. Truth is not determined by democratic vote or tribal consensus; it is grounded in the eternal reality of *al-ḥaqq* established by God.

---

## Verse 44:40

> Surely the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision is the time appointed for all—

**The Day of Decision**
*Inna yawma al-faṣli mīqātuhum ajmaʿīn* — the Day of Decision is the appointed time for them all. *Al-faṣl* comes from a root meaning to separate what has been joined, and the word carries the whole idea: the day when what was mixed is sorted, when the case that was argued is ruled on, and when the group that was travelling together is divided. The Book uses the name for the Day in more than one place, and in the sūrah of the Message it is put in the mouths of the condemned themselves, who ask when this Day of Decision is, if the messengers are telling the truth (77:13-14).
**What the Sorting Will Be**
The verse's promise is that nothing stays lumped together. The criminal and the wronged man who lived next door; the man who took the bribe and the man who lost his case; the one who gave in secret and the one who was praised in public — the Day of Decision takes the tangle apart. The Book gives the picture in the sūrah of Yā Sīn, where the command goes out on that day: *and stand apart this day, you criminals* (36:59) — the separation is spoken aloud and the two groups are made visible to each other. The name of the day is therefore a guarantee about justice as much as a warning, and the sūrah places it at the end of the section about Pharaoh for that reason: a tyrant's account does not close with his tomb.
**The Appointment**
The sentence ends with *mīqātuhum ajmaʿīn* — their appointed time, all of them together. Nobody has a private date, and nobody's case is heard before the court sits. The word *mīqāt* is the same one used for an appointment made in advance, and the verse states that it has been set for every one of the people the sūrah has been describing, from the men who mocked in Makkah to the king who drowned in Egypt.

## Verse 44:41

> the Day no kith or kin will be of benefit to another whatsoever, nor will they be helped

**The Semantic Scope of Lā Yughnī Shayʾan**

The verbal construct *lā yughnī... Shayʾan* means complete inability to avail, protect, enrich, or deflect harm even by the weight of an atom. The indefinite noun *shayʾan* (anything whatsoever) functions as an absolute negative object, emphasizing that not even the slightest fraction of divine penalty can be transferred or mitigated by an earthly ally.

**The Severing of Familial and Emotional Ties**

As the Qur'an vividly depicts in Sūrah al-Maʿārij: *“No friend will ask about a friend, though they will be made to see each other. The criminal would wish that he could be ransomed from the punishment of that Day by his children, and his wife and his brother, and his nearest kindred who sheltered him, and whoever is on earth completely, so it might save him!”* (70:10–14). The verse captures the ultimate tragedy of human alienation: the very people who conspired together in worldly sin will turn their backs upon one another in desperate, solitary terror.

**Dismantling the Illusion of Intercession**

A primary defense mechanism of the Makkan polytheists was their belief that their idols and ancestral spirits would act as influential intercessors (*shufaʿāʾ*) before God, deflecting punishment on their behalf: *“These are our intercessors with Allah!”* (10:18). They imagined that celestial intercession worked like tribal politics, where influential nobles coerced rulers into pardoning favored clients.

## Verse 44:42

> except those shown mercy by Allah. He is truly the Almighty, Most Merciful

**The Authentic Prophetic Insight on Divine Grace**

This profound reality was explicitly confirmed by the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ in a renowned tradition recorded in both *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (6463) and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2816). The Prophet said to his companions: *“None of you will enter Paradise purely by his deeds.”* The companions, astonished by this declaration, asked: *“Not even you, O Messenger of Allah?”* He replied: *“Not even me, unless Allah encompasses me with His mercy and grace!”*

**The Harmonious Synthesis of Al-ʿAzīz and Ar-Raḥīm**

The verse seals its declaration with the magnificent pairing of two divine attributes: *innahū huwa l-ʿazīzu r-raḥīm* — He is truly the Almighty, Most Merciful. The presence of the pronoun of separation (*ḍamīr al-faṣl: huwa*) restricts absolute, balanced perfection in power and mercy to God alone. The name *al-ʿAzīz* means the Invincible, the Almighty, Whose sovereign will cannot be thwarted, Whose authority cannot be resisted, and Who takes decisive vengeance upon unrepentant tyrants.

Paired with it is *ar-Raḥīm*, the Most Merciful, Whose compassion embraces the repentant, Whose grace forgives sins, and Who showers the believers with eternal peace. Classical commentators, including al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr, observed that this specific pairing represents the pinnacle of balanced religious understanding. Power without mercy is brutal tyranny, while mercy without power is sentimental weakness. In God alone, infinite power and infinite mercy unite in absolute harmony.

**The Dual Operation of Divine Majesty on the Appointed Day**

The twin attributes *al-ʿAzīz* and *ar-Raḥīm* explain the dual reality of the Day of Decision. To the obstinate oppressors, the arrogant deniers of the Resurrection, and the persecutors of the righteous, God manifests His attribute of *al-ʿAzīz*: He seizes them with the fiercest blow, shows His unshared sovereignty, and casts them into the Hellfire without any rival power able to obstruct His decree.

Simultaneously, to the humble believers who endured trials with steadfast faith, who sought forgiveness in the dark of night, and who treated fellow creatures with compassion, God manifests His attribute of *ar-Raḥīm*: He covers their faults, welcomes them into Gardens of security, and bestows upon them an eternal triumph. The verse reassures the faithful that the same Almighty power that crushes tyranny is deployed to protect and embrace those who sought divine mercy.

**The Antidote to Despair and Spiritual Complacency**

The contemplation of this verse establishes the ideal spiritual equilibrium required for the human soul: the balance between fear (*khawf*) and hope (*rajāʾ*). The preceding verse (44:41) dismantled all worldly security, inspiring profound awe and fear of standing naked before divine justice. Verse 42 immediately restores hope by unveiling the boundless ocean of divine mercy.

---

## Verse 44:43

> Surely ˹the fruit of˺ the tree of Zaqqûm

**The Ominous Introduction of Infernal Sustenance**

Following the proclamation of divine mercy and judgment, the discourse descends into the terrifying penal geography of the Hellfire, introducing the infernal sustenance prepared for unrepentant evil: *inna shajarata z-zaqqūm* — Surely ˹the fruit of˺ the tree of Zaqqûm. The emphatic particle *inna* confirms the absolute physical and of being reality of this dreaded creation. The text pauses upon the name of the tree itself, allowing the horrifying auditory resonance of the word *az-Zaqqūm* to arrest the listener's focus before detailing its consumption in the subsequent verse.

The tree of Zaqqūm is one of the most terrifying Last-Day realities depicted in the Qur'an. Unlike earthly trees that grow from fertile soil, fresh water, and sunlight, Zaqqūm is a miraculous creation of divine punishment that sprouts directly from the blazing floor of the Hellfire, as described in Sūrah aṣ-Ṣāffāt: *“Indeed, it is a tree that springs out of the bottom of the Hellfire”* (37:64). Its existence is a demonstration of divine omnipotence: the very fire that incinerates flesh and stone provides the furnace in which this bitter, lethal tree flourishes.

**The Prophetic Warning Regarding Zaqqūm**

The terrifying reality of Zaqqūm was impressed upon the early community through chilling prophetic descriptions. In an authentic tradition recorded by at-Tirmidhī (2585), an-Nasā'ī. Ibn Mājah, authenticated by al-Ḥākim, the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ recited this verse and said: *“If but a single drop of Zaqqūm were to fall into the oceans of this world, it would utterly spoil the life and sustenance of all the inhabitants of the earth! How then shall it be for the one whose only food it has become?”*

**The Contrast with the Blessed Tree of Revelation**

In the literary and spiritual architecture of the Qur'an, trees function as profound symbols of spiritual states. In Sūrah Ibrāhīm, divine truth is compared to a pure, blessed tree: *“A good word is like a good tree, its root is firmly fixed and its branches reach into the sky, giving its fruit at all times by the permission of its Lord”* (14:24–25). Similarly, in Sūrah an-Nūr, the divine light is associated with a blessed olive tree that is *neither of the east nor of the west* (24:35).

**The Historical Mockery of the Makkan Polytheists**

When the verses describing the tree of Zaqqūm were first recited in Makkah, the pagan aristocracy reacted with cynical laughter and childish mockery. Chieftains like Abū Jahl and Ibn al-Zibaʿrā tried to neutralize the terrifying warning through sarcastic humor. Abū Jahl summoned his servants, demanding: *“Bring us some dates and butter, for this is the zaqqūm with which Muḥammad threatens us! Let us devour it!”*

---

## Verse 44:44

> will be the food of the evildoer

**The Precise Identification of the Consumer**

The discourse completes the terrifying announcement started in verse 43 by identifying the consumers for whom the tree of Zaqqūm is prepared: *ṭaʿāmu l-athīm* — will be the food of the evildoer. The grammatical predicate *ṭaʿām* (food, sustenance, nourishment) links directly to *the tree of Zaqqūm*, transforming an ominous botanical description into an active, horrific culinary sentence. The singular noun *al-athīm* modified by the definite article functions as a generic category, encompassing all who embody entrenched, habitual criminality and unrepentant moral corruption.

The word *ṭaʿām* carries profound tragic irony. Food is designed by the Creator to provide nourishment, strength, sensory pleasure, and life to the physical body. In the depths of the Fire, food is inverted into an instrument of excruciating punishment. Zaqqūm provides neither nutritional vitality nor relief from starvation: *“No food will there be for them except from a bitter, thorny plant, which neither nourishes nor avails against hunger”* (88:6–7). It is a food designed specifically to burn, choke, and torment.

**The Semantic Weight of Al-Athīm**

Classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Zamakhsharī, observed that *al-athīm* in this Makkan context applied directly to the arch-persecutors of the nascent Muslim community, particularly Abū Jahl (ʿAmr ibn Hishām). Abū Jahl was renowned for his relentless campaigns to eradicate Islam, personally organizing the torture of vulnerable slaves, mocking the Prophet ﷺ, and leading the pagan forces at Badr. The text strips away his prestigious Makkan title *Abū al-Ḥakam* (the Father of Wisdom) and stamps him with his true cosmic title: *al-Athīm* (the ingrained evildoer, the criminal).

**The Inevitable Compulsion to Consume**

Driven to utter madness by this agonizing starvation, they crawl to the tree of Zaqqūm and tear its toxic fruits from the thorny branches, stuffing them desperately into their mouths despite the unbearable stench and blistering bitterness. As Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah depicts: *“Then indeed you, O you who are in error and deny, will surely eat from trees of Zaqqūm and fill with it your bellies”* (56:51–53). The act of eating becomes a desperate, self-inflicted torment, fulfilling the exact divine decree that their food shall be their punishment.

**The Contrast with the Pure Food of Paradise**

The designation of Zaqqūm as the food of the *athīm* stands in sharp, deliberate contrast to the cuisine of the righteous in the Gardens of Paradise. While the evildoer chokes upon thorny, boiling poison, the righteous believers are served *fruits of whatever they desire* (56:20) and *pure drink sealed with musk* (83:25–26), served in serenity and security without headache or intoxication.

---

## Verse 44:45

> Like molten metal, it will boil in the bellies

**The Physical Mechanics of Internal Torment**

The discourse transitions from the botanical identity of the infernal tree to the excruciating physiological reaction that occurs upon its consumption: *ka-l-muhli yaghlī fī l-buṭūn* — Like molten metal, it will boil in the bellies. The comparative particle *kāf* introduces an astonishing physical simile: *al-muhl*. In the old Arabic dictionaries and Qur'anic usage, *al-muhl* means metal that has been subjected to extreme heat until it liquefies: molten copper, boiling brass, melted lead, or the boiling, rancid dregs of heated oil.

The term *al-muhl* appears in several key passages across the Qur'an, consistently associated with the extreme thermal punishments of the Hellfire. In Sūrah al-Kahf, when the damned cry out for water to quench their desperate thirst, their plea is answered with molten agony: *“And if they call for relief, they will be relieved with water like molten metal [ka-l-muhl], which scalds their faces. How wretched is the drink, and how evil is the resting place!”* (18:29).

Similarly, on the Day of Resurrection, the very heavens will be transformed into this state: *“The Day that the sky will be like molten metal [ka-l-muhl]”* (70:8). The inclusion of *al-muhl* in verse 45 stresses that the food consumed by the sinner carries the same apocalyptic heat that dissolves celestial skies. It is an affliction that penetrates from within, reversing the natural biological function of the body into an agonizing furnace of retribution.

**The Contrast with Worldly Digestion and Nourishment**

In the Hellfire, this entire physiological harmony is inverted into an engine of torture. The food of the evildoer offers no nourishment, repairs no tissue, and brings no energy. Instead, it turns the stomach against itself. The molten matter incinerates the stomach lining, dissolves the intestines. Boils the internal viscera with relentless fury, only for the divine power to continuously restore the organs so the torment can be experienced anew: *“Every time their skins are roasted through We will replace them with other skins so they may taste the punishment”* (4:56).

---

## Verse 44:46

> like the boiling of hot water

**The Complementary Functions of Muhl and Ḥamīm**

Classical commentators, including Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī, investigated why both *al-muhl* (molten metal) and *al-ḥamīm* (boiling water) are invoked in consecutive verses. The two similes capture distinct aspects of the internal catastrophe. *Al-muhl* conveys extreme chemical density, toxicity, heaviness, and incandescent, melting heat; it depicts a thick, glowing sludge that burns its way through tissue through raw physical mass and caustic heat.

**The Inescapability of the Thermal Reaction**

In the Hellfire, however, all escape mechanisms are permanently barred. The damned cannot vomit out the boiling matter, nor does the stomach rupture to grant the relief of death. The divine decree holds the boiling liquid suspended within the viscera, sustaining the burning agony without interruption: *“They will be given to drink boiling water that tears apart their intestines”* (47:15). The verse stresses the absolute irreversibility of divine punishment once the threshold of the Fire is crossed.

**The Contrast with the Refreshing Springs of Paradise**

This terrifying description of scalding, boiling fluid stands in dramatic, deliberate opposition to the aquatic delights prepared for the righteous in the Gardens of Paradise. While the evildoer suffers *the boiling of hot water* inside his core, the believers recline beside *flowing springs* (*ʿuyūn*, 44:52) of crystal-clear, refreshing nectar that cools the spirit and bestows eternal vitality.

How, then, can a rational person gamble this fragile physical vessel against the eternal boiling of *al-ḥamīm*? Verse 46 serves as an act of profound divine mercy disguised in terrifying language.

---

## Verse 44:47

> ˹It will be said,˺ “Seize them and drag them into the depths of the Hellfire

**The Absolute Sovereign Command to the Wardens**

The scene shifts dramatically from the internal torment of the evildoer to an authoritative, booming divine command issued across the cosmic courtroom: *khudhūhu fa-ʿtilūhu ilā sawāʾi l-jaḥīm* — ˹It will be said,˺ “Seize them and drag them into the depths of the Hellfire. The imperative *khudhūhu* (seize him, take him into custody) is addressed directly to the stern, unyielding angelic wardens of the Hellfire (*al-Zabāniyah*), described in Sūrah at-Taḥrīm as *“angels, harsh and severe, who do not disobey Allah in what He commands them but do what they are commanded”* (66:6).

**The Violent Humiliation of Al-ʿItl**

The verb *ʿitlu* (from *ʿ-t-l*) conveys extreme physical violence, rough handling, humiliating dragging, and violent thrusting. In the old Arabic dictionaries, *al-ʿatl* means grabbing someone roughly by the scruff of the neck, dragging them face-down upon the ground, or shoving them violently from behind toward a dreaded destination without the slightest gentleness or mercy.

This description delivers a catastrophic blow to the dignity of the proud oppressor. In this world, the pagan chieftains walked with sweeping cloaks, accompanied by entourages that parted the crowds in reverent fear. Now, in the cosmic assembly, they are treated with utter, humiliating contempt. The angels do not escort them with polite protocol; they seize them, twist their limbs, and drag them violently across the abrasive terrain of the Resurrection, fulfilling the prophecy of Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr: *“The Day they will be thrust into the Fire of Hell with a violent thrust”* (52:13).

**The Dramatic Reversal of Worldly Power**

The imagery of verse 47 provides the ultimate religious demonstration of historical and cosmic reversal. In the historical narrative of the sūrah, Pharaoh sat upon his royal throne commanding his guards to enslave and murder the Children of Israel. In contemporary Makkah, Abū Jahl commanded his thugs to pin Bilāl beneath burning stones and harass the Prophet ﷺ.

---

## Verse 44:48

> Then pour over their heads the torment of boiling water.”

**The Execution of External Scalding**

The divine sentencing escalates from violent dragging to external thermal inundation: *thumma ṣubbū fawqa raʾsihī min ʿadhābi l-ḥamīm* — Then pour over their heads the torment of boiling water. The sequential conjunction *thumma* (then) means a horrific progression in the stages of punishment. After having his internal viscera boiled by the fruit of Zaqqūm (vv. 45–46), and after being violently dragged into the midmost depths of the furnace (v. 47), the evildoer is now subjected to an external deluge of scalding fluid poured directly upon the crowning center of his physical being.

**The Anatomical Devastation of Scalding**

Classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī, preserve vivid reports explaining the horrific physical mechanics of this punishment. In traditions transmitted from the Companions, it is explained that when this boiling fluid is poured over the head, its intense heat instantly melts the scalp, dissolves the skull, and penetrates into the brain cavity.

From the brain, the scalding liquid cascades downward through the internal cavities of the body, melting the viscera, tearing through the chest, and exiting through the feet, leaving the entire physical form dissolved in liquefied ruin. Then, by sovereign divine power, the body is instantly reconstituted to its original state so that the cycle of pouring and melting can be experienced again: *“Whenever their skins are burnt through, We will change them for other skins that they may taste the punishment”* (4:56). The punishment encompasses the human being from the crown of the head to the soles of the feet.

**The Symbolic Targeting of the Head (Fawqa Raʾsih)**

Pharaoh held his head high in royal defiance, and the chieftains of the Quraysh tilted their heads back in arrogant laughter whenever the Prophet ﷺ recited the Qur'an. Verse 48 targets this specific symbol of human hubris with devastating justice. The very head that refused to prostrate before the Creator, that concocted conspiracies against the prophets, and that puffed itself up with caste pride becomes the receptacle for boiling torment. The external crown of arrogance is answered with a celestial crown of scalding fluid, stripping away all pretense of nobility.

**The Convergence of Internal and External Boiling**

The sinner is so caught in an inescapable thermal pincer movement: boiling from within and scalding from without. There is no cool zone, no internal refuge, and no external sanctuary. The entire organism is completely enveloped in agonizing heat, fulfilling the Qur'anic description in Sūrah az-Zumar: *“They will have canopies of fire above them and canopies of fire beneath them”* (39:16). The verse portrays an absolute, multi-dimensional totality of retribution that leaves no fiber of the creature untouched.

---

## Verse 44:49

> ˹The wicked will be told,˺ “Taste this. You mighty, noble one

**The Ultimate Psychological Retribution: Sarcastic Mockery**

As the boiling fluid melts the scalp and tears through the viscera, the physical agony is suddenly multiplied by a devastating psychological assault: *dhuq innaka anta l-ʿazīzu l-karīm* — ˹The wicked will be told,˺ “Taste this. You mighty, noble one!” (44:49) The imperative *dhuq* (taste!) is addressed directly to the writhing sinner, uttered either by the Almighty from above the heavens or by the stern angelic wardens executing the sentence. In Qur'anic judicial rhetoric, *taste* means an immediate, unmediated personal experience: you cannot observe this punishment from a detached distance; you must experience its bitter reality upon your own physical and emotional being.

**The Historical Context: The Boast of Abū Jahl**

Classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Zamakhsharī, and al-Qurṭubī, anchor this verse in a specific, documented historical confrontation between the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ and the arch-persecutor of Islam, Abū Jahl (ʿAmr ibn Hishām). On one occasion in Makkah, when the Prophet ﷺ warned Abū Jahl of divine retribution, the haughty chieftain scoffed with monumental arrogance.

**The Meaning of the Alternative Reading: Annaka**

Classical Qur'anic reciters and grammarians documented a profound linguistic nuance in the particle introducing the clause. While the majority reading is *innaka* with a kasrah (an emphatic declaration: *Indeed, you claimed to be the mighty, the noble*), an authentic canonical reading transmitted by early authorities is *annaka* with a fatḥah.

**The Radical Dismantling of Worldly Aristocracy**

Pre-Islamic Arabian society, like imperial Egypt before it, was thoroughly intoxicated by caste privilege. The elite measured human value strictly by two titles: *ʿazīz* (one who has power, political influence, and military strength) and *karīm* (one who has noble lineage, wealth, and aristocratic prestige). Under this brutal caste system, slaves, orphans, and the poor were treated as worthless subhumans.

Verse 49 permanently dismantles this worldly value system. In the court of the Almighty, titles purchased with worldly gold or tribal genealogies are completely meaningless. True *ʿizzah* (might) belongs exclusively to God and His Messenger, and true *karam* (nobility) is achieved solely through piety (*taqwā*): *“Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you”* (49:13). The verse exposes the pathetic emptiness of worldly prestige, showing the mighty chieftain reduced to a trembling, mocked wretch in the hands of the angels.

---

## Verse 44:50

> This is truly what you ˹all˺ used to doubt.”

**The Full Circle of Sūrah ad-Dukhān’s Argument**

With this verse, the religious and thematic architecture of Sūrah ad-Dukhān achieves a breathtaking structural unity. In the opening section of the sūrah, the Almighty diagnosed the psychological root of pagan rebellion: *“In fact, they are in doubt, amusing themselves [fī shakkin yalʿabūn]”* (44:9). They treated the warnings of the clear Book as a game, taking refuge in chronic doubt to avoid moral transformation.

**The Contrast with the Knowledge of Certainty (Yaqīn)**

In Qur'anic account of knowledge, truth is divided into ascending degrees of certainty: the knowledge of certainty (*ʿilm al-yaqīn*), the eye of certainty (*ʿayn al-yaqīn*), and the truth of certainty (*ḥaqq al-yaqīn*). In worldly life, the deniers were invited to attain *certainty* through sound reflection upon cosmic signs: *“if only you had sure faith [mūqinīn]”* (44:7). They refused that noble intellectual ascent, choosing instead the murky fog of *doubt* (*shakk* and *miryah*).

Now, in the Hellfire, certainty is violently forced upon them. They see the Fire with the *eye of certainty*, and they taste the boiling *ḥamīm* with the *truth of certainty*. But this posthumous certainty is completely useless for salvation: *“How clearly they will hear and see on the Day they come to Us! But the wrongdoers today are in clear error”* (19:38). Certainty cultivated in freedom earns Paradise; certainty forced by punishment seals damnation. Verse 50 records that agonizing moment when the denier is forced to confess that the Prophet was right all along.

**The Finality of the Shift to the Righteous**

Verse 50 marks the absolute conclusion of the sūrah's descriptions of the Hellfire and the fate of the deniers. The judicial case against Pharaoh, the people of Tubbaʿ, and the contemporary Quraysh oligarchy is officially closed.

**The Tragic Irreversibility of Posthumous Awakening**

The phrase *“This is truly what you used to doubt”* expresses the ultimate tragedy of human obstinacy. Throughout sacred history, prophets pleaded with humanity to wake up before the arrival of death, warning that the grave is an irreversible threshold. The deniers responded with demands for impossible signs: *“Bring back our forefathers if you are truthful!”* (44:36).

---

## Verse 44:51

> Indeed, the righteous will be in a secure place

**The Luminous Pivot to Celestial Bliss**

Following the terrifying depiction of the Hellfire, the tree of Zaqqūm, and the sarcastic humiliation of the evildoer (vv. 43–50), the discourse pivots with radiant beauty to the eternal reward of the faithful: *inna l-muttaqīna fī maqāmin amīn* — Indeed, the righteous will be in a secure place. The emphatic particle *inna* confirms that this celestial sanctuary is as undeniably real and immutably established as the infernal abyss described earlier. The transition exemplifies the Qur'anic pedagogical art of *al-targhīb wa-l-tarhīb* (the harmonized balance of attraction and warning), ensuring that the human heart is guided by both reverent awe and boundless hope.

Modified by the active participle *amīn* (secure, peaceful, completely safe), the phrase guarantees total immunity from every form of danger, grief, anxiety, and loss.

**The Contrast with Pharaoh’s Lost Splendor**

In contrast, the dwelling of the righteous is designated as a *secure place* (*maqām amīn*). Earthly residences, no matter how splendid, are inherently insecure: they are vulnerable to war, economic ruin, natural disasters, and the inevitable eviction of death. The dwelling of Paradise alone unites supreme splendor with absolute permanence. The righteous receive an eternal estate that can never be seized, taxed, destroyed, or abandoned.

---

## Verse 44:52

> amid Gardens and springs

**The Deliberate Contrast with Pharaoh’s Abandoned Gardens**

A profound and deliberate literary mirror connects this verse with the historical fate of Pharaoh in verse 25. There, the Qur'an described the tragedy of the Egyptian oppressors: *“˹Imagine˺ how many gardens and springs the tyrants left behind!”* (*kam tarakū min jannātin wa-ʿuyūn*, 44:25). The exact same Arabic phrase—*jannātin wa-ʿuyūn*—is employed in both verses, creating a breathtaking religious contrast.

**The Multifaceted Nature of Celestial Springs**

In Qur'anic the teaching about the Last Day, the springs of Paradise (*al-ʿuyūn*) are depicted with astonishing sensory and spiritual richness across many chapters. They are not only bodies of water; they are living fountains of divine grace, flowing with pure, crystal nectars that refresh the body and elevate the spirit.

These springs include *Tasnīm*, the highest nectar reserved for the foremost in faith (*al-muqarrabūn*, 83:27–28); *Salsabīl*, a delicately flavored spring flavored with ginger (*zanjabīl*, 76:18); and the spring of *Kāfūr*, scented with heavenly camphor that brings profound coolness and tranquility to the heart (76:5). In Sūrah ad-Dukhān, the plural *ʿuyūn* encompasses all these celestial waters, bubbling continuously beneath the shaded canopies of the Gardens, offering effortless refreshment to the blessed dwellers without labor or scarcity.

**Water as the Universal Medium of Life and Purity**

Throughout the Qur'anic corpus, water serves as the supreme physical manifestation of divine mercy. It is water that revives the barren, dead desert into lush greenery, providing an ongoing visual rehearsal of the Resurrection: *“And We send down from the sky blessed water, and We produce and so gardens and grain from the harvest”* (50:9).

In the Gardens of Paradise, the flowing springs represent the culmination of this life-giving principle. In this world, water sustains biological life; in the Hereafter, the springs of Paradise sustain eternal, unperishing life, completely free from physical impurities, bacteria, or decay. The presence of flowing springs ensures an environment of perpetual freshness, soothing sounds, and cool breezes that wash away all memory of worldly fatigue.

**The Spiritual Dimensions of the Heavenly Landscape**

Classical spiritual masters, including al-Ghazālī and Ibn al-Qayyim, observed that the *gardens and springs* of Paradise correspond to the spiritual gardens cultivated by the believer in this earthly life. The sincere prayers, charitable deeds, and hours spent in divine remembrance (*dhikr*) are the seeds that blossom into the celestial gardens of the Hereafter.

---

## Verse 44:53

> dressed in fine silk and rich brocade, facing one another

**The Linguistic Heritage of Sundus and Istabraq**

Classical lexicographers, including al-Jawharī and al-Zamakhsharī, observed that *sundus* and *istabraq* represent ancient loanwords absorbed into the eloquent classical Arabic tongue, originating from Persian and Greek textile terminology. *Sundus* designates the sheer, feather-light inner silk that touches the skin with refreshing softness, while *istabraq* designates the heavy, structured outer brocade adorned with raised gold embroidery and lustrous patterns.

**The Profound Fellowship of Mutaqābilīn**

This communal harmony is elaborated in Sūrah al-Ḥijr: *“And We will remove whatever is in their breasts of resentment, [so they will be] brothers, on thrones facing each other”* (15:47). In Paradise, all the social frictions, misunderstandings, and psychological wounds of earthly life are completely healed. The believers recline as an unbroken fraternity of pure love, gazing into the radiant faces of their companions, conversing with joyful intimacy, and sharing their memories of worldly trials and divine deliverance.

**The Contrast with the Isolation of the Damned**

The warm, loving fellowship of *mutaqābilīn* stands in terrifying contrast to the complete social alienation of the damned in the Hellfire. In verses 41 and 47, the evildoers were depicted as completely abandoned by their patrons (*lā yughnī mawlan ʿan mawlan*), dragged face-down into the abyss by harsh angels, and screaming in solitary agony.

In the Fire, there is no fellowship, no comfort, and no shared love. The damned curse one another, blame their leaders for their ruin, and despise the sight of their former companions: *“Every time a nation enters, it curses its sister nation”* (7:38). In contrast, the righteous in Paradise enjoy the highest form of social communion, surrounded by noble friends, righteous family members, and the prophets of God, celebrating their shared triumph in an atmosphere of unclouded joy.

**The Synthesis of Outer Adornment and Inner Purity**

The regal clothing of *sundus* and *istabraq* is the outward physical reflection of the inward *garment of God-consciousness* (*libās al-taqwā*) which the believers wore throughout their worldly lives: *“and the garment of God-consciousness—that is the best”* (7:26).

---

## Verse 44:54

> So it will be. And We will pair them to maidens with gorgeous eyes

**So We Gave Them in Marriage**

The discourse ratifies the honoring of the righteous with majestic, laconic authority: *kadhālika wa-zawwajnāhum bi-ḥūrin ʿīn* — So it will be. And We will pair them to maidens with gorgeous eyes. The single demonstrative particle *kadhālika* (so it is, so it will be) echoes its earlier usage in verse 28 (*kadhālika wa-awrathnāhā qawman ākharīn*), serving as a royal seal upon the decree. Just as divine justice definitively disinherited Pharaoh and awarded his empire to another people, so too divine grace definitively establishes the righteous within their eternal inheritance of honor, beauty, and companionship.

**The Linguistic and Aesthetic Depth of Ḥūr ʿĪn**

The designation of these celestial companions as *ḥūrin ʿīn* has captivated classical lexicographers and commentators across the centuries. Both words carry profound aesthetic and symbolic meaning in the classical Arabic language:.

The second adjective *ʿīn* is the plural of *ʿaynāʾ*, derived from *al-ʿayan*, meaning large, wide, luminous eyes characterized by softness, expressive warmth, and alluring beauty. Classical authorities like Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī noted that because the eye is the direct mirror of the soul, reflecting its inner purity, tenderness, and emotional intelligence, the description *ḥūr ʿīn* means companions who are exceptionally pure of spirit, rich of soul, and overflowing with radiant affection.

**Exegetical Traditions Regarding the Identity of the Companions**

Simultaneously, prominent early authorities, including the eminent Successor al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, emphasized that the righteous women of this earthly life will enter Paradise with an even higher, more radiant station. When an elderly woman asked the Prophet ﷺ whether old women enter Paradise, he gently explained that they do not enter as old women; rather, God recreates them as youthful, breathtakingly beautiful, and purified maidens (*abkāran, ʿuruban atrābā*, 56:36–37). Because the women of this world endured prayer, fasting, patience under trials, and righteous deeds, their spiritual elevation surpasses that of the *ḥūr*, shining as queens of light within their palatial pavilions.

**The Complete Purification of Celestial Companionship**

In Paradise, all these imperfections are completely eliminated. The companions granted to the righteous are described throughout the Qur'an as *purified spouses* (*azwājun muṭahharatun*, 2:25; 3:15). They are purified physically from all bodily waste, odor, and illness, and purified emotionally and spiritually from malice, envy, deceit, and incompatibility. Their union is an eternal harmony of pure love, deep emotional intimacy, and mutual delight, providing the soul with a permanent sanctuary of affection.

---

## Verse 44:55

> There they will call for every fruit in serenity

**The Effortless Sovereignty of Celestial Provision**

The portrait of Paradise turns to the effortless abundance and sovereign ease enjoyed by the righteous: *yadʿūna fīhā bi-kulli fākihatin āminīn* — There they will call for every fruit in serenity. The active verb *yadʿūna* (they call for, they request, they summon) illustrates a station of supreme luxury and effortless command. In the Gardens of Eternity, the dwellers do not toil, cultivate, plow, harvest, or purchase their food; they simply express a desire, or call for whatever they wish, and it is instantly brought before them by attentive celestial attendants or lowered directly to their hands by the bending boughs of the trees.

As Sūrah al-Insān depicts: *“And its shades are close upon them, and its clusters of fruit are brought low in complete surrender”* (76:14). The fruit does not require climbing, labor, or tools; the moment the desire forms in the mind of the believer, the branch gently descends to offer its sweet, aromatic bounty. Consumption in Paradise is stripped of all worldly exhaustion, functioning purely as an experience of sensory joy, gratitude, and divine honoring.

**The Universal Horizon of Bi-Kulli Fākihatin**

The universal prefix *kulli* (every, all) guarantees that no desirable delicacy is withheld. Every fruit known to human history—dates, pomegranates, figs, grapes, and tropical delicacies—alongside countless wondrous varieties created specifically for Paradise that no human eye has ever seen, no ear has ever heard, and no human mind has ever conceived, are available on demand. And, these fruits have extraordinary properties: they are perpetually in season, their taste is never bitter or rotten, and their availability is never interrupted by winter frosts or seasonal scarcity: *“its fruit is constant, and its shade”* (13:35).

First, security from scarcity: They are secure from the fruit ever running out, being depleted, or becoming inaccessible (*lā maqṭūʿatin wa-lā mamnūʿah*, 56:33).

## Verse 44:56

> There they will never taste death, beyond the first death. And He will protect them from the punishment of the Hellfire—

**The Theological Grammar of "Illā l-Mawtata l-Ūlā"**

Classical grammarians and exegetes, including al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and al-Qurṭubī, explored the exceptional clause *illā l-mawtata l-ūlā* (except the first death). In Arabic syntax, this exception is classified as discontinuous (*istithnāʾ munqaṭiʿ*), carrying the meaning of *lākin* (but, however, beyond): “They will never taste death in Paradise; but rather, they only experienced that single, initial death of the earthly world.”

**The Slaughter of Death in Prophetic Tradition**

This eternal immunity from mortality was depicted by the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ through one of the most vivid symbolic narratives in Islamic the teaching about the Last Day. In a renowned tradition recorded in both *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (4730) and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2849), the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:.

*“On the Day of Resurrection, death will be brought forward in the form of a white-spotted ram, and it will be made to stand between Paradise and the Hellfire. A caller will cry out: ‘O people of Paradise!’ They will crane their necks and look up. He will ask: ‘Do you recognize this?’ They will reply: ‘Yes, this is death!’ For each of them had tasted it. Then the caller will cry out: ‘O people of the Fire!’ They will crane their necks and look up. He will ask: ‘Do you recognize this?’ They will reply: ‘Yes, this is death!’ Then the ram will be slaughtered between Paradise and the Fire. The caller will then proclaim: ‘O people of Paradise, eternity and no death! O people of the Fire, eternity and no death!’”* The Prophet then recited: *“And warn them, [O Prophet], of the Day of Regret, when the matter will be concluded; and [yet], they are in [a state of] heedlessness, and they do not believe”* (19:39).

This monumental tradition illuminates the verse with extraordinary emotional power. The slaughter of the ram means the total, permanent destruction of mortality itself. For the dwellers of Paradise, this announcement is the pinnacle of joy: their bliss is certified as eternal, never to be disturbed by the shadow of the grave.

**Divine Protection as the Foundation of Salvation (Wa-Waqāhum)**

The second clause delivers the foundational blessing that made this eternal life possible: *wa-waqāhum ʿadhāba l-jaḥīm* — And He will protect them from the punishment of the Hellfire. The verb *waqā* (from the root *w-q-y*, the exact root of *taqwā*) means actively shielding, deflecting, warding off, and providing an unbreachable barrier against catastrophic danger.

**The Contrast with the Living Death of the Damned**

The eternal life of the righteous in Paradise stands in stark, terrifying opposition to the unending *living death* endured by the damned in the Hellfire. For the dwellers of the Fire, death is the very thing they desperately crave, yet it is perpetually denied to them: *“And they will cry, ‘O Mālik! Let your Lord put an end to us!’ He will say, ‘Indeed, you will remain!’”* (43:77).

As Sūrah al-Aʿlā describes: *“in which he will neither die nor live”* (87:13). The damned are trapped in an agonizing suspension between life and death: suffering lethal trauma without the relief of extinction. The righteous, in contrast, enjoy true, vibrant, luminous life without the slightest taint of mortality. Verse 56 illustrates the ultimate parting of ways: the righteous are freed from death forever into joy, while the unrepentant are trapped forever in a death that never dies.

## Verse 44:57

> as ˹an act of˺ grace from your Lord. That is ˹truly˺ the ultimate triumph

**The Exegetical Synthesis of Faith, Works, and Grace**

Classical theologians, including al-Ghazālī, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, and Ibn Kathīr, harmonized this verse with Qur'anic passages that seem to condition Paradise upon human deeds, such as: *“Enter Paradise for what you used to do”* (16:32). They explained that human righteousness is the *condition* of reception, while divine grace (*faḍl*) is the *cause* of bestowal.

**The Contrast with the Worldly Triumphs of the Deniers**

The pronouncement of *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm* delivers a devastating counter-narrative to the arrogant boasts of the Quraysh elite and the imperial tyrants of history. Pharaoh boasted of his rivers and palaces, viewing his enslavement of the Israelites as a triumph; yet his triumph ended in watery disgrace. The Makkan chieftains boasted of their commercial trade and tribal status, viewing their persecution of the Muslims as political dominance; yet their triumph ended in the dust of Badr and the fires of Hell.

The impoverished believers—men and women who were beaten, starved, and driven from their homes—are the ones who achieve *the ultimate triumph*.

## Verse 44:58

> Indeed, We have made this ˹Quran˺ easy in your own language ˹O Prophet˺ so perhaps they will be mindful

**The Linguistic Miracle: Bi-Lisānika**

The prepositional phrase *bi-lisānika* (in your own tongue, in your language) carries profound historical and literary significance. It means that the Qur'an was communicated in the pure, eloquent dialect of the Quraysh—the very language spoken, understood, and celebrated by the immediate audience of Makkah. The Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ was not an alien speaking a foreign tongue; he was one of their own, reciting words whose vocabulary, idioms, and grammatical structures were the pride of Arabian literary culture.

This facilitation stripped away their final linguistic excuse. Had the Qur'an been revealed in a foreign language, the Makkans would have immediately protested: *“Why are its verses not explained in detail? What! A foreign tongue and an Arab messenger?”* (41:44). By revealing the Book in their mother tongue, God made the message transparent. The clarity of the Arabic prose was so breathtaking that the greatest poets of the peninsula stood dumbfounded, recognizing that this speech had a cadence and psychological depth far beyond human capacity.

**The Facets of Qur'anic Ease (Yusr)**

The commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī, identified the multiple dimensions of ease (*yusr*) encompassed by this verse:.

Third, ease of conveyance: The Prophet ﷺ was granted a tongue of peerless eloquence and clarity, enabling him to deliver complex metaphysical realities in short, unforgettable verses that transformed a fragmented tribal society into the architects of an enlightened global civilization.

**The Structural Symmetry with the Sūrah’s Opening**

A magnificent literary symmetry connects verse 58 with verses 2 and 3 at the beginning of Sūrah ad-Dukhān. The sūrah opened with an oath upon the *clear Book* (*al-Kitāb al-mubīn*) sent down upon a blessed night to *warn against evil* (*kunnā mundhirīn*). Now, at the close of the discourse, the text returns to the Book, explaining how its clarity was achieved: through divine facilitation *in your own language*.

## Verse 44:59

> Wait then! They too are certainly waiting

**The Last Word of the Sūrah**
The sūrah closes with a short command to the Prophet ﷺ: wait, then, for they too are waiting. Three words in the Arabic carry the whole argument of the sūrah to its resting place. The opening word ties the command to everything said before it. The Book is clear, history has already spoken, creation has a purpose, and the Day of Decision is fixed. Once all of that is settled, there is nothing left to argue about.

The Prophet ﷺ is told to stop wearing himself out over people who will not accept the message, and to take up a calm watch instead. The verb means to stand on high ground and watch with steady attention, expecting something to happen. It echoes an earlier command in the same sūrah, where he was told to watch for the day when the sky brings a visible haze (44:10 gives that first command). The command to watch opens that discussion and closes it here, so the sūrah forms a circle.

**Two Kinds of Waiting**
The last clause sets the two sides against each other: they are waiting too. Both parties are watching the horizon, and they are watching for opposite things. The leaders of Quraysh were waiting for the Prophet ﷺ to die, hoping that an assassination, an illness or plain time would end the message and give them back their undisputed place. The sūrah of the Mountain records the whisper they passed around, that he was a poet for whom they were waiting for death to strike, and the command given back to him: wait, then; I am waiting with you (52:30–31 give both).

The Prophet ﷺ was waiting for something else with steady confidence: the proof of what God had promised, the victory of the message in Arabia, the humbling of proud rulers and the settling of accounts on the Day of Decision. Both sides were waiting. Only one side was waiting on something real.

**How the Waiting Ended**
The answer came quickly and completely. Within a few years the waiting of the leaders ended at Badr, where the chieftains who expected the Prophet's ruin marched out and were destroyed, their bodies thrown into a dry well, as the warning of the crushing blow had said (44:16 gives that warning). Eight years later the Prophet ﷺ entered Makkah as a conqueror who chose mercy, clearing the Kaʿbah of its idols while reciting that truth has come and falsehood has vanished, and that falsehood was bound to vanish (17:81 records the words). The waiting of the deniers produced defeat, shame and lasting regret. The waiting of the Messenger produced victory in this world and honour with his Lord in the next.
