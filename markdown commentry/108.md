# Sūrah al-Kawthar (Chapter 108) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary
## Sūrah al-Kawthar 108:1

> Indeed, We have granted you ˹O Prophet˺ abundant goodness.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Opening That Is a Gift**

The sūrah opens without a question, without a warning, without a narrative: "Indeed, We have granted you abundant goodness." The form is the bare declaration of a bestowal, and the declaration is the entire first verse. The sūrah is the shortest in the Book, three verses in all, and its first verse is the statement of a gift so large that the name of the sūrah is the name of the gift. The opening is not a response to a problem stated in the text; the problem is stated in the occasion, and the occasion will be named in the third verse. The first verse simply places the gift in the hand of the one addressed, and the placing is done in the perfective: it is granted, it is done, it is the fact from which the sūrah starts.

**The Word Kawthar and Its Range**

The gift is named in a word, kawthar, that comes from the root of the much, the abundant, the copious, and the word is used in Arabic for the abundance in the number, the measure, the weight, and the quality. The exegetes recorded a wide range of readings of the word in this verse, sixteen or more in the count of one of the summaries, and the range runs from the concrete to the general. The most widespread of the readings takes the word to be a proper name, the name of a river in the garden, and the reading is so established that the phrase for being in the garden, in the common speech of the Muslims, is the sitting by the Kawthar. The name of the sūrah is therefore the name of a place, and the place is the gift, and the gift is stated as already given.

**The River in the Accounts That Describe It**

The accounts that surround the verse give the river its form. In the account of the ascent, the one received through Anas, the Prophet passed a river whose banks were the tents of the hollow pearl, and when he asked the name of it, the answer was the Kawthar. The descriptions that the tradition preserves are in the same key: the banks of gold, the water flowing over the pearl and the ruby, the scent finer than the musk, the water sweeter than the honey and whiter than the milk. The report received in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (3362) states the function of the river: the Prophet is the predecessor of the community at the fountain, and the one who drinks from it will not be thirsty after. The river is therefore not an image without a function; it is the gift that the third verse of the sūrah will connect with the one who is cut off. The river is not given to the one alone. The accounts that the tradition preserves join the gift to the community: the community is the one that will be brought to the pool on the day of the account, and the river is the meeting point of the one who is granted and the many who follow. The abundance of the first verse is therefore an abundance with a width: it is the goodness of the one, and it is the goodness of the line that comes behind him.

**The Abundance Beyond the River**

The other readings of the word tie the gift to the other stations of the prophethood. Some took it to be the gift of the message itself, the prophethood that is the source of the rest. Others took it to be the Book, the abundance of the guidance. Others yet distributed it over the stations of the religion: the prayers, the testimony, the light in the breast that guides to the way. The summary received from al-Ṭabarsī holds the readings together: each is a part of the whole, and the whole is the abundant goodness in the two abodes, this world and the next. The reading that is widest is therefore the reading that contains the others: the gift is the abundance, and the river is the name of the abundance in its lasting form, the form that does not run dry.

**The Shortest Sūrah and the Largest Word**

The form of the sūrah matches its content, and the matching is the method. The Book contains no sūrah shorter than the three verses of the Kawthar, and the shortness is not the shortness of the content; it is the shortness of the form that carries a content without a measure. The gift named in the first verse is the one that cannot be exhausted, and the sūrah that carries it is the one that cannot be lengthened, because the lengthening would be a measure, and the gift is without a measure. The sūrah is therefore the form of the thing it states: small in the eye, without a bottom in the fact. The first verse is the statement, and the statement is the whole of the first verse, because there is nothing left to say that the word does not contain.

**The Gift That the Response Will Complete**

The first verse places the gift, and the sūrah's remaining two verses will state the response and the judgment. The structure of the three verses is the structure of the relation: the bestowal, the worship, and the one who is cut off. The first verse is therefore the premise, and the premise is the gift, and the gift is named, and the naming is the name of the sūrah. The sūrah opens on the name, and the opening is the claim: the abundance is real, it is granted, and it is the fact from which the rest of the sūrah moves. The first verse is the ground, and the ground is the gift, and everything that follows stands on it.

## Sūrah al-Kawthar 108:2

> So pray and sacrifice to your Lord ˹alone˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The "So" That Completes the Gift**

The second verse begins with the conjunction that marks the consequence: "so." The gift stated in the first verse produces the response stated in the second, and the response is two verbs: the prayer and the sacrifice. The sūrah's structure is therefore the structure of the relation: the bestowal calls for the worship, and the worship is the two acts named in the verse. The "so" is the hinge, and the hinge is the claim: the response to the abundant goodness is not the thanks in the words but the prayer and the sacrifice in the act. The sūrah does not say "give thanks"; it says "pray and sacrifice," and the choice of the two verbs is the form of the thanks.

**The Order: The Prayer Before the Sacrifice**

The order of the two verbs is the second argument of the verse. The prayer comes first, and the sacrifice second, and the order is the rule that the jurists derived from the verse, in the case of the feast of the sacrifice: the congregational prayer of the feast is performed before the slaughter, and the slaughter that is performed before the prayer is not the slaughter that the verse commands. The derivation is the standing position of the schools, and it is one of the few cases in which a legal rule rests on the order of two verbs in a single verse. The sūrah's structure therefore carries the law: the worship of the day begins in the standing before the Lord, and only after the standing does the giving of the animal take place. The order is the argument, and the argument is that the relation to the Lord comes before the relation to the gift.

**The Two Meanings of the Second Verb**

The verb that is translated "sacrifice" carries a second meaning in the Arabic, and the second meaning is the raising of the hand. The exegetes recorded both readings, and the reading of the sacrifice is the one that is connected with the feast and the pilgrimage, and the reading of the raising is the one that is connected with the standing of the prayer. The practice that the raising describes is the raising of the hands to the ears at the opening of the prayer, with the word of the greatness, and the schools of the law agree on the raising at the opening, and they differ on the repetition of it in the bowing and the prostration. The two readings of the verb therefore cover the two acts of the verse: the raising is within the prayer, and the sacrifice is after it, and the single verb carries both, in the width of the Arabic.

**"To Your Lord" — The Object That Holds the Two Verbs**

The two verbs share one object, and the object is the Lord: the prayer is offered to the Lord, and the sacrifice is offered to the Lord, and the translation marks the exclusivity of the object with the word "alone." The sharing of the object is the third argument of the verse. The gift of the first verse came from the Lord, and the response of the second goes to the Lord, and the sūrah's structure is a line from the Lord to the one addressed and back. The "alone" of the response is the form of the tawḥīd in the sūrah: the abundance is from one, and the worship is to one, and the two are the same one. The verse does not argue the point; it states it in the grammar, in the single object that the two verbs share.

**The Sacrifice That Feeds the Poor**

The second verb of the verse is the one that connects the sūrah to its neighbor. The sūrah that precedes the Kawthar in the sequence of the warnings ended on the hand that withholds the smallest kindness, and the sūrah of the Kawthar ends its worship in the act of the sacrifice, and the meat of the sacrifice is the meat that feeds the poor. The connection is not stated in the text, and it is the connection of the arrangement: the sūrah of the withheld hand is followed by the sūrah of the offered sacrifice, and the two sūrahs together state the two directions of the hand. The sūrah of the Kawthar therefore completes the movement that the sūrah of the Māʿūn began: the hand is opened where it was closed, and the opening is the sacrifice. The second verse is the station of the opening.

**The Response in Two Acts**

The second verse is the middle of the sūrah, and the middle is the response. The first verse is the gift, and the third will be the judgment, and the second is the act that stands between the two. The form of the response is two acts, and the two acts are the personal and the communal: the prayer is the act of the one, and the sacrifice is the act that ends in the many, because the meat of the sacrifice is divided, and the division is the giving. The sūrah's response is therefore complete in its form: it begins in the standing of the one before the Lord, and it ends in the table of the community. The two verbs of the verse are the two ends of the line, and the line is the worship, from the prayer of the individual to the feeding of the city. The two verbs also state the two directions of the religion. The prayer is the direction upward, the standing of the one before the Lord, and the sacrifice is the direction outward, the act that ends at the table of the many. The practice that the tradition receives joins the three: the owner eats of the meat, the poor are fed from it, and a portion is given. The worship of the sūrah therefore moves from the standing to the table, and the movement is the form of the response.

## Sūrah al-Kawthar 108:3

> Only the one who hates you is truly cut off ˹from any goodness˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word They Used, Returned to Them**

The third verse is the verse that takes the taunt of the enemy and returns it. The word abtar, the one who is cut off, was the word the Quraysh used of the Prophet, and the sūrah uses it back, and the using is the reversal. In the form of the taunt, the word was a charge: the man without the posterity, the one whose name will die with him. In the form of the sūrah, the word is a verdict: the one who is cut off is the one who is cut off from the goodness, and the one who is cut off from the goodness is the one who hates. The sūrah does not argue against the taunt; it changes the meaning of the word, and the changing is the answer.

**The Fact That Provoked the Sūrah**

The occasion of the sūrah is the fact that the taunt stated. The two sons of the Prophet who were born to him in Makkah, Qāsim and ʿAbd Allāh, both died in the age of the toddler, and the third son, Ibrāhīm, who was born after the migration, died in the same age. The man of Makkah who repeated the taunt, in the account the tradition preserves, was al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil: when the name of the Prophet was mentioned, he would say that the man was without the posterity, and that when he died, no one would remember him, and the sūrah was revealed in response. The fact behind the taunt is the fact that the sūrah addresses, and the addressing is the third verse, the last word of the shortest sūrah.

**The Redefinition of the Cutting Off**

The sūrah's answer is the redefinition of the term. The cutting off, in the sense of the sūrah, is not the absence of the children; it is the absence of the goodness, and the absence of the goodness is the state of the one who hates the one to whom the goodness was granted. The word abtar is used in the Arabic for the thing whose effects bring no good, and the sūrah takes the word in that sense: the one whose life brings no good is the one who is cut off, whatever the number of his children. The redefinition is the heart of the verse. The taunt measured the man by the sons, and the sūrah measures him by the relation, and the relation is the one the third verse names: the hatred of the one who is granted.

**The Posterity That Does Not Die**

The Book states its own measure of the permanence that the taunt denies. The day on which the account is kept is the day on which neither the wealth nor the children are of any benefit, and the one who comes before the Lord with the pure heart is the one who is saved (26:88–89). The measure is the heart, not the line of descent, and the sūrah's redefinition is the application of the measure to the case of the taunt. The Prophet's legacy, in the sense of the Book, is the legacy of the heart and the deed, and the legacy is the one that outlives the sons. The name of the one who was called without posterity is recited in the daily prayer of the Muslims, in the testimony that closes the bowing, and the recitation is the fact that the sūrah's redefinition was the fact. The cutting off, in the end, was the one who cut himself off.

**The Hatred as the State of the Cutting Off**

The third verse names the state in a word that is stronger than the taunt: the one who is cut off is the one who hates. The taunt was a statement about a fact, the absence of the sons; the sūrah is a statement about a state, the hatred of the one who is granted. The state is the one that the first two verses set up: the one who hates the gift, and the one who hates the one who is given the gift, is the one who is excluded from it, because the gift is the relation, and the relation is closed to the one who closes it. The sūrah's logic is the logic of the first verse: the abundant goodness is the relation to the Lord, and the one who hates the Lord's grant is the one who is cut off from it. The hatred is therefore not an addition to the cutting off; it is the cutting off, in its form. The state has a logic of its own. To hate the one who is given the gift is to hate the gift in the place where it stands, and the gift stands in the relation. The man who hates the relation excludes himself from it, and the exclusion is the cutting off: not something done to him from outside, but something that happens in him by the nature of the hatred. The sūrah's verdict is therefore a statement about the mechanism, not only about the case.

**The Last Word of the Shortest Sūrah**

The sūrah ends on the one who is cut off, and the ending is the method. The three verses move from the gift to the response to the judgment, and the judgment is placed on the one who hates, and the one who hates is the one who made the taunt. The sūrah leaves its audience holding the verdict, and the verdict is in the form of the word they used: the one who is cut off is the one you thought you were naming. The shortest sūrah of the Book ends on the longest claim: the abundance is the relation, the response is the worship, and the cutting off is the hatred. The last word is the word of the enemy, and the enemy's word is the sūrah's verdict, and the verdict stands where the taunt was spoken.
