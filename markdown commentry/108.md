# Sūrah al-Kawthar (Chapter 108) — Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Shortest Chapter in the Qur'an**

Al-Kawthar, "Abundance," is the one-hundred-and-eighth chapter: three verses, ten words, and the shortest sūrah in the Book. It was revealed in Makkah, though some authorities place it among the early Madinan revelations. Its name comes from the gift promised in verse 1 — *"Indeed, We have granted you ˹O Prophet˺ abundant goodness"* — a word wide enough to cover a river in Paradise and everything else the Prophet ﷺ was given.

**What Al-Kawthar Means**

*Kawthar* comes from a root meaning abundance, whether in number, measure, weight, or quality. Commentators recorded a wide range of readings of the word in this verse; al-Ṭabarsī gathered more than a dozen, and the widest sense holds the others together: the abundant good given to the Prophet ﷺ in this world and the next. The best-known reading takes it as a proper name for a river in Paradise, and the phrase became so established that Muslim speech speaks of being in the Garden as "sitting by the Kawthar." The accounts describe the river's banks of gold, its bed of pearls and rubies, its soil of musk, and water whiter than milk and sweeter than honey. In a report from Anas in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (3362), the Prophet ﷺ told the Companions that his community will be brought to the basin on the Day of Resurrection, and that whoever drinks from it will never be thirsty again. So the gift is not for him alone: the river is where the Prophet and those who follow him meet.

**The Occasion: The Taunt of the Abtar**

The sūrah answers a specific insult. The Prophet ﷺ buried his infant sons in Makkah — al-Qāsim and ʿAbdullāh — and Ibrāhīm, born later in Madinah, died at the same age. In pre-Islamic Arabia a man left without surviving sons was called *abtar*, "severed," and it was assumed that his name would die with him. Al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil al-Sahmī, a chieftain of Makkah, said of the Prophet ﷺ, in the report the tradition preserves, that he was a man without posterity who would be forgotten once he died and that his companions would be rid of him. The sūrah came down answering that taunt, and the answer is delivered in its last verse.

## Sūrah al-Kawthar 108:1

> Indeed, We have granted you ˹O Prophet˺ abundant goodness

**A Gift Stated as Already Given**

The sūrah opens with no question, no warning, and no narrative — only the declaration of a bestowal. In Arabic it is one sentence with an emphatic opening, and the verb is in the completed form: the gift is announced as a fact already in place, not as a promise for later. Nothing in the verse names the problem it answers; the problem lies in the occasion, and the occasion is dealt with in verse 3. The first verse simply places the gift in the hands of the one addressed and leaves it there.

**The Readings of the Word**

Commentators gave *kawthar* a range of meanings running from the specific to the general. Some took it as the name of the river in Paradise. Some took it as the Book, or as prophethood itself, the source of everything else. Others distributed it over the gifts the Prophet ﷺ was given: abundant followers, the intercession, a community that recites his name in every prayer. Al-Ṭabarsī's summary is that each reading describes part of the whole and none cancels another — the generous good granted to the Prophet ﷺ in this life and the next, of which the river is the lasting form. The scale is worth noticing: the sūrah that carries the widest gift in the Qur'an is the shortest sūrah in it, and the gift is named in a single word that has no measure.

## Sūrah al-Kawthar 108:2

> So pray and sacrifice to your Lord ˹alone˺

**The Response the Gift Requires**

The verse opens with *fa*, "so" — the conjunction of consequence. The gift of verse 1 produces the response of verse 2, and the response is two acts: prayer and sacrifice. Notably, the verse does not say "give thanks," though gratitude is what the two acts express. It names things the body does. Prayer is the standing before the Lord; sacrifice is the giving of something from one's wealth to feed others. Together they cover worship in its two directions, upward and outward.

**Why Prayer Comes Before Sacrifice**

The order of the two verbs is the first legal consequence drawn from this sūrah. The schools of law derive from it the rule of the Feast of Sacrifice: the congregational prayer of ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā is performed before the slaughter, and an animal slaughtered before the prayer does not fulfil the rite. This is one of the few cases where a standing rule rests on the order of two words within a single verse. The order carries the point of the sūrah as well: the relation to the Lord comes before the use of the gift He gave.

**A Second Reading of the Second Verb**

Some commentators read *naḥr* not as sacrifice but as the placing of the hand on the chest or the raising of the hands, taking the word from the same root as *naḥr*, the upper chest. On this reading the verse connects to the practice of raising the hands at the opening of the prayer, the *takbīrat al-iḥrām*, which the schools agree on while differing over its repetition in bowing and prostration. The two verbs would then describe one continuous act of worship rather than two separate ones, with the prayer encompassing both. The majority reading remains sacrifice, tied to the ʿĪd and to the pilgrimage.

**"To Your Lord": One Object for Two Verbs**

Both verbs share a single object, and the object is the Lord; the bracketed "alone" in the translation marks what the Arabic already implies. The sharing is the *tawḥīd* of the sūrah in miniature: the gift came from the Lord, and the response returns to Him, with no third party in the exchange. The verse does not argue the point, it states it in grammar — a single object cannot be shared with a rival.

**The Hand Opened**

The sūrah that precedes al-Kawthar in the Book, al-Māʿūn, closes with the worshipper who withholds the smallest kindness from a neighbour (107:7). This sūrah ends its act of worship with a sacrifice whose meat is distributed: the one who offers eats from it, the poor are fed, and a portion is given away. Read together, the two sūrahs show the two directions a hand can take. The worship of al-Kawthar begins with one person standing before his Lord and ends at the table of many.

## Sūrah al-Kawthar 108:3

> Only the one who hates you is truly cut off ˹from any goodness˺

**The Taunt Returned**

The last verse takes the enemy's own word and hands it back. *Abtar*, the one cut off, was the word used against the Prophet ﷺ — the man whose line would end. The sūrah does not argue about the premise or deny it; it changes what the word means. To be cut off is not to lack sons. It is to be cut off from the good, and the one cut off from the good is the one who hates the one to whom the good was given. The word *abtar* is used in Arabic of anything whose effects come to no good, and the sūrah applies that sense, which lets it answer the taunt without contesting the facts the taunt was built on.

**What Actually Survives**

The Qur'an states its own measure of lasting worth: on the Day of Judgment neither wealth nor children will benefit anyone, and the one who comes before his Lord with a sound heart will be saved (26:88–89). By that measure the taunt measured the wrong thing. The legacy of the Prophet ﷺ is the legacy of the heart and the deed, and it has outlasted every line of descent that mocked him: his name is recited with blessing in every Muslim prayer, in the testimony that closes each sitting, and from every minaret in the world. Al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, by contrast, was a wealthy chieftain with sons; his name survives today chiefly as the occasion of this verse. The judgement the sūrah pronounced was borne out by history.

**Why Hatred Is the Cutting Off**

The verse names hatred, not misfortune, as the state of being cut off, and that is the logic of the sūrah from beginning to end. The gift is a relation to the Lord; the response is worship offered back to Him; the one who hates the one who received the gift has shut himself out of that relation. The cutting off is not something imposed on the hater from outside — it is what hating the good does to the person who holds the hatred. So the shortest chapter in the Qur'an closes with its largest claim: the abundance is real, the response is worship, and the loss belongs to the one who hates.
