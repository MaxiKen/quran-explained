# Sūrah al-Ṭāriq (Chapter 86) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Piercing Star and the Unveiling of the Secrets**

Aṭ-Ṭāriq, "The Night Comer" or "The Piercing Star," is the eighty-sixth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 17 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with an arresting celestial oath: *“By the sky and the Night Comer (*wa-l-samā'i wa-l-ṭāriq*)! And what can make you know what is the Night Comer? It is the piercing star (*al-najmu al-thāqib*)!”* (86:1–3). *Aṭ-Ṭāriq* signifies the visitor that arrives in the dark of night, knocking upon the door of human consciousness, identified as a pulsating, radiant star whose light pierces through the fathomless blackness of the cosmos. In seventeen concise, rhythmic verses, Sūrah aṭ-Ṭāriq conjoins cosmic physics with human embryology, establishing that just as every star is watched over in the celestial heavens, so too has every human soul been assigned an angelic guardian. It culminates in the terrifying, intimate revelation of the Day of Judgment: *Yawm tublā al-sarā'ir*—the Day when all hidden secrets are exposed to the light.

**The Drop of Ejected Fluid and the Denial of Resurrection**

The historical context of aṭ-Ṭāriq reflects the stubborn skepticism of the Qurayshi polytheists, who mocked the claim that decomposed bones could be resurrected. Sūrah aṭ-Ṭāriq answers this incredulity by commanding the human being to look at his own physical origin: *“So let man observe from what he was created! He was created from a fluid, ejected, emerging from between the backbone and the ribs”* (86:5–7). The sūrah argues with unshakeable logic: the God Who engineered a conscious, articulate human being out of a microscopic drop of ejected fluid is undeniably competent to resurrect that human being after death: *“Indeed, He, to return him [to life], is Able!”* (86:8).

**The Structural Modulation and the Piercing Light**

The architecture of Sūrah aṭ-Ṭāriq moves across three distinct movements:
1. The Piercing Star and the Angelic Guardian (1–4): Swears by the heavens and the pulsating night star that pierces the darkness, establishing the universal law of verse 4: *“There is no soul but that it has over it a guardian (*in kullu nafsin lammā ʿalayhā ḥāfiẓ*).”*
2. The Biological Miracle and the Unveiling of Secrets (5–10): Directs man to contemplate his creation from the ejected fluid emerging from between the loins and ribs, confirming God's power of resurrection. It delivers the chilling eschatological reality of verses 9–10: *“The Day when all secrets will be put on trial / exposed (*Yawma tublā al-sarā'ir*)! Then man will have no power and no helper!”* On that Day, private motives, hidden hypocrisies, and secret sins will be displayed openly for all creation to see.
3. The Rhythmic Oaths and the Deceptive Plot (11–17): Swears by the sky which returns rain in cycles (*dhāti al-rajʿ*) and the earth which cracks open with sprouting vegetation (*dhāti al-ṣadʿ*), proclaiming the absolute authority of scripture: *“Indeed, the Qur'an is a decisive statement (*qawlun faṣl*), and it is not amusement!”* (86:13–14). It exposes the plots of the Quraysh: *“Indeed, they are planning a plot, but I am planning a plot.”* The chapter concludes with the sovereign divine command of patience to the Prophet: *“So give respite to the disbelievers; leave them for a little while (*fa-mahhili al-kāfirīna amhilhum ruwaydā*)!”*

**The Piercing Star and the Exposed Secret**

The supreme spiritual insight of Sūrah aṭ-Ṭāriq is the correspondence between *Al-Najm al-Thāqib* (the star that pierces the darkness of space) and *Tublā al-Sarā'ir* (the divine light that pierces the secrets of the human heart). A person may hide his greed, lust, and malice behind pleasant smiles and noble speeches in the darkness of this world; but the eye of the Guardian Angel watches over every heartbeat. On the Day of *Aṭ-Ṭāriq*, the light of divine truth will pierce through every facade, leaving no soul with any power or helper except sincere faith in the Lord of the heavens and earth.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:1

> By the heaven and the nightly star

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath in the Pair**

The sūrah that follows al-Burūj opens with the sister oath, and the two openings read as a pair across the boundary of the two sūrahs. The first sūrah swore by the sky full of constellations, the whole ordered heavens seen across the night, and this sūrah swears by the heaven and the nightly star, the single moving point inside the same heavens. One oath takes the pattern, the other takes the point that moves inside the pattern, and the two together cover the sky the way a map and a needle cover a region. The pairing is not merely structural. The two sūrahs are among those recited repeatedly in the daily prayers, and the report that the Prophet ﷺ would recite them together in the afternoon prayer shows how the early community heard them as one unit. The oath of this verse is therefore the continuation of the oath of the first sūrah, the same guarantee stated from the position of the single star rather than the position of the whole. The two sūrahs are also the two halves of the night: the sūrah of the constellations covers the sky as it is seen across the whole night, and the sūrah of the nightly star covers the sky at the hour of the arrival, and the two together cover the life of the sky. The pairing is the pairing of the sūrahs, and the pairing is what the sūrah's opening states about its own place in the Book.

**The Word That Knocks**

The name of the sūrah is the name of the star, al-ṭāriq, and the name is built on the verb for the knocking, ṭaraqa. The word carries the sense of the one who strikes at the door, and the image is the image of the arrival: the doors are closed at night, and the one who comes at that hour must knock to be admitted. The name is therefore the name of the arrival that is not admitted by the opening of the day but by the knocking at the closed door. The sūrah takes the name from the motion rather than from the object, and the motion is the striking, the coming, the breaking of the silence of the closed hour. The exegetes also take the word to carry the sense of the path, because the path is struck by the feet that walk upon it, and the reading keeps the sense of the striking while moving it from the door to the ground. The name is the name of the coming, and the coming is what the sūrah swears by.

**The Readings of the Nightly Star**

The exegetes divide over what the nightly star is, and the division is the division of the name. The readings record a specific star, the star that comes by night and is gone with the day, and they record the celestial bodies in general, the bodies that appear at the night and disappear at the dawn. A body of the readings connects the name to the morning star, the star that is called the visitor because it comes at the hour of the visitors, and the connection keeps the sense of the arrival inside the name. The readings differ on the object and agree on the motion: the object is the star, whatever the star is, and the motion is the coming by night, the arrival at the closed hour. The sūrah's oath is on the motion, and the motion is what the oath guarantees. The division of the exegetes is the division of the name's object, and the name's motion is common to all of them.

**The Oath and Its Question**

The sūrah does something unusual in the two verses that follow the oath. It swears by the nightly star, and then it asks what the nightly star is, as if the oath had sworn by a thing it did not name. The move is the sūrah's signature: the oath states the guarantee, and the question states the object of the guarantee, and the definition that follows the question completes the oath. The sūrah's structure is the structure of the definition: the thing is sworn by first, the thing is questioned second, and the thing is defined third. The three moves are the three verses of the sūrah's opening, and they are the sūrah's way of marking the star as the object of its own argument. The star is not a decoration at the beginning of the sūrah; the star is the sūrah's first exhibit, and the sūrah swears by the exhibit before it describes it.

**The Door with Three Panels**

The first verse of the sūrah is the door of the sūrah, and the door has three panels. The heaven is the panel of the whole, the ordered sky that carries the star; the nightly star is the panel of the point, the moving light inside the whole; and the knocking in the name is the panel of the motion, the arrival that the whole and the point are there to carry. The three panels together are the sūrah's opening image, and the image is the image of the guarantee: the whole order, the moving point, and the arriving motion. The sūrah that follows al-Burūj takes the same image of the guarantee and states it from the night rather than from the day, and the night is the hour of the sūrah, the hour of the closed door and the knocking visitor. The first verse is the door, and the door opens on the definition, and the definition opens on the sūrah's argument about the soul. The door also does the sūrah's quiet work of naming its form. The sūrah's form is the form of the guarantee, and the guarantee is the form the sūrah will carry through its two arguments. The first verse states the form, and the form is what the sūrah's later verses assume.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:2

> And what will make you realize what the nightly star is

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question After the Oath**

The sūrah swears by the nightly star in the first verse, and in the second verse it asks what the nightly star is. The question is not the question of the ignorant: the sūrah does not ask for information it lacks. It is the question of the emphasis, the Book's standing form for marking the next statement as the definition. The sūrah has sworn by the star, and the oath is complete as a guarantee, but the sūrah wants the listener to hold the star as the object of the guarantee, and the question is the instrument of the holding. The form of the question is the form of the call: what will make you realize, the sūrah asks, and the form addresses the listener rather than the star. The star is not questioned; the listener is addressed, and the address is the sūrah's way of making the next verse the definition that the listener receives. It serves as the hinge between the oath and the definition, and the hinge is what turns the sūrah from the guarantee to the content.

**The Book's Standing Form**

The form of the question is the Book's standing form, and the Book uses it at its moments of definition. "And what will make you realize what the Inevitable Hour is?" (69:3). "And what will make you realize what Hell is?" (74:27). "And what will make you realize what the Day of Decision is?" (77:14). "What will make you realize what Judgment Day is?" (82:17). The form appears in the sūrahs that define their objects, the hour, the fire, the day, and the star, and in each case the form marks the next verse as the definition of the named thing. The sūrah's use of the form is the use of the form in its family: the thing is named, the thing is questioned, and the thing is defined. The sūrah stands in the family of the Book's definitional moments, and the standing is the sūrah's statement that what follows is the definition, not the commentary. The form is also the form of the address. The sūrah does not state the definition directly; the sūrah addresses the listener and asks him what will make him realize, and the address is what makes the definition the listener's own reception rather than the sūrah's deposit. The form is the family's form, and the family's function is the sūrah's function.

**The Two Questions in One**

The verse contains two questions in the one form, and the two questions are the two parts of the definition. The first question asks by what the listener will come to realize: what is the means of the knowledge? The second question asks what the object of the knowledge is: what is the nightly star? The form holds the two questions in one breath, and the answer in the next verse gives the object rather than the means, because the means of the knowledge is the answer itself. The sūrah's grammar is the grammar of the definition: the thing is defined by the naming, and the naming is the knowledge. The two questions are the two sides of the one act of defining, and the act is completed in the next verse, where the star is named as the star of the piercing brightness. The two questions are the two halves of the act the sūrah performs on its own opening, and the act is the act of the naming that the oath had only begun. The verse is the question, and the sūrah's answer is the name, and the name is the realization the question asked for.

**The Question as the Marker**

The function of the question in the sūrah's structure is the function of the marker. The sūrah's opening is the sequence of the oath, the question, and the definition, and the question is the marker between the two. Without the marker, the definition would read as a comment on the oath, and the sūrah wants the definition to read as the substance of the oath. The marker is the sūrah's way of separating the two: the oath is the guarantee, and the definition is the content, and the line between them is the question. The marker is also the sūrah's way of addressing the listener at the exact point where the sūrah changes from the act of swearing to the act of stating. The listener is addressed at the change, and the address is the sūrah's reminder that the definition is for the listener, not for the star. The pause of the sūrah is the question, and the pause is where the listener enters the sūrah.

**The Narrowing from the Whole to the Point**

The first verse of the sūrah covered the heaven, the whole of the ordered sky, and the second verse narrows to the single star inside the heaven. The narrowing is the sūrah's first move of argument, and the move is from the whole to the point. The whole was the panel of the order, and the point is the panel of the motion, and the sūrah's argument needs the point rather than the whole, because the motion of the point is what the sūrah's later argument about the soul will echo. The soul of the sūrah's next verses is the moving point inside the whole of the human being, the point that is guarded, considered, and returned, and the star of the opening is the image of that point. The sūrah's narrowing from the heaven to the star is the narrowing from the image to the argument, and the argument is the sūrah's second unit, the unit that begins with the soul in the next verse. What turns the sūrah from the sky to the soul is the question, and the turn is marked by the form.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:3

> ˹It is˺ the star of piercing brightness

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Definition Given**

The sūrah's question is answered in two words of substance: the star, the piercing one. The Arabic, al-thāqib al-shāʿir, gives the star its two marks, the mark of the piercing and the mark of the shining, and the two marks are the sūrah's definition of the object of the oath. The star is not named by its position in the sky; it is named by what it does, and the two things it does are the piercing and the shining. The sūrah's definition is the definition by the function rather than by the name, and the function is what the sūrah's argument needs. The oath in the first verse was the guarantee, and the question in the second was the marker, and the definition in this verse is the substance, and the substance is the star as the light that cuts the darkness. The sūrah's opening is complete at this verse, and the completion is the completion of the guarantee: the star is the thing the sūrah swears by, and the star is the light that pierces. The definition is also the sūrah's answer to the form of the oath. The oath named the star by its motion, the coming by night, and the definition names the star by its light, the piercing, and the two together are the star's whole mark. The sūrah's opening is the opening of the mark, and the mark is what the sūrah's argument carries.

**The Readings of the Star**

The exegetes record the readings of the star with their grounds, and the readings are the readings of the object rather than of the function. The majority takes the star to be the Pleiades, the cluster that the Arab sky knew by name, and the reading carries the sense of the most conspicuous of the star-groups. Others take the star to be one of the planets, Saturn or Venus, and the reading takes the planet as the star that moves inside the fixed heaven. Others take the star to be the meteor, the light that crosses the night and is gone, and the reading takes the motion of the crossing as the mark of the piercing. A minority takes the definition to be open: any star that glitters is the star, because the mark is the glittering rather than the object. The readings differ on the object and agree on the mark: the star is called upon because it pierces the dark, and the piercing is what the sūrah's oath needs.

**The Word for the Piercing**

The word thāqib is the word for the piercing, the cutting through, the making of the hole in the solid. The star's light does not lift the darkness; the star's light cuts it, and the cutting is the image of the sūrah. The darkness of the night is the solid, and the light of the star is the point that goes through the solid, and the going-through is the piercing. The image is the image of the sūrah's later argument: the word of the sūrah is the word that pierces the delusion, and the star of the opening is the image of the word. The sūrah will state in its later verses that the word is the decisive word, and the decisive word is the word that cuts, and the cutting is the piercing of the star. The sūrah's opening image is the image of its later statement, and the image is the light through the solid.

**The Pleiades of the Arab Sky**

The majority reading gives the star the name the Arab sky already gave it: the Pleiades, the cluster of the seven stars that the region knew as the turning of the season. The arrival of the Pleiades marked the change of the season for the peoples of the region, and the cluster was the most conspicuous of the star-groups in the Arabian sky, the group that the night pointed to. The reading takes the star of the sūrah to be the star-group that the listener already knew by sight, and the taking is the sūrah's way of grounding the oath in the listener's own sky. The sūrah does not invent the star; the sūrah takes the star from the listener's night and swears by it. The grounding is the sūrah's method: the guarantee is the guarantee of the thing the listener sees, and the thing the listener sees is the cluster that turns the season.

**The Star That Guarantees**

The function of the star in the sūrah's argument is the function of the guarantor. The sūrah swears by the star, and the oath is the guarantee that the sūrah's statements will stand, and the star is the form of the standing: the light that pierces the darkness is the form of the word that pierces the delusion. The sūrah's later verses will state that the word is the decisive word and that it is not for amusement, and the state of the word is the state the star carries in its light. The star is the image of the word, and the image is what the sūrah's opening is for: the sūrah gives its argument a form before it gives its argument a content. The form is the light through the solid, and the content is the soul, the fluid, the return, and the Day. The star guarantees the content, and the guarantee is the sūrah's first act. The guarantee is the guarantee of the light. The light that pierces the darkness is the form of the word that pierces the delusion, and the form is what the sūrah's opening gives to its argument. The sūrah's argument will be the argument of the word, and the word is the light, and the light is the star.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:4

> There is no soul without a vigilant angel ˹recording everything˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn from the Star to the Soul**

The sūrah has completed its opening, the oath, the question, and the definition, and it turns now to the human being. The turn is the sūrah's first act of argument, and the act is the statement about the soul: there is no soul without the guardian. The sūrah does not announce the turn; the sūrah states the fact, and the fact is the fact about the soul of every person the sūrah addresses. The star of the opening was the moving point in the heaven, and the soul of this verse is the moving point in the human being, and the sūrah's argument moves from the image to the reality without a transition. The connection is the connection of the form: the point that moves inside the whole, the point that is not alone, the point that is held inside the order. The sūrah's statement about the soul is the statement that the point is held, and the holding is the guarding.

**The Guardian Named**

The word the sūrah gives the angel is the word for the guardian, the one who keeps watch, the one who holds the charge. The exegetes record the two functions of the guarding, and the two functions are the recording and the protection. The guardians who record are the angels assigned to the deeds of the individual, the ones who write what is done, and the Book names them in its standing verses: "while you are certainly observed by vigilant, honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺. They know whatever you do" (82:10–12), and "not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺" (50:18). The guardians who protect are the angels set over the people by the command of the Lord: "For each one there are successive angels before and behind, protecting them by Allah's command" (13:11). The two functions are the two sides of the guarding: the holding of the record and the holding of the person, and the sūrah's word carries both. The two functions are also the two directions of the guarding. The recording goes from the soul to the record, and the protection goes from the Lord to the soul, and the two directions together are the guarding's whole motion. The sūrah's word of the guardian is the word of the motion, and the motion is what the guarding is.

**The Totality of the Statement**

The verse states the relation in the form of the total: there is no soul without the guardian. The form is the form of the universal, and the universal is the sūrah's way of removing the exception. The statement is not that most souls are guarded; the statement is that the guarding is the condition of the soul, and the condition has no exception. The sūrah's grammar of the total is the grammar of the encompassing, the same grammar the sūrah of the pit used for the position of the deniers, and the use here is the use of the fact rather than the argument: the guarding is the fact of the soul's standing. The total is also the sūrah's answer to the thought that the soul might be alone: the soul is not alone, and the aloneness is what the sūrah's later verses will remove at the Day, and the removal is the removal of the aloneness of the soul before the Lord.

**The Record That Cannot Be Lost**

The function of the guardian in the sūrah's argument is the function of the record. The sūrah's second unit is the unit of the soul, and the unit moves from the guarding of the soul to the return of the soul, and the record is the link between the two. The soul is guarded now, and the guarding is the keeping of the record, and the record is what will be opened on the Day the sūrah will name in its ninth verse. The sūrah's argument about the soul is the argument of the record: the soul is written, and the writing is kept, and the keeping is opened. The record is the sūrah's bridge from this world to the Day, and the bridge is built in this verse. The guardian is the builder of the bridge, and the bridge is what makes the return of the next verses possible.

**The Soul Under Watch**

The implication of the verse for the human being is the implication of the standing under the watch. The life of the soul is the life under the guardian, and the life under the guardian is the life with the record running. The sūrah's later call to consider, in the fifth verse, is the call to the one who is watched, and the consideration is the consideration of the one whose deeds are being written. The sūrah does not describe the guardian as a terror; the sūrah describes the guardian as the fact, and the fact is what the sūrah's argument rests on. The soul under the watch is the soul that will stand on the Day with the record opened, and the standing is the standing of the one who was guarded. The verse is the sūrah's first statement about the soul, and the statement is the statement of the guard, and the guard is the sūrah's ground for everything that follows. The standing under the watch is also the sūrah's answer to the thought that the soul is alone in its deeds. The soul is not alone in its deeds; the soul's deeds are written, and the writing is the soul's company. The company is the guard, and the guard is the sūrah's first word of the soul's position.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:5

> Let people then consider what they were created from

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Call to Consider**

The sūrah stops describing and begins asking. The previous verse stated the fact of the guardian, and this verse turns to the human being with the imperative: let the person consider. The call is the sūrah's first address to the listener as an agent, and the address is the sūrah's change of method. The sūrah has done its work of stating, the oath and the definition and the guard, and now it hands the work to the listener: the considering is the listener's act, and the sūrah's act is the pointing. The imperative is the Book's standing instrument of the reminder, and the Book uses it at the moments where the argument needs the listener's own motion. The sūrah's call is not the command to believe; the sūrah's call is the command to look, and the looking is what the sūrah's argument requires. The call is also the sūrah's way of marking the change from the sūrah's act to the listener's act. Up to this verse, the sūrah has been stating: the oath, the definition, the guard. From this verse, the sūrah asks, and the asking is the asking that puts the argument into the listener's hands. The argument from here to the end of the unit is the listener's argument, and the sūrah's work is the provision of the material.

**The Object of the Consideration**

The sūrah points the consideration at one object: what the human being was created from. The object is the origin, the matter of the beginning, and the sūrah points at the matter rather than at the idea. The consideration is not the consideration of a doctrine; the consideration is the consideration of the concrete, the thing the human being was made of. The sūrah's method is the method of the concrete: the argument is built on the thing rather than on the word, and the thing is what the listener can hold. The pointing is the sūrah's way of grounding the argument in the listener's own being: the human being is asked to consider the ground of his own existence, and the ground is the matter, and the matter is what the next verses will name. The sūrah's call is the call to the origin, and the origin is the sūrah's premise.

**The Two Readings of the Consideration**

The exegetes record two readings of the call, and the two readings are the two directions of the consideration. The first reading takes the call as the call to consider the manner of the creation, the call to the denier of the return to look at how he came to be, and the reading is the reading of the argument: the one who was created in this manner can be returned. The second reading takes the call as the call to consider that for which the human being was created, the purpose of the creation, and the reading borrows its ground from the Book's own statement: "I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me" (51:56). The two readings are not opposed. The manner and the purpose are the two sides of the one fact of the creation: the human being was made in a manner, and the human being was made for a purpose, and the sūrah's call covers both. The consideration of the manner is the consideration of the proof, and the consideration of the purpose is the consideration of the meaning, and the two considerations are the sūrah's whole request.

**The Argument from the Origin**

The sūrah's logic is the logic of the origin: the origin is the proof of the return. The one who was made from what the sūrah will name in the next two verses is the one who can be returned, because the making was the first act and the returning is the second act, and the power that did the first can do the second. The sūrah's call in this verse is the premise of the conclusion in the eighth verse, and the four verses between the call and the conclusion are the argument. The structure is the structure of the demonstration: the premise is stated as a call, the evidence is stated in the two verses of the origin, and the conclusion is stated in the emphatic affirmation of the return. The sūrah's argument is complete in the four verses, and the four verses are the sūrah's first unit of argument, the unit of the soul. The demonstration is the sūrah's quiet form of argument, and the form is the form of the proof rather than the form of the pressure.

**The Considering the Sūrah Demands**

The sūrah's method is the method of the consideration rather than the method of the command. The sūrah does not order the belief; the sūrah orders the looking, and the looking is what the sūrah's argument requires of the listener. The difference is the difference of the sūrah's respect for the listener: the conclusion of the argument is the listener's conclusion, and the sūrah's work is the provision of the material. The sūrah gives the origin, the sūrah gives the power, the sūrah gives the Day, and the sūrah asks the listener to do the connecting. The connecting is the listener's act, and the act is what the sūrah's call is for. The sūrah's reminder is the reminder that works by the listener's motion, and the motion is the considering, and the considering is the sūrah's method from this verse to the end of the unit. The method of the considering is the sūrah's method of respect for the listener. The sūrah does not take the conclusion from the listener; the sūrah gives the material and waits for the listener's own motion. The waiting is the sūrah's patience, and the patience is what the sūrah will command in its last verse.

## Sūrah al-Ṭāriq 86:6

> ˹They were˺ created from a spurting fluid

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Matter Named**

The sūrah's call to consider is answered by the naming of the matter: the human being was created from the spurting fluid. The Arabic, al-māʾi al-dafāq, gives the fluid its mark, the mark of the spurting, the gushing forth, and the mark is the sūrah's way of stating the origin in its motion rather than in its rest. The fluid is not a substance lying still; the fluid is the substance in its coming, and the coming is the spurting. The sūrah's naming is the naming by the motion, and the motion is what the sūrah's argument needs: the origin was an event, and the event was the gushing, and the gushing is the first act that made the human being. The sūrah states the origin as the act rather than the thing, and the act is what makes the return possible, because the act can be repeated.

**The Book's Standing Formula**

The sūrah's last word is the word of the time, and the time is what the sūrah's last word states: the schemers are granted the little while, and the little while is the sūrah's last statement of the relation of the Lord to the one who schemes.
