# Sūrah al-Ṭāriq (Chapter 86) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Piercing Star and the Unveiling of the Secrets**

Aṭ-Ṭāriq, "The Night Comer" or "The Piercing Star," is the eighty-sixth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 17 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with an arresting celestial oath: *“By the sky and the Night Comer (*wa-l-samā'i wa-l-ṭāriq*)! And what can make you know what is the Night Comer? It is the piercing star (*al-najmu al-thāqib*)!”* (86:1–3). *Aṭ-Ṭāriq* means the visitor that arrives in the dark of night, knocking upon the door of human consciousness, identified as a pulsating, radiant star whose light pierces through the fathomless blackness of the cosmos. In seventeen concise, rhythmic verses, Sūrah aṭ-Ṭāriq conjoins cosmic physics with human embryology, establishing that just as every star is watched over in the celestial heavens, so too has every human soul been assigned an angelic guardian. It culminates in the terrifying, intimate revelation of the Day of Judgment: *Yawm tublā al-sarā'ir*—the Day when all hidden secrets are exposed to the light.

**The Structural Modulation and the Piercing Light**

The architecture of Sūrah aṭ-Ṭāriq moves across three distinct movements:

1. The Piercing Star and the Angelic Guardian (1–4): Swears by the heavens and the pulsating night star that pierces the darkness, establishing the universal law of verse 4: *“There is no soul but that it has over it a guardian (*in kullu nafsin lammā ʿalayhā ḥāfiẓ*).”*

2. The Biological Miracle and the Unveiling of Secrets (5–10): Directs man to contemplate his creation from the ejected fluid emerging from between the loins and ribs, confirming God's power of resurrection. It delivers the chilling Last-Day reality of verses 9–10: *“The Day when all secrets will be put on trial / exposed (*Yawma tublā al-sarā'ir*)! Then man will have no power and no helper!”* On that Day, private motives, hidden hypocrisies, and secret sins will be displayed openly for all creation to see.

3. The Rhythmic Oaths and the Deceptive Plot (11–17): Swears by the sky which returns rain in cycles (*dhāti al-rajʿ*) and the earth which cracks open with sprouting vegetation (*dhāti al-ṣadʿ*), proclaiming the absolute authority of scripture: *“Indeed, the Qur'an is a decisive statement (*qawlun faṣl*), and it is not amusement!”* (86:13–14). It exposes the plots of the Quraysh: *“Indeed, they are planning a plot, but I am planning a plot.”* The chapter concludes with the sovereign divine command of patience to the Prophet: *“So give respite to the disbelievers; leave them for a little while (*fa-mahhili al-kāfirīna amhilhum ruwaydā*)!”*

Sūrah aṭ-Ṭāriq answers this incredulity by commanding the human being to look at his own physical origin: *“So let man observe from what he was created! He was created from a fluid, ejected, emerging from between the backbone and the ribs”* (86:5–7).

The tradition keeps one more honor for the sūrah. An-Nasāʾī recorded from Jābir that Muʿādh led the Maghrib prayer and recited Sūrah al-Baqarah and Sūrah an-Nisāʾ, two of the longest chapters in the Book, and the Prophet ﷺ said to him: "Are you putting the people to trial, O Muʿādh? Was it not sufficient for you to recite 'By the heaven and aṭ-Ṭāriq,' 'By the sun and its brightness,' and the like of them?" The report makes the short Makkan sūrahs the standard of what suffices for a congregation, and it tells how the earliest community heard this sūrah: not as a trifle for a short stop but as one of the chosen pieces, fit to carry an entire prayer on its own.

## Verse 86:1
> By the heaven and the nightly star

**The Door with Three Panels**

The first verse of the sūrah is the door of the sūrah, and the door has three panels. The heaven is the panel of the whole, the ordered sky that carries the star. The nightly star is the panel of the point, the moving light inside the whole; and the knocking in the name is the panel of the motion, the arrival that the whole and the point are there to carry. The three panels together are the sūrah's opening image, and the image is the image of the guarantee: the whole order, the moving point, and the arriving motion. The sūrah that follows al-Burūj takes the same image of the guarantee and states it from the night rather than from the day, and the night is the hour of the sūrah, the hour of the closed door and the knocking visitor.

The word for the star, *al-Ṭāriq*, comes from the root of knocking at night, and the Book follows the oath with a question — what can make you know what the Ṭāriq is — and then with the answer, a star of piercing brightness (86:1–3 — *“˹It is˺ the star of piercing brightness”*). The pattern is used in other short Makkan sūrahs, where an oath is followed by a question and then by a definition, and the effect is to slow the reader down at the door of the sūrah. The night that the oath names is also the setting the sūrah will use for the argument that follows: a visitor to a guarded house, a soul with a keeper, and a creation that has been watched over as carefully as the sky.

**The Name of the Night Visitor**

Qatādah and other early readers gave the name its ground: the star is called the Ṭāriq, the night comer, because it is seen only at night and hidden by day. Ibn Kathir notes that the naming is supported by the usage of the authentic ḥadīth, which prohibits a man to come to his family taruq — meaning that he comes upon them at night unannounced, in the hour when no visitor is expected. The star takes its name from that human hour: it is the arrival that the darkness makes sudden, and the oath swears by arrival itself. The sūrah's first unit will ask who watches the watched, and the watcher it names first is the visitor who comes when the door is supposed to be closed.

## Verse 86:2
> And what will make you realize what the nightly star is

**The Question After the Oath**

The sūrah swears by the nightly star in the first verse, and in the second verse it asks what the nightly star is. The question is not the question of the ignorant: the sūrah does not ask for information it lacks. It is the question of the emphasis, the Book's standing form for marking the next statement as the definition. The sūrah has sworn by the star, and the oath is complete as a guarantee, but the sūrah wants the listener to hold the star as the object of the guarantee, and the question is the instrument of the holding. The form of the question is the form of the call: what will make you realize, the sūrah asks, and the form addresses the listener rather than the star.

**The Book's Standing Form**

The form of the question is the Book's standing form, and the Book uses it at its moments of definition. "And what will make you realize what the Inevitable Hour is?" (69:3). "And what will make you realize what Hell is?" (74:27). "And what will make you realize what the Day of Decision is?" (77:14). "What will make you realize what Judgment Day is?" (82:17). The form appears in the sūrahs that define their objects, the hour, the fire, the day, and the star, and in each case the form marks the next verse as the definition of the named thing. The sūrah's use of the form is the use of the form in its family: the thing is named, the thing is questioned, and the thing is defined. The sūrah stands in the family of the Book's definitional moments, and the standing is the sūrah's statement that what follows is the definition, not the commentary. The sūrah does not state the definition directly; the sūrah addresses the listener and asks him what will make him realize, and the address is what makes the definition the listener's own reception rather than the sūrah's deposit.

## Verse 86:3
> ˹It is˺ the star of piercing brightness

The sūrah's question is answered in one word of substance: *al-ṭāriq*, the comer by night, is then named as *al-najm al-thāqib*, the star that pierces. It is called not by its place among the constellations but by what it does — it pierces the dark and it shines. Commentators take the Ṭāriq to be the morning star, or any star that appears at night; the definition stays at the level of function rather than position, which is exactly what the oath needs. The same root describes the lamp of light that shines out (24:35 — *“His light is like a niche in which there is a lamp, the lamp is in a crystal, the crystal is like a shining star, lit from ˹the oil of˺ a blessed olive tree, ˹located˺ neither to the east nor the west, whose oil would almost glow, even without being touched by fire.”*), so the sense is light that cuts the darkness rather than an entry in a catalogue of fixed stars. From the guarded sky the sūrah moves to the guarded soul: as the sky has its watch, so the human being was created and will be raised, and every secret is kept until the day it is examined.

**The Illuminating**

Ibn ʿAbbās gave the second word of the verse its plain sense: thāqib is the illuminating — the light that passes through what it shines on. The gloss agrees with the verse's own picture, a star whose light pierces the dark rather than resting on it, and it keeps the definition at the level of what the star does. The sūrah does not name the star's place in any catalogue; it names the cut of its light through the night, because the cut is the point. An oath guarantor must be conspicuous, and nothing in the night sky is more conspicuous than the light that reaches the eye from the depth of the dark.

**The Star That Burns the Shayṭān**

ʿIkrimah sharpened the gloss: it is illuminating, and it burns the shayṭān. The reading stands beside the Book's own teaching about the lamps of the lowest heaven (67:5 — *“And indeed, We adorned the lowest heaven with ˹stars like˺ lamps, and made them ˹as missiles˺ for stoning ˹eavesdropping˺ devils, for whom We have also prepared the torment of the Blaze”*). The star that lights the night for men is the same sky's weapon against the eavesdroppers of the unseen, and the gloss lets the oath carry both works at once: the light that guides is the fire that drives off. The guarantor of the sūrah's oath is therefore not a passive ornament of the sky but an active one — it pierces, it lights, and it burns.

## Verse 86:4
> There is no soul without a vigilant angel ˹recording everything˺

**The Guardian Named**

The word the sūrah gives the angel is the word for the guardian, the one who keeps watch, the one who holds the charge. The commentators record the two functions of the guarding, and the two functions are the recording and the protection. The guardians who record are the angels assigned to the deeds of the individual, the ones who write what is done, and the Book names them in its standing verses: "while you are certainly observed by vigilant, honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺. They know whatever you do" (82:10–12), and "not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺" (50:18). The guardians who protect are the angels set over the people by the command of the Lord: "For each one there are successive angels before and behind, protecting them by Allah's command" (13:11). The two functions are the two sides of the guarding: the holding of the record and the holding of the person, and the sūrah's word carries both. The two functions are also the two directions of the guarding. The recording goes from the soul to the record, and the protection goes from the Lord to the soul, and the two directions together are the guarding's whole motion. The sūrah's word of the guardian is the word of the motion, and the motion is what the guarding is.

**The Protection in the Guarding**

Ibn Kathir states what the guarding holds off: every soul has a guardian over it from God, and the guardian protects it from the calamities. The recording side of the work looks toward the Day, when the written record is opened; the protecting side works in the present, while the person moves about unguarded in his own sight. Both run on command, not on the angel's own judgment — the Book says of the successive angels that they guard the person by the command of God (13:11, quoted above) — and the sūrah's comfort rests on the pairing: the soul that no one sees is watched from both directions at once, its deeds toward the record and its days away from the harm.

## Verse 86:5
> Let people then consider what they were created from

**The Call to Consider**

The sūrah stops describing and begins asking. The previous verse stated the fact of the guardian, and this verse turns to the human being with the imperative: let the person consider. The call is the sūrah's first address to the listener as an agent, and the address is the sūrah's change of method. The sūrah has done its work of stating, the oath and the definition and the guard, and now it hands the work to the listener: the considering is the listener's act, and the sūrah's act is the pointing. The imperative is the Book's standing instrument of the reminder, and the Book uses it at the moments where the argument needs the listener's own motion. The sūrah's call is not the command to believe; the sūrah's call is the command to look, and the looking is what the sūrah's argument requires.

**The Two Readings of the Consideration**

The commentators record two readings of the call, and the two readings are the two directions of the consideration. The second reading takes the call as the call to consider that for which the human being was created, the purpose of the creation, and the reading borrows its ground from the Book's own statement: "I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me" (51:56).

**The Weakness the Call Points To**

Ibn Kathir reads the call as an alert: the verse alerts the human being to the weakness of his own origin, and the intent of the alert is to guide him to accept the Hereafter — whoever was able to begin the creation is able to repeat it. The consideration is therefore not a curiosity about biology; it is a lesson about standing. A creature whose beginning is a gush of fluid does not get to argue that his return is beneath the Maker's dignity, and the verse aims the argument at the proudest thing a man owns, his sense of his own substance. The sūrah states the argument in full three verses from here, but it plants the seed at the call: look first at what you came from, and then say whether the second creation is hard.

## Verse 86:6
> ˹They were˺ created from a spurting fluid

**The Matter Named**

The sūrah's call to consider is answered by the naming of the matter: the human being was created from the spurting fluid. The Arabic, al-māʾi al-dafāq, gives the fluid its mark, the mark of the spurting, the gushing forth, and the mark is the sūrah's way of stating the origin in its motion rather than in its rest. The fluid is not a substance lying still; the fluid is the substance in its coming, and the coming is the spurting. The sūrah's naming is the naming by the motion, and the motion is what the sūrah's argument needs: the origin was an event, and the event was the gushing, and the gushing is the first act that made the human being. The sūrah states the origin as the act rather than the thing, and the act is what makes the return possible, because the act can be repeated.

**The Book's Standing Formula**

The naming of the fluid is the Book's standing formula, and the sūrah's verse is the member of the family of the verses that name the origin. "O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop" (22:5). "˹For˺ indeed, We ˹alone˺ created humans from a drop of mixed fluids, ˹in order˺ to test them, so We made them hear and see" (76:2). "And ˹it is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ created you from dust, then ˹developed you˺ from a sperm-drop, then made you into pairs" (35:11). The family of the verses states the origin in the same form, the fluid, the drop, the mixed fluids, and the sūrah's verse stands in the family with its own mark, the mark of the spurting. The standing of the sūrah in the family is the sūrah's statement that the origin is the Book's standing premise, and the premise is what the sūrah's argument builds on. The family's repetition is the family's way of stating the origin's standing. The origin is stated in the sūrahs of the beginning and the sūrahs of the end, and the stating is the Book's statement that the origin is the premise of the return. The sūrah's verse is the premise in the sūrah's form, and the form is the form of the spurting.

## Verse 86:7
> stemming from between the backbone and the ribcage

**The Two Readings of the Place**

The commentators record the two readings of the place, and the two readings are the readings of the region. The first reading takes the two regions, the backbone and the ribcage, as the regions of the two parents, the region of the man and the region of the woman, and the reading takes the two fluids, the fluids of the two, as the source of the creation. The second reading limits the regions to the one parent, the man, and takes the between of the regions as the region from which the fluid gushes, the region between the lower back and the chest. The two readings agree on the form of the statement: the origin is between, and the origin is from the region of the body. The two readings differ on the number of the parties, and the difference is the difference of the reading rather than of the text, and the text's form is the form of the between in either reading.

**The Chain and the Detail**

The first reading has its named chain. Shabib ibn Bishr reported from ʿIkrimah, from Ibn ʿAbbās, that the backbone and the ribcage are the backbone of the man and the ribs of the woman, and the report adds that the fluid is yellow and fine in texture, and that the child is not born except from both — the two fluids joining, by the permission of God, into the single beginning. The chain matters as much as the detail: the reading is not a commentator's guess but an athar carried through named hands from the first exegete of the ummah, and it settles the two-parents reading on the authority of the verse's earliest students. The pairing also answers the verse's shape, which puts the origin between two regions rather than inside one.

## Verse 86:8
> Surely He is fully capable of bringing them back ˹to life˺

**The Conclusion Stated**

The sūrah's argument reaches its conclusion in this verse, and the conclusion is stated in the emphatic form: surely He is fully capable of the return. The sūrah has done its work of the premise, the call to consider, the matter of the fluid, the place of the between, and the conclusion is the statement of the power. The power is the power that made the origin, and the power is what the sūrah's argument requires: the one who made from the fluid can return the made. The emphatic form of the verse is the form of the demonstration, and the demonstration is complete at this verse. The sūrah's first unit of argument is the argument from the origin to the return, and the argument is the argument of the four verses, the call, the matter, the place, and the power. The conclusion is the sūrah's statement, and the statement is what the sūrah's premise proved.

**The Denial the Verse Answers**

The verse is the answer to a specific denial, and the denial is the denial of the pre-Islamic Arabs who accepted the creation and refused the return. The denial is recorded in the Book's own verses: "Do ˹such˺ people not remember that We created them before, when they were nothing?" (19:67). "O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection" (22:5), the address that states the doubt the sūrah's answer removes. The denial was not the denial of the power; the denial was the refusal of the second act, and the refusal was the refusal of the return of the made. The sūrah's verse is the answer to the refusal: the power that did the first act is the power that does the second, and the second is the return. The sūrah's argument is the argument against the specific denial, and the denial is the denial of the age the sūrah addressed. The denial's form is also the form of the question. The deniers did not question the power of the creation; they questioned the power of the return, and the questioning was the questioning of the second act. The sūrah's verse is the answer to that specific questioning, and the answering is the stating of the capability for the second act. The capability is the sūrah's word, and the word is the word of the return.

**The Ease of the Second Act**

The Book states the relation of the two acts with a word that removes the doubt about the second. "And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him" (30:27). The return is easier for the Lord than the origin, and the ease is the Book's statement of the power: the power that did the harder act is the power that does the easier, and the easier is the return.

## Verse 86:9
> on the Day all secrets will be disclosed

**The Day Named**

The sūrah's eighth verse stated the power of the return, and this verse states the date of the return: the Day when the secrets are made clear. The return is not an act without a time, and the sūrah gives the act its time in the same breath. The Day is the Day the sūrah's first unit is moving toward, and the Day is named now, at the point where the argument is complete, so that the argument and its date stand together. The sūrah's structure is the structure of the appointment: the power is stated, and the appointment is stated, and the two together are the sūrah's complete statement of the return. The Day is the sūrah's second unit, and the unit begins with the name of the Day, and the name is the name of the opening of the secrets.

**The Body as Witness**

The sūrah's Day opens the secrets, and the Book states the means of the opening in its standing verses. The body is the means: "On this Day We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to commit" (36:65). "When they reach it, their ears, eyes, and skin will testify against what they used to do" (41:20). "On the Day their tongues, hands, and feet will testify against them for what they used to do" (24:24). The limbs are the witnesses of the Day, and the witnessing is the opening of the secrets: the inner state of the act is opened by the testimony of the body that did the act. The sūrah's word of the opening is the word of the Book's standing doctrine, and the doctrine is the teaching about the body as the witness of the inner. The secrets are opened, and the opening is the testimony.

**The Flag of the Betrayer**

The Two Ṣaḥīḥs carry the report of Ibn ʿUmar that shows the exposure working on one secret at a time. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said that every betrayer will have a flag raised for him behind his back on the Day of Resurrection, and it will be said: this is the betrayal of so-and-so, son of so-and-so. The secret was known to one man alone; the flag publishes it by name, and the shame walks where he walks. The sūrah's word for the Day — the Day the secrets are put on trial — is the general law, and the flag is the law in its most concrete form: the hidden thing is not only seen, it is named, and named after the man who did it.

## Verse 86:10
> Then one will have neither power nor ˹any˺ helper

**The Unit Closed**

The sūrah's first unit reaches its close in this verse, and the close is the statement of the solitude. The unit moved from the soul under the guardian to the soul considered, to the soul created, to the soul returned, to the soul tested, and the unit ends with the soul alone on the Day. The solitude is the sūrah's last word of the unit, and the last word is the statement that on the Day the person has neither the power of himself nor the help of another. The sūrah's unit is the unit of the soul's standing, and the standing is the standing of the one who was guarded and is now opened, and the opened soul stands alone. The close of the unit is the sūrah's way of marking the boundary: the soul's argument is done, and the next oath begins the sūrah's second argument, and the boundary is the solitude.

**The Book's Standing Statement**

The statement of the removed help is the Book's standing statement, and the sūrah's verse is the member of the family of the verses that state the solitude of the Day. "The Day no kith or kin will be of benefit to another whatsoever, nor will they be helped" (44:41). "Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs ˹only˺ to Allah, and you have no guardian or helper besides Allah?" (2:107). "The Day when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit" (26:88). The family states the removal in its several forms, the removal of the kin, the removal of the guardian, the removal of the wealth and the children, and the sūrah's verse states the removal in the form of the power and the helper. The standing of the sūrah in the family is the sūrah's statement that the solitude is the Book's standing doctrine of the Day, and the doctrine is what the sūrah's unit closes on.

Ibn Kathir distributes the two words of the verse with precision. The power that fails is the power within a man himself, and the helper that is absent is any help from other than himself: he cannot save himself from the torment, and no one else can save him from it either. The pair closes every road at once — the road of self-rescue that pride keeps trying to walk, and the road of patronage that the great of every age trusted — and the verse states the double closure in two words, so that no reader can suppose that one road remains. The solitude of the Day is total, and it is total by design: it is the one hour in which the accounting cannot be shifted.

## Verse 86:11
> By the sky with its recurring cycles

**The Second Oath**

The sūrah has completed its first unit, the unit of the soul, and it begins its second unit with the second oath. The first oath was the heaven and the nightly star, and the second oath is the sky and the earth, and the two oaths are the sūrah's two guarantees. The sūrah swears again because the sūrah argues again: the first argument was the argument of the soul, and the second argument is the argument of the word, and each argument carries its own guarantee. The second oath is not a repetition of the first; the second oath is the reprise, the return of the form with the new object, and the return is the sūrah's way of marking the new unit. The form of the oath is the sūrah's signature, and the signature is repeated at the beginning of each unit, and the repetition is the sūrah's structure.

**The Return as the Sign**

The returning of the sky is the sūrah's sign of the resurrection, and the sign is the sign of the return of the dead. The sky returns its rains, and the earth that receives the rains stirs to life: "You see the earth lifeless, but as soon as We send down rain upon it, it begins to stir ˹to life˺ and swell, producing every type of pleasant plant" (22:5). The sign is the sign of the return: the dead earth is brought back by the returning rain, and the dead soul is brought back by the returning Lord. The sūrah's first unit argued the return of the soul from the origin of the soul, and the sūrah's second oath argues the return of the soul from the habit of the sky, and the two arguments are the two sides of the sūrah's doctrine.

**The Named Glosses of the Rain**

Ibn ʿAbbās gave the verse's word its plain sense: ar-rajʿ is the rain — or, in a second report from him, the clouds that hold the rain — so that the oath by the sky of the rajʿ is the oath by the sky that rains and then rains again. Qatādah put the returning to work: the sky returns the sustenance of the creatures every year, he said, and were it not for that return they would all be destroyed, and their cattle with them. The two glosses agree on the thing sworn by: not a sky that is merely high, but a sky that pays out its gift on schedule, season after season. The habit of the sky is the sūrah's exhibit in the argument for the return, and the glosses make the habit concrete — the year's rain is a resurrection in miniature, witnessed by every herd that drinks.

## Verse 86:12
> and the earth with its sprouting plants

**The Pair of the Oath**

The two verses of the oath are the pair, and the pair is the pair of the cycle. The sky returns its rains in the first verse of the pair, and the earth opens for the plants in the second verse of the pair, and the two together are the cycle of the vegetation. The returning of the sky is the first half of the cycle, and the opening of the earth is the second half, and the cycle is what the sūrah's oath covers. The pair is the sūrah's way of stating the world as the cycle: the world is not the world of the two grounds at rest; the world is the world of the two grounds in motion, and the motion is the motion of the returning and the opening. The oath is on the motion, and the motion is what the sūrah's second argument needs.

**The Sign of the Return**

The cycle of the two grounds is the sūrah's sign of the resurrection, and the sign is the sign of the return of the dead. The rain returns from the sky, and the earth opens and gives forth the plant, and the dead season is made the living season by the two motions. The sūrah's argument is the argument from the sign: the Lord who returns the rain and opens the earth is the Lord who returns the soul and opens the Day. "And He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky—causing all kinds of plants to grow—producing green stalks from which We bring forth clustered grain" (6:99). The Book states the cycle in its standing verses, and the sūrah's oath is the sūrah's use of the cycle as the guarantee of its second argument. The sign is the visible proof, and the proof is what the oath carries.

**The Word for the Splitting**

On the earth's word the early tradition is at one. Ibn ʿAbbās read the ṣadʿ as the splitting that brings forth the plant growths, and the reading is carried by Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, ʿIkrimah, Abū Mālik, ad-Daḥḥāk, al-Ḥasan, Qatādah and as-Suddī — a near-complete roster of the first generations at a single word. The agreement is worth pausing on. The sūrah's oath hangs its guarantee on the crack in the ground, the small violence of the seed case and the shoot, and the whole early tradition heard the word the same way: the earth is sworn by not as a mass of soil but as the thing that opens. The pair of the oaths is therefore exact — the sky that pays out, the ground that opens — and the resurrection argument of these verses stands on the pair as on two witnesses.

## Verse 86:13
> Surely this ˹Quran˺ is a decisive word

**The Second Argument Begins**

The sūrah's second oath is complete, and the sūrah's second argument begins in this verse. The argument is the argument of the word, and the argument is stated in the verses from this one to the end of the sūrah. The sūrah's first argument was the argument of the soul, and the argument moved from the guardian to the solitude, and the sūrah's second argument is the argument of the word, and the argument moves from the decisive to the respite. The turn from the soul to the word is the sūrah's last turn, and the turn is marked by the oath that preceded it. The sūrah's statement in this verse is the first statement of the second argument, and the statement is the statement of the word's quality: the word is the decisive word.

**The Reading of the Day**

The commentators record a second reading of the decisive, and the reading takes the decisive to be the Day of Judgment rather than the word. The reading borrows its ground from the Book's standing name of the Day: the Day of Decision, the Day that separates. "And what will make you realize what the Day of Decision is?" (77:14). "Indeed, the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision is an appointed time—" (78:17). "This is the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision which you used to deny" (37:21). The Day is the Day of the separation, and the separation is the work of the Day, and the reading takes the sūrah's word to be the word of the Day. The two readings, the reading of the word and the reading of the Day, are not opposed: the word separates in the world of the living, and the Day separates in the world of the judgment, and the two separations are the two sides of the one decisiveness. The two readings are the two sides of the decisiveness, and the two sides are the word and the Day, and the two together are the decisiveness in its full form.

On the word that separates, the plain glosses agree. Ibn ʿAbbās read faṣl as true, and Qatādah said the same; another early hand read it as a just ruling — a verdict handed down. The glosses and the Day-reading beside them are layers of the same decisiveness: the word is true against the lie it refutes, it rules justly between the claims it judges, and on the Day it separates the two permanent addresses of mankind. The sūrah stakes all of that on the Quran it is reciting, which is why the next verse refuses it the treatment of amusement — a word with this much riding on it cannot be a pastime.

## Verse 86:14
> and is not to be taken lightly

**The Dignity Stated**

The sūrah's statement of the word's quality is complete in the two verses, and the second half of the statement is this verse: the word is not to be taken lightly. The first verse stated what the word is, the decisive word, and this verse states what the word is not, the word that is light, the word that is play, the word that is dismissed. The two verses together are the sūrah's complete statement of the word's standing: the word decides, and the word is not dismissed. The dignity is what this verse states: the word's standing above the treatment that the listener might give it. The sūrah does not ask the listener to accept the word; the sūrah states the word's standing, and the standing is what the sūrah's second argument is about.

**The Treatment Refused**

The sūrah's refusal is the refusal of a treatment, and the treatment is the treatment the deniers gave the word. The deniers treated the word as the tale, as the fabrication, as the poetry, as the thing that is to be answered and not received: "Yet they say, 'This ˹Quran˺ is a set of confused dreams! No, he has fabricated it! No, he must be a poet!'" (21:5).

## Verse 86:15
> They are certainly devising ˹evil˺ plans

The Book states the end of the scheme in its standing form, and the end is the feebling. "As such, Allah frustrates the evil plans of the disbelievers" (8:18).

**The Scheme Named**

The sūrah's argument of the word reaches the schemers in this verse, and the naming of the scheme is the sūrah's statement of the opposition. The sūrah has stated the word's standing, and the sūrah now states the one who stands against the word: the schemers. The verse does not describe the scheme; the verse names it, and the naming is the sūrah's way of placing the scheme in the record. The scheme is the scheme of the deniers of the sūrah's age, the scheme of the community that received the word in the form the sūrah's previous verses refused. The sūrah's statement is the statement of the fact: the schemers scheme, and the scheming is the fact of the sūrah's present. The naming is the sūrah's first word of the pair, and the pair is the pair of the scheme and the plan.

The Book states the relation of the scheme to the plan in the verse that answers the schemers with the swiftness of the Lord. Surely Our messenger-angels record whatever you devise'" (10:21).

**The Target of the Scheme**

Ibn Kathir specifies what the scheme works on: they plot against the people, in their calling them to oppose the Quran. The scheme's target is not the Prophet's ﷺ person but the listeners' access — the plot lives in the persuasion, the mockery, and the pressure that makes the word expensive to accept. That is why the sūrah answers it with a statement about the Quran's standing rather than a threat about the plotters: the scheme works on minds, and the answer to it is the word that decides. The two verses that follow state the counter-move in the Lord's own planning, and the plotting that aimed at other people's faith is left, as the Book says elsewhere, plotting only against itself.

## Verse 86:16
> but I too am planning

**The Flawless Plan**

The Book states the quality of the Lord's plan in its standing form, and the quality is the flawlessness. "I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless" (7:183). The sūrah's reply is the reply that stands on the flawlessness: the plan of the Lord is the plan that is not at fault, and the not-at-fault is what the plan does to the scheme. The flawlessness is also the sūrah's way of stating the plan's standing above the scheme. The scheme is at fault in its end, and the plan is not at fault in its end, and the two ends are the two ends of the opposition.

**The Answer Stated**

The sūrah's answer to the scheme is stated in this verse, and the answer is the statement of the plan. The schemers scheme, and the Lord plans, and the two are stated in the pair of the two verses. The answer is not the denial of the scheme; the answer is the statement of the greater scheme. The sūrah does not say that the scheme of the schemers is nothing; the sūrah says that the scheme of the schemers is inside the plan of the Lord, and the inside is the sūrah's statement. The answer is the sūrah's second act of the pair, and the act is the act of the plan, and the plan is what the sūrah's second argument states at its center. The statement of the plan is also the sūrah's way of stating the relation of the two acts.

**The Scheme Inside the Plan**

The Book states the relation of the scheme to the plan in the verse of the encompassing of the plan. "˹Soon˺ their united front will be defeated and ˹forced to˺ flee" (54:45), the statement of the end of the schemers' front. The front of the schemers is the front that is defeated, and the defeat is the work of the plan. The Book also states the scheme's self-directedness: "And so We have placed in every society the most wicked to conspire in it. Yet they plot only against themselves, but they fail to perceive it" (6:123). The scheme is against the word, and the scheme is against the schemer, and the two directions are the sūrah's statement of the scheme's end. The inside and the self-directedness are the two sides of the scheme's position.

The same statement is made in the sūrah of the Poet: "I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless" (68:45).

## Verse 86:17
> So bear with the disbelievers ˹O Prophet˺. Let them be for ˹just˺ a little while

**The Command of the Patience**

The command is the command addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, and the command is the sūrah's final instruction. The patience is the patience of the bearer of the word, and the patience is the patience that the sūrah's second argument requires. The sūrah has stated the scheme of the schemers and the plan of the Lord, and the sūrah's instruction is the instruction that follows from the two: the bearer of the word bears with the schemers, because the plan of the Lord is the plan that carries the word. The Book states the command in its standing form: "Be patient ˹O Prophet, for your patience is only with Allah's help. Do not grieve over those ˹who disbelieve˺, nor be distressed by their schemes" (16:127). The sūrah's command is the sūrah's use of the standing form, and the use is the sūrah's way of placing the instruction in the Book's law.

**The Respite and the Hour**

The respite is the respite that does not hasten, and the not-hastening is the respite's form. The Book states the relation of the respite to the Hour in its standing verses, and the standing is the standing of the forbearance. "Those who disbelieve in it ˹ask to˺ hasten it ˹mockingly˺. But the believers are fearful of it, knowing that it is the truth" (42:18). The deniers hasten, and the sūrah's respite is the respite against the hastening: the respite is the grant of the time to the schemers, and the grant is not the hastening of the end. The sūrah's instruction is the instruction of the time: the time is the Lord's, and the time is not the schemers', and the bearer of the word does not take the time from the Lord. The respite is the sūrah's statement of the relation of the time to the Lord.

Ibn Kathir glosses the command as a double instruction — wait for them, and do not be hasty about them — and attaches a promise to it: a little while, and you will see what befalls them of torment, punishment and destruction. The respite is therefore not patience without prospect; it is patience with a guaranteed sight at the end of it. The Prophet ﷺ is told to leave the schedule entirely with his Lord, and the leave-taking is not passive — the verse tells him what he will see, so that the waiting itself becomes a form of knowledge. The command closes the sūrah the way the oath opened it: everything is in the hands of the One whose grip the previous sūrah called severe, and the believer's share is steadiness until the sight arrives.

**The Two Ends of the Dealing**

The tradition records the two readings of the dealing that follows the respite, and the two readings are the two ends the sūrah's verse covers. The first reading takes the dealing to be the dealing of this world, and the reading connects the dealing to the defeat of the Makkans at Badr in the second year of the Hijra, the defeat that was the dealing with the schemers of the sūrah's age. "We allow them enjoyment for a little while, then ˹in time We will force them into a harsh torment" (31:24) states the two ends in the Book's standing form, and the sūrah's respite is the respite between the two.
