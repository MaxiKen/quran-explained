# Sūrah at-Taḥrīm (Chapter 66) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

---

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:1
> O Prophet! Why do you prohibit ˹yourself˺ from what Allah has made lawful to you, seeking to please your wives? And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Gentle Rebuke At The Highest Rank**

The chapter opens with a question to the Prophet ﷺ himself. Its tone is kind, yet its point is firm. Even the Messenger may not turn the lawful into the unlawful. Permission and ban belong to Allah alone. A prophet delivers the law; he does not add to it from kindness, love, or pressure at home. The verse names the motive without hiding it: pleasing his wives. That motive is noble in its place, but it cannot set aside a divine gift. Marriage invites care, yet care stops at the border of legislation. The rebuke honours the Prophet ﷺ even as it corrects him. It treats him as the first bearer of the rule that binds all: no soul may forbid what Allah allowed. The Book gives the same warning to all believers elsewhere, against banning good things out of zeal. What holds for the community holds first for its guide.

**What Was Made Unlawful And Why It Matters**

Early accounts differ on the exact trigger. One report links the verse to an oath about a bondwoman after hurt in the household. Another links it to a drink the Prophet ﷺ gave up after displeasure among his wives. A third reading takes the verse as general, covering any case where he denied himself something lawful to keep peace at home. The difference does not change the ruling. Whatever the trigger, the act was the same: a lawful thing set aside by a vow born of domestic pressure. The lesson reaches beyond that house. Many believers ban good food, rest, marriage, or comfort to please others or to look pious. The verse blocks that path. Piety lies in using Allah's gifts with thanks, not in narrowing them to earn praise. The home gains nothing true when peace is bought by shrinking the law.

**Seeking Pleasure In Its Right Place**

Wanting to please one's family is praiseworthy. The Prophet ﷺ was the kindest of men to his wives, and his care for their feelings is well known. The verse does not blame kindness; it sets its limit. Pleasing creatures must never require displeasing the Creator. Gifts, time, kind words, patience, and fair turns among wives all please without sin. Oaths that ban the lawful cross the line. The distinction guards both sides of marriage. A wife should not ask her husband to give up what Allah gave him, and a husband should not offer such a gift to calm a quarrel. True harmony grows from joint obedience, not from one side bending the law for the other. The verse therefore teaches spouses how to love well. Love gives much, forgives much, and bears much, but it does not rewrite what Allah wrote.

**Only Allah Binds And Looses**

The doctrine behind the rebuke is the oneness of lawgiving. *Taḥrīm*, making unlawful, is a divine act. No prophet, scholar, spouse, or state shares it. Humans may promise, vow, or swear, but those words bind the speaker, not the thing. They create a duty to atone if broken, not a new ban on creation. This guard protects religion from two harms. It stops harsh men from loading burdens Allah never set. It stops soft hearts from trading away gifts Allah freely gave. Both harms spring from the same root: treating human words as divine law. The Qur'an returns to this root often, since every age grows its own list of invented bans. The cure stays the same. Ask first whether Allah forbade it. If He did not, no vow, custom, or domestic pact can forbid it. The question at the verse's start enforces that order at the highest example.

**Forgiveness Covers The Error At Once**

The verse ends with two names: All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. The error is corrected, and the cover is spread in the same breath. *Ghafūr* wipes the fault; *Raḥīm* pours kindness after it. The pairing matters. A rebuke without mercy would crush; mercy without rebuke would mislead. Here the Prophet ﷺ learns the rule and feels the mercy together. The pattern repeats for every believer who banned something lawful from zeal or love. The way back is open. Acknowledge the limit, lift the false ban, atone for any oath, and return to the gift with thanks. No shame need linger after that turn. The names also hint at the next verse. Since Allah forgives and shows mercy, He has set a lawful exit from binding oaths. The error finds pardon, and the oath finds a path. Both come from the same mercy.

**A Rule For Every Home After His**

Though the address names the Prophet ﷺ, the ruling walks into every house. No husband may swear away his wife's rights to please his kin. No wife may press her husband to swear away lawful comforts. No parents may ban a lawful marriage to guard pride. No group may declare lawful food, dress, or trade forbidden to prove loyalty. Each such ban repeats the same mistake in smaller form. The verse offers a better way to settle domestic strife. Speak plainly, forgive quickly, divide time fairly, and keep oaths out of anger. Where an oath has already been sworn, the law provides release, as the next verse will show. Homes that live by that order gain two goods. They keep Allah's gifts wide, and they keep hearts free from vows sworn to please a moment. Lawful life stays lawful, and love stays within mercy. That order keeps worship pure and marriage kind, and it frees every home from false vows that burden love and narrow lawful joy.

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:2
> Allah has already ordained for you ˹believers˺ the way to absolve yourselves from your oaths. For Allah is your Guardian. And He is the All-Knowing, All-Wise

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Exit Already Set Before The Oath Was Sworn**

The verse announces relief in the past tense: Allah has already ordained a release. The exit stood ready before the oath was uttered. No believer need stay trapped inside words spoken in haste, anger, or love. The law foresaw human rashness and built a door. That order matters. Mercy here is not an afterthought granted to one house; it is a standing rule for the whole community. The Prophet's ﷺ own oath finds its answer inside that general mercy, not through a special exception. This levels the ground. What frees the Messenger frees his followers. The verse thus turns a domestic strain into a public gift. Every oath that bans the lawful, blocks a duty, or harms a bond can be lifted through the set atonement. The believer need not choose between keeping a harmful oath and breaking it with guilt. A third path exists: dissolve it lawfully.

**What The Release Requires In Practice**

The detailed atonement stands in the longer law of oaths. The set penalty feeds ten poor persons from normal family food, or clothes them, or frees a slave where that applies; one who cannot afford these fasts three days. The Book states the rule in full: “The penalty for a broken oath is to feed ten poor people from what you normally feed your own family, or to clothe them, or to free a bondsperson” (5:89). Fasting follows for those without means. Early jurists differed on what the Prophet ﷺ did in this case. Some say he freed a slave. Others say the verse itself lifted the oath, so no atonement was due. Both views agree on the main point. The oath did not turn the lawful into the unlawful. At most it bound the speaker until released. The law therefore heals twice. It frees the thing from the false ban, and it frees the soul from the rash word through a fixed, merciful price.

**Oaths That Bind And Oaths That Do Not**

Not every strong word counts as a binding oath. Thoughtless slips of the tongue, words spoken without resolve, carry no penalty. The law holds people to deliberate oaths, sworn with intent and resolve. That guard saves daily speech from endless atonement. Still, the verse warns against loose swearing. Oaths should be rare, true, and kept. A believer who swears to do good should keep it. A believer who swears to do wrong or to drop a duty should break it and atone. A believer who swears to ban something lawful should do the same. The rule protects both truth and mercy. Truth gains, since oaths keep weight when used sparingly. Mercy gains, since a rash oath does not lock a soul into sin. Homes gain most of all. Quarrels often breed oaths—never to speak, never to visit, never to give. The law refuses to let those hot words rule a cold future.

**Allah As Guardian Of Those Who Swore Rashly**

The verse names Allah as Guardian, *mawlā*, the One who takes charge of His servants. The title fits the moment. A guardian steps in when his charge errs. Here the charge is a rash oath; the guardian supplies the exit. Guardianship also means nearness. Allah does not leave the oath-bound soul to choose between sin and shame alone. He stays with him, shows the door, and accepts the price. The same guardianship covers the other side of the house. Wives hurt by a vow, kin cut off by a threat, poor persons fed through atonement—all fall under a Lord who manages each affair. Trust in that care changes how believers swear. They swear less, since they know a Guardian hears. They fear less after slipping, since they know a Guardian helps. Strength and softness meet in one name. The Guardian holds the bond of the oath, then gently looses it.

**Knowledge And Wisdom Behind The Atonement**

Two names seal the verse: All-Knowing, All-Wise. Knowledge sees the heart that swore—its anger, love, fear, or haste. Wisdom sets the fitting cure. Feeding the poor turns a private fault into public good. Clothing the needy turns a word of heat into warmth for cold bodies. Fasting turns a slip of the tongue into discipline of the soul. Each price fits the crime. Speech abused the tongue; the cure feeds mouths, covers backs, or tames desire. The pairing also answers a doubt. Why not simply cancel rash oaths for free? Free cancel would cheapen oaths and invite more rashness. A set price keeps speech serious while keeping hope alive. Knowledge knows humans need both awe and ease. Wisdom joins them in one law. The oath keeps weight, the sinner keeps hope, and the poor gain bread. No human code balances those three ends so cleanly.

**Keeping Oaths Honest After Release**

Release does not invite carelessness. The same law that opens the door urges believers to guard their oaths. The Book closes its oath ruling with a call to mindfulness: “So be mindful of your oaths” (5:89). Mindfulness means three habits. Swear only when needed, and only by Allah. Keep what you swear when it is good. Atone and stop when it is wrong. Homes that keep those habits avoid most oath crises. Spouses settle disputes with plain promises, not with bans sworn on pain of divorce. Parents guide children with clear rules, not with threats that must later be undone. Friends reconcile with honest words, not with vows they cannot keep. Where a slip still happens, the path is known. Name the fault, pay the price, and return to lawful life. The verse thus leaves the community both freer and stricter: freer from rash bonds, stricter in guarding speech.

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:3
> ˹Remember˺ when the Prophet had ˹once˺ confided something to one of his wives, then when she disclosed it ˹to another wife˺ and Allah made it known to him, he presented ˹to her˺ part of what was disclosed and overlooked a part. So when he informed her of it, she exclaimed, “Who told you this?” He replied, “I was informed by the All-Knowing, All-Aware.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Trust Given In Private, Then Passed On**

The verse opens inside the Prophet's ﷺ household, where a private word was shared in confidence. He told one wife a matter and asked that it rest with her. She carried it to another wife instead. The act seems small, yet the Qur'an records it. Private trust holds homes together. A husband who cannot confide in his wife loses rest; a wife who cannot hold a confidence loses trust. The verse treats the breach as real wrong, not as light gossip. It names the steps plainly: confiding, disclosing, divine notice, and fair reproof. No names are printed in the verse itself, which guards dignity even while correcting. Early histories identify the two wives, but the ruling does not need those names. Any disclosure of a guarded confidence repeats the same wrong. The lesson spreads from that house to all: what is told in trust must be kept in trust.

**How Allah Brought The Disclosure To Light**

Allah made the disclosure known to His Messenger. The verse does not say how—whether by direct word, by sign, or by clear proof. The silence guards the point. Means matter less than source. The Prophet ﷺ did not guess, spy, or trap; he was told by a Lord who hears private speech. That divine notice carries comfort and warning together. Comfort, since truth reaches the wronged even when no witness speaks. Warning, since no secret disclosure stays hidden from Allah. The phrasing also guards the Prophet's ﷺ conduct. He accused only after knowledge came, not on suspicion. Many homes break on the opposite habit: charges built on doubt, phones searched, doors watched. The verse models a cleaner path. Wait for clear knowledge before reproach. When knowledge comes, reproach with measure. The God who knows also teaches how to use knowledge. Truth must correct, not crush.

**Reproof With Measure, Silence With Mercy**

The Prophet's ﷺ response holds the verse's finest lesson. He mentioned part of what was disclosed and passed over part. He did not list every word, press every detail, or shame her before others. Partial mention proves knowledge; partial silence shows mercy. Commentators differ on who chose the parts. Some say he chose what to raise and what to drop from kindness. Others say Allah showed him part and held part back. Either way the conduct shines. Full exposure would have humiliated; full silence would have excused. He took the middle path: enough to establish the fault, little enough to leave room for return. That balance guides every reproof. Name the wrong clearly so it cannot be denied. Leave aside what adds heat without light. Mercy in reproof does not weaken truth. It opens the heart to receive it.

**Her Denial And Her Startled Question**

When told, she first denied, then asked in surprise who had informed him. The question reveals her state. She knew no human witness stood by, so the news startled her. The verse quotes her words to show how disclosure feels when met with truth: exposed, confused, and searching for a source. Denial often comes first in such moments. Pride resists admission, and fear pictures punishment. The verse does not dwell on the denial to shame her; it moves at once to the answer that settles all denials. Still, the moment warns every soul that hides a fault. Human cover is thin. A word spoken to one ear travels to another, then to another, until it returns to the one wronged. Even where it never returns in this life, it stands recorded. The startled question therefore echoes beyond that room. Who told? The One who hears all telling.

**The All-Knowing, The All-Aware**

The answer names Allah with two titles: All-Knowing, All-Aware. *ʿAlīm* knows all things in full; *Khabīr* knows their inward turns and fine traces. Together they close every gap. Nothing disclosed escapes the first; nothing hidden in motive escapes the second. The titles also fit the two wrongs. Disclosing the secret wronged the husband; hiding the disclosure wronged the truth. Knowledge meets the first, awareness meets the second. The reply carries no threat, yet it warns more than threats. A Lord who knows and who knows finely needs no spies. That truth steadies the honest and unsettles the deceitful. The honest rest, since their private good is also known. The deceitful tremble, since their private harm is also known. The verse thus lifts a household lesson into doctrine. Guarded speech matters because the All-Knowing hears it and the All-Aware weighs it.

**Keeping Confidence As Worship**

The ruling that leaves this verse is simple: confidence is a trust, and trust is worship. A word told in private binds the hearer before Allah. Passing it on without leave breaks that bond, even if the content seems light. Homes need this guard most. Spouses share fears, faults, and plans that no stranger should hold. Children confide in parents; friends confide in friends. Each bond breaks when private words travel. The verse also teaches the wronged how to answer. Prove knowledge without cruelty, mention enough to correct, and pass over the rest. That conduct preserves both truth and love. Where the breach is owned and left, mercy should meet it, as the next verse will urge with repentance. The All-Knowing told the Prophet ﷺ so that a small fault could be fixed before it grew. The same knowledge watches every confidence now. Keeping it is guarding a trust from Allah.

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:4
> ˹It will be better˺ if you ˹wives˺ both turn to Allah in repentance, for your hearts have certainly faltered. But if you ˹continue to˺ collaborate against him, then ˹know that˺ Allah Himself is his Guardian. And Gabriel, the righteous believers, and the angels are ˹all˺ his supporters as well

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Hearts Called Back Before The Fault Grows**

The verse speaks to two wives together, urging both to repent. The call is plural and direct, yet it hides no threat inside the first half. Repentance here means turning from pressure against the Prophet ﷺ back to loyalty with him. The sentence leaves its reward unstated, as if to say the good is too plain to spell out. Early glosses supply it: if you both repent, it will be accepted. That open end is itself mercy. No condition is piled on, no public shame is ordered, no divorce is threatened yet. The door stands wide. The verse thus treats a grave fault with a soft first step. Before warning of allies against them, Allah invites them to become allies with Him. Homes learn from that order. Correct the fault, name the way back, and delay the threat. Many hearts return when the path is shown before the price.

**Inclining From Truth Starts With Small Pressure**

Their hearts, the verse says, have faltered—*ṣaghat*, leaned away from the straight line. The lean began not with denial but with pressure: urging the Prophet ﷺ to keep a ban Allah never set. Small leans grow. A jealous word becomes a pact, a pact becomes a front, a front becomes defiance. Commentators read the lean as a swerve from truth, not a fall from faith. That reading fits the call to repent. Swerve can be straightened; only pride locks it in place. The point reaches every believer. Hearts rarely crash at once. They tilt through flattery, envy, or love misplaced, then find the tilt has become a course. Early return is easy; late return costs more. The verse catches the tilt at its start. It names the lean plainly so it cannot hide as care, then offers the straight path at once. Naming plus inviting: that is how leans are cured.

**Ganging Against Him Means Facing Allah**

The second half turns from invitation to warning. If the two persist and back each other against him, they face not a husband alone but Allah as his Guardian. *Mawlā* here means the One who takes his side, guards his cause, and settles his affair. The scale is stark. Two wives against one man looks like domestic pressure; two souls against Allah's Guardian looks like ruin. The verse does not threaten divorce here—that comes next—but something weightier. To oppose the Messenger in what Allah made lawful is to oppose the Sender. The warning fits the crime. They used alliance to press him; Allah names His own alliance to shield him. The lesson spreads beyond that house. Any pact that presses a believer to ban the lawful or to break a duty faces the same Guardian. Numbers do not frighten Him. Truth does not stand alone while He stands with it.

**Gabriel And The Angels Stand With Him**

The verse lists his supporters in rising order: Allah, Gabriel, the righteous believers, and the angels besides. Gabriel stands first among creatures, the bearer of revelation who knows the Prophet's ﷺ truth from the highest court. His name here is not decoration. The same angel who brought the law now guards its bearer. Then come the angels as a host, countless, obedient, and near. They do not scheme, tire, or switch sides. Their support means the unseen world backs the Messenger. Against that host, household pressure looks thin indeed. The list also comforts every caller to truth after him. One who holds the lawful against a crowd is never truly outnumbered. The God who sent Gabriel to one prophet sends unseen help to all who guard His gifts. The verse thus turns a domestic warning into cosmic reassurance. Heaven itself keeps the balance.

**The Righteous Believers Form His Human Shield**

Between Gabriel and the angels stands a human rank: the righteous believers. Early readings differ on whom the phrase crowns. Some name the two fathers, Abū Bakr and ʿUmar, whose daughters stood in the fault. Some read it as the best of the faithful in every age. Some hear the prophets as a band. Shīʿī readings hear ʿAlī at the head of the righteous. The wide range itself teaches. Righteousness, not blood, sets the rank. Whoever believes truly and acts rightly joins the shield around the Messenger. That shield works through counsel, prayer, example, and public truth. The two wives are thus told, without naming names, that the very men they love and trust stand on his side. The point is tender as well as strong. Return brings reunion with those righteous ones; persistence means standing apart from them. Faith and family pull the same way: back to him.

**Repentance Stays Open Beside The Warning**

Warning never closes the door the verse opened. The call to repent stands first; the list of allies stands second. Order matters. Mercy leads, might follows. Any heart that turns finds the same Guardian who warned it ready to receive it. The Book voices that welcome everywhere: “Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins” (39:53). The two wives need only own the lean, leave the pact, and stand with him instead of against him. The verse models how to warn well. Name the fault without insult, show the true scale of persistence, list the help that guards the wronged, and keep the exit bright. Threat alone hardens; invitation alone may be scorned. Joined, they move hearts. The next verse will raise the stakes to divorce and replacement, yet even there hope remains. Here hope leads, so that warning need never be used.

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:5
> Perhaps, if he were to divorce you ˹all˺, his Lord would replace you with better wives who are submissive ˹to Allah˺, faithful ˹to Him˺, devout, repentant, dedicated to worship and fasting—previously married or virgins

**Expanded Commentary**

**Divorce Stays Possible Even At The Highest House**

The verse speaks a hard truth with a soft word: perhaps. If the Prophet ﷺ divorced them all, Allah could give him better wives. The warning is real, yet it is framed as prospect, not decree. No divorce is ordered here; the path is shown so it need never be walked. The point cuts pride at its root. Nearness to a great husband does not force Allah's favour. Honour must be kept by conduct, not claimed by status. The Book once gave the Prophet's ﷺ wives a plain choice between worldly comfort and Allah with His Messenger, with gracious release for those who chose the world: “I will give you a ˹suitable˺ compensation ˹for divorce˺ and let you go graciously” (33:28). That same freedom stands here. Marriage to the best of men is a gift, not a lock. Keeping it asks for the virtues the verse now lists.

**Better Means Closer To Allah, Not Younger Or Richer**

Better wives here means better in faith, not in beauty, age, wealth, or tribe. The verse defines its own scale through six traits, then closes with a pair that cancels worldly scales: previously married or virgins. Status at marriage does not decide worth. A widow or divorcee who submits and repents outranks a virgin who schemes and pressures. The point rebukes every age that prices wives by youth or past alone. Allah prices by the heart turned to Him. The list also guards the Prophet's ﷺ wives from despair. Better is not a closed club; it is a road they can walk. Each trait named is a deed open to them now: submit, believe, obey, repent, worship, fast. The verse warns and guides in one breath. It shows the replacements' marks so the present wives can wear those marks themselves.

**Submission And Faith Anchor Every Virtue Listed**

The first two traits are *muslimāt* and *muʾmināt*: submissive to Allah and faithful to Him. Submission bends the will to His law; faith fills the heart with trust in Him. Together they form the root of the rest. Without them devotion becomes show, repentance becomes tactic, worship becomes habit, fasting becomes diet. With them each act lives. The order matters. Submission comes before faith here, as outward surrender leads inward trust to grow. A wife who yields her claim to press her husband against the lawful learns trust that Allah will guard her home better than pressure can. The same root holds for husbands. A man who submits his anger to the law and trusts Allah for his home's peace finds the same growth. The verse thus plants the whole list in two words. All else grows from a will bowed and a heart believing.

**Devotion And Repentance Keep Marriage Alive**

The next pair joins constancy to return: devout obedience, *qunūt*, and repentance, *tawbah*. *Qānitāt* stay steady in worship and duty with calm hearts. The Book praises that steadiness in righteous women: “And righteous women are devoutly obedient and, when alone, protective of what Allah has entrusted them with” (4:34). Steadiness guards the home's daily trust—prayer kept, secrets held, goods guarded. Repentance guards it from faults, since no spouse stays flawless. *Tāʾibāt* turn back quickly after a slip, own it without excuse, and repair it without delay. Constancy without repentance turns brittle; a single fault then breaks the pride. Repentance without constancy turns shallow; the same fault returns each week. Joined, they keep love both stable and fresh. The verse places them at the centre of the list, as if to say marriages live here. Daily faithfulness plus swift return covers most of home's need.

**Worship And Fasting Train The Soul For Home**

The last pair names visible worship: dedicated devotion and fasting. *ʿĀbidāt* turn to Allah with prayer, praise, and service. *Sāʾiḥāt* in this verse is read by many as fasting women, those who guard their limbs from sin as they guard their mouths from food. Others hear in it journeying for faith—migration for religion or travel on the path to Allah. Both senses train the same muscle: mastery of desire. Fasting teaches a wife to bear hunger without complaint; it teaches a husband the same. Worship teaches both to kneel before Allah, which makes kneeling to apologise before a spouse easier. Homes starved of worship fill with quarrels over small wants. Homes fed by worship find those wants smaller. The verse therefore ends the list where strength is built. Private prayer and private hunger shape public kindness. The best wives—and husbands—are made in those quiet hours.

**Status At Marriage Does Not Fix Worth**

The verse closes by levelling a pride that haunts many homes: previously married or virgins. Allah names both as equal candidates for better. A past marriage does not stain a faithful woman; virginity does not crown a heedless one. The pair answers the very wives warned here, most of whom were previously married. Their worth never rested on that fact, for good or ill. It rested on submission, faith, devotion, repentance, worship, and fasting. The closing also widens the warning beyond one house. No community may rank women by marital past alone. No man may scorn a widow or divorcee whom Allah calls good. No woman may scorn another for a past Allah has covered. The verse leaves the threat of replacement standing, yet it leaves the path of return standing taller. Wear these six traits, whatever your past, and you are among the better. Hold them daily, and Allah will guard your home and heart.

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:6
> O believers! Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, overseen by formidable and severe angels, who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guarding Starts With The Self, Then The House**

The verse gives the home its highest duty in one line: shield yourselves and your families from the Fire. Order matters. The self comes first, since none can guard a house while burning himself. Prayer kept, tongue held, earnings cleaned, eyes lowered—these form the father's and mother's own shield. Then the shield widens to spouse, children, and all under care. Guarding here means teaching, warning, and living the lesson. Commands without example breed scorn; example without commands leaves ignorance. Both must join. The address names believers, not fathers alone. Mothers guard, elder siblings guard, guardians guard. Each soul answers for those it can guide. The home becomes a small fortress. Its walls are not brick but habits: morning prayer, honest speech, modest dress, lawful food, and nightly return to Allah.

**Teaching And Mercy Are The Shield's Two Sides**

Protection in this verse is built from two acts: obedience and invitation to obedience. Parents obey Allah themselves, then call their families to obey with wisdom and kindness. Harshness alone drives children from religion; softness alone leaves them without it. The verse asks for both firmness and warmth. Teach the short chapters with love, correct faults without mockery, praise good turns quickly, and punish with measure when needed. The Book elsewhere shows believers praying for cover from the Fire; here alone they are ordered to become the cover. That shift honours the home. It is not a hotel where souls wait for judgment; it is a school where souls train for it. Mercy sets the tone, truth sets the course. A child raised under both learns that Allah's law is not a chain but a guard around his joy.

**People And Stones Fuel A Fire Unlike Any Fire**

The Fire's fuel is named as people and stones. Men who fed it with denial become its wood; stones, read by many as the idols carved from rock, burn with their makers. The image shames false worship. Gods that could not shield a fly cannot shield their makers from flame; they join them inside it. Stones also picture hardness. Hearts that stayed hard as rock toward truth meet rocks in punishment. The pairing warns the comfortable. Flesh burns fast, stone holds heat long; together they picture a Fire that catches quickly and never cools. No water, wealth, or kin puts it out. Only present obedience shields from it. Parents should plant that picture early, not to terrify children into despair but to sober them into care. A Fire fed by men and their idols is no tale. It is the end of every road that leaves Allah.

**Stern Angels Who Never Bend Or Break**

Over the Fire stand angels described as formidable and severe. *Ghilāẓ shidād*, thick and strong, means power no sinner can push past and sternness no plea can soften. Their terror is just. They do not hate; they obey. Pity that bends law would wrong the victims of sin, and these guardians never bend it. Their severity also guards the believers now. A guard that could be bribed would invite sin; a guard known to be unbending deters it. Children should learn their names with awe, not with scorn. Mocking Hell's keepers is mocking Allah's order. Honouring their obedience teaches obedience. The verse adds their core mark: they never disobey whatever Allah orders and always do as told. Perfect obedience above shames slack obedience below. If angels with such might still bow fully, how can weak men stand tall against commands?

**Nineteen Keepers And The Terror Of Order**

The Book fixes their number elsewhere: “It is overseen by nineteen ˹keepers˺” (74:30). Nineteen seems few against crowds of the damned, yet the number proves the point. Hell needs no army; Allah's order runs through few with full force. Each keeper holds strength beyond counting, and each acts on command without delay. The small count also mocks the proud. Strong men who boast of their gangs will face nineteen who never tire. Scholars note the trial in the number. Doubters laughed at it; believers saw power perfected through order. The lesson for homes is plain. Strength lies not in numbers but in obedience. A small family that prays, pays dues, and guards tongues outweighs a crowd that feasts and forgets. Parents who feel outnumbered by street, screen, or school should recall the nineteen. Few with Allah outweigh many without Him.

**Only Obedience Shields Where No Excuse Helps**

The verse closes the warning with a seal: the angels always do as commanded. No plea moves them, no bribe bends them, no delay softens them. The Book states the law of that Day elsewhere: “You are only rewarded for what you used to do” (52:16). Deeds done now decide rest then. That seal turns the home's daily grind into rescue work. Waking a sleepy child for dawn prayer shields him. Stopping a lie at dinner shields her. Turning off a foul show shields all. Each small guard builds the wall higher. None of these acts looks heroic, yet together they form the shield the verse orders. The Fire is real, its fuel is ready, its keepers do not sleep. Against them stand only taught hearts and practiced limbs. Believers who build that shield now will find it holding on the Day when no other shield remains. That work begins tonight, with one prayer kept on time, one fault owned without excuse, and one kind word that turns a whole house toward Allah and His mercy.

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:7
> ˹The deniers will then be told,˺ “O disbelievers! Make no excuses this Day! You are only rewarded for what you used to do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Voice That Ends All Pleading**

The verse sounds on the far side of death, where pleading stops. Deniers entering the Fire hear a flat ban: no excuses today. The tone is not mockery but closure. Courts on earth hear pleas because facts may hide; the court of that Day hides nothing. Books are open, limbs speak, angels testify. Excuse in that place is not defence but noise. The call names them as disbelievers, *kāfirīn*, those who covered truth they knew. The title fits the moment. Covering worked in life, where truth could be hid under wealth, jest, or force. It fails where covers burn. The verse thus draws a line across time. Yesterday excuses could be shaped; today only deeds count. The living hear that line while shaping is still possible. Plea now, change now, beg now—before the voice that ends all pleading speaks.

**This Day Means The Day Of Showing**

This Day is the Day of Resurrection, when hidden things show. Faces show, books show, scales show. The Book elsewhere joins creation to that showing: “For Allah created the heavens and the earth for a purpose, so that every soul may be paid back for what it has committed” (45:22). Purpose points to payday. A world built for justice must host a day of justice. That Day unmasks every mask. The polite denier who smiled at truth and the fierce denier who fought it stand in one row. Wealth's robe falls, tribe's shield drops, office's seal melts. Only the heart's record walks forward. The verse's “this” points as if to a finger on the scene. Hearers should picture it near, not far. Nearness breeds care. One who lives with This Day before his eyes guards his tongue at home and his deals in the market. Distance breeds delay, and delay breeds this rebuke.

**Excuses Fail Where Deeds Speak**

Excuses lean on doubt: I did not know, I could not, I was forced, I meant well. That Day dissolves each prop. Knowledge came through messengers, power was measured by the Knower, force was weighed, intents were read. The deniers will see their own deeds so plainly that excuse dies on the tongue. Early glosses note they will know the truth too well to plead. Sight replaces spin. The verse therefore teaches how to use excuses now. Valid excuse today seeks pardon and change: I was wrong, I return, I repair. Vain excuse today rehearses the banned plea: I am fine, others are worse, later I will turn. The first melts into repentance; the second hardens into this ban. Parents should teach children the split early. Own faults fast, fix them fast, and never polish them into tales. Tales told long enough become chains. Chains drag their makers to this voice.

**Reward Means Exact Return, Not Extra Pain**

The closing line states the court's math: they are rewarded only for what they did. *Tujzawna*, requital, means full return of the deed, neither clipped nor padded. The same rule is voiced elsewhere for the same scene (52:16). Pain in that place is not Allah's anger spilling past measure; it is the deed come home. Sweet water poured returns as sweet drink; poison poured returns as poison drunk. The exactness answers a hidden charge. Deniers often picture Hell as tyranny, a strong king crushing weak foes. The verse corrects the picture. Hell is harvest. The seed was theirs, the soil was theirs, the season was granted. Allah only kept the field. Justice this exact comforts the wronged. No tear shed under denial is lost; each returns as comfort. It warns the wrongdoer. No sneer at faith is lost; each returns as heat. Measure for measure, soul for soul.

**Justice Leaves No Room For Complaint**

None will be wronged in that court. The Book seals its justice verse with that pledge: “And none will be wronged” (45:22). Wrong in judgment means punishing the innocent, overpunishing the guilty, or ignoring the victim. All three are barred. The innocent fear no mix-up, since knowledge is perfect. The guilty fear no excess, since measure is exact. Victims fear no loss, since every due returns. The ban on excuses rests on that pledge. Excuse matters where error is possible; where error is nil, excuse is void. The verse thus joins terror to trust. Terror, since no plea bends the Judge. Trust, since no flaw stains Him. Believers live between those poles now. They fear their own faults enough to repent, and they trust Allah's justice enough to bear wrongs without despair. The deniers' ban today becomes the believers' comfort today: a court that hears no spin also makes no slip.

**Truth Seen Too Late Still Condemns**

The verse's deepest sting is timing. Deniers will see the truth at last—clear, bright, and past denial—yet sight will not save them. Belief after the veil lifts is not faith but force. The Book notes that late knowers confess their fault, yet confession without term earns no return. The lesson presses the living with urgency. Sight now, through signs and messengers, still saves. Sight then, through flame and scale, only condemns. Every dawn prayer kept, every lie dropped, every due paid turns present sight into future shield. Every delay gambles that dawn will come again. The verse offers no bridge from that Day back to this one. It only bars the plea. The next verse will open the bridge from this day forward: sincere return while breath lasts. Hear the ban, then haste to the door it leaves open. Today excuses can still become repentance. Tomorrow they become silence.

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:8
> O believers! Turn to Allah in sincere repentance, so your Lord may absolve you of your sins and admit you into Gardens, under which rivers flow, on the Day Allah will not disgrace the Prophet or the believers with him. Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right. They will say, “Our Lord! Perfect our light for us, and forgive us. ˹For˺ You are truly Most Capable of everything.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sincere Return Means Leaving And Not Looking Back**

The verse calls believers to *tawbah naṣūḥ*, sincere return. *Tawbah* means turning: the body quits the sin, the tongue owns it, the heart hates it. *Naṣūḥ* adds purity—return without rot, without a plan to slide back. Early teachers set its marks: leave the fault at once, feel true sorrow, resolve never to return, and repair dues owed to people. Sorrow without leaving is wish; leaving without resolve is pause; both without repair leave debts that cry on the Last Day. The call names believers, not deniers. Even the faithful stain their robes and need washing. No rank outgrows return. The Prophet's ﷺ house, just warned, hears it first; every house hears it next. Sincere return is not a mood for nights but a course for life. Turn fully, mend fully, walk straight—and do not price a return ticket to the pit.

**Hope In Perhaps Hides Certain Mercy For The True**

The verse links return to hope with the word *ʿasā*, perhaps. Perhaps your Lord will cover your faults and lodge you in Gardens. The “perhaps” is not doubt in Allah but awe in the servant. Mercy is sure for the true; the servant stays humble, unsure of his own truth. The Book often clothes sure promises in this soft word to keep hearts tender. Pride says Allah must forgive me; hope says perhaps He will, so I will strive and beg. That softness itself draws mercy. Early voices urge haste inside the hope: repent before death seals the book, and draw near with good deeds before business chokes the soul. Hope without haste is delay dressed as trust. True hope runs. It prays tonight what it missed at dawn, pays now what it withheld at noon, and begs pardon before sleep. Perhaps then turns to joy.

**Cover And Gardens Follow The Turn**

Two gifts follow sincere return: cover for evil deeds and entry to Gardens with rivers below. Cover, *takfīr*, wipes the record and shields from its harm. Gardens give the positive wage: shade, drink, rest, and nearness. The pair answers both fears. Shame fears exposure; loss fears want. Cover hides shame, Gardens end want. The Book ties the same cover to many doors—mindfulness, faith, secret charity, struggle, prayer with alms—yet all doors share one hinge: turning to Allah. Believers voice that hope in their own prayer: “Our Lord! Forgive our sins, absolve us of our misdeeds, and allow us ˹each˺ to die as one of the virtuous” (3:193). The verse here turns that prayer into promise. Turn sincerely, and the Lord who taught the prayer answers it. Rivers will run where tears ran. Shade will spread where shame burned.

**A Day When Allah Honours Prophet And Believers**

The promise lands on a Day described by honour, not fear. Allah will not disgrace the Prophet ﷺ nor those with him. *Lā yukhzī*, no disgrace, means no shame, no exposure, no defeat before foes. The Prophet ﷺ who was mocked for truth will stand crowned. Believers who stood with him—through prayer, dues, and patience—will share his light, not his trial. The phrase “with him” is wide. It holds Companions at his side and every faithful soul after them who kept his path. Togetherness here is creed and conduct, not blood or boast. The Day sorts crowds by that bond. Deniers face the ban on excuses; believers face veils lifted in honour. The verse thus sets return inside company. Repent alone in your room, and you join a train led by the best of men. No disgrace touches that line.

**Light Runs Ahead And Guards The Right**

Their light shines before them and on their right. Light here is faith made visible: guidance held in life now glows on the road. Front light shows the path across darkness and the bridge; right-side light marks honour, since records of the saved rest in right hands. The Book paints the same march elsewhere: believers move “with their light shining ahead of them and on their right” (57:12). Two lights, two guards. None stumble ahead, none fear attack from the honoured side. Hypocrites' lamps die on that road, as the longer passage warns, while the sincere stride on. The image urges present stocking. Prayer feeds the lamp, charity oils it, truth trims it, secret good shields its flame from wind. Small lamps lit daily become torches then. Darkness on that Day is real; only carried light walks through it. Return now is lamp-work for that road.

**Perfect Our Light, A Prayer That Never Ends**

The marchers still pray: Our Lord, perfect our light for us and forgive us. Though honoured and lit, they beg for more light and full pardon. Early glosses hear this cry as they watch others' lamps die; fear breeds plea. Others hear pure love: nearness whets thirst for nearness. Both meet in humility. The saved do not boast; they beg. “Perfect” asks for completion—no flicker, no gap, no end. “Forgive” asks for cover of last traces, since even bright souls feel stains before perfect Holiness. The plea ends with power: You are Most Capable of all things. Nothing in the request strains Him. The verse thus closes return with its own soul: begging to the end. Begin with turning, walk with light, and still beg for more. That prayer, learned now, will rise then from lips trained by years. Blessed are those who rehearse it daily.

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:9
> O Prophet! Struggle against the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and be firm with them. Hell will be their home. What an evil destination

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Order Sent Twice For Two Fronts**

This verse matches another sent in a different hour (9:73). Twice the Book arms the Prophet ﷺ with the same steel: strive against deniers and hypocrites, and stand firm. Repetition marks weight. The order is not for one battle or one town; it is standing policy for truth against falsehood. Two foes need two arts. Open deniers fight from outside with sword and scorn. Hypocrites rot from inside with smiles and plots. One threatens the body, the other the heart. The verse joins them under one verb, *jāhid*, strive with full effort, then splits the method by foe. The double sending also steadies the Prophet ﷺ. After strain at home and pressure from kin, Allah returns him to his public charge. Private mercy at home must not soften public firmness for truth. Both are mercy in their place.

**Striving Against Deniers Joins Sword And Speech**

Against open deniers the struggle runs on two rails: proof and power. Tongue comes first. Call them to Allah with signs, warnings, and clear debate. Show the Book, the cosmos, and the end of past towns. Many return through words alone, and words leave no widows. Where deniers bar the call with force, break treaties, or march to wipe out faith, the sword guards the call. Early commentators join both under this verse: strive with the blade when battle is just, and with preaching that summons to Allah. The pair guards against two errors. Speech without shield lets killers silence truth. Sword without speech turns defence into rage. The Prophet ﷺ held both. He debated in peace, fought in justice, forgave in victory, and never forced faith on a soul. Striving here means guarding the road for truth, not paving it with fear.

**Striving Against Hypocrites Uses Law And Proof**

Hypocrites claim faith while hiding denial, so the sword rarely fits them. The verse is read to mean struggle through proof, exposure, and just penalties. Unmask their doubts with clear teaching. Answer their whispers with open truth. Where they steal, slander, or break public law, apply the set penalties without favour. Where they shirk duty, name the shirking. The Book often fights them with light, not steel: it prints their marks, quotes their excuses, and warns them of disgrace. Firmness here is mostly moral. Do not buy their praise, do not fear their plots, do not bend rulings for their rank. The Prophet ﷺ modelled the line. He prayed over some, refused others, accepted outward claims while guarding the community, and let revelation sort hearts. The aim is cure where cure is possible, and guard where cure is refused.

**Firmness Is Mercy To The Flock, Not Cruelty**

The verse orders harshness, *ughluẓ*, thickness against softening. The word sounds hard, yet its root is care. A shepherd is hard on wolves to be kind to sheep. Softness to prowlers is cruelty to the flock. Firmness here means steady law, plain speech, and no deals that sell truth. It does not mean rage, insult, or wrong. The Prophet ﷺ stayed just while firm: no torture, no treachery, no harm to non-fighters, no forced creed. His firmness showed in resolve—prayer kept in danger, truth told to power, limits held against kin. That steel sheltered the weak. Believers could pray openly, women could keep dues, treaties held weight. Homes learn the same art. Parents are firm with lies, screens, and vice to be kind to souls. Leaders are firm with graft to be kind to the poor. True mercy wears a spine.

**Hell As Home Means No Other Address**

Their refuge is named as Hell. *Maʾwā*, home, means the place one returns to and rests in. For deniers who die denying and hypocrites who die plotting, no other lodging waits. The word mocks their earthly nests. They built fine houses, hoarded stores, and wove alliances, yet their true address stood elsewhere. Home also means fit. Fire fits hearts that burned with pride and plot; cool Gardens would scorn them. The verse fixes the end while struggle runs, so that striving keeps hope. Battles may be lost, debates may be jeered, plots may sting—yet the map is drawn. Falsehood's road ends in flame. That map steadies the striver. He does not need to see the end to trust it. He plants truth, waters it with patience, and leaves the harvest to Allah. Home waits for each planter: Gardens for the true, Fire for the false.

**Evil Destination Ends Every Pact With Falsehood**

The verse seals with a cry: what an evil destination. *Biʾsa al-maṣīr*, how vile the place to arrive. The cry is both verdict and warning. Verdict on those who chose denial and deceit to the end. Warning to any flirting with their path for gain. No pact with falsehood pays. Deniers promise power, hypocrites promise ease; both deliver this address. The Book elsewhere shows the true road's opposite end—light, not flame: “so that He may bring you out of darkness and into light” (33:43). Two roads, two homes. Struggle is the toll on the bright road; surrender is the slide on the dark. The verse leaves no middle inn. One strives with truth against both foes and walks toward light, or one suits both foes and slides toward Fire. The Prophet ﷺ is ordered to the first. Every believer after him hears the same order in his own post. That order asks for courage without cruelty, truth without fear, and patience that outlasts both sword and whisper until Allah judges justly at last. Stand firm, speak truth, and trust His sure help.

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:10
> Allah sets forth an example for the disbelievers: the wife of Noah and the wife of Lot. Each was married to one of Our righteous servants, yet betrayed them. So their husbands were of no benefit to them against Allah whatsoever. Both were told, “Enter the Fire, along with the others!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Wives Of Prophets Stand As Warning**

The verse lifts two ancient wives as a mirror for deniers. Both shared a prophet's home, table, and name. Both heard truth at dawn and dusk from the truest tongue in town. Yet both ended in Fire. Their tale opens the chapter's closing trio, which speaks first to the Prophet's ﷺ own house and then to all. The placement is deliberate. After calls to repent, guard the home, and strive firmly, Allah shows that nearness without faith saves none. Blood, bed, and roof cannot ferry a soul. Only belief with deeds boards the ark. The example is public, not private gossip. Names are spared in the verse's first half and the moral is printed large. Honour by association is the oldest pride; these two graves answer it. Live with a prophet if you will, but you must believe with him to be saved.

**Betrayal Meant Treason To Truth, Not Bed**

Their betrayal, *khiyānah*, was treason against the mission, not filth in marriage. Prophets' homes are guarded from shame of the bed; their trial was treason of the ear and tongue. Noah's wife is reported to have mocked him as mad before his mockers, lending scorn a witness from inside. Lot's wife is reported to have tipped off his wicked townsmen when guests arrived, handing angels' cover to lust. The Book elsewhere marks her end with the town she warned (7:83; 11:81). Both faults share a root: siding with the crowd against the prophet at home. The point cuts every house. A spouse who leaks secrets to mockers, jeers at prayer, or backs kin against truth repeats the same treason in small. Loyalty in marriage means more than fidelity of the bed. It means fidelity to the Lord of the bed.

**Marriage To The Righteous Saves No One Alone**

Each was wed to a righteous servant, yet the bond availed nothing. *ʿAbd*, servant, marks Noah and Lot as owned by Allah, loved, and heard. Still their love could not ferry unbelief. The verse kills the oldest excuse: my husband prays, my father teaches, my tribe is pious. On the Last Day each soul answers for its own creed. The Book states the court's math plainly: a soul is paid for what it earned, and longer rope only grows the pile for deniers: “They are only given more time to increase in sin” (3:178). Years beside a prophet only stacked their proof higher. Privilege of nearness became weight of witness. The lesson comforts as well as warns. None is doomed by a corrupt spouse either, as the next verse will show. Each soul stands on its own feet. Nearness helps those who walk; it sinks those who lean.

**Husbands Of God Cannot Shield Against God**

The verse stresses the null shield: their husbands availed them nothing against Allah. Noah, who bore a thousand years of scorn, could not block one spark for his wife. Lot, who offered his honour for his guests, could not buy his wife one shade. Prophets own no share in judgment. They warn, weep, and plead within leave, but they do not overrule. The line answers finanzielle pride in reverse. Just as no wealth buys a verdict, no marriage buys one. The court runs on truth held, not hands held. The phrase “against Allah whatsoever” closes every gap—no plea, no past service, no secret pact. Believers learn how to love kin rightly. Pray for them, teach them, live brightly before them, and plead for them while breath lasts. But never promise them cover you do not own. Cover belongs to Allah; means belong to us.

**Enter With The Others Ends All Privilege**

Both heard the same sentence: enter the Fire with the entrants. *Udkhulā*, enter, both of you, levels them with crowds they once courted. No separate gate for prophets' wives, no soft corner for old rank. “With the others” stings most. They chose the town over the prophet; they join the town in the end. Company kept in life becomes company kept in fire. The sentence also answers timing. It sounds as fact, past and sure, though its day still waits. Decree this firm reads as done. The living still choose their “others.” Walk with mockers for laughs now, and risk marching with them then. Walk with the righteous for Allah now, and hope to enter with them then. Homes decide this march daily. Jest at prayer pulls toward one crowd; haste to prayer pulls toward the other. The verse bids each soul to pick its “with” before the word is said.

**The Prophet's House Hears Its Own Lesson**

These closing verses circle back to the chapter's start. Wives who pressed the Prophet ﷺ to ban the lawful hear what treason to truth earned before. The warning is tender but plain. High marriage never licensed low conduct. Noah's and Lot's wives prove the rule from afar; the call to repent proves mercy near. The Book seals the justice behind it: souls are paid for their own earning, “And none will be wronged” (45:22). No wife of a prophet is wronged by that math, and no wife of a tyrant is saved by it without faith. The verse therefore steadies two hearts. It steadies the proud, who lean on rank, by showing rank's null shield. It steadies the afraid, who fear another's sin will sink them, by promising personal scales. Believe yourself, act yourself, and enter with the saved. No other bond boards the ark.

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:11
> And Allah sets forth an example for the believers: the wife of Pharaoh, who prayed, “My Lord! Build me a house in Paradise near You, deliver me from Pharaoh and his ˹evil˺ doing, and save me from the wrongdoing people.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Queen In A Tyrant's House Chooses Faith**

The verse turns the mirror. After two wives of prophets lost in Fire, one wife of a tyrant rises as light for believers. Āsiyah held Pharaoh's palace, wealth, and crown, yet she counted them as dust beside faith in Moses' Lord. Her choice shames every excuse. None can plead a hard house after hers. Husbands may mock, kin may press, rulers may threaten—none match Pharaoh, who claimed godhood and killed boys. Still she believed. Her faith was not quiet taste but open stand. She heard the signs, trusted them, and held them under torture. The example speaks first to the Prophet's ﷺ wives: rank never excuses pressure against truth. It speaks then to all: no address blocks heaven. Palace or hut, faith can bloom where Allah wills. The worst marriage on earth housed one of its best hearts.

**Near You Before House, Love Orders The Prayer**

Her prayer begins with order that teaches love. She asks for a house in Paradise, yet she places “near You” before walls and roof. Nearness outranks housing. Many beg for comfort first and add Allah's name after; she begs for Him first and takes comfort inside Him. *ʿIndak*, with You, means favour, care, and presence—not place, since Allah sits in no quarter. The phrase also answers her palace pain. She lived near a tyrant who owned rooms without number. Rooms meant nothing; nearness meant all. So she trades a near tyrant for a near Lord. Believers learn the ranking for every plea. Ask first for His face, His pardon, His company; then ask for home, health, and bread within that circle. Prayers ordered thus rarely go wrong. They seek the Giver before the gift, and the gift arrives cleansed by the seeking.

**Deliverance Asked From Man, Deed, And Crowd**

Her plea seeks three rescues in one breath. Save me from Pharaoh, from his doing, and from the wrongdoing people. From the man, whose hand tortured. From his doing, whose craft built idols, racks, and lies. From the crowd, whose cheers kept the machine fed. The triple ask shows full sight. Tyranny is not one fist but a ring: a head, a work, and a chorus. She begs exit from all three. The words also guard her own soul. She does not ask to rule his place, seize his gold, or curse his name with hate. She asks to leave his orbit clean. Early accounts say she spoke these words under torment and was shown her palace in light before her soul rose. True or emblem, the picture fits the prayer. Torture touched her skin; it never touched her rank. Allah lifted what Pharaoh crushed.

**Torture Could Not Break A Praying Heart**

Tradition holds that Pharaoh had her racked for faith in Moses, yet she held fast and prayed this verse. Pain pressed her body; hope held her heart. Her stand teaches the physics of trust. Bodies break, hearts anchored beyond breakage do not. She did not deny to save skin, nor curse to ease pain. She asked for nearness and exit, then bore what came. Her calm answers the chapter's earlier strain over small pressures. Two wives pressed a kind prophet over household hurt; one queen faced a killer king and never pressed truth to bend. Small trials deserve the same steel in small. A mocking spouse, a jeering kin, a hard office—none warrant selling faith for peace. The Book praises those who trust under harm: “And in Allah let the faithful put their trust” (14:12). She lived that line on the rack. Praying hearts outlast breaking hands.

**Four Women Crowned Above All Women**

Early reports crown her among four perfect women: Khadījah, Fāṭimah, Mary, and Āsiyah, wife of Pharaoh. The list itself preaches. A merchant widow who believed first, a daughter who guarded her father, a virgin who bore a prophet, a queen who left a tyrant—four roads, one crown. Worth here is faith under trial, not ease, birth, or beauty. Another report, however tender, must be weighed with care against firm texts, and scholars treat tales of heavenly marriages as weak beside the Qur'an's plain praise. What stands sure is this verse. Allah Himself sets her as example for all believers, men and women alike. That divine setting outranks every list. Crowns from people fade; a crown in the Book stays. Girls and boys should learn her name early. Greatness is not the palace you keep but the prayer you keep inside it.

**Corruption Beside Her Could Not Stain Her**

The verse's core doctrine shines: another's rot does not doom the righteous. Just as prophets' wives fell despite holy husbands, Pharaoh's wife rose despite a foul one. Scales weigh souls singly. The Book states the personal reckoning behind it: each soul is paid for its own earning, “And none will be wronged” (45:22). No tyrant husband's guilt stains a faithful wife; no mocking wife's guilt stains a faithful husband; no wrongdoing crowd's roar drowns a single true prayer. The comfort reaches hard homes now. A believer wed to a scoffer, raised by harsh kin, or ruled by a vile boss still owns a straight road. Keep faith, pray this queen's prayer in your own words, and leave their deeds while honouring due rights. Pharaoh owned her house, food, and guard. He never owned her heart. Hearts belong to Allah; He builds their true houses near Him. Hold fast, pray on, and trust His near reward.

## Sūrah at-Taḥrīm 66:12
> ˹There is˺ also ˹the example of˺ Mary, the daughter of ’Imrân, who guarded her chastity, so We breathed into her ˹womb˺ through Our angel ˹Gabriel˺. She testified to the words of her Lord and His Scriptures, and was one of the ˹sincerely˺ devout

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mary Completes The Pair Of Saving Examples**

The verse joins Mary to Pharaoh's wife as the second light for believers. Two dark wives opened the trio; two bright women close it. The balance is exact. Loss and gain both wear a woman's face, so neither sex can claim a monopoly on faith or ruin. Mary, daughter of ʿImrān, stands here not in her full story—that runs in her own chapter—but in four strokes: guarded chastity, divine breathing, true testimony, and ranked devotion. Each stroke answers a lie her people told. They charged her honour; Allah prints her guard. They doubted her son; Allah names His breath. They called her lone; Allah ranks her with the devout. The example speaks to the Prophet's ﷺ house first: purity, trust, and worship outshine pressure and pride. It speaks to all next: the highest rank opens to the humblest maid who guards and believes.

**Guarded Chastity Answers Lying Tongues**

She guarded her private part, *aḥṣanat farjahā*, kept it locked against all but Allah's leave. The guard was full: eyes lowered, doors shut, speech clean, heart fenced. When her people later pointed and accused, the guard stood as her silent witness. The Book elsewhere records their slur and her vindication through the infant's speech (19:27-28). The verse here prints the verdict in advance: guarded. The point reaches every youth in a loose age. Chastity is not old shame but kept honour. Locks held in private become crowns worn in public, if not in this town then before Allah. Parents learn the guard's shape. Teach sons and daughters to fence eyes first, for eyes pick locks. Fill homes with prayer, work, and modesty, so desire finds no idle hour. Mary kept herself in a temple town buzzing with tongues. Purity is possible where God is feared.

**Breathed Spirit And The Birth Without Father**

Allah breathed into her of His spirit, and Jesus came without a father. *Rūḥ*, spirit, here means a mighty, honoured breath from Allah, carried to her womb. The translation names the carrier as Gabriel, the trusted runner of revelation. The act is creation, not begetting. Allah says be, and wombs, dust, or nothing obey. The virgin birth crowns His power to begin life past all means. It also shields Mary's name. A child without a father could spell shame; Allah stamps it as sign. The Book pairs her guard with His breath in one breath: because she guarded, He honoured. Cause and crown meet. Doubters ask how; believers ask who. Who breathed? The Lord of spirits. That answer ends the how. Science maps means; it never bars the Maker from new means. The birth stands as mercy to her and proof to the world.

**Gabriel Or Spirit Of Jesus, Both Bow To Allah**

Early readers differed on whose spirit is meant. Some hear Gabriel himself, the Spirit who brings down orders, breathing Allah's command into her. They link this verse to the same event told elsewhere (21:91). Others hear the spirit of Jesus, a noble created soul Allah made and sent. A third hearing takes the words at face value: a spirit from Allah, honoured by nearness, not part of Him. The debate is honest, yet its root is one. All agree the breath came from Allah, none claims a son for Him, and all bow to His command “be.” The verse needs no party. What saves is not picking a gloss but confessing the Giver. Gabriel bows to Allah, Jesus bows to Allah, spirits bow to Allah. Mary bowed first among them in this tale. Follow her bow, and the gloss you hold will not harm you.

**Testifying To Every Word And Every Book**

She confirmed the words of her Lord and His Books. *Kalimāt*, words, covers decrees, promises, and commands whispered to her heart and shown in her life. *Kutub*, Books, covers the revealed scrolls before her—Torah, Psalms, and all true writ. *Ṣaddaqat*, testifying true, means she held them as fact and lived them as law. Her faith was not narrow pride in one page but wide trust in all pages from Allah. The trait rebukes pick-and-choose religion. Some take prayer and drop dues; some take fasting and drop chastity; some take one prophet and scorn another. Mary took all. Her son's Gospel would later confirm the same root, and the Qur'an confirms them both. The verse therefore sets her as a seal of whole faith. Believe every word He spoke, honour every Book He sent, and live the line they draw.

**Counted Among The Devout, Crowned By Line**

The verse ends by ranking her among the devoutly obedient, *qānitīn*. The masculine plural here honours her with the full band of steadfast men and women, as if to say her rank overflows her sex's roll. The Book praises that steadiness in righteous women: “And righteous women are devoutly obedient and, when alone, protective of what Allah has entrusted them with” (4:34). Mary lived that line in its hardest form: obedient in temple, in town, in birth pains under a palm, and under slurs after. Some hear a second praise in the phrase: she sprang from devout stock, daughter of a vowed house. Lineage graced her start, but conduct crowned her end. The chapter that began with a prophet's household strain closes with heaven's household stars. Pharaoh's wife and ʿImrān's daughter shine over every home. Guard chastity, trust the breath of Allah, confirm His Books, and stand devout.
