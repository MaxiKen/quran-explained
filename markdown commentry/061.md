# Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah (Chapter 60) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:1

> O believers! Do not take My enemies and yours as trusted allies, showing them affection even though they deny what has come to you of the truth. They drove the Messenger and yourselves out ˹of Mecca˺, simply for your belief in Allah, your Lord. If you ˹truly˺ emigrated to struggle in My cause and seek My pleasure, ˹then do not take them as allies,˺ disclosing secrets ˹of the believers˺ to the pagans out of affection for them, when I know best whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal. And whoever of you does this has truly strayed from the Right Way.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Alliance Is a Bond, Not a Mood**

The noun the verse forbids is *awliyāʾ*, the plural of *walī*: the person who has charge of your affair, the patron whose support you lean on when you cannot stand alone. That is a relationship of weight before it is a feeling, and the prohibition keeps the two apart. What made the act plausible was *mawadda*, the warm affection the believers still carried for their own people in Mecca — parents, cousins, the companions of childhood. The Book does not treat such warmth as wicked in itself. It reserves its strongest language for the love owed to God: “Still there are some who take others as Allah’s equal—they love them as they should love Allah—but the ˹true˺ believers love Allah even more.” (2:165). A believer may feel tenderness for a relative who rejects the message; he may not hand that relative the place that belongs to God and His Messenger, which is what *walī* means in practice. The law does not police the heart’s heat. It forbids a transfer of loyalty, and it names the affection as the reason the transfer is dangerous: soft feeling, not hard doctrine, is what usually opens the door.

**The Letter Carried Out of Madinah**

This verse was revealed in the run-up to the march on Mecca in 8/630, and the incident behind it survives in detail. Ḥāṭib ibn Abī Baltaʿah, an Emigrant who had fought at Badr, wrote to leading men of Quraysh to warn them that the Prophet was preparing to come against them. The Prophet sent ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib with al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām and al-Miqdād ibn al-Aswad, telling them that a woman travelling at Rawḍat Khākh carried a letter. They found her, and when she denied having anything, she brought it out of her braid (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3007). The document would have cost the Muslims the advantage of surprise against a city that still held their confiscated homes and their estranged kin. To call this a lapse of courtesy would be generous. The verse’s own description is *disclosing secrets ˹of the believers˺ to the pagans* (60:1) — intelligence handed to a party that had already driven the Messenger and his community out of Mecca, and the disclosure of a military plan is not a private matter between friends.

**“I Was Attached to Quraysh, Not One of Them”**

Confronted with the letter, Ḥāṭib did not lie. He explained that he was a man attached to Quraysh by alliance rather than by blood; every other Emigrant had relatives in Mecca who would shield his dependents and his property, while he had none. He wanted to place a favour with them for the sake of his family, and he insisted that he had not acted out of disbelief, apostasy, or any preference for unbelief after Islam (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3007). The Prophet accepted the account — “he has spoken the truth” — and when ʿUmar asked permission to strike the neck of “this hypocrite,” he refused, on the ground that Ḥāṭib had witnessed Badr, adding that perhaps God had looked upon the people of Badr and said, do as you please, for I have forgiven you (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3007). Both men are understandable: Ḥāṭib reasoned like a man with no clan in a world of clans, and ʿUmar judged the plain appearance of treason. The verse decides between them at the level of principle, not of persons: family protection may never be purchased with the community’s safety.

**Mercy for the Man Did Not Authorize the Act**

The Prophet’s word did not cancel the revelation, and the sūrah does not present the two as rivals. The prohibition stands as the opening command of the chapter precisely because the deed was a breach; the letter is the occasion of the law, not an exception to it. What the Prophet shielded was a person whose Badr service had been accepted by God; what the revelation shielded was the community’s security. Mercy that dissolves a rule is no longer mercy but the end of law, while mercy that spares a man and leaves the rule standing is the pattern here. The rebuke is worth measuring in full: the verse does not merely call the act unwise, it says of the one who does it that he *has truly strayed from the Right Way* (60:1). Nor does the revelation name Ḥāṭib. The rule is stated in the third person and left anonymous — a kindness to one man and a warning to every reader who will ever hold a secret of the believers.

**Secrecy Cannot Outrun the One Who Knows**

Against every strategy of concealment the verse sets a single sentence: God knows best what the believers hide and what they show. The letter had been written quietly and trusted to one woman, then hidden in her hair — the most carefully buried object in the whole affair — and the sūrah opens by announcing it to the community through the Prophet. The Book treats this pairing of hidden and open as a fixed law of its moral world: “He certainly knows best what ˹is hidden˺ in the heart.” (39:7). Loyalty that requires secrecy has already answered the question it was trying to avoid. The clause also exposes the self-deception in Ḥāṭib’s calculation: he weighed what the Quraysh could do for his relatives and what his relatives could do for him, and left out of the arithmetic the one party who was watching the entire transaction. A believer’s integrity is measured where no witness is present, because the Witness who matters never leaves.

**Straying From the Middle of the Road**

The verse’s closing phrase is exact and worth unpacking: *sawāʾ al-sabīl*, the middle of the road, where *sawāʾ* is the even, level centre of a path and *sabīl* is the road itself. A traveller who has lost the centre is not simply slower; he is walking on the shoulder, and the next step is the ditch. That image is stronger than a verdict of sin, because a lost traveller’s trouble is not one wrong movement but a wrong sense of direction, and his error repeats until he corrects the map he is using. Ḥāṭib’s map was the map of tribal obligation, and it had served him well until it put love of family above the safety of the faith that had reordered his loyalties. The Book states the standard for the same situation in the same balanced language: “Do not let the hatred of a people lead you to injustice. Be just!” (5:8). The middle of the road is where affection for kin survives and loyalty to God is not traded away for it.

**The Same Boundary Drawn Elsewhere**

The prohibition is not unique to the opening of this sūrah, and its repetitions show what pressure each version answers. “O believers! Do not take your parents and siblings as trusted allies if they choose disbelief over belief.” (9:23) names the family. “O believers! Do not take disbelievers as allies instead of the believers.” (4:144) names the community. The sharpest form names the whole social world: “You will never find a people who ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day loyal to those who defy Allah and His Messenger, even if they were their parents, children, siblings, or extended family.” (58:22). Read together, the verses trace a boundary of allegiance rather than a policy of hostility toward outsiders as such, and the sūrah itself goes on to distinguish the two categories with care.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:2

> If they gain the upper hand over you, they would be your ˹open˺ enemies, unleashing their hands and tongues to harm you, and wishing that you would abandon faith.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Conditional That Describes a Policy**

Arabic *in yaṯqafūkum* comes from *thaqifa*, to come upon someone, to catch him, to find him within reach — the word used of a hunter closing on his quarry. The conditional does not express doubt about whether the encounter will happen; it tells the believers what is stored behind the courtesies of the enemy. Affection was flowing one way. The believers were still writing letters and nursing old ties, and the reply would be violence the moment the balance of power shifted. This is the reasoning the verse offers for the prohibition that precedes it: an alliance is a contract, and a contract requires two parties. The verse therefore does a piece of moral accounting. It measures the relationship by what the other side does when it holds the upper hand, not by what it says while it does not. The same test appears in Sūrat al-Tawbah, where the Book says of treaty-breakers that if they gained the upper hand they would respect neither kinship nor pact (9:8). Power does not create enmity; it reveals what was already there, and the wise man reads the intention before the occasion arrives.

**Hands First, and Then Tongues**

The two instruments of the hostility are named in order: hands stretched out in violence, then tongues stretched out in harm. Classical commentary takes the hands as fighting and killing, and the tongues as insult, mockery, and slander. The sequence is worth noticing, because the Qur’an measures words as weapons. In Sūrat al-Aḥzāb, the hypocrites who stayed behind and then returned to the safety of Madinah are described as slashing the believers with razor-sharp tongues once the danger has passed (33:19) — the same injury, delivered from a place of safety. Believers are told plainly that they will hear much hurtful speech from those given the Scripture before them and from the polytheists, and that patience is the answer to it (3:186). The verse therefore prepares a community for a war that will be fought on two fronts. Blades take lives; tongues take reputations, morale, and the confidence of the weak, and the Qur’an refuses to grade the second as a lesser harm.

**The Wish That Runs Beneath Both**

The sentence ends with desire: they wish you would abandon faith. The verb is *yawaddū*, from the same root as *mawadda*, the affection named in the preceding verse, and the echo is the point. Whatever warmth the believers were sending to Mecca, what came back was not neutral coexistence but a wish aimed at the heart of their religion. The Book records the same wish from the same quarter again and again. “Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them.” (2:109). And “Some of the People of the Book wish to mislead you ˹believers˺. They mislead none but themselves, yet they fail to perceive it.” (3:69). A wish of that kind turns every other relationship into an instrument. The friendship is real as far as the enemy is concerned, but its object is not the believer’s welfare; it is the believer’s reversal. That is why the verse can describe men as both affectionate and hostile in the same breath, and why the earlier prohibition was a matter of self-preservation rather than of pride.

**Reading Interests Instead of Manners**

Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah teaches a hard lesson in judgement: courtesy is not evidence of intention. A community that mistakes good manners for good will is defenceless, because manners cost nothing while intentions are expensive. The criterion given here is conduct under pressure — what a party does when the upper hand is in its grip. At the same time the verse is not a licence for suspicion of every stranger, and the Book guards the other side of the balance: “Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you, but do not exceed the limits.” (2:190). The hostility described is not a suspicion but a record: these are the people who deny what has come to you of the truth and who drove the Messenger and the believers from their homes (60:1). Judgement rests on what is known, not on what is feared, and the sūrah will later mark the difference between enemies who fight and those who do not.

**What the Verse Refuses to Become**

Nothing in the verse commands retaliation, and it is important not to read into it an order that is not there. Its function is diagnostic: it tells the believers whom to trust and why the trust was misplaced. Had it been a command to fight, it would have said so; the next commands in this stretch of the sūrah keep to the work of separating categories of people, and the fighting verses elsewhere carry their own conditions and limits. The rule here is about the exchange of confidence, and it restores to the believers the freedom to stop performing affection they did not owe. A man who knows what his enemy wants can still deal with him justly; he simply no longer hands him the keys. This is the practical mercy in a warning verse: it does not make the world more hostile, it makes the believer less deceived.

**The Same Warning in Sūrat al-Tawbah**

Sūrat al-Tawbah states the identical situation — enemies who are trusted while powerless and rapacious when strong. “If they were to have the upper hand over you, they would have no respect for kinship or treaty. They only flatter you with their tongues, but their hearts are in denial, and most of them are rebellious.” (9:8). That verse adds a detail this one implies: the flattery is itself the instrument, smooth speech covering a settled refusal. It also asks the question that follows from the revelation of al-Mumtaḥanah’s opening: “Will you not fight those who have broken their oaths, conspired to expel the Messenger ˹from Mecca˺, and attacked you first? Do you fear them?” (9:13). Two sūrahs, one diagnosis. The enemy is defined by his aim, not by his tone of voice, and the community that has been driven from its homes is told to see clearly rather than to pretend.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:3

> Neither your relatives nor children will benefit you on Judgment Day—He will decide between you ˹all˺. For Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Bond That Cannot Be Cashed**

Ḥāṭib ibn Abī Baltaʿah wrote his letter for one reason: his relatives in Mecca had no protector, and he wanted to buy them one. This verse removes the currency he was spending. *Arḥām* — the word rendered as relatives — is literally the wombs, the ties of kinship that link one body to another; the verse sets those ties beside children, the other half of a man’s stake in the future, and declares that neither will benefit him on the Day of Judgment. The wording is not a moral judgement of family love. It states a fact about that court. Everything that buys safety in this world — clan, offspring, a name that opens doors — is absent from the reckoning, and the man who assumed that what protects him here would protect him there has misread the exchange rate between the two markets. The Book repeats the point across many sūrahs in varied words: the Day when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit (26:88), the Day when no parent will be of any benefit to their child, nor a child to their parent (31:33). It repeats the point because it is the hardest lesson for a tribal society to absorb.

**“He Will Decide Between You”**

The verb *yafṣilu* comes from a root meaning to separate, to cut between things that have run together, and it gives Arabic its words for decisive judgement. That image explains the verse. In this life the believer and the denier are mixed: they share tables, marriages, inheritances, and the same crowded streets, and a man can believe for years that the mixing will never be undone. On that Day the separation is executed. Commentators read the clause in two ways, and both are grammatical: it may mean that God will judge between you and settle the dispute, or that He will divide the company into two sides. The first reading fits the court imagery; the second reads the scene itself, the rows sorted by faith. Taken together they say the same thing. The loyalties a man kept loose here will be cut apart there, and the cutting is done by the One who knows exactly where each thread ran, without appeal and without the possibility of a mistake in the sorting.

**Two Ways to Attach the Day**

The sentence admits a second syntactic arrangement that the exegetes record. One may read it as “Neither your relatives nor children will benefit you on Judgment Day—He will decide between you ˹all˺.” (60:3) so that the negation belongs to the whole clause of benefit: whatever the day, kin and children are not the currency. Or one may attach the time phrase to the first clause only — the relatives and children will not benefit you on that Day (60:3) — leaving the separation between the two groups as an independent statement. The two readings differ in scope, not in doctrine. The first is the more sweeping and the more likely to have been intended, since the Book uses the same argument elsewhere without limiting it to a single day: “Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope.” (18:46). The second keeps the verse tightly anchored to the resurrection scene that the sūrah has been building. Neither permits the reader to keep a lever that the verse has just removed.

**Every Soul Arrives Alone**

The Qur’an describes the resurrection as the collapse of genealogy into individuals. The trumpet is blown and there is no kinship between them on that Day, nor does anyone ask about another (23:101) — the very thing that every tribe takes for granted, the mutual inquiry of relatives on the day of catastrophe, is gone. And the Book states the principle without qualification: “No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another. Then to your Lord is your return, and He will inform you of what you used to do.” (39:7). This is why the sūrah’s teaching is not harshness. A community that thinks family can cover a defect will keep building on sand, and the single greatest mercy that can be granted to it is to be told, in advance, that the sand will not hold. The believer who is separated from his kin by their disbelief loses a bond in time; the verse argues that the bond was already on loan, and that only the faith he carries into that court will be his own possession.

**God Sees the Deed, Not the Lineage**

The verse seals the matter with a single attribute: “For Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.” (60:3). The object of the seeing is works — *ʿamal* — not ancestry, not the size of a family, not the eloquence of a claim. This closing formula is one the Book uses when it wants to place responsibility exactly where it belongs, and it recurs with the same function: “O believers! Stand firm for Allah and bear true testimony. Do not let the hatred of a people lead you to injustice. Be just! That is closer to righteousness. And be mindful of Allah. Surely Allah is All-Aware of what you do.” (5:8). Sūrat al-Mumtaḥanah opens with a man who acted out of family feeling and closes this early passage by removing family feeling from the scale of judgement. What remains on the scale is the deed. The believer is not asked to stop loving his kin; he is told that love will not testify for him, and that only his own record will be read aloud.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:4

> You already have an excellent example in Abraham and those with him, when they said to their people, “We totally dissociate ourselves from you and ˹shun˺ whatever ˹idols˺ you worship besides Allah. We reject you. The enmity and hatred that has arisen between us and you will last until you believe in Allah alone.” The only exception is when Abraham said to his father, “I will seek forgiveness for you,˹” adding, “but˺ I cannot protect you from Allah at all.” ˹The believers prayed,˺ “Our Lord! In You we trust. And to You we ˹always˺ turn. And to You is the final return.

**Expanded Commentary**

**What the Word “Example” Carries Here**

*Uswa* is not a lesson learned from a story; it is a pattern a person follows, the model whose condition you adopt and reproduce. The verse says that Abraham and those with him have been exactly that for the believers — *qad kānat lakum uswatun ḥasana* — and it places the model next to the hardest command the community had received, the cutting of loyalties to its own people. The Book uses the same phrase of the Prophet himself: “Indeed, in the Messenger of Allah you have an excellent example for whoever has hope in Allah and the Last Day, and remembers Allah often.” (33:21). Abraham is named as the pattern for the same work in a different age, which is the Qur’anic way of showing that the demand is not an innovation of Madinah. What the believers are being handed is not an anecdote about a distant ancestor but a tested method for a situation that felt unbearable, because the community of Madinah was being asked to shed the affections that had formed them. Abraham had lived the same severance in Ur and Ḥarrān, and he had survived it with his faith intact.

**“Those With Him”: Prophets or Believers?**

Commentators divide on the identity of the group. Al-Ṭabarī takes “those with him” as the prophets who followed Abraham’s way and declared the same distance from their own communities, which would make the verse a panorama of prophetic practice. Al-Rāzī and others take the phrase as Abraham’s believing followers, the family and companions who joined his declaration, which makes the verse a model for ordinary believers rather than for prophets alone. The disagreement turns on how one reads the clause “when they said to their people” (60:4). If the speakers are the prophets, the sentence describes a repeated scene, since every messenger said something of this kind to his own people. If the speakers are Abraham’s household, the sentence describes one specific meeting in a single town. The second view fits the verse’s immediate purpose better, because it makes the audience in Madinah the direct heirs of a community rather than the spectators of a list, and the practical content of the model is the same either way. What is transferred is a posture toward idolatry, not a set of biographical details.

**A Declaration of Clear Break**

The declaration itself is built from two Arabic words that carry the weight: *burāʾāʾ*, meaning free of, clear of, quit of a thing, and *kafarnā bikum*, the verb of denial used here not as a theological slogan but as the closing of a relationship. The dissociation has two objects, and the verse names both: you, and whatever you worship besides Allah. That order matters. The rupture is first a rejection of the worship that has taken the place of God, and only then a distance from the worshippers, which is why the model is not hatred of persons but refusal of a false claim. The Book records the same declaration from Abraham in another sūrah with the same elements: “˹Remember, O Prophet˺ when Abraham declared to his father and his people, ‘I am totally free of whatever ˹gods˺ you worship, except the One Who originated me, and He will surely guide me.’” (43:26–27). The exception in that verse is the whole secret of Abraham’s freedom: he had something to leave for, not merely something to leave.

**Enmity With a Door Left Open**

The most striking clause is the one that sets a term to hostility: enmity and hatred have arisen between us and you forever, until you believe in Allah alone. Permanent and conditional sit side by side, and the exegetes resolve the apparent contradiction the same way: the enmity is durable for as long as its cause lasts. The break is not a mood that may soften with the seasons; it is a stated position, and the position is defined by its exit. If the people of Mecca believe, the wall comes down at once, and the sūrah itself will later describe exactly that turn of events in the community that Ḥāṭib feared. That is the difference between a feud and a boundary. A feud wants an enemy; a boundary wants the truth to be acknowledged, and it is content to be abolished by that acknowledgement. The Book gives the same instruction in another place and adds the outcome: “Good and evil cannot be equal. Respond ˹to evil˺ with what is best, then the one you are in a feud with will be like a close friend.” (41:34). The door in this verse is left open for precisely that reversal.

**The Exception Made for a Father**

The verse then excludes one sentence from the model: Abraham’s promise to seek forgiveness for his father, with the admission attached to it that he had no power to avail him anything against Allah. The promise was real, and Abraham kept it for as long as it could be kept — the Book elsewhere records that his prayer was in fulfilment of a promise he had made, and that when it became clear to him his father was an enemy of Allah, he repudiated him (9:114). So the exclusion is not a rebuke of a misconducted prayer; it is a statement that this particular act belongs to a stage of Abraham’s story that cannot be imitated as a rule. The exegetes agree that a Muslim is not commanded to seek forgiveness for those who die in denial, since the request presumes a repentance that is not there. The verse teaches something sharper still by including the exception at all. Even a prophet’s affection for his father could not shield a defiant worshipper of idols from the consequence of his choice, and Abraham said so himself rather than pretending otherwise.

**A Prayer of Trust at the End**

The section closes with a prayer: “Our Lord! In You we trust. And to You we ˹always˺ turn. And to You is the final return.” (60:4). Three verbs, and each one answers a temptation in the situation. *Tawakkul*, entrusting one’s affairs to God, replaces the tribal protector that a man like Ḥāṭib wanted to secure. *Ināba*, turning back to God repeatedly, describes the lifelong motion of a creature who drifts and returns. *Maṣīr*, the journey’s end, reminds the one who prays that all roads, including the road of the Emigrants, finish at the same destination. The translation places these words in the mouths of the believers, and that reading makes good sense of the passage: the community that has just lost its earthly supports is taught what to say instead. This is the model in its practical form. Abraham left a city, and what he carried out of it was a trust that no city could take back, which is the whole lesson the sūrah wants a frightened community to learn.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:5

> Our Lord! Do not subject us to the persecution of the disbelievers. Forgive us, our Lord! You ˹alone˺ are truly the Almighty, All-Wise.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prayer Against Becoming the Test**

*Fitna* names the ordeal that sifts a person: a thing is put to the test, and what it truly is comes out. The believers here ask God not to place them in that position for others — *lā tajʿalnā fitnatan li-lladhīna kafarū* — and the commentaries give the phrase two readings. On the first, the community asks not to be defeated by the disbelievers, because a defeat would be read as evidence: the enemy would conclude that his way was the true one and the believers were abandoned. On the second, the community asks not to become the occasion of punishment, since the harm done to them would be answered by God, and the harmers would then be destroyed for the sake of the harm. The first reading fits the sequence of the sūrah more exactly, because the whole passage concerns a community being driven out and warned against trusting its persecutors, and the danger of a defeat that teaches the wrong lesson is what a weak community fears most. The two readings are not far apart in substance: the second describes what happens to the enemy, the first describes what the enemy learns.

**Why a Believer’s Defeat Teaches a Lie**

The petition rests on a sober view of how people read history. A community that says God is with it and then disappears from the field has taught a lesson its enemies will absorb at once. The Qur’an speaks of the other side of this situation in Sūrat al-Burūj, where the persecutors of believing men and women who do not repent are promised the punishment of Hell and the torment of burning (85:10). Before that verse, the Book states the arrangement plainly: “We have made some of you a trial for others.” (25:20). The trial is structural. Believers are tested by opposition, and opponents are tested by the spectacle of believers who hold on. So the prayer in this verse is not a request for comfort; it is a request that the community’s endurance be allowed to testify, and that the testimony not be broken by collapse. The same concern shapes the Book’s account of earlier communities, who were afflicted with suffering and adversity until they asked when God’s help would come, and were answered that help is always near (2:214).

**Moses’ People Prayed the Same Way**

The prayer has a precedent in the Book in nearly these words. When the people of Moses lost their children to Pharaoh’s campaign of slaughter and were still without a state of their own, they said: “In Allah we trust. Our Lord! Do not subject us to the persecution of the oppressive people, and deliver us by Your mercy from the disbelieving people.” (10:85–86). The similarity is not accidental. Sūrat al-Mumtaḥanah belongs to a community in the same shape: believers without power in the very city that has expelled them, facing an enemy that could still reach their family members. The Book repeatedly gives such communities the same vocabulary instead of new answers, and the reuse is itself a form of instruction. What was asked in Egypt and what is asked in Madinah is what the weak have always needed: not merely safety, but safety that does not come at the cost of the truth they are carrying. The verse teaches the believers to ask for the version of relief in which God is still the one who is proven right.

**The Forgiveness Sits in the Middle**

The prayer is built in three parts — protect us, forgive us, name Your power and wisdom — and the forgiveness in the middle carries its own argument. A community that begins by asking to be spared humiliation also admits that it has given its enemies material to work with. Sins weaken a people from the inside before any siege begins, and the Qur’an states the connection: “Indeed, Allah would never change a people’s state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺.” (13:11). An earlier verse of the Book addresses the same logic from the other direction: Allah would never punish a people while His Prophet was among them, nor while they sought forgiveness (8:33) — the presence of the righteous and the practice of asking are both shields. Taken together these verses explain the order of the petition here. Before asking that the enemy be checked, the community asks that its own record be cleared, because the wound that matters first is the one inside the camp.

**Power and Wisdom Chosen for the Close**

The prayer ends with two names: the Almighty, the All-Wise. The selection answers the two questions a frightened community carries. Power answers whether defeat is inevitable; wisdom answers whether the outcome, whatever it looks like, is being arranged by someone who knows what He is doing. A believer can be given safety by power alone, and can be given suffering by wisdom alone, and the combination of the two names means that neither the victory nor the trial is accidental. The pairing runs through the Book as a signature ending: the same two names close the first verse of Sūrat al-Ṣaff, where all creation glorifies the One who is Mighty and Wise (61:1). It matters that the sūrah puts such names at the end of a prayer made by people who had lost their homes. The names are not a consolation prize for the powerless; they are an exact description of the God the powerless are leaning on, and of the two qualities their situation requires Him to have.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:6

> You certainly have an excellent example in them for whoever has hope in Allah and the Last Day. But whoever turns away, then surely Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Example With a Condition Attached**

The model returns, and this time the verse states the price of admission: the example in Abraham and his companions is for whoever hopes in Allah and the Last Day. The qualification is not decorative. A community can admire a hero without following him, and admiration costs nothing while imitation costs a great deal — a house, a name in the family register, the protection of a clan. Hope in the meeting with God is the one motive strong enough to pay those costs, and the Book names it in the same terms elsewhere: “Whoever hopes for the meeting with Allah, ˹let them know that˺ Allah’s appointed time is sure to come.” (29:5). So the verse does not offer Abraham as an interesting precedent. It offers him as a pattern to be copied by people who expect to stand before God and be asked about what they copied. Where that expectation is present, the sacrifice looks like a trade with a certain return; where it is absent, the same sacrifice looks like a loss, and no argument can make it look like anything else.

**Hope as the Engine of Imitation**

The Arabic verb behind hope in this verse, *yarjū*, describes expectancy with desire — waiting for something good that one believes will come. The Book uses it as the motive for right action in several places: those who hope for the meeting with their Lord are told to do good deeds and not to associate anything with Him in worship (18:110); the Prophet himself is given as an example for whoever has hope in Allah and the Last Day (33:21). The logic is economical and exact. Every departure made in the path of God involves giving up something that exists now for something that has been promised, and only hope can hold the two in view at the same time. The Emigrants of Madinah had left their houses, and Abraham had left his father’s household: the same transaction, differently charged. The verse therefore reduces the whole question of loyalty to a question about desire. Not what a man feels for his kin, but what he expects from his God, decides where his weight rests, and the Book states this as a law of human motivation rather than as a moral demand.

**Turning Away Harms No One Else**

The second half of the verse provides for the case of refusal, and it does so without the ordinary apparatus of threat: whoever turns away — then surely Allah is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy. The refuge of the Book here is not punishment but the independence of God, which is the most sobering thing that can be said to a person who imagines his obedience is needed. The same pair of names appears when earlier prophets made the same point to their own rebellious peoples. Moses said it to Israel: “If you along with everyone on earth were to be ungrateful, then ˹know that˺ Allah is indeed Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.” (14:8). The sūrah of the Cave and the sūrah of Fāṭir say the same to humanity at large: “It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.” (35:15). The refusal contemplated in this verse is not a defeat for God. It is a self-inflicted loss, and the verse names the loss by naming the One who does not need the refuser.

**Praiseworthy Even Without the Praise**

The second name deserves its own attention, because it answers the pride that the first name might wound. *Ghanī* says God needs nothing; *Ḥamīd* says He is the praised one regardless. The combination rules out two errors at once. Against the idea that the creature’s worship adds something to God, the verse places His self-sufficiency; against the idea that a world which rejects Him is therefore a pointless or worthless world, it places His praiseworthiness, which does not rise or fall with the world’s applause. Praise belongs to Him because of what He is, not because of what He is given, and that is why the Book can ascribe praise to Him in the same breath as it reports widespread ingratitude. A believer who understands the two names together stops looking for leverage over God. He obeys, and he does not imagine that his obedience is a favour being done to the One who commanded it. It is a favour being done to himself, and the verse says so by naming the object of the whole question as the point.

**Nothing Compelled, Nothing Concealed**

One more consequence follows from the way the verse is built. The refusal is left standing as a possibility, and the community is not instructed to force the pattern on anyone: “Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood.” (2:256). The Qur’an persuades and warns, states the model, states the cost, and leaves the human being to decide. That is the same posture as its opening commands in this sūrah, which forbid a certain alliance rather than punishing those who keep it. The Book’s method rests on the difference between the community’s borders and a person’s conscience, and the verse marks the border precisely where the argument ends. Whoever is moved by hope in God and the Last Day will follow Abraham; whoever turns away does not injure the One he turned from, and does not sit outside the reach of revelation when it names him plainly and gives him the reason, not merely the verdict.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:7

> ˹In time,˺ Allah may bring about goodwill between you and those of them you ˹now˺ hold as enemies. For Allah is Most Capable. And Allah is AllForgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**“It May Be” — a Door Left Open**

The verse turns on a single word: *ʿasā*, which expresses expectation rather than doubt. God may bring about goodwill between you and those of them you now hold as enemies. The construction is even more pointed in Arabic, since it sets God, the believers, and the hostile party inside one promise, with *mawadda*, warm affection, as the thing placed between them. Two words from earlier in the sūrah return here, and they return reversed. The *mawadda* that the believers were forbidden to hand to the enemy in verse 1 becomes a gift God promises to create; the *ʿadāwa*, the enmity declared against idolaters in the story of Abraham, reappears in the participle *ʿādaytum*, "those you have made enemies." Nothing has been softened, and no command has been withdrawn. What the verse adds is a horizon. Enmity between the community and a particular people is not a permanent state of creation; it is a historical condition with a possible end, and the initiative for that end belongs to God, who arranges hearts as He arranges seasons.

**A Marriage That Bound Two Houses**

The reports place the verse among the hardest moments of the community’s early life in Madinah. When the story of Abraham and his companions was recited, many Muslims announced that they had no further use for their idolatrous relatives, and the severance weighed on them heavily. Then came this promise (al-Wāḥidī records the occasion). How it was realised can be read in the Prophet’s own household: he had married Umm Ḥabībah bint Abī Sufyān, a woman who had emigrated to Abyssinia for her faith, and her father Abū Sufyān was the chief architect of Quraysh’s hostility to the community. A marriage had already tied the two families together before the politics between them changed, and within two years the change came: Mecca was entered in 8/630 without a battle of annihilation, and the man who had led the opposition accepted Islam. God’s method in the verse is therefore not magic. He changes hearts through events — a marriage, a journey, a treaty, a defeat that does not humiliate — and He asks the believers to leave room for those events.

**Capable of What Wealth Cannot Buy**

The middle of the verse states the reason for confidence: “For Allah is Most Capable.” The claim had already been demonstrated in the same city, and the Book points to the proof. The tribes of Madinah, al-Aws and al-Khazraj, had been locked in a feud for generations that no mediator could settle; the Qur’an calls them out by name as the evidence: “He brought their hearts together. Had you spent all the riches in the earth, you could not have united their hearts. But Allah has united them. Indeed, He is Almighty, All-Wise.” (8:63). That verse is a bill of accounts presented to anyone who thinks reconciliation is bought with money and diplomacy. The same Creator addressed the harder problem in this verse, where the parties were not two tribes of the same city but a community and the city that had expelled it, with confiscated property and unsold grievances between them. A promise of affection across that distance would be hollow if the One making it were anything less than the One who created the affection in the first place.

**Forgiveness Aimed at Former Enemies**

The verse closes with two names that seem, at first glance, to belong to a different subject: Forgiving, Most Merciful. They are not an afterthought. The enmity contemplated here has a long history of injury, and the reconciliation being promised would be impossible if every act committed in the course of it remained chargeable. The Book applies the same logic to the men of Mecca who were taken captive at Badr: “If Allah finds goodness in your hearts, He will give you better than what has been taken from you, and forgive you.” (8:70). And at Ḥudaybiyyah, the Book reminds the believers of the restraint that had already been exercised on both sides: “He is the One Who held back their hands from you and your hands from them in the valley of ˹Ḥudaibiyah, near˺ Mecca, after giving you the upper hand over ˹a group of˺ them.” (48:24). The names Forgiving and Merciful at the end of this verse tell the injured party what the future of a successful reconciliation will require of them, and what God has already promised to supply.

**What the Injured Are Asked to Keep**

The verse does not ask the believers to pretend that nothing happened, to disband their defences, or to reopen the flow of confidence that the opening verses closed. It asks them to keep one door unlocked — the possibility that God will change the men who expelled them. That is a demand with real content. A community that has decided that the present must repeat the past has quietly denied God’s capability, and has converted its own wound into an obligation that the future may not touch. Hope of the kind named in the previous verse is the exact counterweight: the person who expects to stand before God and find the ledger settled has no need to collect the whole debt himself, and no reason to insist that the enmity he inherited must survive him. What the verse offers, finally, is not sentiment about enemies but a discipline of the imagination. God is capable of making affection where there is hatred now, and the believer who believes that will behave differently the moment the change appears.

**The Same Promise in Sūrat al-Rūm**

The Book states the promise of created affection as one of the signs by which God is known. “And one of His signs is that He created for you spouses from among yourselves so that you may find comfort in them. And He has placed between you compassion and mercy. Surely in this are signs for people who reflect.” (30:21). That verse speaks of marriage, and it uses the same word that appears here for the relationship of two nations — *mawadda*, with *raḥma* added to it, the very pair of attributes with which this verse ends. The community of Madinah had experienced both halves of the pattern. It had been a people of feuds who became brothers, and the Book makes that history the ground of its confidence: “Remember Allah’s favour upon you when you were enemies, then He united your hearts, so you—by His grace—became brothers.” (3:103). What had happened between tribes was possible between peoples.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:8

> Allah does not forbid you from dealing kindly and fairly with those who have neither fought nor driven you out of your homes. Surely Allah loves those who are fair.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Boundary Is Conduct, Not Creed**

After the hard commands, the verse draws the limit of their reach: Allah does not forbid you from dealing kindly and fairly with those who have neither fought you over your faith nor driven you from your homes. The criterion given is not belief or unbelief but aggression. A polytheist who has stayed at his post, plowed his field, and never lifted a hand against the community is not covered by the prohibition, and the Book refuses to treat his creed as sufficient cause for hostility. The point is exacted by the verb: those who *have not fought you in religion and have not expelled you*. The verse is therefore a boundary marker inside the sūrah, not a softening of it. The chapters that forbid alliance with the enemies of God are the same chapters that forbid doing harm to the harmless. The Book never describes truth and falsehood as equal, but it does describe people as distinct from one another by what they have done, and that distinction is the legal architecture of the Muslim community’s relations with outsiders for the rest of its history.

**Asmāʾ’s Mother Comes to Madinah**

The occasion for the verse is recorded from Asmāʾ bint Abī Bakr, whose mother came to her in Madinah while still a polytheist, seeking some gift or favour from her daughter. Asmāʾ sent the question to the Prophet: my mother has come to me, wanting something from me — should I keep good relations with her? He answered: yes, keep good relations with her. The report is found in the two most reliable collections, and the verse was recognised as the ruling that answered her question (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2620; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1003). The detail that makes the report so instructive is that the woman was a polytheist of Mecca and the mother of a Muslim woman, and the answer was still towards kindness. Kindness to parents is not conditional on agreement in faith, and Islam did not dissolve the affections that God had placed in the family. Nor did the verse confine the concession to relatives: the words are general, so every Muslim may deal generously and fairly with any peaceful non-Muslim, and the closing sentence, that God loves those who are fair, applies to the dealings themselves.

**The Word Behind “Dealing Kindly”**

*Tabarrūhum* comes from the root that gives Arabic its word for filial duty, *birr*, the dutiful kindness a child owes a parent, and the verb carries the sense of treating someone generously, gently, and without ulterior motive. The Book uses the same moral vocabulary when it commands kindness to parents and uses it again when it commands the opposite of hostility. Alongside *birr* the verse places *iqsāṭ*, giving others their due and more precisely giving them the fair share of a thing, from the same root as *qisṭ*, the just balance. The pair is the most complete formula possible for ordinary coexistence: warmth in the treatment and fairness in the dealings. Both words are needed. Kindness without justice becomes partiality that wrongs a third party; justice without kindness becomes cold correctness, and the verse will not settle for either. “Indeed, Allah commands justice, grace, as well as courtesy to close relatives. He forbids indecency, wickedness, and aggression.” (16:90). The Book collapses the distance between the outer and the inner here: what is required of the believers is a settled posture towards people, not a permission slip for dislike.

**Justice Owed Even Where Feeling Is Absent**

That God loves the fair is a statement worth pausing on, because it means fairness can be an act of worship by itself. The Book states the standard in the strongest terms for a community that had every reason to feel aggrieved: “O believers! Stand firm for Allah and bear true testimony. Do not let the hatred of a people lead you to injustice. Be just! That is closer to righteousness.” (5:8). Hatred is named as the thing most likely to make a believer wrong someone, and the Emigrants of Madinah had far more than a little hatred to manage. The verse under discussion therefore supplies a test that runs in both directions. It forbids the believer to convert his faith into a licence for aggression against the uninvolved. And it forbids him to convert his grievance into a reason to withhold what a fair person owes. A community in the middle of a conflict is asked to keep counting what each person has actually done, which is exactly the work that anger makes difficult.

**Kindness Is Not the Same as Alliance**

Two different relationships are in play, and the whole passage turns on keeping them apart. *Birr*, the kindness commanded here, is the ordinary good conduct of neighbours, trading partners, and relatives. *Wilāya*, the alliance forbidden in the opening verses, is the transfer of one’s weight and protection to the other side. The verse therefore allows the first with those who have not fought, and the following verses fix the rule for those who have. Nothing in the concession permits the believer to consult the enemies of the community on its affairs, or to shelter those who are working to harm it, or to prefer their security to the security of Muslims. A man can be good to a neighbour and still refuse him the keys, and the Book expects him to know the difference. What this verse settles is the everyday question that a believer with non-Muslim kin keeps asking: may I be good to them? The answer is not merely that he may; the Book tells him that fairness of this kind is a mark of the people God loves.

**When the Cause of the Boundary Ends**

The rule is phrased so that it dissolves when its cause dissolves. "Those who have neither fought nor driven you out" is the description of the people in question, and the shape of the sentence implies that their descendants or successors, who no longer do those things, are no longer addressed by the prohibition. The Book shows the same instinct in the verses that regulate war: “If the enemy is inclined towards peace, make peace with them. And put your trust in Allah.” (8:61). It goes further and prescribes the response that can end a feud permanently: “Good and evil cannot be equal. Respond ˹to evil˺ with what is best, then the one you are in a feud with will be like a close friend.” (41:34). So the boundary here is not a wall with a permanent tenant. It is a line drawn around conduct, and conduct can change; when it changes, the command that governed the relationship changes with it, which is precisely the kind of outcome the next verse's definition of the hostile party leaves room for.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:9

> Allah only forbids you from befriending those who have fought you for ˹your˺ faith, driven you out of your homes, or supported ˹others˺ in doing so. And whoever takes them as friends, then it is they who are the ˹true˺ wrongdoers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Acts That Define the Enemy**

The verse states whom the prohibition does reach, and it does so with three specifications in rising order. There are those who fought you for your faith; those who drove you out of your homes; and those who supported others in doing so. The first clause covers the armed hostility, the second the dispossession that followed, and the third closes the space that the first two might have left open. Anyone who aided the expellers with money, weapons, shelter, or encouragement belongs in the same category, and the Book is explicit that the accomplice is not a neutral party. The precision matters because the previous verse had drawn the boundary by conduct, and a boundary drawn by conduct requires a definition of the conduct. The Qur’an supplies one rather than leaving it to the anger of the injured. A believer is not authorised to decide for himself that someone is his enemy; the criteria are given, and they are all verifiable acts — fighting, expulsion, and support for expulsion. Grievance alone does not qualify a person for the prohibition, which is exactly the safeguard this verse provides.

**Aiding the Wrongdoer Makes You a Partner**

The third clause rests on a principle that runs through the Book: sharing in an injustice is sharing the act. The verse names the expellers as the ones who *drove you out of your homes, or supported ˹others˺ in doing so*, and the phrasing places the supporter in the same sentence as the perpetrator. In another place the community is told plainly: “Cooperate with one another in goodness and righteousness, and do not cooperate in sin and transgression.” (5:2). The same logic appears in the law of war in the same sūrah’s wider context: those who “attacked you first” are the ones who must be answered (9:13). A modern reader can see why the clause was necessary. Expulsion is rarely the work of an army alone; it is financed by merchants, blessed by poets, executed by mobs, and remembered by scribes. If the verse had named only those who carried the sword, the flatterers and financiers would have considered themselves outside the matter. The Qur’an refuses that division of labour, and it holds the whole apparatus of hostility responsible as a party.

**Wrongdoers, Not Merely Misguided**

The verdict at the end of the verse escalates, and the escalation tracks the earlier commands: whoever takes such people as friends, then it is they who are the true wrongdoers. The word is *ẓālimūn*, those who place a thing where it does not belong — the root sense of injustice, a distortion in the arrangement of things. The man who leans on the people who expelled his community has misplaced the object of his loyalty, and the damage is not only to the community. He has wronged his own soul, because he has entrusted his weight to those who will not carry it and turned his back on the One who will. This is why the verse does not treat his act as a minor lapse of judgement. Earlier in the sūrah a man was described as having strayed from the road; here the person who does the same thing knowingly is described as having displaced justice itself in his own house. The Book grades the fault by what the offender has done with what belonged to God.

**The Question the Verse Puts Back to the Reader**

The verse supplies its own diagnosis of how the fault works. Loyalty in these cases is not usually a decision arrived at openly; it is a slow transfer of trust, made in small payments, each one reasonable at the time. The Book answers this with a question aimed at the direction of the deal: “O believers! Do not take disbelievers as allies instead of the believers. Would you like to give Allah solid proof against yourselves?” (4:144). The image is legal and uncomfortable: the proof offered against the accused is his own record, handed in voluntarily. The verse under discussion states the same conclusion in a shorter form by naming the friend-taker himself as the wrongdoer, and it does not add a circumstance in which that verdict could be avoided. The only thing that changes it is a change in the other party’s conduct, which means the believer always has somewhere to look for hope and nothing to gain by pretending the evidence is unclear.

**The Book Names Its Enemies Precisely**

The Qur’an uses this kind of list elsewhere when it wants the community to know who has forfeited its confidence. Sūrat al-Tawbah names them by their deeds: “Will you not fight those who have broken their oaths, conspired to expel the Messenger ˹from Mecca˺, and attacked you first? Do you fear them? Allah is more deserving of your fear, if you are ˹true˺ believers.” (9:13). In both places the marker is the same — expulsion, oath-breaking, aggression — and in both places the community is asked why it fears the human party more than its Lord. What the present verse adds is the anti-clause that protects the innocent: only these are the ones whose friendship is forbidden, which means that a Muslim is not permitted to borrow the ruling and apply it to people who have done none of the things listed. The precision cuts in both directions, and a law that names its enemy this exactly is also a law that defends the uninvolved, which is why the verse can end by saying that it is the other side, not the believer, that has committed the injustice.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:10

> O believers! When the believing women come to you as emigrants, test their intentions—their faith is best known to Allah—and if you find them to be believers, then do not send them back to the disbelievers. These ˹women˺ are not lawful ˹wives˺ for the disbelievers, nor are the disbelievers lawful ˹husbands˺ for them. ˹But˺ repay the disbelievers whatever ˹dowries˺ they had paid. And there is no blame on you if you marry these ˹women˺ as long as you pay them their dowries. And do not hold on to marriage with polytheistic women. ˹But˺ demand ˹repayment of˺ whatever ˹dowries˺ you had paid, and let the disbelievers do the same. That is the judgment of Allah—He judges between you. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**After Ḥudaybiyyah: The Case Nobody Had Planned For**

The treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah in 6/628 contained a clause that the Meccans were eager to have: any man of Quraysh who came over to the Prophet without his guardian’s permission would be sent back, while anyone from the Prophet’s side who went over to Quraysh would not be returned. The arrangement was humiliating in appearance and was accepted because the Book calls the treaty itself a clear victory (48:1). Then the clause produced a case the Meccans had not considered. Women began arriving in Madinah as believers. Their husbands demanded them back under the letter of the agreement, because the agreement spoke of persons, not of men. The verse answers with a ruling rather than a story. It assumes the situation, orders a procedure, and pronounces the women outside the reach of the clause, so that what had been a general term in a treaty became, in practice, a term about male runaways. The community was being taught that the covenant of faith has a weight that social arrangements had to accommodate, not the other way around.

**“Test Them” — the Sūrah’s Name Comes From Here**

*Famtaḥinūhunna* — examine them, put them to the test — comes from a root used for assaying metal to separate the pure from the alloy, and the sūrah takes its name, al-Mumtaḥanah, from this command. The examination was practical, not mystical. A woman might have left Mecca because she believed, or because a husband ill-treated her, or because she followed a man to Madinah; the community was told to establish her intention before acting, since the ruling that follows depended entirely on her being a believer. The same verse immediately sets a limit on human testing, and the limit is the mercy in it: “their faith is best known to Allah.” (60:10) The community judges what it can see, and God holds the secret of the heart. This division of labour protects both sides. The woman is not asked to prove her conscience beyond an honest account of it, and the community is not permitted to keep interrogating what only God can inspect.

**Why She Could Not Be Sent Back**

The prohibition is stated with the symmetry of a legal rule: these women are not lawful wives for the disbelievers, nor are the disbelievers lawful husbands for them. That sentence does the work of the whole passage. Returning the woman would hand her back into a marriage that the revelation no longer recognised, and would make the Muslim community an enforcer of a contract it deemed void. The Qur’an had already established the principle in an earlier sūrah, where marriage to idolaters is forbidden in both directions: “Do not marry polytheistic women until they believe… And do not marry polytheistic men until they believe” (2:221, in meaning). The treaty with Quraysh could not override a religious prohibition, and the community could not lawfully deliver a believing woman into the authority of a man whose religion she had left. What looked like a violation of the agreement was, in the exegetes’ explanation, the removal of a party from the agreement’s scope: the clause had been designed with runaway men in view, and its plain sense could not be stretched to cover this.

**Money Instead of Persons**

The verse then protects the one real stake the estranged husband had left: “˹But˺ repay the disbelievers whatever ˹dowries˺ they had paid.” (60:10) It is a remarkable order, because it asks the Muslim community to pay a man who was, in every other respect, its enemy, and to pay him in full for a marriage he had already lost. The classical explanations add a practical point: the woman herself would rarely have the money, since the dower had long since been spent on the household, so the community as a whole was charged with the repayment, from its treasury rather than from her savings. Two principles are visible in that arrangement. Separation is carried out without confiscation; the release of a person is not financed by robbing the one she leaves. And the Muslim community accepts responsibility for the consequences of its own religious ruling, paying rather than asserting a right to take. The law of this verse is as careful about the enemy’s money as it is about the believer’s conscience.

**Marriage to the Emigrant Women, and Mirror Rules**

Having removed the obstacle, the verse explicitly clears the way for the women to be married in Madinah: there is no blame on the believers if they marry them, provided the dowries are given. The requirement is not a formality. It places the migrant woman in an ordinary legal relation rather than in a state of dependence on charity, and it makes her new husband a debtor in the same way every husband is. Then the rule turns to the mirror image: Muslim men who had wives in Mecca among the idolaters were told not to keep those marriages going, and to demand back the dowries they had paid, just as the disbelievers were demanding back theirs. There is no asymmetry to be found anywhere in the passage. The women who came to Islam are kept; the men who provide for them are bound; the Meccan husbands are paid; the Muslim husbands are repaid; and the Book says plainly that the whole arrangement is the judgment of Allah, who judges between you, and that He is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**The Woman the Meccans Demanded Back**

The reports of the occasion put a name to the case that tested the rule. ʿUqbah ibn Abī Muʿayṭ had a daughter, Umm Kulthūm, who accepted Islam in Mecca and made her way to Madinah. Her brothers came from Mecca to reclaim her, invoking the treaty, and the demand was refused; the exegetes cite the episode when explaining how the revelation settled the question of the women specifically. What the reports do not describe is any uncertainty in the Prophet’s answer or any political hesitation in the community’s response. A treaty that had cost the Muslims their dignity on the day it was signed was not stretched to surrender the first woman to arrive under its shadow. The Book’s ruling had given the community a clear principle to apply, and the principle held under the pressure of the Meccans’ complaint, which is the ordinary test of every legal rule that has ever been adopted for high reasons.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:11

> And if any of your wives desert you to the disbelievers, and later you take spoils from them, then pay those whose wives have gone, the equivalent of whatever ˹dowry˺ they had paid. And be mindful of Allah, in Whom you believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Half of the Arrangement**

The previous verse dealt with women arriving in Madinah and the men they left in Mecca. This verse handles the reverse traffic: Muslim wives who go over to the disbelievers, and the men they leave behind, whose dowers went with them. The problem was real on both sides of the line, and the Book refuses to legislate for only the side it favours. The instruction here is that when a Muslim has lost a wife to the enemy camp, he receives the equivalent of what he had spent on her. That is the plain reading of *fa-ātū lladhīna dhahabat azwājuhum mithla mā anfaqū* — give those whose wives have gone the like of what they spent. The verse names the loss as financial before it is emotional, and it treats the money as a recoverable claim rather than as a casualty of war. Anyone who has watched a conflict narrated by only one side can see the originality in that. The revelation is careful that its own community does not become the party that keeps two sets of books.

**“And Then You Have Your Turn”**

The medium of payment is stated in a phrase that has occupied the commentators: *fa-ʿāqabtum*, from a root that carries the idea of succession, of one thing following another and taking its place. Read in this context, it covers the moment when the balance has shifted back — when the Muslims have their turn in an exchange, or when they have taken spoils in battle, or when they themselves have suffered losses of the same kind and the symmetry of the situation has been restored. The exegetes took it mainly as the arrival of wealth in the community’s hands, whether from war gains or from the public treasury. The phrase is worth noticing for what it assumes. The community is expected to have resources because God has promised to sustain it, and one of the uses of those resources is to settle claims owed to men who are, as far as the sūrah is concerned, the enemy. The Book does not create a category of obligations that exist only when the other side obeys first.

**Why the Community Pays**

The reciprocity set up in the previous verse could not function automatically, because the party that held the Muslim wives was not going to be guided by the Qur’an. The reports say as much: the idolaters refused to repay the dowers of Muslim men whose wives had gone to them, while the Muslims went ahead and repaid the dowers of the women who had come to them. The gap between the two behaviours is precisely what this verse addresses, and it explains why the Muslim treasury carries the cost instead of each man chasing his own former relative through a war. A believer should not lose his property because his adversaries ignore a rule, and he should not decline to keep his own side of an agreement because the other side has failed to keep theirs. Both principles are already active in the sūrah. The result is a system that keeps its accounts clean while the enemy keeps his, which was the only way an ordering principle could survive contact with a conflict.

**A Handful of Cases and a Permanent Rule**

The reports of the earliest community indicate that this situation arose only a few times in the Prophet’s lifetime, and the small number is instructive. The law was not written for a mass movement or a habitual institution; it was written for the handful of people who fell into the gap between two communities, because no one else would write for them. That is a recurring habit of the Book. A rule about the orphan’s property, the convert’s inheritance, the woman whose husband has abandoned her faith — the details appear because the cases existed, and the law arrives to give the individual a place inside the general scheme. The handful of lost wives would have been invisible in a treaty, an army roster, or a treasury ledger. The revelation counts them anyway, and the community is charged with the small, awkward piece of bookkeeping that their situation required, which is one of the plainer signs of a law intended for people rather than for positions.

**Be Mindful of God, in Whom You Believe**

The verse ends without a threat and without a promise of reward: “And be mindful of Allah, in Whom you believe.” (60:11) The clause at the end is unusual. Ordinarily the Book calls people to mindfulness before stating what is required; here it asks for mindfulness after telling them to part with money they had lost, and it grounds the appeal in the belief the listeners already have — you believe in Him, so act as those who do. It is the most persuasive form of argument the Book uses with its own community, because it puts the obligation where the listener’s own commitments already are. A man who has accepted that God is the provider of all that he owns has small reason to object when a command asks him to hand over a portion of it, and a man who believes in the judgement of God has every reason to keep his accounts straight even when no human court will make him. The instruction turns on a single, quiet point: the one whose rights you defend must include the God you say you trust.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:12

> O Prophet! When the believing women come to you, pledging to you that they will neither associate anything with Allah ˹in worship˺, nor steal, nor fornicate, nor kill their children, nor falsely attribute ˹illegitimate˺ children to their husbands, nor disobey you in what is right, then accept their pledge, and ask Allah to forgive them. Surely Allah is AllForgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Pledge Taken Directly From Women**

*Bayʿah* is a covenant of allegiance, the same word used for the oath of the men of Madinah and for the pledge under the tree at Ḥudaybiyyah, and this verse applies it to women arriving in Madinah on their own. ʿĀʾishah described how the Prophet administered it: he would recite this verse and accept the pledge from women by words alone, and his hand never touched a woman’s hand (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7214). The manner of taking the pledge carries as much instruction as the pledge itself, since the community was watching and the convention of a spoken oath, administered publicly and without contact, showed that the covenant with women was a religious act rather than a private arrangement. Nothing in the verse requires a husband, a guardian, or a male sponsor to bring the woman to the Prophet. She comes, she pledges, and she is regarded. That independence is not incidental to the passage; the sūrah has been legislating for women who were separated from their men by the decision of conscience, and it presumes throughout that a woman’s faith is her own affair.

**The Six Terms and the Order They Follow**

The pledge lists six commitments, and their arrangement repays attention. First the doctrine: nothing is associated with Allah. Then property: they will not steal. Then chastity. Then offspring: they will not kill their children. Then family honour: they will not bring a fabricated slander. Then obedience to the Prophet in what is right. The order moves from the root of the religion outward to the sphere of the household, which is where a woman’s obligations were most likely to be contested in a society organised around tribal standing. The fifth item has a specific meaning in Arabic: the lie fabricated between the hands and feet is a child passed off as another man’s, so the phrase is not poetry but a legal remedy against the one falsehood that could destroy a family’s claims and inheritances. Each term names something the new community refused to inherit from the old one, and each was capable of being checked in an ordinary life, which is what made the pledge a real instrument rather than a ceremonial sentence.

**The Practice the Pledge Ended**

Fourth in the list stands the killing of children, and the Book elsewhere names what was being forbidden. The Qur’an describes the pre-Islamic Arab father given news of a daughter: his face darkens… and he hides from the people because of the bad news, asking himself whether to keep her in disgrace or bury her alive; then it pronounces its judgement on that reasoning, “Evil indeed is their judgment!” (16:58–59). Sūrat al-Takwīr makes the buried girl a witness at the resurrection: “and when baby girls, buried alive, are asked for what crime they were put to death” (81:8–9). The pledge therefore abolished by covenant what the community had practised by custom, and it did so by putting the practice among the sins that bar entry to the faith rather than among the errors that a convert might mend at leisure. The verse also shows the moral reach of the pledge. It is not a document about worship alone. It binds the woman to protect the very lives that her society had taught her were expendable, and it makes that protection part of her covenant with God and His Prophet.

**Obedience Limited “in What Is Right”**

The last term reads: nor disobey you in what is right. The Qur’an does not leave the Prophet’s authority unbounded in this pledge; it binds the woman to obey him *in maʿrūf*, in what is recognised as good. The restriction is not a reservation about the Prophet, whose commands are the standard of the good; it is a protection for the woman, and a rule for everyone who holds authority after him. No leader, no husband, no father may extend this pledge to cover a sin. The word *maʿrūf* is deliberately wide and deliberately ordinary: it means what people of sound judgement recognise as right when they hear it described, not whatever an authority happens to declare. So the pledge draws a line around its own obedience, and the line is the difference between submission and surrender. A woman who was told to steal, or to hide a crime, or to deny her faith, could answer that the covenant she gave the Prophet did not require it, and she would be appealing to the wording of the very verse that recorded her oath.

**Accept the Pledge, Seek Forgiveness**

The command to the Prophet closes the instruction: then accept their pledge, and ask Allah to forgive them. There is no list of conditions added, no period of probation imposed, and no insistence that the woman has proved herself beyond doubt. The community’s task was to test that she believed, and once that was established the door stood open. The Book then places the seeking of forgiveness in the same sentence as the acceptance, which means the community takes responsibility for the convert’s future rather than merely admitting her to the register. Forgiveness is asked for sins that may lie ahead, for the weaknesses of a life that is being restarted, and the names at the close suit exactly this: Forgiving, Most Merciful. A community that reads the verse as a licence for suspicion of newcomers has misread both the command and the two names attached to it.

**Their Own Standing Before God**

Because the pledge was taken from women directly, the verse establishes in the plainest way that women are religiously independent persons. The Qur’an elsewhere lists the qualities of believing men and believing women in matching pairs and assigns them a single outcome: “Surely ˹for˺ Muslim men and women, believing men and women, devout men and women, truthful men and women, patient men and women, humble men and women, charitable men and women, fasting men and women, men and women who guard their chastity, and men and women who remember Allah often—for ˹all of˺ them Allah has prepared forgiveness and a great reward.” (33:35). The pledge of this verse and that promise belong together. A woman’s faith is not administered by her husband, her reward is not credited to him, and her covenant is her own. The sūrah that began by forbidding misplaced loyalty ends its legislative core by addressing women as the contracting party in their own right, which is the strongest possible statement of what the community’s covenant was designed to do.

## Sūrah al-Mumtaḥanah 60:13

> O believers! Do not ally yourselves with a people Allah is displeased with. They already have no hope for the Hereafter, just like the disbelievers lying in ˹their˺ graves.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chapter Closes Where It Began**

The sūrah opens with a command not to take God’s enemies as trusted allies, and it closes with a command of the same kind: do not ally yourselves with a people Allah is displeased with. The symmetry is not an accident of editing. Everything between the two commands has been an education in what the line means and where it runs: the disclosure of a secret to the enemy, the removal of hope in clan or children, the model of Abraham, the permission to deal kindly with those who fought nobody, the rulings for women crossing the line, the money that had to be paid. Now the chapter returns to the opening theme with a sharper picture of the party in question. It does not say merely that such a people are hostile; it says that they are the objects of God’s displeasure, which turns the question of alliance into a question about which side of a divine judgement a man chooses to stand on. The command is short, and by this point in the sūrah it needs no further argument.

**Who Are the People Under Displeasure?**

The occasions reported for the verse concern poor Muslims of Madinah who sold information about the community’s movements to the Jews of the city in exchange for provisions. The exegetes differ on the reference of the phrase, and each reading has its grounds. Some identify the people as the Jews of Madinah, whose opposition to the Prophet had brought them under judgement; others see the hypocrites, who concealed their collaboration; others read the words as a general description of everyone who has earned God’s anger by choosing the world over the Hereafter. The readings are not contradictory so much as differently weighted, and the general one has the strongest claim to be the verse’s purpose, since a law that applied only to a vanished group would be a report rather than a command. What the verse fixes is the criterion: not this tribe or that community, but the condition of having despaired of the Hereafter. Anyone in that condition is on the wrong side of the line, whichever city he is standing in.

**Despair of the Hereafter**

The root behind “despaired” is *yaʾs*, the loss of hope so complete that a person stops expecting anything at all — the same word used of a man who has given up on a debt or on a relative he will never see again. To despair of the Hereafter is to have priced it at nothing: the man who sells the community’s safety for provisions has already made the calculation that his next meal is worth more than his standing before God. The verse does not say such people commit an occasional wrong; it says their whole outlook has settled into a verdict that no good is waiting on the other side. The Book treats this as a moral position with consequences, because what a person expects shapes what he will trade away. A man who believes in a reckoning will guard his record even when hungry. A man who has given up on the reckoning has nothing left to guard, and the small betrayal for a small payment becomes entirely reasonable to him.

**“Just Like the Disbelievers Despair of Those in the Graves”**

The comparison that closes the verse supplies the image that is missing from the abstraction. The disbelievers of Mecca had despaired of the inhabitants of the graves — the dead were gone, they thought, and no one in a cemetery would ever be raised, questioned, or rewarded. From that position, everything done in this life was final. The verse says the people it warns against have despaired of the Hereafter with exactly that kind of certainty. Their despair is not confusion or doubt; it is the settled conclusion of a man who has looked at the end of life and seen nothing. The exegetes offer two refinements of the image: that the graves stand for the disbelievers’ deceased relatives, of whom they expect no return, and that a person who dies in denial has no good to hope for. Both readings sharpen the same point. The ones being courted as allies have no future to share, and a man who attaches his own future to them has made an alliance with people who have already declared the future empty.

**Why the Alliance Is Not Merely Forbidden but Absurd**

The verse’s instruction rests on something more than a rule. It rests on the fact that the power to reward and to judge lies wholly with the One these people have written off. An ally who cannot deliver is a poor ally; a party whose expectations end at the grave cannot deliver anything beyond the grave, and that is the only quarter where a believer’s account is settled. The poor Muslims of Madinah who took provisions in exchange for information gained a meal, and the verse names the transaction in the plainest possible way by putting the hunger and the betrayal side by side without softening either one. What it offers instead is the perspective that the rest of the sūrah has been building: a community with a future has no need to purchase the present, and a man who expects to be judged has no reason to fear the judgement of men. That is the note on which the chapter closes, and it is the same note on which it opened, only now with the whole sūrah standing behind it.
