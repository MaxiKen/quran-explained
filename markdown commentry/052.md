# Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr (Chapter 52) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sacred Mountain and the Inevitable Judgment**

Aṭ-Ṭūr, "The Mount," is the fifty-second chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 49 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with five solemn cosmic and historical oaths: *“By the Mount [Sinai], and by a Book inscribed, in a parchment unrolled, and by the Frequented House [in the heavens], and by the canopy raised [the sky], and by the sea set on fire—indeed, the punishment of your Lord will occur; of it there is no preventer!”* (52:1–8). In prophetic tradition, Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr holds a legendary place as the catalyst that shattered the skeptical resistance of Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim before his conversion to Islam: Jubayr reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (4854) that he arrived in Madinah as an envoy of the pagan Quraysh while the Prophet ﷺ was leading the sunset prayer (*Maghrib*), reciting Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr: *“When I heard him recite: 'Were they created by nothing, or were they themselves the creators? Or did they create the heavens and the earth? Nay, they have no certainty!' (52:35–36), my heart almost flew from my chest, and that was the first time faith settled in my heart.”* With rhythmic, chiseled verses that resound like thunder, Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr is an uncompromising intellectual and spiritual trial of human skepticism.

**The Assembly of the Sacred Landmarks**

The historical context of aṭ-Ṭūr is the fierce, defensive cynicism of the Makkan aristocracy, who accused the Prophet ﷺ of being a soothsayer (*kāhin*), an insane poet (*shāʿir majnūn*), or an imposter forging speech for political ambition. Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr grounds its discourse in five sacred landmarks that link terrestrial history with cosmic realities: Mount Sinai (*aṭ-Ṭūr*), where Moses received the Torah; the inscribed celestial parchment; the *Bayt al-Maʿmūr* (the Frequented House in the celestial heavens directly above the Kaʿbah, circumambulated perpetually by seventy thousand angels who never return); the celestial sky; and the ocean set ablaze (*al-baḥr al-masjūr*). It confronts the Makkans with the absolute irreversibility of the coming Judgment: when the sky rolls with terrifying convulsion and the mountains move like drifting clouds, no worldly defense, wealth, or tribal alliance can avert the decree.

**The Structural Movement and the Litany of Interrogations**

The architecture of Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr moves across three distinct, powerful movements. The opening movement (1–28) announces the certainty of retribution, describes the deniers pushed forcefully into the Fire which they had branded an illusion, and contrasts their agony against the serene bliss of the righteous in the Gardens of Delight: reclining on couches lined in ranks, wed to companions of pure beauty, their righteous offspring joined with them in Paradise without their deeds being diminished in the least (52:21). It records their touching conversation in Paradise, reflecting on their earthly lives: *“Indeed, we were previously, among our families, fearful [of God's displeasure]. But Allah conferred favor upon us and protected us from the punishment of the Scorching Fire. Indeed, we used to call upon Him; indeed, He is the Beneficent, the Merciful”* (52:26–28). The second movement (29–47) forms the intellectual climax of the sūrah—a relentless barrage of fifteen rhetorical questions that leave the skeptic intellectually stripped:
- Reminding the Prophet that he is, by the favor of his Lord, neither a soothsayer nor a madman.
- Answering their wish that he die of misfortune: *“Say, 'Wait, for indeed I am with you among those who wait'”* (52:31).
- Challenging them: *“Or do their minds command them to this, or are they a transgressing people? Or do they say, 'He has made it up'? Nay, they do not believe! Then let them produce a statement like it, if they should be truthful!”* (52:32–34).
- Delivering the four devastating existential dilemmas of verses 35–37: Were they created from nothing? Are they themselves the creators? Did they create the heavens and the earth? Do they possess the treasuries of your Lord, or are they the controllers?
- Asking if they have a stairway into heaven to eavesdrop on divine secrets, or if God has daughters while they have sons, or if the Prophet asks them for a fee that weighs them down with debt.
The closing movement (48–49) turns to the Prophet with sublime, intimate tenderness: *“So be patient for the decision of your Lord, for indeed, you are in Our eyes (*fa-innaka bi-aʿyuninā*). And exalt with praise of your Lord when you arise, and of the night exalt Him, and at the setting of the stars.”*

**In the Eyes of God: Bi-Aʿyuninā**

The foundational spiritual consolation of Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr is contained in the tender divine assurance of verse 48: *Fa-innaka bi-aʿyuninā* ("For indeed, you are in Our eyes / under Our watchful, loving gaze"). The Prophet ﷺ was mocked, slandered, and surrounded by hostile conspiracies, yet he was told that he was dwelling permanently within the protective gaze of the Almighty. Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr teaches that no matter how loud the mockery of skepticism becomes, the soul that anchors itself in the midnight remembrance of God and the dawn setting of the stars is sheltered in the fortress of divine love.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:1
> By Mount Ṭûr

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Oath Begins With a Place, Not a Principle**

The sūrah opens on a mountain. Not on a name of God, not on an attribute, not on a general truth about justice or the last day, but on a specific piece of geography: *al-ṭūr*, the Mount. In ordinary Arabic *ṭūr* simply means a mountain, any mountain; the definite form used here, and in the other places the Qur'an uses it, points to one mountain in particular, the one where God addressed Mūsā. Two verses give that mountain its fuller name and remove the ambiguity: the tree of the blessed valley is described as growing on “Mount Sinai” (23:20), and the opening oath of another short sūrah pairs “the fig and the olive ˹of Jerusalem˺” with “Mount Sinai” (95:1–2). So the word is not a general appeal to high places. It is a named witness.

Naming a place rather than stating a claim does something a claim cannot do. A claim can be disputed; a place stands there. The mountain is where a human being was spoken to directly and given a law, and the oath therefore puts at the head of this sūrah the fact that revelation is an event that happened somewhere, at a time, to a man who could be named. Everything the sūrah goes on to say about judgment rests on that prior fact.

**Why Sinai and Not Another Mountain**

Sinai is the mountain of the covenant, and the Qur'an returns to it at every point where the idea of a binding law needs a witness. God called Mūsā “from the right side of Mount Ṭûr, and drew him near, speaking ˹with him˺ directly” (19:52). He is told to go and see the fire at the edge of the valley and come back with guidance (20:80; 28:29–30). Later the sūrah of the Stories tells the Prophet ﷺ that he was not at the side of the Mount when the call went out to Mūsā (28:46) — a reminder that this knowledge reaches him from elsewhere, not from presence.

This is why Sinai carries more weight in an oath than any other mountain. It is not the tallest or the most dramatic. It is the one where speech came down and obligation went up. When the Qur'an swears by it at the head of a warning about the last day, it is saying: the same God who spoke there will hold the account here. The mountain is the fixed point that ties promise to fulfillment.

**The Same Mountain Lifted Over a Community**

Sinai appears in this Book a second way, and the second appearance sharpens the oath. When the covenant was taken from the Children of Israel, the mountain was not merely behind them: “And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, ‘Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and observe its teachings so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺’” (2:63). The scene is repeated with the answer attached: they said they heard, and disobeyed (2:93).

The mountain thus does double duty in the memory of the community. It is the place of the address and the instrument of the pledge, hanging over the heads of those who gave it. An oath by that mountain invokes both meanings at once: revelation came, and revelation was answered with a commitment that some of those who made it did not keep. A sūrah about the certainty of punishment opens with a reminder that certainty was offered before and met with evasion.

**God Swears by Creation; People May Swear Only by God**

The oath-form raises a question that the tradition answered plainly. A human being is not permitted to swear by a created thing at all. The Prophet ﷺ said, according to ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar, that whoever must take an oath should swear by Allah or stay silent (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). Another report puts the matter even more strongly: swearing by other than Allah is a form of association.

The asymmetry is the point. God swearing by the Mount does not elevate the Mount to the status of a deity; it marks how much weight the thing carries in the order He made. When a creature swears by a creature, he gives something finite the guarantee-function that belongs only to the One who is answerable for nothing. The rule protects the oath from inflation. The divine oath here is the opposite movement: the Creator points at His own work and lets it testify to the seriousness of what follows.

**A Sūrah Named After Its First Word**

The chapter takes its name from this single word, and that name is how the early community knew it. It is a Meccan sūrah, most likely from the later Meccan period, and it is reported to have been revealed soon after Sūrah 32. Its subject follows directly on the closing promise of the sūrah before it, that the Day they are told of will come (51:60).

The name also entered the record of how the Prophet ﷺ prayed. Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim reported that he heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ reciting Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr in the Maghrib prayer (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 765). In the longer form of the report he describes the effect on himself: when the recitation reached the challenge about creation, “it was as if my heart was about to fly” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4854). A sūrah that opens by swearing on a mountain was recited aloud, in a public prayer, to listeners who had not yet believed.

**The Mountain Cannot Be Argued With**

An oath in Arabic does not argue; it stakes. The speaker puts something he values behind his word so that the listener can measure his seriousness. The five things this sūrah swears by are not ideas but objects: a mountain, a book, a house, a sky, a sea. Each is stable, visible, and beyond the control of the person being addressed.

That choice of objects is deliberate in a chapter that ends with the heavens shaking and the mountains blown away. What is sworn by in the first verses is exactly what is undone in the ninth and tenth. The witness is first established as solid, and then dismantled, so that the hearer understands the scale of what is coming. A world firm enough to swear on is not firm enough to last. The next oaths extend the same method from the ground upward.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:2
> And by the Book written

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Witness, This One Inscribed**

The oath moves from a place to a document. *Kitāb mastūr* is a book that has been written, the passive participle fixing the sense: something was written into it, and the writing remains. The word *mastūr* is not neutral. It does not mean a book that could be written or a book that might be filled; it means a book whose writing already exists, finished and fixed before the listener hears about it.

The choice follows the mountain naturally. Sinai is where the speech was given; a book is what such speech becomes when it is preserved. The pairing is the oldest form of religious evidence: an event and a record of the event. Both are being invoked here as guarantees of what the sūrah will assert a few verses later, that the punishment of your Lord is real and unavoidable.

**Four Candidates, and What Each One Means**

The commentators identify this inscribed book in four ways, and the disagreement is not idle, because each identification changes what the oath guarantees.

The first is the Torah, which suits the pairing with Sinai and reads the two oaths as a single reference: the mountain and the book revealed on it. The second is the Qur'an itself, the book in the hands of the people being addressed, which makes the oath an appeal to the very thing they are denying. The third is the Preserved Tablet, *al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ*, the source of all revelation, described as the thing in which the noble Qur'an is “˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet” (85:22) and “in a well-preserved Record” (56:78). The fourth is the register of human deeds, which is laid open on the last day: “And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it” (18:49).

The strongest reading takes the phrase at its widest, because the sūrah's argument needs all of them. A book that is written is at once the archive of what God said and the ledger of what people did. Both are already complete.

**Written, Not Merely Spoken**

The emphasis on inscription deserves attention because speech alone can be forgotten, distorted, or denied. Writing is the form that resists all three. The Qur'an makes this its own argument when it describes itself as clear revelations “˹preserved˺ in the hearts of those gifted with knowledge” (29:49) — preserved in chests, that is, in living memory, which is the other kind of inscription this Book recognises.

That duality matters for the oath. A book that is written has two homes: the tablet and the person. The Torah was written on tablets and carried by a community; the Qur'an was written and memorised at once, which is why its transmission never depended on a single copy. Swearing by a written book is swearing by something that cannot be talked out of existence, and it puts the denier in a peculiar position: the evidence he is rejecting is in his hands.

**The Register That No One Else Reads For You**

If the inscribed book includes the record of deeds, then the oath carries a sharper edge than a general appeal to scripture. Every person arrives with his own book, and the reading is not delegated: “Read your record. You ˹alone˺ are sufficient this Day to take account of yourself” (17:14). The wicked read it and complain that nothing was left out, small or large (18:49).

An oath by that book is an oath by the accuracy of the account. Nothing will be misreported, nothing will be added, and nothing will be dropped. The fear of the wicked in that verse is not fear of being accused wrongly; it is the fear of being described exactly. This is why the sūrah can move so quickly from oath to verdict. The evidence is not being assembled at that moment. It has been assembled all along.

**A Book Written Before the Case Is Heard**

The ordering of the two witnesses is instructive. The mountain comes first, then the book. Revelation happened, and then it was fixed in writing. That order is also the order of divine knowledge: the decree precedes the act, and the record is kept as the act unfolds.

The Qur'an insists on this repeatedly. Nothing happens on earth or in yourselves that is not already in a record before it is brought into being (57:22). The purpose stated there is not to frighten but to steady: so that no one grieves over what escaped him or exults over what he was given. Applied to this oath, the same idea gives the warning its shape. The punishment that will come to pass is not an improvisation. It is written, and the writing is older than the crime it answers.

**The Same Root Means Both Writing and Obligation**

Arabic packs a second meaning into this word, and the oath gains force from it. The root *k-t-b* produces *kitāb*, a book, and it also produces the passive *kutiba*, used throughout the Qur'an for a duty that has been laid on someone. “O believers! Fasting is prescribed for you—as it was for those before you—so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺” (2:183). The law of retaliation is described with the same passive (2:178). To write, in this language, is also to bind.

That double sense is not a curiosity. It explains why a book can be sworn by in the same breath as a mountain where a covenant was taken. A written thing in the Qur'anic world is never merely informational; it obligates. The inscribed book in this verse is therefore evidence and demand at once. It records what was said on the mountain and it states what is owed in return.

The listener who hears a book being sworn by is being told that a debt exists and that it is documented. The verse does not say whose debt, but the whole of the sūrah that follows answers that question, first for those who deny and then for those who kept to it.

**Why the Oath Refuses to Explain Itself**

One further feature of this verse is what it does not do. It does not say which book. The indefiniteness is left standing, and the commentators have been arguing about it for fourteen centuries. That silence is not a defect in the text; it is a rhetorical method.

An oath that names its object precisely invites the listener to debate the object. An oath that leaves the referent open invites him to supply every book he knows, and each one testifies against him in a slightly different way. The Meccan listener who heard this in prayer would think of the Torah his neighbours revered, of the Qur'an he was refusing, and, if he thought far enough, of his own account. The next verse describes the physical form of what is written here, and that description narrows the picture without closing it.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:3
> on open pages ˹for all to read˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Material of the Writing**

This verse supplies the physical description the previous oath left out. *Raqq* is thin parchment or vellum, the prepared skin on which the ancients wrote; *manshūr* is spread out, unrolled, displayed. The image is concrete: not a sealed scroll, not a hidden archive, but a sheet pulled open in front of the person who is asked to look at it.

The specificity is unusual for an oath. Oaths in this Book tend to be large — the sky, the dawn, the pen. Here the material is named, and the state of the material is named. The point of that naming is openness. A book written on parchment that is rolled up and stored can be doubted; a book unrolled in daylight cannot. The witness being offered to the deniers is one they can inspect.

**Unrolled: A Word the Qur'an Uses Against Its Critics**

The participle *manshūr* returns in a passage that reads like a commentary on this one. The Meccan leaders demanded a private revelation of their own, and the reply was blunt: “In fact, each one of them wishes to be given a ˹personal˺ letter ˹from Allah˺ for all ˹to read˺” (74:52). The same word for unrolled pages appears there, attached to a demand the Qur'an refuses.

The contrast is exact and it is instructive. When the demand for unrolled pages comes from people who have rejected the pages already given, the demand is refused. When the same word describes what God offers, the offer is genuine and public. Revelation is not scarce and hidden, doled out to whoever presses hardest; it is spread open, and the objection is never that it cannot be seen.

**Open to Whom, and What Follows From That**

The parenthetical in the translation — for all to read — captures the consequence of *manshūr*. An unrolled text has an audience wider than its keeper. Two things follow, and the sūrah depends on both.

First, ignorance is no longer available as a defence. A book that is closed can be excused by those who never saw it; a book unrolled in the market cannot. Second, public exposure makes the text liable to attack, and the Qur'an accepts that liability rather than avoiding it. Its challenge to produce a chapter like it (2:23) presumes a text open enough to be imitated or refuted.

The same idea governs the record of deeds. On the day described later in this sūrah, no one will be handed a summary by another person. Each will be told to read his own book (17:14). The openness sworn by here is the openness that will be enforced there.

**Parchment and Preservation**

There is a further resonance between the material and the message. Parchment is durable but not eternal; it can burn, and it can be scraped clean and reused. The Qur'an's own claim about its text is not that the parchment lasts but that the writing does: the noble Qur'an sits “in a well-preserved Record, touched by none except the purified ˹angels˺” (56:77–79).

That distinction answers a question the material raises. If revelation exists on skin, what keeps it intact? The answer given is not a stronger medium but a different one — a record kept where nothing corrupts it, mirrored in the memorised text carried by people. The physical copy is the open page that can be seen; the preservation is elsewhere. Swearing by the open page swears by the availability of the revelation, not by the fragility of its carrier.

**Why the Oath Runs in Pairs**

Structurally, this line completes the second oath rather than starting a new one, and the pattern it establishes runs through the opening of the sūrah: an object, then a qualification of that object. The mountain is named; the book is named and then described; the house will be named and described as frequented; the sky as raised; the sea as set on fire.

That rhythm does something argumentative. Each object alone could be dismissed as a poetic flourish. Each object with its qualification becomes evidence. The mountain is not scenery, it is the place of the address. The book is not a symbol, it is writing on a spread-open sheet. The qualifications force the listener to consider the object's actual condition, and in each case the condition is one of witness rather than ornament. The oaths accumulate, and the answer they are building toward is stated a few verses later in a single sentence about the punishment of your Lord.

**A Page Old Enough to Be Dated**

The word *raqq* names a real material, and one such page from the earliest period of Islam still exists and has been tested. Two parchment leaves in the Cadbury Research Library at the University of Birmingham, containing parts of Sūrahs 18 to 20 in the early Ḥijāzī script, were radiocarbon dated at Oxford, and the analysis placed the skin of the animal with 95.4% probability between 568 and 645 CE — within the lifetime of the Prophet ﷺ or within two decades of his death.

That finding matters for this verse because it removes the discussion from the realm of symbol. When the Qur'an swears by writing on spread-open parchment, it is swearing by the ordinary writing technology of its own world: skin, prepared, inked, and unrolled for reading. Such pages survive. They can be handled, dated, and compared against the text recited today, and in the Birmingham case the wording on the leaves matches the text in current use.

The oath therefore rests on something that has held up under laboratory scrutiny fourteen centuries later. A promise sworn by a mountain and a written book is a promise with physical evidence attached.

**The Reader Standing Over the Sheet**

The image has one more function, which is to place the hearer in a position. An unrolled page implies a body leaning over it, reading. Nothing in the verse is addressed to a passive audience; the object sworn by is one that requires a reader.

That is consistent with the whole of Meccan revelation, which constantly addresses people who can see and hear and then asks why they have used neither. The complaint is not that the signs were concealed but that they were passed over. A page spread open is the least coercive form of proof there is. It can be read or ignored, and the choice belongs to the one standing above it.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:4
> And by the ˹Sacred˺ House frequently visited

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Third Witness: The House That Is Never Empty**

The oath now turns from writing to a building. *Al-bayt al-maʿmūr*, the House frequently visited, takes its name from a verbal form meaning to be inhabited, populated, or crowded with visitors. *ʿImārah* in Arabic is the opposite of ruin; a house that is *maʿmūr* is one that people keep coming to, so that it is never left desolate.

The oath therefore swears by traffic. Not by the stone, though the stone is there, but by the fact that the place is in constant use. In a chapter that is about certainty, this is a particular kind of evidence: a place is credible as a witness when it can be shown to have never been abandoned, and this one is described as being visited without interruption.

**The Night Journey Fixes Its Location**

The Prophet ﷺ identified this house during the ascension. In the report narrated by Mālik ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿah, when the Frequented House was shown to him he asked Jibrīl about it and was told: this is al-Bayt al-Maʿmūr, into which seventy thousand angels enter every day, and when they leave they never return to it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3887). In the same account Ibrāhīm is seen reclining against it.

Two details in that report do the real work. The number is large enough to be beyond counting by anyone on earth, and the clause “they never return” makes the traffic irreversible: each group of angels comes once and is replaced by another. The house is not visited by the same crowd returning out of habit. It is a destination to which new worshippers arrive continually, so its population is constantly renewed. Classical scholars located this house in the heavens above, corresponding to the Kaʿbah below, and reported disagreement over which heaven it occupies.

**The Earthly Counterpart**

The pairing of a heavenly house with an earthly one is what gives the oath its force for a Meccan audience. The Kaʿbah was in front of them, and the Qur'an had already described it in terms of sanctity and public use: “Allah has made the Ka’bah—the Sacred House—a sanctuary of wellbeing for all people” (5:97). Earlier it is called a centre and a sanctuary for the people, with Ibrāhīm and Ismāʿīl entrusted to purify it for those who circle it, stand in it, bow and prostrate (2:125).

Both houses are defined by the same feature: they exist to be visited. A sanctuary that no one comes to is a ruin with a name. The oath by the Frequented House is an oath by the continuity of worship, and the continuity is measurable in feet — people walking to it, angels entering it, day after day.

**Three Levels the Commentators Read Into It**

Beyond the heavenly house and the Kaʿbah, exegetes have taken the phrase in a third direction: the heart of the believer, inhabited by knowledge and by the remembrance of God. This reading is not a rival to the other two so much as their application. A house that is frequented is a house kept in use; a heart kept in use by prayer is *maʿmūr* in the same sense.

The reason this third reading survives in the tradition is that the word carries its own moral weight in Arabic. To populate a place is to tend it, and the Qur'an uses the same root for the tending of mosques and for the ruin that follows abandonment (9:17–18). A building is only a house while someone maintains it. The oath thus includes, quietly, the case of the person who lets his own interior go to ruin while the heavenly house fills up every day.

**What the House Testifies To**

The three witnesses so far cover revelation in three states: given on a mountain, fixed in writing, and embodied in a place of worship that is never empty. Each answers a different objection. Revelation could be doubted as a claim; the mountain answers it. It could be doubted as a memory; the book answers it. It could be doubted as a private experience of a few; the crowded house answers it.

That third answer matters most for a message that began with a single man in a cave. A religion that is only one person's experience can be dismissed as one person's experience. A religion that fills a house every day, on earth and above it, cannot be dismissed that way. The oath by the Frequented House is the social proof of revelation, sworn on the thing itself.

**Seventy Thousand a Day Is an Argument About Scale**

The number in the ascension report is not incidental. Seventy thousand angels enter the house every day and none of them comes back. Over a year that is more than twenty-five million visits; over a century the figure outruns any attempt to total it. The report is not giving a statistic so much as a direction: the traffic does not thin out.

Scale of that kind does something to the objection that worship is a human invention. A practice invented by people would be expected to fluctuate with them — crowded at its founding, empty when the founders die. The house described here never empties, and its population is not human at all.

The same root is used in the Qur'an for the tending of mosques, where the point is ethical rather than statistical: “The mosques of Allah should only be maintained by those who believe in Allah and the Last Day, establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and fear none but Allah” (9:18), and the verse before it excludes from that work those who openly disbelieve (9:17). Maintaining a house is a claim about the person doing it. The Frequented House is maintained continually, and the oath by it is an oath by the fact that the claim keeps being made.

**Continuity Against the Shaking**

One last feature of this witness prepares for the sūrah's second half. Later verses describe the heavens shaking and the mountains being blown away, and the world that is sworn by here will not survive that day intact. The house is not exempt.

What survives is the visiting. The angels who enter never return; the people who circle the Kaʿbah die and are replaced. The traffic continues while the individual participants change, and that is the picture of a worship that does not depend on any one generation. Swearing by the frequented house swears by a pattern rather than a moment, and a pattern is what a warning about the future needs. The verses that follow will say what happens when the pattern is broken.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:5
> And by the canopy raised ˹high˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ceiling Over Everything Else**

The fourth oath goes up. *Al-saqf al-marfūʿ*, the canopy raised, is the sky, and the word for canopy is the same word used for the roof of a house. The Qur'an is describing the heavens the way a person describes a ceiling: as something put in place above the room, with a function.

That is how the Book describes it elsewhere. “And We have made the sky a well-protected canopy, still they turn away from its signs” (21:32). The verse that follows in that passage is a rebuke, and the rebuke is the point: a roof is not decoration, it is provision, and the people who live under it walk past it without noticing. Swearing by the raised canopy is swearing by the largest piece of evidence in daily view.

**Raised, and Held Up Without Visible Support**

The participle *marfūʿ* — raised, elevated — carries the whole weight of the description, and the Qur'an elsewhere explains how strange that raising is. “It is Allah Who has raised the heavens without pillars—as you can see— then established Himself on the Throne” (13:2). The clause “as you can see” is important: the absence of supports is not a metaphysical claim but an observation anyone can make by looking up.

Other passages make the same point with different images: seven mighty heavens built above (78:12), and the rhetorical question in the sūrah of the Overwhelming that asks about “the sky—how it was raised ˹high˺” (88:18). The repeated wonder is not at the sky's colour or distance but at its elevation without collapse. A canopy that stays up with nothing holding it is the clearest daily demonstration that the arrangement is not self-supporting.

**The Canopy as Protection, Not Just Cover**

A roof does more than shade; it separates what is inside from what is outside. The Qur'an's canopy language is protective, and 21:32 attaches the idea explicitly with *maḥfūẓ*, well-protected. The sky is a barrier as well as a ceiling.

This gives the oath a second edge. People living under a protected canopy tend to think of protection as the natural condition of things, the way a person in a house forgets the weather outside. The sūrah's later verses will describe that same sky shaking violently and splitting into gates (78:19). What is sworn by as stable in the fifth verse is the thing that fails in the ninth. The oath is not a reassurance about the sky; it is a statement of how much would have to give way for the promised day to arrive, and the answer is: this.

**From Ground to Roof: The Sequence of the Oaths**

The order of the five witnesses is worth noting because it is spatial. A mountain on the ground, a written book in human hands, a house that people enter, a canopy overhead, and finally the sea. The sequence climbs from earth to sky and then returns to the water, enclosing the whole inhabited world.

That enclosure is the argument. There is nowhere in the world that is not covered by these oaths, and therefore nowhere for the person being addressed to stand outside their testimony. A listener in Mecca could look at every one of the five: the mountain on the horizon, the scripture being recited, the Kaʿbah in the city, the sky above him, and the sea a few days' journey to the west. The witnesses are local as well as cosmic, and that is what makes the oath difficult to wave away as poetry.

**Seven Layers, Not One Sheet**

The canopy in this verse is a single roof, and the Qur'an elsewhere describes the same roof as a stack: seven heavens, one above the other, with the moon placed within them as a reflected light and the sun as a radiant lamp (71:15–16). The creation account in al-Baqarah uses the same number and the same direction of movement — He turned towards the heaven and formed it into seven heavens (2:29).

The layering changes how the image works. A single sheet is a cover; seven stacked layers are a structure with depth, and depth implies design rather than mere separation. Each layer has an occupant and a function, which is why the ascension account describes the Prophet ﷺ passing through them one at a time and meeting someone at each.

The oath by the raised canopy is therefore an oath by a built thing. The Qur'an's sky is not empty space but architecture, and architecture has an architect. That inference is the shortest route from the verse to the claim the sūrah makes four verses later, and it is why the sky is sworn by rather than merely mentioned.

**Guarded As Well As Raised**

The canopy is not only held up; it is defended. The Qur'an describes the lowest heaven as adorned with lamps and turned into a means of driving away eavesdropping devils (67:5), and the sūrah of al-Ḥijr states the same protection from another angle: constellations placed in the sky, the sky adorned for all to see, and protection from every accursed devil, except one who tries to steal a hearing and is chased by a visible flare (15:16–18).

Two ideas sit inside that description. The first is that the sky is a bounded space rather than an open field; something is kept out of it. The second is that the boundary has consequences for anyone who tries to cross it. The protection is not passive.

Both ideas bear on the oath. A raised canopy that is also guarded is a structure under authority, and authority is what the sūrah is asserting when it swears by it. The hearer is being pointed at an arrangement that has rules, borders, and enforcement — the same arrangement that will later be described as producing a judgment nobody can push aside.

**A Sign That Cannot Be Denied and Is Denied Anyway**

The sky is the sign the Qur'an most often says people ignore. The verse in al-Anbiyāʾ states the paradox directly: a well-protected canopy, and still they turn away from its signs (21:32). The failure is not one of evidence but of attention.

The sūrah's own argument depends on that diagnosis. A people who do not look up will not be moved by a warning either, because a warning is a claim about the future and they have already shown that they do not attend to the present. The oath by the raised canopy is therefore a demand for attention before the warning arrives: look at what is holding up your roof, and then decide whether the One who holds it can be mistaken about what comes next. The last of the five oaths turns to the water that the same arrangement keeps in place.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:6
> And by the seas set on fire

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Witness Is Water That Burns**

The five oaths end on the least stable of them. A mountain, a book, a house, a sky — each is fixed in its place. Then the sea, and not the sea as sailors know it, but the sea *masjūr*, a word that means kindled or filled to overflowing. The Qur'an's own use of the same idea is blunt: “and when the seas are set on fire” (81:6).

For a Meccan audience the sea was the edge of the world they could not cross or control. Trade routes ran to it, ships disappeared into it, and nobody built anything permanent on it. Swearing by the sea in its burning state is swearing by the thing that least resembles a witness, and that is precisely why it closes the series: after the solid objects have testified, the last word belongs to the element that swallows them.

**One Word Carrying Two Meanings at Once**

The root *s-j-r* in Arabic holds both senses that commentators find here. To *sajara* a thing is to fill it, and it is also to kindle a fire in it. Both readings have been given for this verse: the sea filled and heaving at its banks, or the sea ablaze.

The Qur'an supports each reading in a different place. “and when the seas are set on fire” (81:6) supports the burning. “and when the seas burst forth” (82:3) supports the overflowing. Nothing forces a choice, and the tradition does not force one either: the verse can be read as describing a sea that bursts its limits and a sea that catches fire, since the day described in this sūrah produces both.

The doubled sense suits the argument. A sea that is merely full is still a sea; a sea on fire is something the world has no category for. The oath points at a change of kind, not degree.

**The Report of Fire Beneath the Water**

A report in Sunan Abī Dāwūd, from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr, says that no one should sail except a pilgrim performing ḥajj or ʿumrah or a fighter in God's cause, because beneath the sea there is a fire and beneath the fire a sea. Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr graded this report weak, and it should be cited with that qualification rather than as a settled fact.

A later explanation is more careful and more useful. Ibn Rajab explained the imagery by connecting it to the day of resurrection: the seas will burst into one another and become a single sea, and then they will be kindled and become fire belonging to the Fire. On that reading the verse is not describing a hidden geography under the present ocean but a transformation of the ocean at the end. The weak report supplies a picture; the careful explanation supplies the theology, and the theology is the one the sūrah's own verses support.

**Opposites Sworn Together**

Fire and water are the classic pair of incompatibles in Arabic thought, and the Qur'an uses their separation elsewhere as a sign: the two seas that meet with a barrier between them that they do not transgress (55:19–20). What is kept apart in the present order is joined in this oath.

That joining is the sūrah's method in miniature. Everything the world relies on for stability — the difference between land and water, fire and flood, inside and outside — is a feature of the arrangement, not a necessity of things. An oath that puts fire into the sea says that the arrangement can be revoked. Nothing in the world's grammar is safe from the One who wrote it.

The listener is meant to feel the vertigo. The oaths began on a mountain, the most permanent thing he could point to, and end on water burning. Between those two points sits everything he owns.

**A Sign Taken From Daily Life and Reversed**

Arabic uses the same root in everyday speech. An oven is *masjūr* when it is fired up, and the Qur'an describes Noah's oven bursting with water as the sign to board the Ark (11:40). In that story the domestic object behaves impossibly — an oven that floods — and the impossible behaviour is the signal that the world is being rearranged.

This verse uses the same device at a larger scale. The sea behaving like a furnace is the sign that the same rearrangement has begun. Ordinary objects doing impossible things is the Qur'an's standard marker for the day, and it explains why the oaths are drawn from ordinary objects rather than from abstractions. A warning built on familiar things lands harder than one built on strange ones, because the hearer recognises each object and then recognises the distortion.

**The Sea That Feeds, Before the Sea That Burns**

The Qur'an's ordinary description of the sea is generous, and knowing it makes this verse sharper. The sea is subjected to human use so that people may eat tender seafood from it and extract ornaments to wear, with ships ploughing their way through it so that they may seek God's bounty and give thanks (16:14). A parallel verse compares two kinds of water, one fresh and pleasant to drink and the other salty and bitter, and notes that from both come food and jewellery and shipping (35:12).

Those verses describe a resource. This one describes the same resource ablaze. The contrast is not incidental: the oath chooses the sea at the moment when it stops being useful and becomes an instrument of the end.

That reversal has a moral shape to it. The sea that fed people and carried their trade is the sea that will burn, and the people who took its bounty without gratitude are the ones addressed by the change. The Qur'an attaches thanksgiving to the sea twice in a single verse, which suggests the point was needed.

**The Oath Series Ends and the Answer Begins**

Five witnesses have now been produced: a mountain of revelation, a book of writing, a house of worship, a canopy overhead, and a sea on fire. The first four are things people take for granted; the fifth is what happens when the taking-for-granted ends.

That ordering is the argument of the whole opening. The world is not being described poetically; it is being inventoried, and the inventory is meant to establish that nothing in it is exempt. The response to these oaths arrives in the next verse as a single statement about the punishment of your Lord, and it lands with the accumulated weight of all five witnesses behind it.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:7
> Indeed, the punishment of your Lord will come to pass—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer the Oaths Were Building Toward**

Arabic oaths come in two parts: the thing sworn by, and the statement that the oath guarantees. Five verses of swearing have gone by, and this verse is the payoff. The structure is what gives the sentence its force: nothing is asserted until the witnesses are on the table, and once they are, the assertion arrives without argument.

The statement itself is short. The punishment of your Lord *wāqiʿ*. That single word carries the weight of everything before it. There is no conditional, no threat, no negotiation. The five oaths have already done the arguing; what remains is the announcement.

**Wāqiʿ Means Falling, Not Merely Threatened**

The word chosen is an active participle from *waqaʿa*, to fall or to occur. A participle describes a state already in progress, not an intention. The punishment is not said to be coming in the way a rumour is coming; it is described as something whose occurrence is under way.

The same word opens a later Meccan sūrah: “When the Inevitable Event takes place, then no one can deny it has come” (56:1–2). The description there removes the last room for doubt — the event is named as inevitable before it happens, and the denial of its coming is itself ruled out in the second line. The parallel verse in Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt states the same thing in a single line: “And the Judgment will certainly come to pass” (51:6).

Three chapters, three formulations, one claim. The certainty being asserted is not a mood but a description of an event already scheduled.

**Why It Is Called the Punishment of Your Lord**

The possessive is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ: your Lord. Two things are happening at once in that phrase.

The first is reassurance. The man being told that punishment will fall is the one being accused of inventing the warning. Calling God *your* Lord identifies whose case this is. The coming punishment is not the Prophet's grievance against his opponents; it belongs to the One who sent him, and its arrival will settle the dispute over his truthfulness.

The second is warning, and it is aimed at everyone listening. The same Lord who punishes is the Lord who provides, who raised the canopy and filled the sea. The possessive therefore refuses to let the audience place themselves outside the relationship. It is not an impersonal force descending on other people; it is the Lord of the person being addressed, which means the Lord of every person in the room.

**Certainty Stated as a Present Fact**

The Qur'an habitually describes the future in the language of the present when it wants to remove doubt about it. A thing that has not happened but is certain is spoken of as though the listener can already see it, and the grammar does the persuading that argument could not.

This verse is a clean example. There is no “will perhaps,” no “may,” no condition attached. The punishment is *wāqiʿ*, occurring. Compare the way the sūrah later describes the deniers being shoved into the Fire: the scene is narrated as though it is happening now, in the present tense of Arabic, to people who are currently alive and disputing.

That technique has a purpose beyond rhetoric. A warning stated conditionally invites the listener to test the condition. A warning stated as a fact transfers the burden: the only open question is which side of it the listener will be on.

**What This Statement Rules Out**

Three possibilities are closed by the wording. Cancellation is closed, because a thing that is falling cannot be recalled. Postponement is closed in principle, since the verse gives no term and no condition — a point the sūrah returns to when it describes the wrongdoers as unaware that another torment awaits them before the day itself. And bargaining is closed, because an event already in motion has no place for terms.

The Qur'an makes the last point explicit elsewhere: “On that Day no soul will be wronged in the least, nor will you be rewarded except for what you used to do” (36:54). The punishment is not a negotiable penalty; it is the settled consequence of a record that has already been kept.

**Certain in Fact, Unnamed in Time**

The verse states that the punishment will occur and says nothing about when. That combination is used throughout the Qur'an, and it is deliberate. The Hour is described as tremendous for the heavens and the earth, known to God alone, and arriving as a surprise (7:187). Elsewhere the nearness of the judgment is stated while the heedlessness of the audience is stated beside it: “˹The time of˺ people’s judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away” (21:1).

Certainty without a date produces a particular psychology, and the Qur'an is managing it. A punishment with a known date can be prepared for and then resumed afterwards; a punishment with no date has to be treated as imminent at every moment. The person who does not know when the audit happens keeps his books in order.

This is why the sūrah never offers a timetable. The warning is absolute and the schedule is withheld, and the two features together are what make the warning operative rather than theoretical. The listener cannot place himself safely on a calendar.

**A Warning That Is Also an Offer**

Stated as fact, the warning is still addressed to people with time. The sūrah's whole second half consists of instructions — remind, be patient, glorify — which only make sense if the outcome can still be changed by conduct before the day arrives.

That is the mercy inside a verse that sounds like a sentence. Certainty about the event is not certainty about who will be inside it. The Book describes the punishment as falling and simultaneously describes the path out of its reach, and it does both in the same chapter. The next verse removes any hope that someone else might stand between a person and that falling.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:8
> none will avert it—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Negation Made Deliberately Heavy**

The Arabic of this line is *mā lahu min dāfiʿ*: there is, for it, no averting party. The structure is a negation, a pronoun of possession, an indefinite noun, and a verbal noun — four elements where a plain statement would need two. Arabic uses the extra particle *min* before the indefinite noun to intensify the negation, so that the sense is not “hardly anyone will avert it” but “no averting party of any kind exists for it.”

The heaviness is the message. A listener could reasonably expect that an event of this size might have intermediaries: someone with influence, someone to appeal to, some arrangement to be made. The verse forecloses that expectation grammatically, by piling negation on negation until nothing is left standing. Nothing pushes it back. There is no one to push.

**Who Might Have Been Expected to Avert It**

The expectation the verse denies was a real one in Mecca, and it had several forms. Wealth was the first: a man with money could pay blood-money, buy protection, or redeem a relative. Lineage was the second: a clan stood behind its members and answered for them. Tribal patrons were the third: an alliance could settle a case before it reached judgment.

The Qur'an takes each of these in turn and dissolves it. “the Day when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit” (26:88) — the two resources a man actually relies on. Elsewhere the believers are told to give in charity before a day arrives in which there will be no bargaining and no friendship (2:254). The scene at the end of time is described as one in which a person flees from his own brother, mother, father, wife and children (80:34–36), which is the same point stated socially: the network that averts trouble in this world does not travel.

**Intercession Exists, But It Is Not a Defence**

The Qur'an does not deny intercession outright; it relocates it. “Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?” (2:255) — intercession is possible and is entirely dependent on permission. Elsewhere the condition is stated twice over: no intercession will benefit anyone except what the Most Merciful permits and is pleased with (20:109), and intercession is useless with Him except for those He permits (34:23).

The distinction matters for reading this verse. A defender averts a punishment by standing between the accused and the sentence. An intercessor granted permission is not standing in the way of anything; he is delivering a gift the sentence already accounts for. This verse denies the first thing, the averting party, and it does so absolutely. Nothing in the Qur'an's account of the last day includes a party who blocks the outcome.

**Averting Is Not the Same as Delaying**

One confusion the verse prevents is between stopping an event and postponing it. Postponement happens constantly and is described in the Qur'an as a method: “I only delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless” (7:183). The respite is real, and it is what makes repentance possible.

But delay is not defence. The same sūrah that describes the delay describes the wrongdoers as having another torment before the day arrives, and most of them do not know it (52:47). Time granted is time used, and the verse in al-Aʿrāf says plainly that the planning is flawless — meaning the extension does not disturb the outcome at all. This verse closes the door on the other idea, that someone might stop the event itself. Nobody does. The event arrives; only its timing was ever in question, and that question was settled before the person was born.

**No Averting Also Means No Effort**

The denial covers action as well as influence. A person might imagine running, hiding, or resisting. The Qur'an answers that imagination directly with a question and its own reply: man will ask on that day where the escape is, and the answer is that there is no refuge (75:10–11).

That exchange is the practical version of this verse. If there is no escape by flight and no defence by influence, then the only variable left is conduct before the day. The verse is not designed to produce despair; it is designed to remove alternatives, so that the reader stops looking for a way around the outcome and starts dealing with the outcome's cause.

**The Same Denial Stated Three Ways**

The Qur'an returns to this negation often enough that the repetition is itself a teaching. In al-Baqarah the believers are warned about a day in which no soul will help another, no intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help given (2:48). In al-Infiṭār the day is described as one on which no soul will be of any benefit to another whatsoever, because all authority on that day belongs to God entirely (82:19).

Each version removes a different escape. The first removes the helper, the mediator, and the payment — the three things that settle a case in this world. The second removes the reason those things work, which is delegated authority: on that day there is none, so nobody has standing to intervene.

This verse contributes the sharpest formulation of the three, because it denies not the outcome but the possibility of an averting party. The other verses say what will not be accepted; this one says no one is in a position to offer. The negation is about capacity, not permission, and capacity is the harder thing to recover.

**The Weight of Two Short Lines**

Read together with the verse before it, this line completes the oath's answer in eight Arabic words. Something will fall, and nothing will hold it back. The five witnesses sworn by at the opening of the sūrah exist to make that sentence credible.

The economy is characteristic of Meccan revelation. Long argument is wasted on people who have already decided not to listen; a compact statement backed by five concrete objects is not. The listener is left with an image rather than a debate — a falling thing with no hands beneath it — and the rest of the sūrah works on that image, first by describing what the falling looks like for those who denied it and then for those who did not.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:9
> on the Day the heavens will be shaken violently

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day Is Named by Its Motion**

The verse identifies the day by what the sky does on it: *tamūr al-samāʾ mawrā*, the heaven moves with a *mawr*. The cognate noun and verb describe a swaying, swirling, back-and-forth movement — the motion of something that has lost its fixedness. A branch in wind, a ship in swell, a drunkard on his feet: the word belongs to unsteady things.

Applying it to the sky is a category violation, and that is the point. The sky is the reference frame of the world. People orient themselves by it, set their calendars by it, and treat it as the one thing that does not move relative to everything else. A day on which the sky itself sways is a day on which orientation becomes impossible.

The verse does not describe destruction so much as instability. Nothing is said about breaking here; that comes in other verses. What is described is the withdrawal of steadiness from the most steady thing there is.

**Other Words for the Same Ending**

The Qur'an describes the sky's ending with several different images, and each one names a different aspect of the collapse. The heavens will split apart, becoming rose-red like burnt oil (55:37). The sky will burst open (84:1). It will be split open and become many gates (78:19). It will be torn until it is frail (69:16). On another day the sky and the clouds will be split apart (25:25).

The variety is not inconsistency. Splitting describes rupture; becoming gates describes openings; becoming frail describes loss of strength; becoming rose-red describes a change of substance. Churning, the word used here, describes loss of stability. Together the images cover what happens to a structure when it fails: it moves, it opens, it weakens, it changes colour.

No single verse tries to give a physics of the end. Each one takes the sky from the angle a person would actually notice it from — motion first, because motion is what the eye catches before anything else.

**A Sea Word Used for the Sky**

The verb in this verse belongs to the vocabulary of water. *Mawr* is used of the sea's heaving, and of a vessel surging. The sky is being described with the sea's own motion, one verse after the sea was sworn by in its burning state.

That adjacency is unlikely to be accidental. The two great fixed masses of the world — the water below and the vault above — are described in this opening with each other's language. The sea burns; the sky heaves. The elements trade properties, which is the Qur'an's way of saying that the properties were assigned, not inherent.

An ocean that burns and a sky that sways are both violations of what those things are. The listener who has just heard the oath by the burning sea recognises the same reversal in the next line, and the reversal is the argument: nothing here is what it is by necessity.

**Why the Sky Fails First**

The order is significant. Later verses describe the mountains being blown away, and the mountains are the ground under people's feet. But the sky is named first, and the sky is the thing nobody expects to be touched.

This sūrah's own opening explains why. The canopy raised high was sworn by as a witness. An oath invokes the stability of the thing sworn by; the day then removes that stability, and the oath is thereby confirmed rather than broken. What was solid enough to swear on is shown to be temporary, which is exactly what the oath claimed.

The sequence also matches how a person experiences catastrophe. The ground shakes and people look up. The first sign of the end is not in the earth but overhead, in the one place everyone assumed was safe.

**A Shaking Nobody Can Steady**

The sky is held up without pillars, as the Qur'an states explicitly: the heavens were raised without pillars that can be seen (13:2). A structure with visible supports can be shored up, braced, or repaired. A structure held by nothing visible can only be held by whoever holds it.

That is the theological point inside a physical description. When the sky shakes, there is nothing to grab. No beam to brace, no foundation to reinforce. The same power that keeps it up is the only thing that could stop the shaking, and the verse describes the shaking as what that power has decreed for that day.

The helplessness is total, and it is meant to be felt in the body. People who have lived through an earthquake describe the same thing: not fear of falling, but the discovery that the floor was never under their control. This verse puts that discovery above the head.

**The Calendar Ends With the Sky**

The sky is not only a ceiling; it is the world's clock. The Qur'an states the function explicitly: the sun was made a radiant source and the moon a reflected light with precisely ordained phases, so that people may know the number of years and the calculation of time (10:5). Months, seasons, planting, festivals, and the direction of prayer all depend on a sky that keeps a regular pattern.

A churning sky keeps no pattern. When the verse says the heavens will shake violently, it is describing the end of timekeeping as well as the end of a structure. There will be no next month, no season, no reckoning of years, because the mechanism that produced them will be in motion rather than in orbit.

That loss has a moral edge in a Book that constantly measures human life against appointed terms. Every community has a term that is written and cannot be advanced or delayed (7:34). The day when the sky churns is the day the measuring instrument itself is withdrawn, and with it the last chance to plan around a deadline.

**The Witness Is Being Withdrawn**

Every one of the five objects sworn by at the opening of this sūrah is affected by the day described here. The mountain is blown away two verses later. The canopy is shaking in this one. The sea has already been described as burning. The house and the book belong to a world that is passing.

That pattern shows what the oaths were for. They were not decorative invocations; they were an inventory of the stable, offered as evidence and then withdrawn as evidence of how little stability there was. The day of shaking is the completion of the argument begun by the mountain. The world is firm enough to swear by and too weak to last, and both facts are true at once.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:10
> and the mountains will be blown away entirely

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb Comes From Winnowing Grain**

The word used for what happens to the mountains is *nasf*, and *nasafa* in Arabic means to winnow — to toss grain into the air so that the wind carries off the chaff and leaves the seed. It is a farming verb, and it describes a total separation in which the substance is not damaged but dispersed.

Applying it to mountains produces one of the strangest images in the Qur'an. The heaviest, most rooted things on earth are described with the verb used for the lightest material in agriculture. The contrast is the point: on that day the difference between a mountain and a husk is a matter of degree, not kind.

The verb also implies an agent. Grain is winnowed by someone. Chaff does not fly off by itself; it is thrown, and the wind takes it. The passive form in this verse leaves the agent unnamed, which is consistent with the whole passage — the day is described in terms of what happens, and the one who makes it happen is never in doubt.

**The Stages the Qur'an Gives**

Different verses describe the mountains' end at different moments, and read together they form a sequence. The earth and the mountains will shake violently, and the mountains become dunes of shifting sand (73:14). They are lifted and crushed with a single blow (69:14). They are blown away and become like a mirage (78:20). They become like carded wool (101:5). They pass away like clouds while looking solid (27:88).

Each image captures a different property being lost: firmness, shape, weight, substance, visibility. Carded wool has no structure. A mirage has no body. A cloud has no permanence. Shifting sand has no position. Nothing is left of what made a mountain a mountain.

The Qur'an also gives the result rather than the process: the mountains will be wiped out completely, leaving the earth level and bare, with neither depressions nor elevations to be seen (20:105–107). That is the end state — a flat plane with nothing standing on it.

**The Mountain Sworn By in the First Verse**

This sūrah began by swearing on the Mount, and the mountain it swore by is the kind of thing being blown away here. The circle is deliberate and it is the sūrah's central irony.

An oath in Arabic stakes the speaker's word on the permanence of the thing invoked. To swear by a mountain is to say: this is as reliable as anything gets. Then, four verses after the day begins, the mountains are gone. The oath is not falsified by that; it is confirmed. The verse swore by the mountain as evidence that God's promise holds, and the removal of the mountain demonstrates exactly how much more solid that promise is than anything physical.

Nothing in the world is a better guarantee than the One who made the world. The oath and the destruction together say that.

**Why Mountains Are the Symbol Chosen**

In Arabic, as in most languages, the mountain is the standing image of permanence. The Qur'an uses it that way too: the mountains were placed as pegs so that the earth would not shake with its inhabitants (16:15), and as landmarks for guidance (16:16). They are the fixed points by which people travel and orient themselves.

Choosing them for the description of the end is therefore a rhetorical decision, not a geological one. The point is not that rocks are removed; it is that the category of the permanent is emptied. If the pegs that hold the earth down can be winnowed like chaff, then no feature of the world qualifies as fixed.

That has an immediate application for the people being addressed. Tribal honour, ancestral status, and inherited wealth were the mountains of Meccan society — the things a man leaned on. A day that removes mountains is a day on which none of those holds either.

**Total Removal, Not Damage**

*Naṣf* leaves nothing behind. This is not erosion, which wears a mountain down over centuries and leaves a smaller mountain. It is not damage, which implies something still standing in damaged condition. The word describes material that has been separated from itself and carried off.

The result described in Sūrah Ṭā Hā confirms it: the earth is left level and bare, with no hollows and no rises (20:106–107). A surface with no elevation is a surface with no landmarks, no shelters, no high ground to retreat to. The description removes not only the mountains but the possibility of taking cover behind them.

People who imagine the end as a disaster to survive will look for high ground. This verse closes that option, and it closes it with a farming word rather than a military one, which makes the image harder to dismiss as exaggeration.

**When the Pegs Come Out of the Earth**

The Qur'an's own image for the mountains is a tent peg: “Have We not smoothed out the earth ˹like a bed˺, and ˹made˺ the mountains as ˹its˺ pegs” (78:6–7). The comparison is Bedouin and exact. A tent is a light structure that the wind would lift if nothing held it down; the pegs are what make a dwelling possible on open ground.

That image governs this verse. Blowing the mountains away is pulling the pegs out of a tent, and what follows from pulling pegs is not damage to the fabric but the disappearance of the dwelling. The earth as a place to live is a function of its fixity, and the fixity is what is removed.

The sequence in Sūrah an-Nabaʾ is worth noting: the bed is smoothed first, then the pegs are named. Stability is described before the thing that provides it. This verse reverses the order by taking the pegs out, and the reversal is the whole eschatology in one clause.

**Reference Points Gone**

The final effect of the verse is on the person rather than on the landscape. Navigation depends on fixed points: stars above, mountains below. When the sky shakes in the previous verse and the mountains are winnowed in this one, both sets of reference points are gone at once.

That is the condition in which the sūrah's audience will stand. No orientation, no shelter, no landmark to recognise. The only thing left is the account each person carries, and the sūrah has already sworn by the written book that contains it. The world's furniture is cleared away so that nothing distracts from the one record that was never part of the furniture.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:11
> Then woe on that Day to the deniers—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict Delivered in a Single Noun**

The word *wayl* is a noun of calamity in Arabic — destruction, ruin, perdition — and it is used as an exclamation rather than a sentence. There is no verb in the phrase, no subject performing an action. Woe, on that day, to the deniers. The grammar is a pronouncement rather than a description, the way a judge's gavel is not an argument.

The Qur'an uses this construction deliberately when a case has already been argued. Everything the sūrah needed to establish was established in the opening oaths: revelation is real, the day is certain, nothing can avert it. What remains is not persuasion but sentencing. The verse begins with *fa*, a particle of consequence, so that the woe reads as the result of what came before rather than as a new assertion.

For the audience, the effect is a sudden change of register. Ten verses of scenery and oath have prepared them, and the eleventh drops the scenery entirely.

**What the Word Means, and a Report That Does Not Settle It**

The basic sense of *wayl* is destruction, and it appears in the Qur'an attached to specific behaviours: to those who defraud in measures (83:1), to every slanderer and backbiter (104:1), to those who write the book with their own hands and then sell it (2:79).

Some early exegetes explained the word as naming a place — a valley or a well in the Fire into which the condemned fall. The report carrying that explanation is not established by a chain that compels assent, so it is better treated as one reading among others than as a fact about the geography of Hell.

The stronger case is for the plain sense. A word used as an exclamation of ruin does its work by naming ruin, not by locating it. The reader does not need a map of the Fire to understand what has been pronounced on him; he needs to know that the pronouncement is final.

**A Refrain the Qur'an Repeats Ten Times**

The same sentence appears as a refrain in Sūrah al-Mursalāt, where “Woe on that Day to the deniers!” (77:15) closes ten separate blocks of argument. Each block presents a different fact — the sending of messengers, the destruction of earlier peoples, the creation of the human being from a base fluid — and each is followed by the same verdict.

The technique does something an argument cannot. A conclusion stated once can be answered; the same conclusion stated ten times, after ten different premises, cannot be answered without dismissing all ten. The listener is not being shown a proof so much as surrounded by one.

This verse performs the same function in miniature. It is the only occurrence of the refrain in aṭ-Ṭūr, and it sits exactly where the sūrah's own argument completes: after the oaths, after the announcement, after the shaking sky and the blown-away mountains.

**Who the Deniers Are**

The word used is *al-mukadhdhibūn*, from a doubled root meaning to call someone a liar rather than merely to be mistaken. The person described here did not fail to find the truth; he contradicted it.

The Qur'an supplies a definition in a parallel verse: woe on that day to the deniers, those who deny Judgment Day, and none denies it except every evildoing transgressor (83:10–12). The definition is diagnostic. Denial of the day is not an intellectual position held in isolation; it is connected to conduct, and the verse traces it to transgression.

That connection matters for the reader's self-assessment. The charge is not ignorance but contradiction, and contradiction is something a person can check in himself. The Meccan leaders who called the Prophet ﷺ a liar were not short of information; they had the Qur'an in front of them.

**The Woe Is Dated**

The phrase “on that Day” is what separates this woe from every ordinary calamity. People in this world suffer ruin and recover from it: a business fails, a family breaks, a reputation is lost. Each of those is a woe with an end.

This one is attached to a day, and the day has no following morning. The dating also answers the objection that the warning is unfalsifiable because it is vague. The Qur'an consistently ties its warnings to a specific event that will be witnessed by everyone at once, and it describes that event in physical detail — the shaking sky, the removed mountains, the pushed crowds.

The specificity is the point. A general threat can be argued with; a dated one can only be awaited or prepared for.

**The Denial Becomes the Charge**

The structure of the sentence is worth noticing: the woe is pronounced on the deniers for denying. The crime and the punishment are not matched by coincidence; the punishment is the completion of the crime.

A person who contradicts a truth has chosen a position in which the truth will eventually appear without him being able to retract. The day described in this sūrah is when the appearance happens. The denial does not produce the day; the day exposes the denial, and the exposure is the ruin.

This explains why the following verses take the form of a taunt rather than a lecture. What the deniers are told in the Fire is not new information. It is their own former claim, read back to them in a setting where the claim can no longer be maintained.

**Addressed to People Who Are Still Alive**

The verse is spoken in the present to listeners who have not yet died. That is the condition under which a woe functions as a warning rather than a report.

The Qur'an's warnings are always delivered inside the window in which they can be escaped. A person hearing this verse in Mecca in the seventh century could act on it; a person hearing it today can act on it. The woe is a description of an outcome, and outcomes of this kind are described so that they can be avoided.

The sūrah goes on to describe the opposite outcome in detail, for people who were not deniers. The two descriptions together make the point that the sentence is not universal. It has a subject, and the subject is identified by conduct.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:12
> those who amuse themselves with falsehood

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb Means Wading**

The word translated as amusing themselves comes from *khawḍ*, which in Arabic means to wade or plunge into water — to go in up to the knees, then the waist, then the chest. The image is of a person entering something that surrounds him, and the Qur'an applies it to talk: those who wade into falsehood.

That is a much stronger picture than “talking nonsense.” A wader is not standing at the edge commenting; he is inside, moving, and progressively less able to get out. The verb carries the idea of immersion, and immersion is what makes vain talk dangerous. Nobody drowns in an inch of water.

The second verb, *yalʿabūn*, means to play or amuse oneself, and it names the mood rather than the activity. Together the two verbs describe people who are both submerged and entertained, and the combination explains how an entire life can be spent without ever touching the question the sūrah has just raised.

**Two Failures, Not One**

It is worth separating the verbs, because they describe different things and a person can have one without the other.

Wading is the argumentative failure. It is the endless discussion of revelation that never resolves, the objections raised for the pleasure of raising them, the debate that continues after the point has been conceded. The Qur'an forbids even sitting in on it: “And when you come across those who ridicule Our revelations, do not sit with them unless they engage in a different topic. Should Satan make you forget, then once you remember, do not ˹continue to˺ sit with the wrongdoing people” (6:68).

Playing is the frivolous failure. It is treating serious matters as entertainment, which the Qur'an identifies as a way of taking religion itself: “And leave those who take this faith ˹of Islam˺ as mere play and amusement and are deluded by ˹their˺ worldly life” (6:70).

The verse joins the two because the deniers did both. They argued and they joked, and the arguing gave the joking a respectable cover.

**The Tongue Is the Instrument**

The Prophet ﷺ identified this organ as the one that most often destroys a person. In the report narrated by Muʿādh ibn Jabal, he was asked for a deed that would admit him to Paradise and keep him from the Fire, and after listing the basics the Prophet ﷺ took hold of his own tongue and said: restrain this. When Muʿādh asked whether people are held to account for what they say, the answer was: may your mother be bereaved of you, Muʿādh — is there anything that topples people onto their faces in the Hellfire but the harvest of their tongues? (Sunan al-Tirmidhī 2616, graded ṣaḥīḥ).

The word *ḥaṣāʾid*, harvests, belongs to the same agricultural register as the winnowing verb used for the mountains in this sūrah. What the tongue sows is reaped, and the reaping is what people are thrown into.

**What They Were Wading In**

The confession of the people of the Fire identifies the activity precisely. Asked why they are where they are, they answer: “We used to indulge ˹in falsehood˺ along with others” (74:45). The phrase *along with others* is the social dimension — nobody wades alone, and the company is part of the immersion.

Another verse turns the same verb into a command of abandonment: “So let them indulge ˹in falsehood˺ and amuse ˹themselves˺ until they face their Day, which they have been threatened with” (70:42). The command is not permission; it is withdrawal. Once a person has chosen the water he wants to stand in, further argument only adds to the wading.

The Qur'an is describing a recognisable situation. Every society has its arguments that consume energy and produce nothing, and the participants in them usually believe they are doing something serious.

**Denial Is an Activity**

The verse describes the deniers with two present-tense verbs. They are wading and they are playing — not they waded, not they will play. The continuous aspect is a statement about how denial actually operates.

Nobody denies a truth once and is finished with it. Denial has to be maintained, and maintaining it requires activity: new objections, new jokes, new company, new distractions. That maintenance is the wading. It is work, and it is exhausting, which is why people who have been denying something for years rarely stop to examine it.

The verse therefore diagnoses rather than insults. It says what the deniers are doing with their time, and the description is accurate enough that a reader can recognise himself in it.

**Amusement as the Deepest Rejection**

The second verb deserves more weight than it usually gets. Amusement is not the opposite of belief; indifference is, and amusement produces indifference.

The Qur'an treats this as a spiritual condition with a cause. Those who take religion as play and amusement are described as deluded by the worldly life (6:70), and the delusion is the mechanism: when everything feels light, nothing feels urgent, and a warning that requires action gets treated as another item of entertainment.

This explains why the sūrah's warnings are so physical. A shaking sky and winnowed mountains are not images that can be enjoyed. They are chosen to break the mood of the people described here, whose danger is not that they will argue with the warning but that they will be amused by it.

**The Verse That Follows the Confession**

Read in sequence, the two verses form a single charge: woe to the deniers who are wading and playing. The first names the position, the second names the activity that sustains it.

That pairing is the sūrah's method throughout. It does not merely announce outcomes; it describes the behaviour that leads to them, so that the outcome can be traced backwards and interrupted. The next verse begins the description of the day the wading ends, and the transition is abrupt — from a pastime to a push toward the Fire.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:13
> ˹It is˺ the Day they will be fiercely shoved into the Fire of Hell

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb Describes a Push, Not a Walk**

The word *yudaʿʿūn* comes from a doubled root meaning to push or thrust away with force. Doubling in Arabic usually intensifies or repeats the action, so the sense here is not a single shove but a series of them — driven along, unable to stop, unable to turn.

The addition of the cognate noun *daʿʿā* confirms the reading. Arabic uses this construction, a verb followed by its own source noun, to remove any doubt about the manner of the action. It is not a metaphor for guidance or for being led somewhere unpleasant. It is physical compulsion, repeated.

The contrast with the sūrah's other description of movement is instructive. The righteous will be reclining on thrones; these people cannot stand still. The postures in this chapter divide humanity into those who are settled and those who are driven, and the difference is stated in verbs rather than in adjectives.

**The Direction Is Named**

The push is *ilā nār jahannam*, toward the Fire of Jahannam. Naming the Fire with its proper name rather than a general word removes the possibility of reading the verse as a general statement about bad consequences.

Jahannam is the Qur'an's specific designation for the place of punishment, and it is used throughout with concrete accompaniments: gates that open for arrivals, keepers who question them, a fuel of people and stones. This verse places the arrival at that place, and the manner of arrival is part of the picture.

Arriving anywhere under compulsion means the arrival was not chosen at the last moment. Every choice that produced it was made earlier, in a setting where alternatives existed. The shove is the visible form of a decision made long before.

**What Happens at the Threshold**

Other verses describe the same moment from other angles, and together they fill in the scene. The angels take the souls of the disbelievers beating their faces and their backs (8:50), and the same description is used for the moment the angels take their souls at death (47:27). The people of the Fire are dragged on their faces and told to taste its touch (54:48).

In Sūrah az-Zumar the arrival is described as an organised process: those who disbelieved are driven to Hell in successive groups, and when they arrive its gates are opened and the keepers question them about whether messengers came to them (39:71). The answer given there is yes — the messengers came, and the punishment has come to pass.

Nothing in these accounts suggests confusion or delay. The process is orderly, which is more frightening than chaos would be.

**An Alternative Reading of the Same Letters**

Some exegetes read the verb differently, taking the sense as being called to the Fire with a mighty calling rather than being pushed into it. The consonantal skeleton of the Arabic permits both, and the difference changes the emphasis rather than the outcome.

The pushing reading stresses physical force; the calling reading stresses announcement. Weighing them, the pushing reading is stronger here, because the doubled form and the cognate noun are natural for violent motion and awkward for a summons. The calling reading also loses the intensity that the doubled form supplies.

Both readings agree on the substance: the arrival is compelled and it is public. Whether the person is thrust in or summoned in, he does not enter by his own decision, and he does not enter alone.

**The Crowd Arrives Together**

The plural form of the verb matters. They are shoved, not he is shoved. The sūrah is describing a collective movement, and the Qur'an elsewhere makes the collectivity explicit: the wicked are driven to Hell like a thirsty herd (19:86).

That image contains the isolation inside the crowd. A herd moves as a mass with no individual direction, and each member is going the same way for the same reason. The people of the Fire will be able to see one another and recognise one another, and the Qur'an describes their conversations there — leaders and followers blaming each other, companions disowning companions.

For the Meccan audience the social dimension was pointed. The opposition to the Prophet ﷺ was organised around clans and assemblies, and the verse tells those assemblies that they will be handled as assemblies.

**The Day Is Named Twice**

The previous verse ended with “on that Day,” and this verse begins by naming the day again. Arabic rhetoric uses this repetition to hold the listener's attention on the time rather than on the event.

The effect is to make the day itself the subject. The Fire is where they end up, but the day is what surrounds them. Everything described in the sūrah from the shaking sky onward is dated to that same day, and the repetition binds the images into one occasion rather than a series of separate warnings.

That matters for how the warning works. A person can argue with a threat; a person cannot argue with a date. The sūrah has refused from the beginning to give the date, and it insists here on the fact that there is one.

**No Interval Between the Push and the Fire**

There is no trial, no hearing, and no appeal in this verse. The sequence is movement and then arrival, and the reason is that the hearing already happened.

The Qur'an describes the record as laid open and the person as told to read it himself, sufficient on that day to take his own account (17:14). The questioning at the gates of Hell in az-Zumar is not a trial either; the keepers ask whether messengers came, and the answer is admitted immediately (39:71).

What this verse describes is therefore the execution of a sentence, not the process of reaching one. That is why it is so brief and so physical. Argument is over. The only verb left is the push.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:14
> ˹They will be told,˺ “This is the Fire which you used to deny

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence Built Around a Pointing Finger**

The statement opens with *hādhihi*, the demonstrative for something near: this. The speaker is not describing something distant or recalling something from the past; he is indicating an object present to the senses of the person addressed.

That single word does the whole rhetorical work of the verse. Everything the deniers rejected was rejected as absent — a promise about a day nobody had seen, made by a man nobody could verify. The reply is a demonstration rather than a rebuttal. The thing that could not be seen is now within pointing distance, and the sentence that accompanies the gesture is as short as the gesture.

The Qur'an uses this method elsewhere. At the end of the account of the deniers held before their Lord, the question put to them is: “Is this ˹Hereafter˺ not the truth?” and their answer is an oath on God that it is (6:30). The admission comes from seeing, not from argument.

**Who Is Speaking**

Verses fourteen through sixteen form a single speech, and the tradition identifies the speakers as the angels in charge of the Fire. The Qur'an describes those keepers as appointed over it and unmoved by pleading, and it describes them questioning the arrivals at the gates (39:71).

The identity of the speaker matters for the tone. This is not the gloating of an enemy. The angels have no personal grievance against the condemned; they are carrying out an instruction, and the content of what they say is factual. The taunt is in the situation, not in the speaker's heart.

That distinction keeps the verse from being read as vindictive. The Fire is administered, and what is said at its door is what is true.

**“Which You Used to Deny” — The Tense of Habit**

The phrase *allatī kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūn* puts the denial in the continuous past: you used to be denying it, over and over. The verb form describes a repeated action rather than a single event.

That choice of tense is accurate to the behaviour described two verses earlier, where the deniers are wading and playing. Denial of this kind is a practice. It has to be renewed every time the subject comes up, and every renewal is another instance of the same verb.

The verse therefore reads the denial back as a pattern rather than an accident. Nobody will be punished in the Fire for a momentary doubt. The charge is a settled habit, and the habit is one the person can observe in himself while he still has the chance to stop it.

**The Same Words in Two Other Chapters**

This sentence is not unique to aṭ-Ṭūr, and the repetitions sharpen its meaning. In al-Mursalāt the condemned are told to proceed into the Fire they used to deny (77:29), and the refrain that follows each block of argument in that sūrah is the same woe pronounced on the deniers.

In al-Muṭaffifīn the wording is shorter still: they will be told, this is what you used to deny (83:17), after the statement that a veil has been placed between them and what they desired.

The three versions have one feature in common: the object of the denial and the object of the punishment are the same thing. Nothing extra is added at the end. The person is not punished for denying the Fire and then given something else; he is given the Fire, and the sentence explains the connection.

**Denial Ends Where Presence Begins**

There is an epistemological point inside this verse. The deniers rejected the Fire on the grounds that they had no access to it, and their rejection was a claim about evidence. The day described here supplies the evidence and removes the claim.

The Qur'an states the general principle: on that day the veils are lifted and sight becomes sharp (50:22), and elsewhere it diagnoses the problem as not being about eyes at all — it is not the eyes that are blind but the hearts in the chests (22:46). Both statements agree that the deficiency was in the person, not in the availability of evidence.

Once the deficiency is cured by presence, the denial becomes impossible. That impossibility is the punishment. To be shown that you were wrong about the one thing you organised your life around, in a moment when correction is no longer available, is the ruin the previous verse pronounced.

**The Sentence Contains No Insult**

What is absent from this speech matters as much as what is in it. There is no name-calling, no description of the condemned as fools or enemies, no rhetorical flourish. The sentence is a statement of identity: this is that.

The restraint is characteristic of the Qur'an's accounts of the last day. The speeches made there are factual, and the horror comes from the facts. A judge who insults the accused weakens the sentence; a sentence that merely states the record cannot be weakened.

For the reader this restraint has a practical effect. An insult can be dismissed as hostility. A statement of fact cannot be dismissed, only accepted or refused, and the verse leaves the reader with exactly that choice while the choice is still open.

**The Accusers Become the Accused**

In Mecca the charge of fabrication ran in the opposite direction. The Prophet ﷺ was the one being told that he had made things up, and his opponents occupied the position of sober judges assessing a claim.

This verse reverses the positions without saying so. The people who claimed to be evaluating evidence are now standing in front of the evidence, and the ones evaluating them are the keepers of the Fire. No counter-argument is recorded in the verse, which is itself a comment: the wading and playing described earlier produced a great deal of speech and no defence.

The reversal is the sūrah's answer to its own setting. It was revealed to a small community being accused of invention by a confident majority, and it states, in one sentence, which side of the argument will be standing when the evidence arrives.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:15
> Is this magic, or do you not see

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charge Is Handed Back as a Question**

The verse puts the deniers' own accusation into interrogative form: *a-siḥrun hādhā*, is this magic? They had used the word as an assertion in the world, and it is now returned to them in the Fire as a question they cannot answer.

The transformation is exact. An accusation functions as a dismissal — say the word and you do not have to look at the thing. A question functions as an examination, and the person asked is expected to look. The verse takes the instrument of dismissal and turns it into an instrument of attention.

There is a second reading, equally strong: the question is sarcastic, meaning “this is not magic, is it?” Both readings converge on the same point. The word they used to explain the revelation away has no application to what is in front of them.

**The Accusation They Made in the World**

The charge of sorcery was standard, and the Qur'an records it against messenger after messenger. The man who saw the signs and then turned away said: “This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but magic from the ancients” (74:24). Those who see a sign say their eyes have been dazzled and that they must have been bewitched (15:15). The pattern is generalised in Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt: no messenger came to those before them without being called a magician or a madman (51:52).

The charge has a specific function. It does not dispute the event; it reclassifies it. Magic is also a real occurrence, so calling a miracle magic concedes the phenomenon while removing the inference. That is precisely why it fails here: the classification was never tested, only asserted.

**“Or Do You Not See?” — The Second Half**

The alternative offered is sight. *Af lā tubṣirūn*: or do you not see? The question implies that seeing is now available and that the answer to the first question is obvious to anyone who looks.

The Qur'an describes the change in perception directly. The denier will be told: you were totally heedless of this, and now the veil has been lifted, so today your sight is sharp (50:22). The veil was not over the object but over the person.

This is why the verse pairs the two questions rather than asking one. Magic was the explanation offered in place of looking. Now that looking has become unavoidable, the explanation has no work to do. The verse does not argue against the charge of magic; it makes the charge irrelevant by supplying the sight that was refused.

**Sorcery Would Have Been Distinguishable**

The accusation fails on its own terms, and the Qur'an shows why. When the magicians of Pharaoh opposed Mūsā, they recognised what they were seeing as something outside their craft, and they fell in prostration (7:120–121). The professionals in the field identified the difference immediately.

That account is the answer to the charge. A sorcerer can tell magic from what is not magic, and the distinction does not require faith. What the Meccan opponents lacked was not expertise but willingness, which is why the Qur'an describes the charge as a habit of speech rather than a finding.

The verse in the Fire closes the matter by removing even the habit. There is nothing left to say, because the person is inside the thing he was calling a trick.

**A Question With No Addressee Left to Answer**

The rhetorical structure deserves notice: two questions, no answer recorded. The Qur'an frequently records the responses of the condemned — they will cry out, they will swear by God, they will ask to be sent back (6:27–30). Here there is nothing.

Silence in this Book is a verdict. When a question is asked and no reply is given, the reader is meant to supply the only possible reply, which is the admission that the charge was false. The verse spares the audience the sound of that admission while leaving its content unmistakable.

For the listener in Mecca, the silence was also a prediction. The men who were calling the Qur'an magic would one day be unable to repeat the sentence, and the sūrah tells them so while they still can.

**Sight Alone Is Not Faith**

There is a limit built into the verse, and the Qur'an states it plainly. Seeing the truth on that day produces admission, not belief — the people held before their Lord will swear that the Hereafter is real, and the reply will be to taste the punishment for their disbelief (6:30).

The distinction is between knowledge and trust. Knowledge can be compelled by evidence; trust cannot. That is why the sūrah's warnings are addressed to the living rather than to the damned, and why the second half of the chapter turns to instruction for the Prophet ﷺ rather than to further description of the Fire.

The verse therefore works in two directions at once. It closes the case of those who can no longer act, and it opens the case of those who can.

**The Word That Named the Qur'an Is Asked to Name the Fire**

The final effect of the verse is on the word *siḥr* itself. In Mecca it was the strongest available dismissal, the word that ended arguments. In the Fire it is the word that ends nothing, because the thing it was used to dismiss is standing there.

Language in this sūrah keeps doing this. The denial is read back, the accusation is returned, the amusement is interrupted. Words that functioned as shields in one setting function as evidence in another, and the change of setting is what the whole sūrah is about.

The next verse drops the question entirely and states the sentence, and from that point the speech in the Fire contains no questions at all.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:16
> Burn in it! It is the same whether you endure ˹it˺ patiently or not. You are only rewarded for what you used to do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Speech in the Fire Ends With Three Statements**

The address that began with a pointing finger closes with an instruction, an equivalence, and a justification. Enter it and burn. Whether you are patient or impatient makes no difference. You are being given nothing but what you did.

The closing speech has no question in it. That is a change from the verse before, where the accusation of magic was returned as a question. Once the question is asked, nothing further needs asking; what remains is the sentence and the reason for it.

The three statements are ordered deliberately. The command states what will happen, the equivalence removes the one thing a condemned person usually holds on to, and the justification explains why the situation cannot be changed.

**Patience Has a Market, and This Is Not It**

*Ṣabr* — patient endurance — is one of the most rewarded qualities in the Qur'an. Those who endure will be given their reward without limit (39:10). The believers are told to seek help through patience and prayer (2:153). Patience in this world converts suffering into something else: it lessens a trial, it earns reward, and it changes the person enduring.

In the Fire it does none of that. The verse states the fact flatly: it is the same for you. The virtue is not denied; its function is. Patience is a way of using time, and the person in the Fire has run out of the kind of time in which patience pays.

That is why the sentence is so devastating. It does not say that patience will not be asked of them. It says that patience will not be worth anything, which is a different and colder statement.

**The End of Leverage**

The equivalence *sawāʾun ʿalaykum* closes every avenue of negotiation. In this world, disposition matters: a person who accepts a penalty calmly is treated differently from one who fights it. A prisoner can improve his conditions; a debtor can arrange terms; a defendant can appeal.

The Qur'an gives the condemned their own words on this point, and the words match the verse: “˹Now˺ it is all the same for us whether we suffer patiently or impatiently, there is no escape for us” (14:21). The admission comes from inside the situation, and it names both halves — the equivalence of patience and impatience, and the absence of escape.

Removing leverage is what makes the sentence final. A penalty that can be modified is a continuing relationship; a penalty that cannot be modified is an ending.

**“You Are Only Rewarded for What You Used to Do”**

The justification uses the language of reward, *tujzawn*, for a punishment. The word is neutral: it means to be given what corresponds to an action, whether good or bad. Using it here is a statement about the nature of the punishment.

The Qur'an makes the same statement in the same terms elsewhere: no soul will be wronged in the least, nor will anyone be rewarded except for what he used to do (36:54), and the principle is stated as a general rule of divine conduct — God does not wrong people in the least, but people wrong themselves (10:44). The scale is precise down to the weight of an atom, on both sides (99:7–8).

The effect of this clause is to remove God from the position of an aggrieved party. The Fire is not a reaction. It is a correspondence, and the correspondence was set by the conduct.

**The Continuous Past Again**

*Kuntum taʿmalūn* — you used to do — repeats the grammatical form used two verses earlier for the denial. The charge in both cases is a pattern rather than an incident, and the reward corresponds to the pattern.

This matters for how the verse should be read by someone still alive. A single failure is not the subject of this sentence. The subject is a settled direction of conduct, sustained over time, and the verse says that the direction is what gets paid out.

That reading is not softening. It is more demanding than a rule about isolated acts, because it means the thing being measured is the shape of a life. It is also more hopeful in the same measure, because a direction can be changed while there is still time.

**The Verse Closes a Movement**

Structurally this is the end of the first half of the sūrah. The oaths produced an announcement; the announcement produced a day; the day produced a push, a taunt, a question, and now a sentence. Nothing further will be said about the deniers until the chapter's closing verses.

The closure is signalled by the change of voice. The speech inside the Fire is finished, and the sūrah turns without transition to the people who are not there. That abruptness is characteristic of Meccan revelation: the two outcomes are placed side by side with no editorial comment connecting them.

The reader is left to make the comparison himself, which is more effective than being told what to conclude.

**Why the Justification Is Included**

A sentence could have been pronounced without explanation. The verse adds one, and the addition is an act of respect toward the condemned and toward the reader.

The Qur'an never presents punishment as arbitrary. Every account of the Fire includes the reason, usually in the condemned person's own words or in a statement of correspondence. The purpose is not to persuade those already inside; it is to make the account intelligible to those outside, so that they can see that the outcome follows from conduct.

That intelligibility is the whole function of the warning. A punishment whose logic is hidden produces fear without direction. A punishment whose logic is stated produces something the reader can act on.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:17
> Indeed, the righteous will be in Gardens and bliss

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Turn Happens Without a Connector**

The verse begins with *inna*, a particle of emphasis, and moves straight from the Fire to the Gardens. There is no “on the other hand,” no “as for the others.” The two states are placed against each other with nothing between them, and the contrast does the work that a transition would have done.

This is the sūrah's method throughout its second half. The first movement ended with a sentence spoken inside the Fire; the second opens with a location and its inhabitants. The reader has been shown one destination in detail and is now shown the other, in the same register, with the same concreteness.

The absence of a connector also refuses to make the two outcomes symmetrical in tone. They are not alternatives of equal weight being weighed. One is stated as a fact and the other as a fact, and the person reading has to decide which one he is walking toward.

**Al-Muttaqūn: Those Who Took Precautions**

The word used is *al-muttaqūn*, and its root means to shield or guard. A person with *taqwā* has placed something between himself and what he fears. The usual translation, the righteous, flattens the image; the word describes conduct rather than character.

That conduct is defined negatively as much as positively. The person with *taqwā* avoids specific things: he leaves what he would otherwise take, he says nothing where he would otherwise speak, he stays away from the gatherings the sūrah has just described. The precaution is the defining act, and it is performed in the ordinary conditions of life, not in crisis.

The verse's placement makes the meaning sharper. The people in the Fire are there because they did not guard against it while guarding was possible. The people in the Gardens are there because they did.

**Gardens and Bliss, Joined by “And”**

The phrase *fī jannātin wa naʿīm* puts the Gardens and bliss in a coordinate construction: gardens, and delight. Elsewhere the Qur'an uses the genitive form, the Gardens of Bliss, treating delight as a property of the place (5:65; 10:9; 22:56; 37:43; 56:12).

The difference is not trivial. The genitive form describes a location qualified by a mood. The coordinate form names two things: a place, and a state. Both are given, and neither is reduced to the other.

That distinction answers a real question about paradise. Is it a place that happens to be pleasant, or a feeling that happens to be described in spatial language? This verse declines the choice. There is somewhere to be, and there is something to feel, and the two are listed as separate gifts.

**Why Both Are Necessary**

A place without delight is a landscape. Delight without a place is a mood, and moods pass. The Qur'an's descriptions of the Hereafter are consistently resistant to both reductions.

The Gardens are described with physical detail: rivers of water, milk, wine and honey, fruit of every kind (47:15). Thrones, cups, pitchers, servants, spouses, shade. At the same time the state of the inhabitants is described: no idle talk, no sin, no bitterness in the heart (15:47). Neither list works alone.

This verse is the compact statement of that method. It gives the noun for the place and the noun for the feeling, joined by a conjunction that keeps them distinct and equally real.

**The Plural “Gardens”**

The word is *jannātin*, plural. The Qur'an speaks of Paradise in the plural throughout, and the plural has consequences.

The most obvious is rank: the Hereafter is described as far greater in rank and in favour than the differences of this world (17:21), and ranks imply distinctions. The plural also suggests that paradise is not a single hall where everyone stands together but a set of places, each occupied, each particular.

The plural is also grammatically natural in Arabic for expressing abundance, and the two readings are not opposed. A place described in the plural is both vast and varied, and the person entering it enters somewhere specific rather than somewhere general.

**What They Guarded Against**

The identity of the *muttaqūn* is best read against the previous verse. The deniers are inside a punishment that their own conduct produced, and the verse states the correspondence plainly.

The *muttaqūn* are the people who read the same correspondence in advance. They accepted that conduct has an outcome and arranged their conduct accordingly. That is the whole of *taqwā* in this context: not fear as an emotion but foresight as a practice.

The sūrah's structure makes the comparison unavoidable. Two groups, one Book, one set of consequences. The difference between them is not information; both were told. The difference is what they did with the telling.

**A Garden Is an Enclosed Place**

The word *jannah* comes from a root meaning to cover or conceal. A garden is a piece of ground hidden by its own foliage, walled or hedged, with an inside and an outside. The same root produces the word for the unseen beings called jinn and for anything that shields.

That etymology gives the plural in this verse more content than a translation can carry. The Gardens are not open ground; they are enclosures, and an enclosure is the opposite of exposure. The day described earlier in this sūrah leaves the earth level and bare, with no hollows and no rises anywhere (20:106–107) — a plain with nowhere to be hidden. The Fire has the same problem in another form: its inhabitants are sent to a shade of smoke that provides neither coolness nor shelter (77:30–31).

Against both, the Gardens are defined by having an inside. The preposition *fī*, in, is the verse's most important word, because it describes residence within something that encloses. What the *muttaqūn* guarded themselves with in this world is answered by a place that guards them in the next.

**A Statement of Location Before a Description of Life**

The verse says where they are before saying what they do. The following verses fill in the detail: what they are given, what they are protected from, what they eat, where they sit, whom they face.

That order is deliberate. The location is the ground of everything else, and the location is described as being *in* the Gardens rather than arriving at them. The preposition denotes settled residence.

Settlement is the point of contrast with the previous movement. The deniers were being pushed, in motion, unable to stop. The righteous are in a place. The sūrah divides humanity by posture as much as by destiny, and the first word about the second group is a word about being somewhere.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:18
> enjoying whatever their Lord will have granted them. And their Lord will have protected them from the torment of the Hellfire

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Actions of the Same Lord**

The verse contains two verbs with the same subject, and the repetition of the subject is the point. Their Lord gave them. Their Lord shielded them. Nothing in between comes from another source, and no part of either action is credited to them.

Arabic could have omitted the second mention of the Lord, since the subject carries over. The text keeps it, and the keeping is emphatic: the one who provides is the one who protects. The two functions are not distributed among different agents, and neither is delegated.

That framing does something to the reader's understanding of the reward. What the inhabitants of the Gardens have is not a wage collected from an employer but a provision from a Lord, and a provision can be generous in ways a wage cannot.

**Fākihīn: A Word Built on Fruit**

The participle translated as enjoying comes from the root *f-k-h*, which produces *fākiha*, fruit. To be *fākih* is to be occupied with fruit, and by extension to be given over to delight and light-hearted pleasure.

The word choice is precise. This is not the satisfaction of a need; it is the enjoyment of something that is not necessary. Fruit in the desert world of the Qur'an is not staple food but luxury — the thing eaten after the meal, offered to a guest, given as a gift.

The verse therefore describes a state rather than an activity. The people are not busy producing, earning, or defending. They are engaged in enjoyment, and the enjoyment is continuous rather than episodic, which is why the participle is used instead of a past-tense verb.

**Protection Stated Second, but Logically First**

The order of the two clauses is worth examining. First the giving, then the shielding. Chronologically the shielding comes before the enjoyment, since a person who is inside the Fire cannot enjoy anything.

The Qur'an uses this order elsewhere: the inhabitants of the Gardens will never taste death beyond the first death, and He will protect them from the punishment of the Hellfire (44:56). There again the protection is named after the blessing, as the guarantee that the blessing will not be interrupted.

The placement after the giving suggests a different emphasis: the shield is mentioned as the reason the enjoyment is complete. A pleasure that might be taken away at any moment is not a pleasure but an anxiety with intervals.

**Safety Is a Condition of Delight**

The pairing in this verse states a psychological fact that the Qur'an relies on repeatedly. Enjoyment requires the absence of threat. A person who fears loss cannot enjoy possession, which is why the descriptions of paradise keep returning to what is removed from it: no idle talk, no sin, no grief, no fatigue, no death.

The Fire is the opposite condition. Its inhabitants are told that patience and impatience are equivalent, which is a statement that nothing they do can improve their situation. The people in the Gardens are told nothing of the kind, because there is nothing to be told.

The verse thus defines paradise by subtraction as much as by addition. What is given is described in one clause; what is removed is described in the other, and the removal is what makes the giving durable.

**Given, Not Merited Alone**

The clause “whatever their Lord will have granted them” (52:18) frames the whole reward as a gift, and the Qur'an is consistent about this even when it uses the language of reward.

The Prophet ﷺ stated the principle without qualification. Abū Hurayrah reported that he said: pursue the right course and keep close to it, and know that none of you will be saved by his deeds. When the Companions asked whether that applied even to him, he said: not even me, unless Allah encompasses me with His mercy and favour (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2816).

The statement does not make deeds irrelevant; the same verse in aṭ-Ṭūr that follows speaks of eating and drinking for what they used to do. It relocates the cause. Deeds are the condition under which mercy operates, and mercy is what produces the outcome. That is why the verse here credits the Lord with the giving rather than the people with the earning.

**Sorrow Removed as Well as Fire**

The protection named here is not the only thing taken away from the inhabitants of the Gardens, and the Qur'an lets them describe the removal in their own words. They will say: “Praise be to Allah, Who has kept away from us all ˹causes of˺ sorrow. Our Lord is indeed All-Forgiving, Most Appreciative” (35:34), and the next verse names the place: the Home of Everlasting Stay, where neither fatigue nor weariness will touch them (35:35).

Sorrow, fatigue and fire are three different threats, and each one destroys a different kind of good. Fire destroys the person. Sorrow destroys enjoyment while the person remains. Fatigue destroys the capacity to enjoy even when nothing is wrong.

The removal of all three is what makes the shield complete. A person protected only from the Fire could still grieve, and a person protected from grief could still tire. This verse names the first removal; the others are described elsewhere, and together they account for why the enjoyment in the previous clause is stated as a continuous state rather than a moment.

**The Name Used Is “Their Lord”**

*Rabbuhum* appears twice in the verse. The word *rabb* carries the sense of one who nurtures, maintains, and brings something to completion — closer to a guardian than to a distant sovereign.

Its use here is significant given what the sūrah has just described. The Lord who punishes the deniers is called *rabb* in the seventh verse: the punishment of your Lord. The same title is used for the one who gives and protects. There is no division between a harsh deity and a generous one.

That continuity is the theological centre of the chapter. The one who arranged the oaths, who fixed the day, who administers the Fire is the one who provides fruit and removes fear, and the difference between the two outcomes lies in the people rather than in Him.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:19
> ˹They will be told,˺ “Eat and drink happily for what you used to do.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Invitation of a Host**

The speech in the Gardens opens with an imperative that is not a demand. Eat and drink. Nobody needs to be told to eat in a place of delight, which is why the command is not an instruction but a welcome — the form of words a host uses when he sets food in front of a guest and will not accept refusal.

The contrast with the other speech in this sūrah is exact. In the Fire the imperative was enter it and burn. Here it is eat and drink. Both are commands, both are addressed in the plural, both are followed by a justification using the same grammatical formula. The parallel structure makes the difference in content impossible to miss.

The Qur'an's paradise is consistently social in this way. It is not a state of private bliss but a table, and a table implies a host, a guest, and a permission to begin.

**Hanīʾan: Wholesome, and Without a Bill**

The adverb *hanīʾan* is difficult to translate and worth unpacking. It comes from a root meaning to be agreeable, wholesome, and free of harm — food that does not sicken, hospitality that carries no obligation, a gift that is not a loan.

Three things are packed into the word. The food causes no harm, unlike the food and drink of the Fire. The hospitality carries no reservation, which is how the classical commentators gloss it: the host gives without stint and without expectation of return. And there is no reckoning attached to the enjoyment, which distinguishes it from every meal in this world, where someone pays.

The Qur'an describes the same quality elsewhere: the drink of paradise causes neither headache nor intoxication (56:19). The negation of harm is part of the description of the pleasure, not an afterthought to it.

**“For What You Used to Do” — The Same Formula as the Fire**

The clause *bi-mā kuntum taʿmalūn* is identical in form to the justification given to the condemned in verse sixteen. The same words explain both outcomes, and that identity is the sūrah's central claim about justice.

The reward is not arbitrary generosity and the punishment is not arbitrary severity. Both correspond to conduct, and the correspondence is stated in the same grammar. What differs is the conduct, not the method.

The continuous past form appears again: what you used to do. The reward corresponds to a pattern of action sustained over time, which is why the invitation is not a one-off but the standing condition of the place.

**The Same Sentence in Two Other Chapters**

The wording is not unique. In al-Ḥāqqah the righteous are told: “Eat and drink joyfully for what you did in the days gone by” (69:24). In al-Mursalāt the same words appear verbatim: “Eat and drink happily for what you used to do” (77:43).

The three versions differ in one respect worth noticing. Al-Ḥāqqah adds “in the days gone by,” which places the deeds explicitly in the past and the reward explicitly after them. This verse and al-Mursalāt leave the timeframe to the tense of the verb.

The repetition is a feature of Meccan style. The scene is so central to the message that it is restated in different chapters with almost no variation, like a refrain in a liturgy. The listener who has heard one version will recognise the others.

**Eating and Drinking Without Need**

In this world eating is compelled by hunger and drinking by thirst. The Qur'an's descriptions of paradise keep the forms of eating and drinking while removing the compulsion, and the removal changes the meaning of the act.

The rivers described in the Qur'an are of water, milk, wine and honey — none of them necessary for survival in that setting (47:15). Fruit is available in whatever variety is wanted, and the meat is of whatever bird is desired (56:20–21). The absence of scarcity means the act is pure pleasure rather than relief.

That is what the invitation conveys. Eat and drink are verbs of need in ordinary life; spoken in the Gardens they are verbs of enjoyment, and the adverb *hanīʾan* confirms that no need or harm is attached to them.

**A Public Address to a Company**

The command is plural, and the following verses describe the setting as communal: thrones lined up, cups passed around, servants moving among the guests, people turning to one another in conversation.

The plurality matters. The reward is not distributed to isolated individuals who then enjoy it separately. It is a shared occasion, and the sharing is part of the reward. The Qur'an removes from this company the thing that poisons company in this world: the verse in Sūrah al-Ḥijr says whatever bitterness was in their hearts will be taken out, and they will be on thrones facing one another in friendship (15:47).

An invitation spoken to a group creates a group. The first words heard in the Gardens are words that make the hearers into a company.

**Two Speeches, One Chapter**

The sūrah contains two addresses from beyond, and placing them side by side is the fastest way to see what the chapter is doing.

The first says: this is the fire you denied, is it magic, burn in it, patience and impatience are the same, you are paid for what you did. The second says: eat and drink happily for what you did.

Both speeches end on conduct. Neither ends on grace or on wrath as an abstraction. The difference between the two addresses is entirely a difference in what the people addressed had been doing, and that is the whole of the sūrah's warning to the living.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:20
> They will be reclining on thrones, ˹neatly˺ lined up ˹facing each other˺. And We will pair them to maidens with gorgeous eyes

**Expanded Commentary**

**Reclining Is the Opposite of Being Shoved**

The verse opens with a posture. *Muttakiʾīn* describes a person leaning back on his arm or on a cushion, the position of someone who has nowhere to go and nothing pressing. It is the posture of a guest at a banquet, not of a traveller or a worker.

The contrast with the earlier part of this sūrah is exact. The deniers were described with a verb of violent motion — shoved, driven, unable to stop. The righteous are described with a participle of rest. The chapter divides humanity by what their bodies are doing as much as by where they end up.

Rest in the Qur'an's paradise is not idleness. It is the end of striving, and the end of striving is only a good thing for someone whose striving was directed somewhere. The people reclining here are the ones who took precautions earlier, and the reclining is what precaution buys.

**Surur: Thrones in Rows**

The word for thrones, *surur*, is the plural of *sarīr*. The Qur'an describes them elsewhere as jewelled thrones (56:15) and embroidered couches (56:15 in another reading of the same scene), and it describes their arrangement as face to face (56:16).

The participle *maṣfūfa* here means arranged in rows, set in order. The image is not of scattered seats but of an ordered assembly, like a dining hall where the places have been laid out.

There is a verbal resonance that the classical commentators noticed. The word for throne shares a root with *surūr*, joy, and with *sirr*, a secret. Al-Qushayrī glossed the reclining on couches as remaining in joy and felicity, and joy was defined by some as delight that stays hidden. The throne in this reading is a seat of joy, and the word carries that meaning without stating it.

**Facing One Another**

The arrangement has a social purpose, and the Qur'an states it in another sūrah: whatever bitterness was in their hearts will be removed, and they will be on thrones facing one another in a friendly manner (15:47). Elsewhere the righteous are described as facing each other on thrones (37:44) and reclining face to face (56:16).

Facing is a deliberate detail. People who resent each other sit side by side or apart; people at peace sit opposite. The removal of bitterness is what makes the facing possible, and the Qur'an names the removal first.

The contrast with the Fire is direct: close friends will be enemies to one another on that Day, except the righteous (43:67). The relationships that survive the day are the ones described here, and the seating arrangement is their visible form.

**“We Will Pair Them” — The Verb of Marriage**

The verb *zawwajnāhum* is the verb used for marrying one person to another. It is not a verb of possession or of service; it is the word used for a union with a bond attached.

The same promise appears in ad-Dukhān: so it will be, and We will pair them to maidens with gorgeous eyes (44:54). In ar-Raḥmān the description is of maidens reserved in pavilions (55:72), and in al-Wāqiʿah they are likened to pristine pearls (56:22–23).

The pairing completes the picture begun with the thrones. A banquet with rows of seats facing one another is a company; a company in which each person is paired is a settled society. The verse moves from rest, to order, to relationship, in three phrases.

**Ḥūr ʿīn: The Words and What They Mean**

The phrase is usually translated as maidens with gorgeous eyes, and both words are physical descriptors. *Ḥūr* is the plural of *ḥawrāʾ*, describing an intense contrast between the very white and the very dark of the eye — a quality prized in classical Arabic poetry as a mark of beauty. *ʿĪn* is the plural of *ʿaynāʾ*, a wide, full eye.

The description is therefore specific rather than vague. It names a feature of the face rather than an abstract attractiveness, in the manner of Arabic description generally, which prefers a particular to a general term.

The Qur'an's comparison for them is pearls, hidden and pristine (56:23), an image that carries both value and seclusion. The descriptions consistently emphasise what is protected and unspoiled rather than what is displayed.

**Images Drawn From the Highest Comfort They Knew**

Every element in this verse is borrowed from the best that the first audience could imagine: thrones, ordered rows, a company seated face to face, spouses, pearls. Nothing in it comes from a world outside their experience, and that is a deliberate feature of Qur'anic description rather than a limitation.

The description is also explicitly incomplete. The Prophet ﷺ reported that God said: I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and what has never crossed the mind of man (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3072; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2824). The Qur'an states the same idea without the metaphor: “No soul can imagine what delights are kept in store for them as a reward for what they used to do” (32:17).

The two statements together define how to read the thrones. They are pointers toward a reality that exceeds them, chosen because a description entirely beyond human reference would convey nothing at all. The verse gives enough to want and not enough to contain, and the gap between the two is where the promise actually lives.

**A Social State, Not Only a Reward**

Read as a whole, the verse describes a community. Rest, ordered seating, mutual facing, pairing. Each element is relational, and none of them is a possession in the ordinary sense.

That reading fits the surrounding verses. The next verses describe fruit and meat provided continually, a drink passed from one to another that produces no idle talk and no sin, youthful servants like pearls, and conversation between the seated guests about how they used to fear God among their people.

The picture is of a settled company at ease with one another. The thrones and the pairing are not decorations added to individual bliss; they are the structure of the bliss, which in this sūrah is consistently described as something people have together.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:21
> As for those who believe and whose descendants follow them in faith, We will elevate their descendants to their rank, never discounting anything ˹of the reward˺ of their deeds. Every person will reap only what they sowed

**Expanded Commentary**

**Families Are Joined by Rank, Not Only by Merit**

The verse addresses a specific fear: that a parent who reaches a high station will be separated from children who reached a lower one. The promise removes it. The descendants who followed in faith are raised to the rank of their parents.

The verb *alḥaqnā*, We joined, means to attach one thing to another so that they are together. The joining is upward — the lower is brought to the higher — and the text is explicit that the difference in deeds is not the obstacle it appears to be.

That is a statement about the nature of the reward. If paradise were only a wage, ranks would separate families permanently. The verse says the ranks are real and the families are kept together anyway, which can only happen if something other than wages is operating.

**The Report Behind the Promise**

A statement attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās explains the verse directly: God elevates the ranks of the believers' offspring to the rank of their parents even though the offspring did not perform as well as the parents, so that the parents' eyes are comforted — and he then recited this verse. The report is recorded by al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Abī Ḥātim through Sufyān ath-Thawrī, and some have attributed the same wording to the Prophet ﷺ rather than to Ibn ʿAbbās; the attribution to the Companion is the better supported.

A related report in Musnad Aḥmad, from Abū Hurayrah, describes the reverse direction: God raises a righteous servant's rank in Paradise, and when the servant asks how he earned it, the answer is that his son prayed for his forgiveness. Ibn Kathīr judged that report's chain authentic while noting it is not in the Ṣaḥīḥ in that wording.

Both reports make the same point from opposite ends: the family is joined, and the joining is a comfort rather than an accounting adjustment.

**Nothing Is Discounted**

The verse closes the loophole the promise might open. If the child is raised, does the parent pay? *Wa mā alatnāhum min ʿamalihim min shayʾ* — We did not deprive them of anything of their deeds.

The negation is doubled in Arabic for emphasis, and it answers the objection before it is raised. The elevation of the child is not a transfer from the parent's account. Nothing is deducted, nothing is shared out, nothing is reduced.

That detail matters theologically. A reward system in which one person's gain is another's loss would not be a gift but a redistribution, and the verse is describing a gift. The generosity is additive on both sides, which is a claim about God's capacity rather than about human merit.

**The Condition Is Faith, Not Blood**

The elevation applies to descendants who followed *in faith*. The phrase is not incidental; it is the condition that keeps the promise from becoming a doctrine of inherited salvation.

The verse therefore excludes the child of a believer who did not believe. Lineage is not the mechanism; faith is, and faith is the one thing a parent cannot supply for a child. The verse is generous without being hereditary.

This is consistent with the Qur'an's treatment of family elsewhere. Noah's son is drowned despite his father's pleading, and the plea is refused on the grounds that he is not of the family in the relevant sense (11:45–46). Belonging is defined by belief in both chapters.

**Every Person Is Held in Pledge**

The last clause changes register: every person will reap only what they sowed. The Arabic uses *rahīn*, held in pledge or pawned, from the same root as *rahn*, a mortgage.

The image is exact. A debt binds the debtor until it is paid, and a person's deeds bind the person until they are settled. The parallel verse in Sūrah al-Muddaththir states it flatly: every soul will be detained for what it has done (74:38). In an-Najm the same principle appears in the language of striving: each person will only have what they endeavoured towards (53:39).

The clause sits here as a counterweight. The verse has just described an exception — a rank granted beyond the deeds — and it immediately restates the rule, so that the exception is understood as an exception.

**Grace and Justice in a Single Verse**

The two halves of the verse are in tension, and the tension is the teaching. The first half describes a favour given beyond merit; the second states that every person is bound to what he earned.

The resolution is in the direction of the favour. What is given is company, not credit. The child does not receive the parent's reward; the child is brought to where the parent is. The parent's account is untouched, and the rule that each soul is bound to its own deeds remains intact.

This distinction runs through the Qur'an's eschatology. Intercession, elevation, and companionship are all forms of favour that operate without violating the correspondence between deed and outcome. The verse states both principles in one breath so that neither is mistaken for the whole truth.

**The Fear the Verse Answers**

Separation from family is one of the losses the Qur'an names on the day of judgment, and it names it twice: kinship will not exist on that day, and people will not even ask about one another (23:101). The righteous are shown the opposite, and the opposite is described in domestic terms.

The people in this verse are not solitary. They are parents with children, and the reward includes the family that the earlier part of the sūrah showed being seated together on thrones. The promise is that the seating arrangement continues, and that it is not broken by differences in rank.

For the first listeners — a small community whose families were split by belief — this was not an abstract comfort. It was an answer to a question they were living with.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:22
> And We will ˹continually˺ provide them with whatever fruit or meat they desire

**Expanded Commentary**

**Provision That Does Not Run Out**

The verb *amdadnāhum* comes from a root meaning to extend or to supply continuously — the image is of a supply line that keeps feeding a thing rather than a single delivery. The same verb is used for the extension of a term or the stretching out of a shade.

Applied to food, it changes the picture completely. A gift is given once; a supply is maintained. The verse describes the second, and the bracketed insertion in the translation captures it. The inhabitants of the Gardens are not handed a feast and left to manage it; they are being fed on an ongoing basis.

That is the difference between a reward and a household. What this verse describes is a household in which the resident never has to think about provision again, and the freedom from that thought is itself part of the reward.

**Desire Sets the Menu**

The clause *mimmā yashtahūn* — from whatever they desire — places appetite in the position that scarcity occupies in this world. Here the limit on food is supply; there the limit is removed and desire determines what appears.

The Qur'an states the same arrangement elsewhere: any fruit they choose, and meat from any bird they desire (56:20–21), and there they will have fruits and whatever they desire (36:57). In al-Mursalāt the phrasing is shorter: and any fruit they desire (77:42).

The repetition is worth noticing because it answers a practical question about paradise: what happens when appetite has no external constraint? The answer given is that appetite becomes the ordering principle, and nothing bad follows from it — a point developed in the verses that follow.

**Fruit and Meat Were Feast Foods**

The two items named are not staples. In the Arabian world of the revelation, grain and dates were ordinary food; fruit of other kinds and meat were what appeared at a feast, at a wedding, or at the table of a host with something to give.

Naming those two, and naming them as continuously supplied, describes a permanent feast rather than a permanent ration. The choice is deliberate: the Qur'an describes paradise in the vocabulary of generosity rather than sufficiency.

There is a social dimension to this. Meat in that setting was shared food, slaughtered for guests. A place where meat is continually supplied is a place where hospitality never stops, which fits the surrounding verses about cups passed around and servants moving between the guests.

**Desire Purified Rather Than Suppressed**

The word *yashtahūn* is from *shahwa*, appetite or craving, and the Qur'an uses it elsewhere for cravings that need restraint. Here the same word is used without qualification and without consequence.

The difference is not that appetite disappears but that its setting changes. In this world appetite operates inside scarcity, competition, and bodily harm; eating what one desires can destroy health, and desiring what another has produces conflict. The verse removes the scarcity, and the surrounding verses remove the harm.

That is the Qur'an's consistent method in describing the Hereafter: it keeps the human faculties and removes what corrupts their exercise. The same method appears with wine, with speech, and with family.

**What the Verse Adds to the Scene**

Read in sequence, the description has been building. The righteous are in Gardens and bliss. They enjoy what their Lord gave them and are shielded from the Fire. They are invited to eat and drink. They recline on thrones lined up, facing one another, paired with spouses. Now the food itself is described, and how it comes.

Each verse adds a layer without repeating the previous one. The invitation in verse nineteen was a welcome; this verse is the mechanism. The thrones were the setting; this is what happens at them.

The accumulation is what makes the description concrete. Paradise in this sūrah is not a mood but a place with furniture, company, and service, and the specificity is the point of the description. A reward described only as bliss can be imagined in any shape a person prefers; a reward described as continuous supply of chosen food has to be pictured as a household, and a household is something the reader already understands.

**The Contrast With What the Others Are Given**

The sūrah has already described the provision of the Fire, and the contrast is exact in reverse. There the inhabitants are told to burn, and their drink is a scorching wind and boiling water; elsewhere the Qur'an describes a drink of boiling water that tears apart the insides (47:15).

Both descriptions are about food and drink, and both use the language of what the person receives. The difference is not in the category but in the content, which is the sūrah's method throughout: two outcomes described in the same terms so that the comparison is unavoidable.

The provision here is continual, chosen, and harmless. The provision there is compulsory, painful, and endless. Nothing further needs to be said about which is which.

**A Household, Not a Banquet**

The final effect of the verse is to describe residence. A banquet ends; a supply does not. The continuous aspect of the verb turns the scene from an occasion into a home.

That matters for how the whole passage should be read. The thrones, the cups, the servants, the spouses and now the food are not decorations added to a spiritual state; they are the furniture of a settled life. The Qur'an's paradise is repeatedly described with the vocabulary of dwelling — settled in the Home of Everlasting Stay (35:35) — and this verse supplies the detail that makes dwelling possible.

The next verse describes what happens when the food and drink are in hand and the company is seated: the cup goes around, and with it something is absent that the world cannot remove from any feast.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:23
> They will pass around to each other a drink ˹of pure wine,˺ which leads to no idle talk or sinfulness

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cup Goes Around**

The verb *yataṭāwafūn* is from a root meaning to circle or go around, and it is used for circling a house as well as for moving among people. Here it describes a cup being passed from hand to hand, with the company arranged in a ring or in rows facing one another.

The detail is social rather than gastronomic. What the verse describes is circulation: nobody drinks alone, and nobody is served in isolation. The cup is the thing that moves between the seated guests, and its movement is what turns a group of individuals into a company.

That fits the arrangement described in the previous verse — thrones lined up, facing each other. The seating makes the passing possible, and the passing makes the seating meaningful.

**What Is Not in the Cup**

The second half of the verse is a negation: no idle talk and no sinfulness. *Laghw* is empty speech, the talk that goes nowhere; *taʾthīm* comes from a root meaning sin, and the commentators read it as covering false speech, accusation, and incitement to wrongdoing.

The negation is the heart of the verse. Wine in the world of the revelation was associated with exactly these two things: gatherings that dissolved into abuse and boasting, and behaviour that produced sin. The verse keeps the drink and removes its effects, and it removes them by name rather than by implication.

The parallel verse in al-Wāqiʿah states the same removal from the bodily side: a drink that causes neither headache nor intoxication (56:19). In aṣ-Ṣāffāt the wine of paradise is described as crystal-white, delicious to drink, and neither harmful nor intoxicating (37:45–47).

**Wine Forbidden Here and Promised There**

The Qur'an prohibits wine in this world in unambiguous terms, classing it with gambling as an abomination of Satan's handiwork (5:90). It promises wine in the next. The two statements are not in tension once the descriptions are read.

What is prohibited is a substance with specific effects: intoxication, harm, enmity, and the loss of prayer. What is promised is a drink described by the absence of every one of those effects — no headache, no intoxication, no idle talk, no sin. The name is shared; the thing is not.

The Qur'an makes the distinction itself when it describes the rivers of paradise as including rivers of wine delicious to drink, listed beside water, milk and honey (47:15). A river of wine that can be drunk without consequence is not the wine that is forbidden.

**Two Removals, and Why Both Are Named**

Idle talk and sinfulness are different failures, and the verse removes both. Idle talk wastes a gathering without harming anyone. Sinful talk harms.

The pairing is instructive because it describes the two ways a feast goes wrong. A group can be ruined by malice or ruined by triviality, and the world's gatherings usually contain both. The verse describes a company from which both have been removed, which is why the following verses can describe the conversation of the seated guests as worth recording.

In al-Wāqiʿah the same idea is stated from the other side: there they will never hear any idle or sinful talk, only good and virtuous speech (56:25–26). The negation and the affirmation belong to the same description.

**The Contrast With the Wading of Verse Twelve**

The deniers earlier in this sūrah were described as wading and playing — immersed in empty talk and amused by it. The word for what they were doing is close in sense to the *laghw* removed here.

The contrast is precise and it is not accidental. The same substance — talk — is what ruins one gathering and what fills the other. In the Fire the talk is blame and disowning; here it is gratitude and recollection, described in the verses that follow.

The verse therefore does more than describe a drink. It states the difference between a company held together by emptiness and a company held together by something worth saying.

**Passed, Not Owned**

The cup is not the property of any one guest. It circulates, and the circulation is described with a verb of movement rather than possession.

That detail carries a meaning about the nature of the reward. Nothing in this scene is held against another person; nothing is rationed; nobody's share depends on somebody else's loss. The same principle appeared two verses earlier, where the elevation of the children was explicitly stated not to reduce the parents' reward.

The Qur'an's paradise is consistently described as a place without competition, and the small details are how it says so. A cup that goes around is a cup nobody needs to guard.

**The Image the Hearers Already Knew**

The cup was already a loaded image for the first audience. Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry had a well-developed tradition of describing wine, the drinking session, the cupbearer and the morning after, and the vocabulary of that tradition was common property.

The Qur'an takes the image and changes what happens inside it. The cup is there, the company is there, the circulation is there. What has been removed is everything the tradition associated with the scene: the quarrel, the boast, the loss of control, the regret.

That method is more persuasive than rejection would have been. A hearer who loved the poetry recognises the scene and notices immediately what is missing from it. The verse does not argue that worldly drinking is corrupt; it shows a drinking session with the corruption taken out, and lets the comparison do the work.

**A Feast Without a Morning After**

Every feast in the world ends, and the ending is part of what makes it a feast. This one is described with a continuous verb of provision in the previous verse and a circling verb here, and no ending is mentioned anywhere in the passage.

What makes that possible is the removal of harm. A pleasure that damages cannot be repeated indefinitely; a pleasure that does not damage can. The verse's negations are therefore not moral decoration but the condition of permanence.

The description is making a claim about why the world's pleasures end: not because they are pleasures, but because of what is mixed into them. Remove the mixture and the pleasure does not have to stop.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:24
> And they will be waited on by their youthful servants like spotless pearls

**Expanded Commentary**

**Servants Described as Jewels**

The verse names the people who serve and then gives a comparison instead of a description. *Ghilmān*, youths, and the simile is a hidden pearl. Nothing is said about their faces or their clothes; the image does the work.

A pearl is the one jewel that is not cut or shaped. It comes out of its shell already formed, and its value lies in its surface being untouched. The comparison therefore says something about the state of these servants rather than about their appearance: nothing about them has been worn, marked, or used up.

That is the reverse of what service does to a servant in this world. The verse describes attendance without the damage that attendance normally causes, and the simile is how it says so in two words.

**Maknūn: Stored, Covered, Protected**

The participle *maknūn* means stored away or kept under cover — a pearl still in its shell, or one kept in a box, untouched by hands and air. The same root gives *kinān*, a covering.

The word choice is precise because an exposed pearl loses its lustre. A pearl that has been handled is worth less than one that has not. By choosing the covered pearl, the verse describes the highest possible condition of the thing compared.

The Qur'an uses the same image for the spouses of paradise in another chapter: like pristine, well-protected pearls (56:22–23). The repetition of the simile across two different descriptions suggests it is doing consistent work: purity that has never been exposed to what spoils it.

**Eternal Youths Elsewhere**

Two other passages describe the same attendants, and together they fill in what this verse leaves as an image. In al-Wāqiʿah they are eternal youths who circle among the inhabitants with cups, pitchers, and a drink from a flowing stream (56:17–18). In al-Insān immortal youths will circulate among them with vessels of silver and cups of crystal (76:19).

The word for eternal in the first of those is *mukhalladūn*, from a root meaning to be made to last. The description is of youths who do not age, and the absence of ageing is what makes the pearl comparison work.

All three passages use verbs of circulation. The attendants are not standing at attention; they are moving, and the movement is part of the scene described in the previous verse, where the cup is passed around.

**Service Without Servitude**

There is a real transformation here, and it is worth stating plainly. The Qur'an's paradise contains service, and the service is described in the language of beauty rather than subjection.

In this world a servant is defined by need: he serves because he must. The attendants described here are not defined by need at all; they are described as jewels, and jewels do not lack anything. What they provide is attendance, and attendance in this setting is closer to honour than to labour.

The same transformation applies to the rest of the scene. Eating without hunger, drinking without intoxication, sitting without striving, and being served without anyone being diminished. The forms of worldly life are kept and their burdens removed.

**Beauty as a Category of the Reward**

The Qur'an's descriptions of paradise include beauty as a distinct good, not merely as decoration. The garments are of fine silk and brocade (44:53), the thrones are jewelled (56:15), the vessels are of silver and crystal (76:15–16), and the attendants are compared to pearls.

This matters because it settles a question about the nature of the reward. The good in paradise is not only moral or spiritual; it includes what is pleasing to look at, and the Qur'an states that without embarrassment.

The descriptions are also modest in a particular way. The comparison used here is a covered jewel rather than a displayed one, which is characteristic of the Qur'an's aesthetic vocabulary: beauty that is protected rather than exhibited.

**“For Them” — Whose Servants Are They**

The phrase *lahum* — for them — is attached to the youths, and the commentators have drawn two readings from it. The plain reading is possession in the sense of assignment: these are attendants appointed for the inhabitants. The other reading, noted by ar-Rāzī, is that they are the inhabitants' own children, serving their parents as an honour rather than as a task.

The second reading is speculative, and the verse does not require it. What it does show is that the phrase carries weight: the attendants are not a general staff but are assigned to particular people, which keeps the scene personal rather than institutional.

Either way the description is of service that belongs to someone, and of a household rather than a hall.

**A Commodity the Audience Actually Handled**

The comparison would not work with an unknown object, and pearls were not unknown. The Qur'an lists them among what comes out of the sea: out of both waters come forth pearls and coral (55:22). Elsewhere it names the ornaments extracted from the sea as one of the benefits of subjecting it to human use (16:14), and repeats the detail in a comparison of two kinds of water (35:12).

Pearls were trade goods on the Arabian coast, handled by divers and merchants, and their value was common knowledge. Choosing that object for the simile means the hearer knew the standard being applied: an unmarked pearl is worth more than a marked one, and everyone in the room knew it.

There is a further resonance. This sūrah opened by swearing on a sea set on fire. The same sea that will burn is the sea that produces the pearl used to describe the attendants of paradise, and the verse thereby ties the end of the chapter back to its opening oath.

**The Scene Complete**

Taken with the verses around it, the picture is now fully assembled. Thrones in rows, faces turned toward one another, spouses paired, food supplied continually, a cup circulating, and attendants moving among the guests like pearls.

Every element in that list has a worldly counterpart, and every one of them has been altered in the same direction: kept in form, freed from cost. That consistency is what makes the description persuasive rather than merely ornamental.

The next verse changes the register from description to speech. The company has been seated, fed, and served, and now it begins to talk — and the subject of the conversation is the life these people left behind.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:25
> They will turn to one another inquisitively

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Short Verse That Opens a Conversation**

This is one of the shortest verses in the passage, and it does a single job: it makes the company speak. Everything before it described a scene; everything after it records words.

The verb *aqbala baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin* means they turned toward one another, from a root meaning to come forward or approach. The turning is physical, and it presupposes the seating described three verses earlier — people arranged facing one another can turn toward each other without moving.

The second verb, *yatasāʾalūn*, is a reciprocal form: they question one another, each asking and each being asked. The conversation is not a lecture from one to the rest but an exchange, and the reciprocal form is what tells us that.

**Memory Survives, and It Is the Subject**

The verses that follow show what they are asking about, and the answer is their own past: we used to be in awe of God among our people, and God graced us and protected us. The conversation is autobiographical.

That has a consequence worth stating. The inhabitants of the Gardens remember their earthly lives, including the fear they felt in them. Paradise does not erase memory; it changes what memory does. The same events, recalled from safety, produce gratitude rather than distress.

The Qur'an describes a related removal elsewhere: whatever bitterness was in their hearts will be taken out (15:47). Bitterness is removed; recollection is not. The distinction is what makes the conversation in this verse possible.

**What the Others Cannot Do**

The verse gains its force from its opposite, which the Qur'an states elsewhere. When the Trumpet is blown, there will be no kinship between people on that day, and they will not even ask about one another (23:101). In another verse the wrongdoers are described as blind and dumb on that day, unable to speak (28:66 in context of their silencing).

The contrast is between two companies. One cannot speak to itself; the other cannot stop. The difference is not in sociability but in the state of the relationships — where every bond has become an accusation, speech stops, and where every bond has been purified, speech flows.

This verse therefore belongs to a pattern in the sūrah: each feature of the reward is the inverse of a feature of the punishment already described.

**A Parallel Scene With More Detail**

Sūrah aṣ-Ṣāffāt records the same conversation at greater length. There too the people of paradise turn to one another inquisitively (37:50), and one of them says: I once had a companion in the world (37:51), who used to argue with him about resurrection. The speaker then looks down and sees that companion in the middle of the Fire, and addresses him (37:54–57).

That scene confirms what this verse implies: the questioning includes the fate of people who were close in the world and are not present in the Gardens. The verse here is compact where aṣ-Ṣāffāt is narrative, but both describe the same act — a company recalling its former life out loud.

The earlier verses in aṣ-Ṣāffāt also describe the same turning as blame among the wrongdoers: they will turn on each other, throwing blame (37:27). The same verb of turning produces two different conversations.

**Questioning as a Form of Gratitude**

The content of the exchange, given in the next three verses, is not complaint or comparison. It is an account of fear, then grace, then prayer.

That shape tells us what kind of conversation this is. Gratitude in the Qur'an is frequently expressed as narration — telling what happened, in order, with the cause named. The speakers in this passage do exactly that, and the questioning is the form the narration takes in a group.

The humility is notable. They do not say they earned this; they say they were afraid and were then shown favour. The conversation in paradise, on this account, is a mutual recitation of grace rather than a comparison of achievements.

**Why the Sūrah Records the Conversation**

A description could have ended with the thrones and the pearls. The sūrah goes on to record speech, and the reason is that the reward it is describing is not a set of objects but a state of a community.

The objects — thrones, cups, food, attendants — are described in four verses. The conversation gets four as well. The balance is deliberate: paradise in aṭ-Ṭūr is as much a company as it is a place, and a company is constituted by what its members say to each other.

This also answers an implicit objection to the earlier descriptions. A place of endless eating and reclining could be read as mere pleasure. A place where people talk about their fear of God cannot.

**Questions That Cross Differences of Rank**

The sūrah has already stated that the people gathered here are not of one station. Verse twenty-one described descendants being raised to the rank of their parents because the parents' deeds exceeded the children's. Ranks exist, and they are real.

The conversation in this verse happens across those differences. The reciprocal verb says that each questions the others, which means the exchange is not one-directional from higher to lower or from elder to junior. A company in which only one rank speaks is a hierarchy; a company in which all speak is a community.

That detail completes the promise of verse twenty-one. The families are joined, and the joining is shown here in operation: people of different degrees sitting facing one another and talking to one another as equals in speech, however unequal in rank.

**The Verse Turns the Chapter Inward**

Structurally this short line does something important. The first movement of the sūrah was public and accusatory — oaths, a day, a push, a taunt. The second movement described a reward in objects. This verse moves the focus to the interior of the rewarded people: what they remember, what they say, what they credit.

The shift prepares the turn that comes at verse twenty-nine, when the sūrah addresses the Prophet ﷺ directly and then answers his accusers. Before that turn, the sūrah wants the reader to have heard the voice of people who made it — speaking not about their reward but about the fear that preceded it.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:26
> They will say, “Before ˹this reward˺ we used to be in awe ˹of Allah˺ in the midst of our people

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Sentence Spoken in the Gardens**

Of everything these people could say, this is what they say: we used to be afraid. Not we were righteous, not we suffered, not we were right and our opponents were wrong. The opening of the conversation is a confession of fear.

That ordering is deliberate. The verse places the emotion of the worldly life at the head of the heavenly speech, which tells the reader what the Qur'an considers the substance of a believing life. It is not achievement. It is the state of being continually aware that something is at stake.

The phrase *min qablu*, before, marks the distance. The fear belongs to a time that has ended, and the speakers know it has ended, which is why they can speak of it calmly.

**Mushfiqīn: Fear With Concern Attached**

The word translated as in awe is *mushfiqūn*, from *ishfāq*. The term is not the word for terror; it describes fear mixed with solicitude — the fear of someone who cares about the outcome and is therefore attentive to it.

The Qur'an uses the same word for the believers who give charity while their hearts are fearful that it may not be accepted (23:60). That usage fixes the sense: the fear is not paralysing, it is motivating. The person who is *mushfiq* does something about what he fears.

A different word would have changed the meaning. Terror produces flight; *ishfāq* produces care. The verse describes people who were careful, and the care is what brought them here.

**“In the Midst of Our People” — The Cost Was Social**

The phrase *fī ahlinā*, among our families, is the most historically specific detail in the verse. The fear was not private. It was experienced inside a community, in front of relatives and neighbours, and it carried a social price.

For the first listeners this was a description of their own situation. The early Muslims in Mecca were a minority inside their own clans; belief divided households, and the division was felt at the level of meals, marriages, and protection. To be visibly afraid of God among people who mocked the fear was an expensive position.

The verse names that expense without exaggerating it. It does not list persecutions or injuries; it says we were in awe among our people, and leaves the reader to know what that cost.

**Humility in the Grammar**

The sentence credits nothing to the speakers. They were afraid — a state, not an accomplishment — and the next verse will attribute the outcome entirely to God's grace.

That humility is characteristic of the Qur'an's accounts of the successful. The people who reach the Gardens consistently describe themselves in terms of deficiency and God in terms of generosity. The parallel speech in another sūrah says the same thing: praise be to God, who has kept away from us all causes of sorrow (35:34).

The absence of self-congratulation is not modesty as a manner. It is an accurate account. The verse has already been preceded by the statement that no one is saved by his deeds alone, and the speakers here are describing exactly that.

**Fear in the Past Tense**

The verb is in the past, and the tense is doing work. The fear that defined their earthly life is over, and the verse records it as a completed state rather than a permanent condition.

This answers a real question about the religious life. Is the fear meant to last forever, or is it a phase? The verse says it belongs to a time and a place — before, among our people — and that it has an end. The people speaking are no longer afraid of anything, and the sūrah has already told us why: their Lord shielded them from the torment of the Fire.

The removal of fear is a named feature of the reward elsewhere: no fear will be upon them, nor will they grieve (2:38). What this verse adds is the memory of the fear, retained without its pain.

**Fear Was the Precaution**

The people described in verse seventeen were the *muttaqūn*, those who shielded themselves. This verse describes what that shielding felt like from the inside: awe among one's own people.

The connection is exact. *Taqwā* is precaution, and precaution has an emotional content, which is *ishfāq*. The verse supplies the interior of the word used earlier, and it locates that interior in a social setting rather than in solitude.

That location matters. Precaution taken alone is easy; precaution taken while surrounded by people who think it unnecessary is the thing the verse commends. The speakers are describing not fear but fear maintained under social pressure.

**The Fear of God and the Fear of People**

The verse names one fear and implies its rival. To be in awe of God among people who do not share the awe means living with two competing fears at once, and the Qur'an is direct about which one must win.

The warning at Uḥud was framed in exactly those terms: that was only Satan trying to make you fear his followers, so do not fear them — fear Me, if you are true believers (3:175). The prophets are described as those who deliver the messages of God and fear none but Him (33:39), and the believers who maintain the mosques are those who fear none but Allah (9:18).

The speakers in this verse are people who made that choice and kept it inside their own families, which is the hardest setting for it. Their fear of God did not disappear when it became socially expensive, and the verse records that fact as the substance of their life rather than as an achievement.

**A Condition Still Available**

The verse is a record of something said in paradise, and it functions as a description of something available now. The fear it names can be felt by anyone reading it, in the midst of any people, at any time.

That is how the Qur'an's accounts of the Hereafter work. They are not reports about other people so much as descriptions of states the reader can occupy, with the outcome attached. The speakers here are identified by a condition, and conditions can be adopted.

The next verse gives the other half of the sentence — what God did about the fear — and the two halves together form the shortest complete account of salvation in this sūrah.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:27
> So Allah has graced us and protected us from the torment of ˹Hell’s˺ scorching heat

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Fear**

The previous verse named the fear; this verse names what ended it. The conjunction *fa* makes the sequence immediate — we were afraid, and God was gracious to us. Nothing intervenes between the two.

The structure of the sentence is worth noting because it excludes other agents. They do not say that their people relented, that circumstances improved, or that they found a way out. They say God graced us. The fear ended by an act from outside themselves.

That exclusion is the theological content of the verse. A person who is afraid in the midst of a hostile community cannot remove the danger by his own effort; he can only avoid what he is afraid of. What the verse describes is the removal of the danger itself.

**Two Verbs: Grace and Shielding**

The verse contains two acts, and they are different in kind. *Manna ʿalaynā* — He was gracious to us, from a root meaning to bestow a favour. *Wa waqānā* — He shielded us, from *waqā*, to guard or protect.

The first describes a gift; the second describes a defence. The gift is unqualified — no object is named, so it covers everything given. The defence has a specific object: the torment of the scorching heat.

The verb *waqā* is the same root as *taqwā* and as *muttaqūn* in verse seventeen. The people who shielded themselves were shielded in return, and the same root describes both actions. That verbal echo is the sūrah's whole account of the transaction: precaution on one side, protection on the other.

**Samūm: The Wind That Enters the Pores**

The word translated as scorching heat is *samūm*, and its etymology is exact. It comes from *masām*, the pores of the skin, and describes a hot wind so fine that it penetrates the body through them.

The image is from desert experience. The *samūm* is not merely hot air; it is a wind that cannot be kept out, that gets inside clothing and inside the body. There is no shelter from it, because it enters through the skin itself.

The Qur'an uses the same word for the condition of the companions of the left in al-Wāqiʿah: they will be in scorching heat and boiling water, and in the shade of black smoke that provides neither coolness nor shelter (56:42–44). The three elements together describe a punishment with no relief — heat that enters, water that burns, and a shade that does not shade.

**Why Heat Rather Than Fire**

The Fire has already been described in this sūrah, in detail, with its door and its keepers. Here the speakers name one of its torments rather than the place itself.

The choice is psychologically precise. A person who has escaped a danger does not usually name the danger in general terms; he names the part of it he was most afraid of. The heat that enters through the pores is the aspect of the Fire that no strength or endurance could resist, and it is the aspect they mention.

That specificity also connects the verse to the one before it. They were afraid, and what they were afraid of is named here for the first time. The fear was not vague; it had an object.

**Grace Named Before Protection**

The order of the two verbs places the favour first. They were given something before they were saved from something, and the order suggests that the protection is part of the favour rather than separate from it.

This matches the description in verse eighteen, where the giving and the shielding are both attributed to their Lord in a single verse. The two acts are never separated in this sūrah, and neither is presented as the whole of the reward.

The order also corrects a common way of speaking about salvation. A person delivered from danger tends to describe the deliverance and forget the gift. These speakers begin with the gift.

**The Same Protection Promised and Now Confirmed**

Verse eighteen stated the protection in the third person as a promise: their Lord shielded them from the torment of the Hellfire. This verse states it in the first person as an experience: He shielded us.

The change of voice is the sūrah's way of confirming its own promise. What was announced has now been reported by someone who received it, and the report uses the same verb.

That device is used throughout the Qur'an. A promise is made, and later a voice from inside the fulfilled promise describes what it was like. The reader is given both the announcement and the testimony, which is a stronger form of assurance than either alone.

**The Shade That Does Not Shade**

The word *samūm* appears in a passage that completes the picture. The people of the left are told to proceed into a shade of smoke rising in three columns, providing neither coolness nor shelter from the flames (77:30–31), and al-Wāqiʿah describes the same three elements: scorching heat, boiling water, and a shade of black smoke (56:42–44).

The three elements are chosen to remove every form of relief. Heat that enters the body cannot be escaped by moving. Water that burns cannot be drunk. A shade that provides no coolness cannot be sat in.

The speakers in this verse mention only the heat, and the restraint is telling. They name the one element they were most afraid of, and they name it as something they were kept from. The description of the Fire in the Qur'an is extensive precisely so that a person can know what he is avoiding, and these speakers show what knowing it looks like from the other side.

**What the Speakers Do Not Claim**

The verse contains no reference to their own deeds. It names God's grace and God's protection and stops there. The only thing they said about themselves was in the previous verse, and that was fear.

The restraint is consistent with the sūrah's teaching that the reward exceeds the deed. The Prophet ﷺ stated the principle plainly: none of you will be saved by his deeds, and when asked whether that included him, he said not even me, unless God encompasses him with His mercy and favour (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2816).

These speakers are describing that principle from inside it. They are in the Gardens, they know why, and the reason they give is not their own.

---
## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:28
> Indeed, we used to call upon Him ˹alone˺ before. He is truly the Most Kind, Most Merciful.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Only Deed They Name Is Prayer**

Across four verses the inhabitants of the Gardens describe themselves, and the single act of worship they mention is supplication. They were afraid, they were graced, they were protected — and now: we used to call upon Him before.

Nothing else is listed. No fast, no battle, no charity, no pilgrimage. Of everything a believer does, this is what the verse selects, and the selection is a statement about what supplication is.

The Qur'an makes the same identification elsewhere in a single line: your Lord says, call upon Me and I will answer you (40:60). The act of calling is treated as the essence of worship rather than one item within it, and this verse shows people in paradise giving the same account of their own lives.

**“Him Alone” — The Bracket Is the Point**

The translation inserts *alone* in brackets, and the insertion is warranted by the context and by the Arabic idiom. The speakers are describing the direction of their prayer, and the direction is what distinguished them from the people around them.

The verse before mentioned being in awe among their people, and the people among whom they were afraid were people who called on other powers alongside God. The Qur'an records that practice repeatedly and answers it repeatedly: those you call upon besides God cannot create a fly even if they combined to do it (22:73).

Calling on God alone was therefore the specific point of difference. It was also the specific offence in Mecca, and the reason the speakers were feared and mocked among their families. The verse names the practice that cost them.

**Al-Barr: The Name That Appears Once**

The divine name used here is *al-Barr*, the Most Kind or the Source of Goodness, and this is its only occurrence as a name of God in the entire Qur'an. Its uniqueness gives it weight.

The root *b-r-r* is common in the Book, but usually applied to people. The *abrār* — the pious, the righteous — are promised Gardens and bliss (82:13), they drink from a cup mixed with camphor (76:5), and they are described as being in bliss (83:22). The same root produces *birr*, the wide category of goodness that the Qur'an defines in terms of belief, charity, prayer, patience and keeping promises (2:177).

Applying the word to God places Him as the source of the quality He commands in people. The *abrār* are *barr* because He is *al-Barr*, and the verse uses the name at the exact moment when the speakers are describing goodness shown to them.

**Ar-Raḥīm Paired With It**

The name is followed by *ar-Raḥīm*, the Most Merciful, and the pairing is not arbitrary. *Birr* describes beneficence that is given; *raḥma* describes the compassion behind the giving.

The two together cover both the act and the disposition. A gift can be given without kindness of heart, and kindness of heart can exist without a gift. The verse asserts both, and it asserts them as a present fact — *innahu huwa al-barr ar-raḥīm*, He is truly the Most Kind, the Most Merciful — rather than as a past event.

That present tense matters. The speakers are not only reporting what God did for them; they are stating what God is, in a chapter where the same Lord has just been described administering a Fire. The two descriptions belong to one being.

**Three Sentences, One Account**

Read together, verses twenty-six to twenty-eight form the shortest complete account of the religious life in this sūrah. We were afraid among our people. God graced us and shielded us. We used to call on Him, and He is kind and merciful.

The three sentences have a shape: a condition, an act of God, and a practice with a conclusion. The condition is fear; the act is grace and protection; the practice is supplication; the conclusion is a statement about God's character.

Nothing in that sequence requires special ability or station. Fear is available to anyone, calling is available to anyone, and the outcome described is attributed entirely to the one called upon.

**The Speech Ends the Second Movement**

Verse twenty-eight closes the description of the reward that began at verse seventeen. Twelve verses have described the Gardens, and the last word in them is spoken by their inhabitants rather than by the narrator.

The effect is to end the description with a voice. The reader has been shown thrones, food, cups, pearls, and a conversation; the final thing is a testimony. The testimony is also the summary: fear, grace, prayer, kindness.

After this the sūrah turns. The next verse addresses the Prophet ﷺ with a command, and the following verses take up the accusations against him. The testimony of the rewarded people stands immediately before the accusations of the deniers, and the juxtaposition is the argument.

**Supplication as the Act That Remains**

Of the descriptions in this passage, this is the one that a reader can imitate immediately. Nobody can recline on the thrones described earlier or eat the fruit supplied in verse twenty-two. Anyone can call.

The verse therefore does practical work. It identifies the practice that connected the people in the Gardens to the God who shielded them, and it identifies it in the words of people for whom the connection worked.

The closing names are the reason it worked. The people who called were answered because the one called upon is *al-Barr ar-Raḥīm*, and those names are stated as facts about Him rather than as hopes about this instance. That is what makes the verse usable: it does not report a private outcome but describes a standing arrangement, and the arrangement is open to anyone who does what the speakers did.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:29
> So ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺. For you, by the grace of your Lord, are not a fortune-teller or a madman

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command Followed by Two Denials**

The verse is built from three parts. A command in one word: remind. A grounding phrase: by the grace of your Lord. And two negations: you are not a soothsayer, and you are not possessed.

The order is significant. The command comes first, before the answer to the accusations. The Prophet ﷺ is told what to do before he is told what he is not, and the doing is not made conditional on the clearing of his name.

That structure answers a situation in which the accusations were functioning as a reason to stop. The verse refuses the reason. The command to remind stands on its own, and the denials are attached to it as reassurance rather than as a precondition.

**Kāhin: What the Charge Meant**

The word *kāhin* refers to a figure well known in pre-Islamic Arabia: a soothsayer who claimed access to hidden knowledge, usually through contact with jinn, and who delivered his pronouncements in short rhymed prose.

The charge was specific, not vague. A *kāhin* was a paid professional who sold predictions, and the accusation implied that the Qur'an was that kind of product — manufactured speech delivered for a fee, claiming a supernatural source.

The Qur'an denies the charge in its own terms: nor is it the mumbling of a fortune-teller, yet you are hardly mindful (69:42). The denial is attached to a description of the revelation as a disclosure from God, which is the opposite of a soothsayer's claim: the *kāhin* asserted private access, and the Prophet ﷺ was commanded to publish everything he received.

**Majnūn: The Second Charge**

The second accusation was possession. In the Arabian understanding, a *majnūn* was a person overcome by a jinn, and the term covered both madness and inspiration, since both involved speech the speaker did not control.

The Qur'an denies it directly and repeatedly. Sūrah al-Qalam opens with an oath: by the grace of your Lord, you are not insane (68:2). In at-Takwīr the description is of the messenger's character: your companion is not mad (81:22). The charge is recorded as a general pattern in Sūrah adh-Dhāriyāt: no messenger came to those before them without being called a magician or a madman (51:52).

The pairing of the two charges here is typical. Soothsayer and madman are the standard pair, and both are ways of explaining extraordinary speech without accepting its source.

**“By the Grace of Your Lord”**

The phrase *bi-niʿmati rabbika* grounds the negation in God's favour rather than in the Prophet's own qualities. The verse does not say he is not a soothsayer because of his education, his character, or his reputation. It says he is not one by the grace of his Lord.

That framing does two things. It removes the defence from the realm of argument — no amount of self-description would convince people who had decided otherwise — and it places the Prophet's standing where the Qur'an consistently places it, as something given rather than earned.

The same phrase appears in the parallel denial in Sūrah al-Qalam, where it follows an oath. The repetition suggests it was a formula of assurance used at moments when the accusations were heaviest.

**Why the Command Is Placed Here**

The verse sits between the testimony of the rewarded in verses twenty-five to twenty-eight and the series of accusations in verses thirty to forty-three. Its position is the argument.

The people who have just spoken are people who were afraid of God among their families. The people about to speak are people saying he is a poet and that they await his ruin. The command to remind is inserted between the two, which locates the Prophet's task precisely: he is addressing the first group, in the presence of the second.

Sūrah Qāf states the same division explicitly: We know best what they say. And you are not there to compel them to believe. So remind with the Quran only those who fear My warning (50:45). The audience for reminding is the people who fear.

**Reminding Without Compulsion**

The command is *dhakkir*, from a root meaning to remind, and the Qur'an is careful about its limits. So remind, for your duty is only to remind; you are not there to compel them to believe (88:21–22).

The restriction is not a concession; it is a description of what reminding is. A reminder addresses someone who already knows something and has set it aside. It does not produce belief by force, and the Qur'an never claims that it does.

That definition explains the tone of the whole sūrah. It is built as a series of reminders — oaths, scenes, testimonies, challenges — rather than as a series of proofs. Each one assumes a hearer who can recognise what is being said.

**A Soothsayer Was Paid, and He Was Not**

Part of the charge was commercial. The *kāhin* earned a living from his pronouncements, and the accusation carried the implication that the Qur'an was a product being sold.

The Qur'an answers that implication directly and repeatedly, in the voice of the messengers rather than the narrator. Say: if I had ever asked you for a reward, you could keep it — my reward is only from Allah (34:47). Noah's people are told the same thing (11:29), and the phrase becomes a refrain of the prophetic office: I do not ask you for any reward for this message (26:109).

The people making the charge knew the facts, since the Prophet ﷺ had been trading in their own market for decades before the revelation. The denial is therefore addressed as much to the record as to the audience, and the sūrah returns to the question of reward at verse forty, where it is put as a question rather than a statement.

**Both Charges Attack the Source**

The two negations have one target: the origin of the speech. A soothsayer's words come from jinn; a madman's words come from nowhere. Both explanations allow the listener to hear the Qur'an and dismiss it.

The verse removes both, and what remains is the only alternative the accusations were designed to avoid: the speech comes from God. That is why the command to remind does not wait for the denial to be accepted. The reminding continues regardless, and the denials are for the one doing the reminding rather than for those rejecting him.

The next verse records a third accusation, and the sūrah's answer to it is shorter than the accusation itself.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:30
> Or do they say, “˹He is˺ a poet, for whom we ˹eagerly˺ await an ill-fate!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Charge, and the Most Flattering**

After soothsayer and madman comes poet. Of the three, this is the one that concedes the most. A poet is a craftsman of language, and calling the Qur'an poetry acknowledges that its language is extraordinary.

The charge is recorded elsewhere in the same words. The disbelievers say: this is a set of confused dreams, no, he has fabricated it, no, he must be a poet (21:5). In Yā Sīn the denial is explicit: We did not teach him poetry, nor is it fitting for him (36:69).

The three accusations together cover the available explanations. A soothsayer receives from jinn, a madman produces noise, and a poet produces art. Each explanation preserves the listener's position by removing the divine source, and the Qur'an answers each in turn.

**Rayb al-Manūn: The Turning of Time**

The phrase they use, *rayb al-manūn*, is compact and idiomatic. *Manūn* means time or death — the power that cuts people off — and *rayb* is the vicissitude or doubt that time brings. The sense is: we are waiting for time to take him.

The commentators read the phrase as either the calamities of time or the arrival of death, and both readings come to the same expectation: this movement will end when the man does.

That expectation is worth examining because it is a strategy. It does not require disproving the Qur'an or answering its challenge. It requires only waiting, on the assumption that time is on the side of the waiters.

**What Waiting Actually Assumed**

The strategy contained a premise, and the premise was that the message depended on the man. If the Qur'an were a man's composition, his death would end it, and the waiting would be vindicated.

The Qur'an's own account of revelation denies the premise. It is a reminder sent down, and its source is not the one delivering it. A message whose authority is its sender survives the death of its messenger, and the sūrah's structure assumes this throughout: the command to remind is given without reference to the Prophet's own survival.

The strategy also had a practical problem, which the next verse addresses with two words. Waiting is a mutual activity, and the other party can wait too.

**Why the Qur'an Is Not Poetry**

The denial in Sūrah Yā Sīn is stated as a fact about the Prophet ﷺ rather than as an argument: We did not teach him poetry, nor is it fitting for him (36:69). The verse in al-Ḥāqqah adds the classification: it is the word of a noble messenger, and it is not the word of a poet — how little you believe (69:41).

The differences are substantive. Arabic poetry worked within fixed metres and conventional themes, and it was composed to please patrons and tribes. The Qur'an does not follow the metres, and its subject matter is unlike the poetry of its setting.

There is also a moral distinction the Qur'an itself draws. As for poets, they are followed merely by deviants; do you not see how they rant in every field, only saying what they never do? (26:224–226). The charge of poetry carried with it, in that culture, an association between speech and conduct that the Qur'an did not accept for itself.

**The Exception the Same Verses Make**

The condemnation of poets is not absolute, and the exception is instructive: except those who believe, do good, remember Allah often, and avenge the believers after being wrongfully slandered (26:227).

The exception defines poetry by its use rather than its form. Speech in metre is not condemned as such; speech that is disconnected from belief and action is. The distinction is the same one the Qur'an applies to all language, including its own.

That is why the charge of poetry could not stick. The accusation implied that the Qur'an belonged to a category defined by saying what one does not do, and the life of the man delivering it was open to inspection by the people making the charge.

**The Medium They Assumed**

Calling the Qur'an poetry placed it inside a specific institution. Poetry in pre-Islamic Arabia was not entertainment; it was the archive of tribal honour, the medium of praise and satire, and the instrument by which a tribe's reputation was made or damaged. A poet had patrons and opponents, and his verse functioned within that economy.

The charge therefore implied more than a literary classification. It implied that the Qur'an was a partisan composition, produced to advance a man and his clan within a competitive field.

The Qur'an rejects that frame at its root. It describes itself as a reminder to the whole world (81:27), addressed beyond tribe and patron, and it declines the conventions of the medium it is accused of using. A composition that ignores metre, refuses patronage, and addresses everyone is not operating in the economy the charge assumed.

**A Charge Made in Public**

The verse records the accusation as something said, and the saying was public. The Meccan opposition was conducted in assemblies, in poetry, and in insult, and the Qur'an quotes it back with the direct speech intact.

The recording has a purpose. An accusation that is quoted can be examined, and this one is examined in the verses that follow through a series of questions — do their minds command them to this, do they say he invented it, let them produce something like it.

The sūrah treats the accusations as claims rather than as noise, and it answers each with an argument. That treatment is itself a statement: the message is confident enough to take its opponents' words seriously.

**The Waiting Ends Badly for the Waiters**

The verse does not answer the expectation directly; the next verse does, in a single imperative. What is prepared here is the reversal.

The people waiting for time to take the Prophet ﷺ were waiting for an event that would vindicate them. The Qur'an's answer is that the same time is working on them, and that the outcome will not be decided by who dies first.

History recorded the result. The men who awaited his ruin died at Badr, and the movement they expected to end with him continued after his death, as the sūrah's own logic required. The waiting was not the strategy it appeared to be.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:31
> Say, “Keep waiting! I too am waiting with you.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Whole Answer in Two Verbs**

The accusation in the previous verse was a strategy: we will wait for time to take him. The reply accepts the strategy and joins it. Wait — I am waiting with you.

There is no refutation, no argument, no appeal to evidence. The verse takes the accusers' own chosen method and removes the one advantage it gave them, which was that they were waiting while he was being waited for.

That is a complete answer in the smallest possible space. A strategy only works if one side is passive; once both sides are waiting, the strategy has no content left, and the outcome depends on facts rather than on patience.

**The Verb Is Unusual**

The word used is *taraddaṣū*, from a root meaning to watch for something expected, to keep one's eye on an outcome. It is stronger than ordinary waiting; it carries the sense of lying in wait.

The participle form that follows, *min al-mutaraddaṣīn*, places the speaker inside the same class as the people addressed: I am one of those who wait. The construction is the same partitive form used elsewhere in the Qur'an to enrol a person in a category, and here the category is deliberately the opponents' own.

The rhetorical effect is exact. He does not say your waiting will fail; he says he is doing it too, which forces the question of what each side is waiting for.

**The Same Formula in Six Other Places**

This reply is not unique to aṭ-Ṭūr, and the repetitions show that it was a standing answer. Hūd tells his people: then wait, I too am waiting with you (7:71). The same words appear in Yūnus (10:102) and in Ṭā Hā: each of us is waiting, so keep waiting — you will soon know who is on the Straight Path and is rightly guided (20:135). In as-Sajdah the command is shorter: turn away from them and wait; they too are waiting (32:30).

The consistency across chapters, revealed at different times to different audiences, indicates a fixed situation rather than a single occasion. Prophets were told to wait; their opponents were waiting; the Qur'an repeatedly puts both facts in one sentence.

**What the Answer Changes**

The accusation assumed an asymmetry. The waiters were confident and the one waited for was under threat; time was presumed to be on the side of the confident.

The reply converts the asymmetry into symmetry, and symmetry changes what waiting means. When both sides wait, the outcome is decided by something outside the waiting, and the verse leaves it there without naming what that something is.

The omission is the strength of the reply. A prophet who predicted a date would be tested by it. A prophet who says he is also waiting states a confidence without supplying a date that could be checked and falsified in the short term.

**The Waiting Was Settled at Badr**

The sūrah is Meccan, and the answer it gives was tested within the Prophet's lifetime. At Badr, in the second year after the emigration, the Meccan leaders who had made these accusations were killed or captured, and the movement they expected to die with its founder grew stronger.

The Qur'an had already spoken of the event in Meccan terms: their united front will be defeated and forced to flee (54:45). The companions who heard that verse did not know when it referred to, and its fulfilment at Badr was reported by those present as the moment the reference became clear.

The point for this verse is not the battle but the pattern. Waiting was not a neutral activity; it was a wager, and the wager was settled.

**Why the Verse Is So Short**

The surrounding verses are dense — oaths, scenes, descriptions of the Gardens and the Fire. This one is a single imperative and a single statement, and its brevity is deliberate.

A short answer to a long accusation signals that the accusation does not require a long answer. The verse treats the waiting as a settled matter rather than a claim to be examined.

There is also a tone to notice. The reply contains no anger and no threat. It is the calmest possible refusal to be hurried, delivered by a man in a weak position to men in a strong one.

**Waiting Was Also an Instruction to the Prophet**

The reply tells the opponents to wait, and the sūrah tells the Prophet ﷺ the same thing in different words. A few verses later he is commanded to be patient with his Lord's decree (52:48), and the same command appears in al-Qalam with the example of Jonah attached (68:48) and in al-Insān alongside a refusal to yield to any evildoer (76:24).

The two waitings are not the same act. The opponents wait as a strategy, expecting an outcome they have predicted. The Prophet ﷺ waits as an obedience, without knowing the date and without being permitted to force it.

That difference is what the verse is really pointing at. Both sides are waiting, but only one of them knows what waiting means. A person waiting under instruction is not idle; he is doing what he was told, and the instruction in this sūrah is to remind, to be patient, and to glorify.

**A Waiting That Has Not Ended**

The verse has a second horizon. The waiting described here is not only about a battle or a death; it is about the day the sūrah opened with, when the heavens shake and the mountains are winnowed.

Both sides in the dispute are moving toward that day, and both are described in this sūrah as arriving there. The deniers are shoved into the Fire and told that this is what they denied; the righteous are reclining and describing their former fear.

The reply therefore covers more than the immediate argument. Wait, it says — and the sūrah has already described what both parties will be waiting for.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:32
> Or do their ˹intelligent˺ minds prompt them to this ˹paradox˺? Or are they ˹just˺ a transgressing people

**Expanded Commentary**

**Aḥlām Is Not Simply Intellect**

The word translated as minds is *aḥlām*, the plural of *ḥilm*. In Arabic the term covers intelligence, but its centre of gravity is restraint — the capacity to hold back, to weigh, to refuse the first impulse. A *ḥalīm* is a person who does not act on anger, and one of God's names, *al-Ḥalīm*, means the Forbearing.

That sense changes the question. The verse is not asking whether they thought about it; it is asking whether their judgment — their trained capacity to weigh things — could possibly produce the claims they are making.

The choice of word is a compliment turned into an accusation. The verse addresses them as people who have the faculty in question, and then asks whether the faculty could have delivered what they delivered.

**The Question Cannot Be Answered Yes**

*A taʾmuruhum aḥlāmuhum bi-hādhā* — do their minds command them to this? The verb of commanding is important: a mind that functions properly issues directives, and the verse asks what directive could have produced a soothsayer, a madman, and a poet all at once.

The three charges in the preceding verses are mutually inconsistent. A soothsayer receives from a supernatural source; a madman produces incoherence; a poet composes for effect. A single person cannot be all three, and the charges were made in the same breath by the same people.

The Qur'an points at the inconsistency without spelling it out. The question is rhetorical because no answer is available: the mind that produced those claims was not commanding, it was rationalising.

**The Alternative Offered Is the Real Diagnosis**

The second half of the verse supplies the explanation the first half rules out: or are they a transgressing people? The word *ṭāghūn* comes from a root meaning to exceed a limit, to overstep.

The diagnosis is about motive rather than ability. The claims did not come from reasoning that went wrong; they came from a will that had already decided and then looked for reasons.

That distinction is central to the Qur'an's account of unbelief. It repeatedly describes people who see clearly and still refuse, and it locates the failure in the direction of the person rather than in the quality of the evidence. The verse names that failure with a single participle.

**What Transgression Explains That Intelligence Cannot**

The two options in the verse are not equally weighted. The first is excluded, and the second is left standing as the only remaining account.

Transgression explains several things the charges cannot explain. It explains why three incompatible accusations were made together — any accusation served, so consistency did not matter. It explains why the challenge to produce a sūrah was never taken up. And it explains why the waiting strategy was preferred to the examination the message invited.

The verse therefore does more than accuse. It offers a mechanism, and a mechanism can be checked by the person described.

**Where Aḥlām Sits in the Qur'an's Vocabulary of Thinking**

The Qur'an has several words for the thinking faculties, and each does different work. *ʿAql* is the intellect that grasps; *fiqh* is understanding that reaches a conclusion; *tadabbur* is reflection on consequences. *Ḥilm* is the faculty that governs the others — the judgment that decides whether to act on what has been grasped.

Using *aḥlām* here is therefore a precise choice. The verse is not asking whether they understood the message; it is asking whether their judgment delivered the verdict it delivered.

That distinction matters because the accusation is not ignorance. The people addressed had heard the Qur'an repeatedly, in prayer and in public, from a man they had known for forty years. Nothing was hidden from them. The verse locates the failure in the seat of judgment rather than in the organs of perception, which is why the second half of the verse offers transgression as the explanation.

**A Series Built From Questions**

This verse opens a run of interrogatives that continues to verse forty-three, each beginning with *am*, or. The questions move through creation, control, access to the unseen, offspring, payment, and scheming.

The form is deliberate. An assertion can be met with a counter-assertion; a question requires the addressee to supply an answer, and in each case the available answers are either absurd or damaging.

The series also shifts the ground. The first questions were about the Prophet; these are about the questioners. By verse thirty-five the interrogation has moved to their own existence, which is the point where the accusations stop being useful.

**The Qur'an's Standing Appeal to Reason**

The verse is one of many that treat reason as the faculty that ought to reach faith. The Qur'an constantly asks whether people do not reflect, do not understand, do not travel and observe. It does not present faith as opposed to thought but as its conclusion.

That makes this accusation sharper. The charge is not that they lack the faculty but that they have bypassed it. The minds addressed here are described as commanding — a faculty with authority — and the verse asks whether it exercised that authority.

The answer implied is that it did not, and that the failure was voluntary.

**A Question the Reader Can Test**

The verse is addressed to people in Mecca, and its form allows anyone to apply it. The test is not whether a person agrees with the Qur'an but whether the reasons he gives for disagreeing are consistent with each other.

The charges the verse examines fail that test on their face. Three incompatible explanations, none of them tested, all of them asserted at once, with a strategy of waiting preferred to any examination — the pattern is recognisable without reference to the historical setting.

That is why the sūrah asks the question rather than answering it. The question does the work.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:33
> Or do they say, “He made this ˹Quran˺ up!”? In fact, they have no faith

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Plainest Charge of All**

After soothsayer, madman, and poet comes the simplest accusation: he invented it. The verb *iftarāhu* means to fabricate or forge, and it is used in the Qur'an for the gravest kind of falsehood — inventing a lie against God.

The charge has the advantage of requiring no explanation. It does not need a theory about jinn, inspiration, or literary craft. It says only that the words came from the man and nowhere else.

That simplicity is also its weakness, and the next verse exposes it. An invention can be compared with other inventions, and the comparison is a test.

**The Reply Is a Diagnosis, Not a Refutation**

The verse does not answer the charge directly. It states a fact about the people making it: *bal lā yuʾminūn*, in fact they do not believe.

The particle *bal* signals a correction — not that, but this. The verse replaces the apparent reason for the charge with the actual one. They are not saying he invented it because they examined the evidence and concluded that; they are saying it because they have not believed.

That reversal is the sūrah's method throughout this section. Each accusation is met not with evidence but with a statement about the state of the accuser, on the principle that the accusation is a symptom rather than a finding.

**Why the Diagnosis Comes Before the Argument**

The order matters. A reader might expect the challenge — produce something like it — to come first, with the diagnosis as a comment afterwards. The verse does the opposite.

The reason is that argument presupposes willingness. A person who has decided not to believe does not evaluate evidence; he filters it. Presenting a challenge to such a person produces another accusation rather than an attempt.

The Qur'an states the principle elsewhere in similar terms: even if every sign came to them they would not believe until they see the painful punishment (10:97). The diagnosis in this verse explains why the challenge that follows is addressed to their honesty rather than to their skill.

**Fabrication Is the Gravest Category of Lie**

The word *iftarā* is not neutral, and the charge was not a light one. The Qur'an treats inventing a lie against God as the worst wrong a person can commit: who does more wrong than the one who fabricates lies against Allah, or claims to have received revelation when nothing was revealed to him, or says he can reveal the like of Allah's revelations? (6:93). The same formulation appears in a shorter version: who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His signs? (6:21).

The accusation therefore cut both ways. If the Prophet ﷺ had invented the Qur'an, he would fall under the description in 6:93 exactly, and the people making the charge knew the verse.

The Qur'an turns the category back on them. A fabrication against God is the gravest lie, and a false accusation of it is not far behind. The verse's refusal to argue, and its move straight to the diagnosis, is consistent with treating the charge as a moral fact rather than a technical dispute.

**The Same Charge in Other Chapters**

The accusation is recorded repeatedly, and the repetitions vary usefully. In Yūnus: or do they say he made it up? Tell them to produce one sūrah like it and seek help from whoever they can (10:38). In Hūd the challenge is ten sūrahs (11:13). In al-Furqān the charge is elaborated: this is nothing but a fabrication which he made up with the help of others — and the reply calls the claim unjustified and untrue (25:4).

The elaboration in al-Furqān is instructive. When the charge of invention is pressed, it grows: first he made it up, then others helped him. Each addition makes the claim harder to sustain, since the helpers would have to be named and their contribution produced.

The Qur'an answers every version the same way, with a test rather than a denial.

**What Invention Would Actually Require**

The charge implies a comparison the accusers never performed. To say a text was invented is to say it resembles other invented texts in origin, and the resemblance can be examined.

The Qur'an's content makes the examination awkward for the charge. It contains law, narrative, eschatology, and argument, delivered over twenty-three years in circumstances that alternated between persecution and power. An invention composed to serve a man's interests would be expected to change with those interests.

The verse does not develop this point because the next verse states the test more efficiently. But the test is what the charge could not survive, and the sūrah knows it.

**Disbelief as the Cause, Not the Result**

The verse reverses the assumed order. The accusers presented their charge as the reason for their unbelief: we do not believe because he invented it. The verse says the charge exists because of the unbelief.

That reversal is a claim about how people actually reason. A conclusion reached first produces reasons afterwards, and the reasons inherit the direction of the conclusion rather than determining it.

The Qur'an makes this claim often and in different registers. The disbelievers' falsehood has been made appealing to them, and they have been turned away from the Path (13:33). The pattern named there is the pattern diagnosed here.

**The Challenge Is Prepared, Not Avoided**

The verse ends on the diagnosis, and the reader might expect the sūrah to move on. It does not; the very next verse states the test in the imperative.

That sequence is significant. The diagnosis is not a substitute for the challenge but a preparation for it. Once the reason for the charge has been named, the challenge is no longer a debate to be won but a test to be taken or refused.

The people addressed could produce a sūrah if the charge were true. The verse says they will not try, and the reason it gives is the reason they cannot.

---
## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:34
> Let them then produce something like it, if what they say is true

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Challenge in Its Shortest Form**

The verse is the Qur'an's challenge stated with nothing added: let them bring a discourse like it, if they are truthful. No quantity is specified, no helper is excluded, no deadline is set.

The brevity is the point. A challenge that specifies conditions can be disputed on the conditions; a challenge that specifies none cannot. Whatever counts as a discourse like the Qur'an, the demand stands, and the people addressed are the ones best placed to know what that would require.

The conditional clause — if what you say is true — makes the challenge a test of their own claim rather than a demand from the other side. If he invented it, then invention is possible, and they are inventors too.

**The Challenge Is Graded Elsewhere**

Other verses state the same challenge at different scales, and the gradation is instructive. In al-Isrāʾ: if all humans and jinn came together to produce the equivalent of this Qur'an, they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported each other (17:88). In Hūd the demand is ten sūrahs like it (11:13). In al-Baqarah it is a single sūrah (2:23).

The three levels move from the whole book, to ten chapters, to one. The shortest version in aṭ-Ṭūr is simply *a discourse like it*, which is closer to the single-unit formulation.

The gradation removes the usual objection to challenges of this kind: that the demand is too large. At its lowest setting the demand is one chapter, and the people addressed composed poetry and oratory daily.

**The People Best Equipped to Answer**

The challenge was made to a society whose central cultural achievement was language. Pre-Islamic Arabia produced poetry of high technical skill, memorised and transmitted orally, and Mecca in particular hosted annual gatherings at which verse was recited and judged.

The audience was not illiterate bystanders asked to do something beyond them. They were professionals in the medium being challenged, and the challenge was made to them publicly, repeatedly, and over years.

That context is what makes the challenge meaningful. A demand made to people unable to attempt it proves nothing; a demand made to experts who decline to attempt it says something about the demand.

**No Answer Was Produced**

The historical record on this point is simple. No sūrah like the Qur'an was produced by the Prophet's opponents in his lifetime, and the challenge has not been successfully answered since.

Attempts were made. Rival claimants to prophecy in the generation after the Prophet ﷺ produced compositions in imitation of Qur'anic style, and their productions were judged by their own contemporaries as parody rather than equivalent. The literary assessment has not changed.

The failure is not attributed in the Qur'an to a lack of effort but to the nature of the thing. The verse in al-Isrāʾ states it as a permanent fact about the combination of humans and jinn, not as a temporary failure of nerve.

**What "Like It" Includes**

The challenge is usually discussed in terms of eloquence, and eloquence is part of it, but the wording is broader. *Ḥadīth mithlih* — a discourse like it — covers the whole of what the Qur'an is.

That includes its legal content, which no Meccan poet had any reason or ability to produce; its narrative of earlier prophets, delivered to a society without access to those scriptures; its consistency across twenty-three years of composition in radically different circumstances; and its predictions, several of which were fulfilled within the lifetime of the hearers.

A composition that matched the Qur'an's style but contained none of that would not be like it, and the challenge is framed to include everything.

**The Conditional Clause Does the Work**

*In kuntum ṣādiqīn* — if you are truthful. The clause appears in the other versions of the challenge as well, and it converts the verse from a boast into a test.

A person who claims that a text was invented is making a factual claim, and factual claims have consequences. The verse attaches the consequence: produce one. The refusal to accept the consequence is evidence about the claim.

The Qur'an uses this method elsewhere. When asked for a sign, it points to what has already been given and asks what further sign could convince people who reject the first (29:50–51). The challenge is not an offer of proof but a demand for consistency.

**Answering Would Have Ended the Matter**

The strategic situation makes the failure to answer harder to explain. For the Meccan leaders, producing a single sūrah like the Qur'an would have settled the dispute completely and permanently, without blood, without exile, and without the loss of their commercial standing.

They had every motive to try. Instead the record shows them pursuing other routes: negotiation over the terms of the message, pressure on his clan, persecution of the weaker believers, and eventually armed confrontation. None of those routes made sense if the challenge could have been met.

The Qur'an notes the preference for signs over the text itself: if only some signs had been sent down to him from his Lord — and the answer is that signs belong to God, and that the Book already recited to them should be enough (29:50–51). The pattern is consistent: the challenge is avoided, and something else is requested in its place.

**The Challenge Remains Open**

Unlike most arguments in the sūrah, this one has no time limit. It is addressed to them, and it has been repeated by readers of the Qur'an ever since, in the same terms.

That openness is part of its function. A message that cannot be matched does not become matchable with time, and the verse is staked on that. The people who heard it in Mecca are dead; the text is not, and neither is the demand.

The following verses turn from the message to the messengers and to the people who reject them, beginning with a question that has no relation to poetry at all: where they came from.

---
## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:35
> Or were they created by nothing, or are they ˹their own˺ creators

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Stopped a Man in Prayer**

This verse has a documented effect on a documented listener. Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim reported: I heard the Prophet ﷺ reciting Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr in the Maghrib prayer, and when he reached this passage — were they created by nothing, or are they the creators, or did they create the heavens and the earth — it was as if my heart was about to fly (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4854).

Jubayr was not a believer at the time. He had come to Medina after Badr to ransom the Meccan captives, and he heard the recitation in the mosque. He later embraced Islam, and he identified this hearing as part of what led him there.

The report is evidence about the verse's rhetorical force, given by a man who was hostile to it when he heard it.

**Three Possibilities, and the Third Is the Answer**

The verse presents two options and leaves the third unstated. Were they created from nothing? Are they their own creators? The missing option — created by a Creator — is the one the listener supplies, and supplying it is the act of conviction.

The first option is not available. Something cannot come from nothing without an agent, and the phrase *from nothing* in this context means without a maker at all. The second is a contradiction: a thing that creates itself must exist before it exists in order to do the creating.

The verse does not argue through these points. It states them as questions, and the questions close as they are asked.

**The Same Question in Al-Wāqiʿah**

The argument appears elsewhere with a concrete example. Have you considered what you ejaculate? Is it you who create a child out of it, or is it We Who do so? (56:58–59).

The shift from the abstract to the biological is deliberate. The generation of a human being is an event every society observes and none controls, and the question about who creates it cannot be answered by the people involved.

Both passages use the same structure: a question about origin, with the answer implied by the impossibility of the alternatives. Neither one lectures; both ask.

**What “From Nothing” Would Mean**

The first option deserves more than a dismissal, because it is the option most often defended. To say a thing came from nothing is to say that its existence has no explanation at all — not an unknown explanation, but no explanation.

That claim is not a scientific statement; it is a refusal of the question. Every other event the Qur'an discusses is treated as having a cause, and the people addressed lived in a world where nothing happened without a doer: no tent was pitched by nobody, no well dug itself.

The verse puts the claim in that everyday frame. It does not use technical vocabulary, and it does not need to. The absurdity is available to anyone who has ever asked who did something.

**Self-Creation as a Contradiction**

The second option fails for a structural reason. To create is to act, and to act one must exist. A self-creator would have to exist before existing, which is not a difficulty of degree but a contradiction in terms.

The verse states it as a present-tense question — or are they the creators? — which sharpens the point. It is not asking about a past event they might have missed; it is asking whether they currently possess creative power, and they can test that immediately.

The question is answered by the body of the person asked. Nobody in the audience could produce a human being, and the following verses extend the same test to the heavens and the earth.

**The Same Question With a Smaller Object**

The argument appears elsewhere with the human being's own beginning as the evidence. Were they not once a sperm-drop emitted, then a clinging clot, then developed and perfected in form, producing both sexes (75:37–39)? The same question in al-Mursalāt: did We not create you from a humble fluid (77:20), and in as-Sajdah the descent of the human being is traced to an extract of a humble fluid (32:8).

The progression is described in stages, and the stages are observable. Nobody disputes the sequence from a drop to a clot to a formed body; the dispute is about who performs it.

That is why the verse in this sūrah works so well in oral delivery. The listener who hears the question has already lived the answer, since every human being present came into existence by the process described. The question does not require learning; it requires honesty about a process everyone knows.

**The Question Is About Worship, Not Cosmology**

The verse is usually read as a proof of God's existence, and it functions as one, but its purpose in the sūrah is narrower and sharper. It is addressed to people who already believed that God exists.

The Qur'an records their admission repeatedly: if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth they will say God (31:25). The problem was never the acknowledgement; it was the refusal to draw the consequence.

The verse in al-Muʾminūn states the consequence directly: did you think We created you without purpose, and that you would never be returned to Us? (23:115). Creation implies a purpose and an accounting, and the question in this verse is the first step toward both.

**The Interrogation Turns on the Questioners**

The preceding verses were about the Prophet: is he a soothsayer, a madman, a poet, an inventor? This verse changes the subject to the people asking, and the change ends the accusations.

A man accused of invention can be examined; the examiners cannot examine themselves and continue examining him at the same time. The question about their own origin puts them in the position they had assigned to him.

That is the structure of the whole run of questions from here to verse forty-three. Each one takes a claim the opponents were making and turns it into a question about themselves — their creation, their control, their access to the unseen, their offspring, their payment, their schemes.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:36
> Or did they create the heavens and the earth? In fact, they have no firm belief ˹in Allah˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Widens From the Person to the Cosmos**

The previous verse asked whether they created themselves. This verse asks whether they created the heavens and the earth, and the widening is the argument.

A person might imagine some special privilege in his own case — that he is somehow self-originating, or that his existence is a brute fact. That imagination cannot survive the next step. The heavens and the earth are not the property of anyone, and nobody in the audience had any claim on them.

The two questions together cover the whole of what exists: the person asking and the world he stands in. If neither was made by him, then both were made by someone else, and the identity of that someone is not in doubt.

**They Already Answered This Question**

The Qur'an is explicit that the people addressed accepted the premise. If you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will definitely say Allah (31:25). The same admission is recorded in al-ʿAnkabūt, where the question adds the sun and the moon to the list (29:61), and in az-Zukhruf, where the answer is the Almighty, the All-Knowing (43:9). In az-Zumar the question is followed by a challenge to consider whether their other gods could undo harm or withhold mercy (39:38).

The admission was not hidden or reluctant. It was public, and the Qur'an quotes it back to them.

That is what makes this verse's second half so pointed. The question is not being asked to establish a fact they deny. It is being asked to expose the distance between what they say and what they do.

**The Gap Between Admission and Worship**

The diagnosis is stated in one phrase: they have no firm belief. The admission existed; the belief did not, and the difference between the two is the whole problem.

Knowledge that does not reach the will is not belief in the Qur'an's sense. Iblīs knew who God was and still refused; the people of earlier prophets recognised the signs and still rejected them. The Book consistently treats belief as a direction of the person rather than a possession of information.

The verse in al-ʿAnkabūt puts it as a question: if they say Allah, then how are they deluded? (29:61). The delusion is the mechanism — the admission is held in one part of the mind while the rest of the life is arranged as though it were untrue.

**Creation Implies Knowledge**

The Qur'an draws a further inference from the fact of creation, and it states it as a rhetorical question: how could He not know His own creation, when He is the Most Subtle, the All-Aware? (67:14).

The reasoning is straightforward. A maker knows what he made, and a maker of this order knows it in detail. The inference matters for the sūrah's argument because the people addressed were denying an accounting, and an accounting requires a Knower.

That is why the verse about creation is immediately followed by a statement about belief. The denial of the reckoning is not a separate error; it is a consequence of not really holding the belief in the Creator.

**Creation Also Implies the Ability to Repeat**

A second inference is drawn elsewhere, and it addresses the specific objection the Meccans raised most often: resurrection. Can the One Who created the heavens and the earth not resurrect these deniers? Yes, He can — He is the Master Creator, the All-Knowing (36:81).

The same argument appears in al-Aḥqāf: do they not realise that Allah, Who created the heavens and the earth and did not tire in creating them, is able to give life to the dead? (46:33). The point is one of relative difficulty: if the greater act was performed, the lesser is not in question.

This verse therefore carries more than a cosmological claim. It contains the answer to the objection that dominated the Meccan rejection, and the sūrah's opening verses about the shaking sky and the winnowed mountains already assumed that answer.

**Yaqīn: Certainty Rather Than Information**

The word used for belief here is *yaqīn*, certainty. The verse does not say they have no knowledge; it says they have no certainty, and the distinction is deliberate.

Certainty in the Qur'an is knowledge that has settled — the kind that governs behaviour and survives pressure. It is used for what the prophets had and what the deniers lacked, and the same word names the state that comes at death, when worship becomes unavoidable (15:99 in the sense of certainty's arrival).

The verse describes people who had the material for certainty and did not convert it. That is a different failure from ignorance, and it is a failure the reader can diagnose in himself.

**Creation Without Fatigue**

The Qur'an adds a detail to the creation claim that strengthens the resurrection inference. Indeed, We created the heavens and the earth and everything in between in six Days, and We were not even touched with fatigue (50:38). The sūrah then puts the objection in the form of a question: were We incapable of creating them the first time? In fact, they are in doubt about their re-creation (50:15).

The absence of fatigue matters for the argument. A human maker tires, and a tired maker cannot guarantee a second act. The verse removes that limitation, which means the second creation is not a question of capacity but of will.

That is why the diagnosis in this verse is about belief rather than knowledge. The people addressed had no difficulty with the power of God; they had difficulty with His intention to raise them, and the difficulty was not intellectual. The verse names it as an absence of certainty, and the rest of the passage works on that absence rather than on the evidence.

**The Series Continues on the Same Method**

The run of questions from verse thirty-two onward follows one pattern: a claim is taken, turned into a question, and shown to have no answer. Self-creation had no answer. Cosmic creation has none.

The next questions move from what they made to what they control — the treasuries of the Lord, the stairway to heaven, the distribution of prophecy. Each one asks the same thing in a different domain: what access do you have that would justify your judgment?

The answer in every case is none, and the verse states the reason once and for all: they have no firm belief. Everything else follows from that.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:37
> Or do they possess the treasuries of your Lord, or are they in control ˹of everything˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question of Access**

After asking what they created, the verse asks what they own. *Khazāʾin rabbik* — the treasuries of your Lord — and the question is whether they hold the keys.

The shift is significant. A person who cannot claim to have made the world may still claim to have standing within it: wealth, rank, influence, a voice in who gets what. The verse asks whether that claim extends to God's stores.

The treasuries in the Qur'an are the reserves from which everything is distributed. Sūrah al-Ḥijr states the principle: there is not any means of sustenance whose reserves We do not hold, only bringing it forth in precise measure (15:21). The measure is the point — distribution is deliberate, not random.

**What the Treasuries Contain**

The commentators identify the treasuries in several ways, and each reading fits the context. They are the stores of God's mercy, as in the parallel verse in Ṣād which asks whether they possess the treasuries of your Lord's mercy (38:9). They are the stores from which prophecy is sent to whom God wills. They are His knowledge, or the secrets hidden from human perception.

All four readings answer the same objection. The Meccan complaint was not about the content of the message but about its recipient, and the verse in az-Zukhruf records it verbatim: if only this Qur'an had been revealed to a great man from one of the two cities (43:31).

The complaint was about distribution. The verse answers by asking who does the distributing.

**The Complaint Behind the Question**

The objection in 43:31 is worth stating plainly, because it was a real argument in its setting. Mecca and Ṭāʾif had recognised notables — men of wealth, lineage, and oratory — and the revelation had gone to an orphan who had never claimed any of those distinctions.

The objection assumed that divine favour follows social rank. That assumption is what the verse dismantles, and the following verse in az-Zukhruf dismantles it explicitly: is it they who distribute your Lord's mercy? We have distributed their livelihood among them in this worldly life and raised some above others so that some may employ others in service (43:32).

The argument is from the lesser to the greater. If they do not control the distribution of livelihood, which they observe daily, they cannot control the distribution of revelation, which they have never observed at all.

**The Prophet's Own Disclaimer**

The Qur'an puts the same denial in the Prophet's own mouth, and the denial is comprehensive. Say: I do not say to you that I possess Allah's treasuries or know the unseen, nor do I claim to be an angel — I only follow what is revealed to me (6:50). Hūd says the same to his people (11:31).

The disclaimer is important for reading this verse. The Prophet ﷺ was not claiming access to the treasuries either. He claimed to have received something from them, which is a different claim and one that can be examined.

That is the asymmetry the verse exposes. Neither side holds the keys; one side has been given something and can show it.

**“Or Are They in Control”**

The second half uses a rare word, *al-musayṭirūn*, from a root meaning to dominate or to have authority over a thing such that one measures it out. The commentators read it as the ability to dispense from the treasuries — to decree as God decrees.

The word choice fits the context. The objection to the revelation was an objection to a decision, and a decision presupposes authority. The verse asks whether they hold that authority, and the rare word carries the sense of administrative control rather than mere strength.

The question is rhetorical because the answer is visible. They did not control the weather, the seasons, birth, or death, and every one of those is a form of distribution.

**Distribution as God's Prerogative**

The verse's underlying claim is that God distributes, and that His distribution does not follow human estimates of worth. The Qur'an states this repeatedly: He gives wisdom to whom He wills (2:269), He expands provision for whom He wills and restricts it (13:26), and He chooses for His message whom He wills.

That claim is uncomfortable in every society, including the one hearing it. It means that rank, wealth, and eloquence are not credentials for revelation, and it means that a person's estimate of who deserves what has no standing in the matter.

The verse puts the claim as a question rather than an assertion, which leaves the hearer to supply the answer and own it.

**The Two Cities and the Men Named in Them**

The complaint recorded in 43:31 was not abstract, and the exegetes identify the men the objectors had in mind. The notables of Mecca included al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, whose wealth and standing were proverbial, and ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah; the notable of Ṭāʾif was ʿUrwa ibn Masʿūd ath-Thaqafī. These are the names the classical commentators give for the great men of the two cities.

The objection was therefore about specific people, and it can be tested against them. None of the men named produced a revelation. One of them, ʿUrwa, negotiated for the Quraysh at Ḥudaybiyyah, embraced Islam in the year 9 AH after the conquest of Mecca, and was killed by his own tribesmen when he went back to call them to it — the message he had once thought should have gone to someone else.

That outcome is the sharpest form of the verse's argument. The distribution the objectors wanted to control was made without them, and the recipients of what they thought should have gone elsewhere included some of the objectors themselves. The treasuries were not distributed by rank, and the events proved it.

**Why the Question Is Rhetorical**

Nobody in Mecca claimed to hold the treasuries of God. The question is not refuting a claim they made; it is testing a claim they implied.

Their objection to the Prophet's selection implied that they knew better than the one who selected. Their objection to the message implied that they could judge its source. The verse takes those implications and asks whether the position required to make them exists.

It does not, and the verse moves on immediately. The next question asks whether they have a stairway to the heavens to listen in — a further test of the access their objections assumed.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:38
> Or do they have a stairway, by which they eavesdrop ˹on the heavens˺? Then let those who do so bring a compelling proof

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Ladder to Heaven**

The image is deliberately absurd, and the absurdity is the argument. *Sullam* is an ordinary word for a stairway or ladder, the kind used to reach a roof. The verse asks whether they have one that reaches the heavens.

The question follows directly from the one before it. Having established that they do not control the treasuries, the verse asks whether they have some other means of access — a way up, a way in, a way to hear what is decided above.

The image would have been comic to the audience, and the comedy has a purpose. An objection that requires a ladder to heaven is not a serious objection, and the verse says so by drawing the picture rather than by making the point.

**Yastamiʿūn: Listening In**

The verb is from a form meaning to listen deliberately, to strain to hear — and in this context, to eavesdrop. The word carries the sense of listening to something not addressed to you.

That nuance matters. The verse is not asking whether they have received revelation; it is asking whether they have overheard the heavenly council. The distinction separates a messenger, who is addressed, from an eavesdropper, who takes what he can catch.

The Qur'an reserves the second description for those who claim knowledge of the unseen without being sent, and it uses it here to characterise the position of the objectors.

**The Jinn Had That Access, and Lost It**

The concept of eavesdropping on heaven was not invented for this verse; it was part of the audience's worldview, and the Qur'an addresses it in the voice of the jinn themselves. Earlier we tried to reach heaven for news, only to find it filled with stern guards and shooting stars. We used to take up positions there for eavesdropping, but whoever dares eavesdrop now will find a flare lying in wait for them (72:8–9).

The same protection is described from God's side: the lowest heaven is adorned with lamps and made a means of driving away eavesdropping devils (67:5), and the sky is protected from every accursed devil except one who steals a hearing and is pursued by a visible flare (15:17–18).

The point for this verse is that the access they might appeal to has been closed, and closed for those beings who actually had it.

**The Demand for a Proof**

The second half of the verse changes from image to demand: let their listener bring a clear authority. *Sulṭān mubīn* is a compelling proof, a warrant that settles a matter.

The demand is repeated elsewhere in the same terms. In aṣ-Ṣāffāt: or do you have any compelling proof? Then bring us your scripture, if what you say is true (37:156–157). In Fāṭir the question is sharper: did We give them a book, so that they stand on a clear proof from it? (35:40).

The form of the demand is important. It is not asking for an opinion or a tradition; it is asking for a document. A person who has overheard the heavens would have something to show, and the verse asks him to show it.

**What They Would Have to Produce**

The Qur'an specifies elsewhere what would satisfy the demand. Bring your book, if you are truthful (37:157). Have they taken gods besides Him? Say: produce your proof (21:24). Do they have a share in divinity, or did We give them a book so that they stand on a clear proof from it? (35:40).

In every case the requirement is a revelation of their own — something comparable to the Qur'an, with a source they can name. The Meccan objection to the message rested on claims about the unseen, and claims about the unseen require a warrant.

The verse therefore applies to them the standard they refused to apply to the Prophet ﷺ, whose warrant was in their hands.

**Revelation Is Not Eavesdropping**

The contrast between the two positions is the verse's real subject. A messenger is addressed, commissioned, and given a text to deliver publicly. An eavesdropper takes fragments, in secret, with no commission and no text.

The Qur'an describes its own origin in the first terms: it was revealed by God through the trustworthy spirit into the heart of the messenger (26:192–194), and the Prophet ﷺ is told that he could not have known the accounts he recites except by revelation (11:49 in the sense applied to Yusuf's story).

The people objecting were in neither position. They had not been addressed, and they had not been given anything. The verse asks them to produce what their position would require, and the question ends there.

**The Same Image in Al-Anʿām**

The ladder is not unique to this sūrah, and the parallel use confirms its function. In al-Anʿām the Prophet ﷺ is told: if you find their denial unbearable, then build — if you can — a tunnel through the earth or stairs to the sky to bring them a more compelling sign (6:35).

The verse there is a rebuke of the demand rather than of the messenger. The people were asking for signs beyond what had been given, and the answer is that no amount of sign-gathering would satisfy a person who has decided against the message.

Read with this verse, the two passages divide the same territory. Al-Anʿām addresses the demand for more evidence; aṭ-Ṭūr addresses the claim to have already obtained it elsewhere. Both use the image of ascent to describe an access nobody has, and both close the question with the same economy.

**The Series Reaches Its Point**

This is the fourth question in the run, and the pattern is now clear. Do their minds command them? Did they create? Do they own the treasuries? Do they have a ladder?

Each question takes a different faculty or resource — judgment, creative power, wealth, access — and asks whether the objectors hold it. The answer is no in every case, and the accumulation of negatives leaves their position unsupported.

The next question turns to a specific belief they held rather than to a capacity they claimed, and it exposes the belief by comparing it with their own preferences.

---
## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:39
> Or does He have daughters ˹as you claim˺, while you ˹prefer to˺ have sons

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Belief Being Answered**

The Meccan pagans held that the angels were daughters of God. The Qur'an records the claim and refutes it repeatedly: ask them whether your Lord has daughters while they have sons (37:149), and has He taken daughters from what He created and favoured you with sons? (43:16).

The belief was not marginal. It belonged to a wider pattern of ascribing offspring to God, which the Qur'an treats as the most serious category of error. In Maryam the claim is described in cosmic terms: they say the Most Compassionate has offspring — you have made an outrageous claim, by which the heavens are about to burst and the earth to split apart (19:88–90).

The verse here does not refute the claim in general. It takes one feature of it — the gender of the offspring — and turns that feature into the argument.

**Their Own Preference Is the Evidence**

The second half of the verse names their preference: they chose sons for themselves. The Qur'an describes the preference in unflattering detail. Whenever one of them is given the news of a baby girl, his face grows gloomy as he suppresses his rage; he hides from the people, wondering whether to keep her in disgrace or bury her alive (16:58–59).

The verse in an-Naḥl states the contradiction directly: they attribute daughters to Allah — glory be to Him — the opposite of what they desire for themselves (16:57).

The argument is not that daughters are lesser in themselves. It is that the people making the claim did not believe that, and their own behaviour proved it. They assigned to God what they refused for themselves.

**The Argument From Their Own Standard**

The rhetorical form is precise. The verse does not say the claim is false and then give reasons; it asks whether the claim is consistent with the claimants' own preferences.

That method is stronger than a direct refutation because it cannot be answered by restating the belief. A person can defend a belief against external argument; he cannot defend it against his own conduct.

The Qur'an uses this method often. It asks the pagans whether they would accept for themselves what they assign to God, and the question closes as soon as it is asked.

**Offspring of Any Kind Is the Real Target**

The gender of the offspring is the entry point, not the whole of the objection. The Qur'an denies offspring to God absolutely: He has never had offspring, nor was He born, and there is none comparable to Him (112:3–4). In al-Anʿām the reasoning is given: He is the Originator of the heavens and the earth — how could He have children when He has no mate? (6:101).

The argument is not about biology but about category. Offspring implies a partner, a likeness, and a succession, and each of those contradicts what the Qur'an asserts about God.

Maryam states the conclusion: it does not befit the majesty of the Most Compassionate to have children (19:92). The verse in aṭ-Ṭūr reaches the same conclusion by a shorter route, through the preferences of the people making the claim.

**Why the Verse Uses Preference Rather Than Theology**

A theological refutation was available, and the Qur'an gives it elsewhere. This verse does not use it, and the choice is instructive.

The people addressed were not engaged in careful theology. They held a belief inherited from their society and had not examined it. A theological argument would have been answered with tradition; an argument from their own conduct could not be answered at all.

That is the method of the whole run of questions in this passage. Each one locates the test inside the person questioned — his creation, his control, his access, his preferences — rather than in an external standard he could dispute.

**The Contradiction Is Practical**

The verse identifies a belief that costs its holders nothing and contradicts their practice. That combination is common, and the Qur'an treats it as evidence about the belief.

A claim that is never tested by the claimant's own choices is not a conviction. The people who assigned daughters to God were the same people who buried daughters alive, and the two facts cannot be held together honestly.

The verse exposes the dishonesty without comment. It does not need to moralise; the juxtaposition does the work.

**The Practice the Verse Presupposes**

The preference named in this verse was not a matter of taste; it had a consequence, and the Qur'an names that consequence in the starkest terms available. In at-Takwīr the events of the day include this: when baby girls, buried alive, are asked for what crime they were put to death (81:8–9).

The question in that verse is addressed to the child, not to the father, and the form is a legal one — a victim asked for the charge against her. The implication is that no charge existed, and that the killing will be adjudicated.

This is the practice behind the preference in aṭ-Ṭūr. The people who assigned daughters to God were the people who killed daughters at birth, and the verse sets the two statements side by side without needing to add a word. The contradiction is not rhetorical; it is documented in the same Book.

**What the Question Leaves Standing**

After the question, nothing remains of the claim. If the offspring are daughters, the claimants' own preference contradicts the ascription. If the offspring are sons, the claimants did not say so. And if there are no offspring, the claim collapses at its root, which is where the Qur'an's other refutations place it.

The verse therefore closes the question rather than opening a debate, and it does so in eight words.

The next question moves from what they believe about God to what they believe about the Prophet — specifically, whether he is charging them for the message, and whether the charge is too heavy for them to bear.

---
## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:40
> Or are you ˹O Prophet˺ asking them for a reward ˹for the message˺ so that they are overburdened by debt

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Financial Objection**

After the metaphysical questions comes a practical one: is he charging you? The verse puts it as a question with an implied absurdity — as though the burden of payment were the reason for their refusal.

The objection was not invented by the Qur'an. Prophets were commonly accused of seeking gain, and the Qur'an records the accusation against Noah in the mouths of his people: we see you in clear error, and the reply includes the denial of payment (11:29 in context).

The verse's placement in the series is deliberate. The previous questions asked what the objectors had — minds, creative power, treasuries, a ladder. This one asks what they owe, and the answer is nothing.

**The Answer Is a Matter of Fact**

The denial is not an argument; it is a statement about a verifiable fact. The Prophet ﷺ had traded in Meccan markets for decades before the revelation, and his opponents had dealt with him.

The Qur'an puts the denial in the voice of the messengers repeatedly. Say: if I had ever asked you for a reward, you could keep it — my reward is only from Allah (34:47). Noah says: I do not ask you for any payment for this message; my reward is only from Allah (11:29). The same phrase recurs in ash-Shuʿarāʾ as a refrain across several prophets' speeches (26:109).

The repetition across chapters and messengers establishes a pattern: the prophetic office in this Book is uniformly unpaid, and the uniformity is part of the argument.

**Why the Objection Collapses**

The verse adds a detail that makes the objection self-refuting: so that they are overburdened by debt. The word translated as overburdened comes from a root meaning to be weighed down, and it is used for a debt that presses on a person.

If the Prophet ﷺ had been demanding payment, the objection would have been a complaint about price rather than a refutation. The verse takes it further and asks whether the price was even being paid, since nothing was ever demanded.

An objection that does not correspond to events is not an objection. It is a reason manufactured after the refusal, and the verse exposes it by stating it plainly.

**Reward as the Test of Motive**

The question of payment is a question about motive, and the Qur'an treats it as decisive. A teacher who is paid may teach for the payment; a messenger who refuses payment cannot be accused of that.

The disclaimer in Sūrah Sabaʾ is framed as a challenge: if I had ever asked you for a reward, you could keep it (34:47). The formulation invites the audience to produce the demand, and no one could.

That is why the verse sits inside the series of questions. Each question tests a possible explanation for the rejection — that the message is false, that the messenger is dishonest, that the source is inaccessible, that the cost is too high. Every explanation fails on the facts.

**The Refusal of Payment Was Public Policy**

The Prophet ﷺ did not merely avoid demanding payment; he refused it when offered. The Qur'an records the instruction: say, I do not ask you for any reward for this except love for my near kin (42:23) — where the exception is not a payment but a relationship.

The distinction matters for the verse's argument. A messenger who accepts gifts while refusing fees occupies a position that cannot be bought, and the Meccan leadership knew the difference because their own poets and soothsayers were paid.

The charge in the earlier verses of this sūrah — that he was a soothsayer — carried the implication of payment. This verse removes the factual basis of that implication.

**What the Verse Removes From Their List**

By this point the series has eliminated the available explanations. Their minds did not command the accusations; they created nothing; they hold no treasuries; they have no stairway; their theology contradicts their own preferences; and the messenger charges them nothing.

What remains is what verse thirty-three named: they do not believe. The verse states it as a fact about them rather than a conclusion about the message, and the long series of questions exists to establish that the fact is not caused by anything in the message.

The elimination is complete, and the remaining verses describe what happens to people who refuse without a reason.

**A Message With No Price Has No Gate**

Refusing payment had a consequence the objectors may not have calculated. A message that costs nothing cannot be rationed, and a message that cannot be rationed goes to whoever asks for it.

The Qur'an records the tension this produced. Sūrah ʿAbasa opens with a rebuke: the Prophet ﷺ frowned and turned away because a blind man came to him interrupting, while he attended to a notable who considered himself self-sufficient (80:1–10). The correction establishes the rule — the one who comes eagerly is the one to be attended to, and the one who is indifferent is not to be preferred.

That rule is only possible because there is no price. A paid teacher serves the payer; an unpaid messenger serves the seeker. The objection in this verse assumed that the message operated like the first kind of service, and the whole of the Prophet's practice showed that it did not.

**The Question Is Addressed to the Prophet, Not to Them**

One structural detail deserves notice. Every other question in the series is directed at them; this one is directed at the Prophet ﷺ — are you asking them?

The change of address is significant. The verse is not asking him to defend himself; it is asking him to confirm a fact that clears him. The question functions as a public statement of his position, delivered in the form of a question so that the answer is supplied by the record rather than by him.

That is the Qur'an's method with the Prophet's own standing throughout: it establishes it by asking, and the facts answer.

---
## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:41
> Or do they have access to ˹the Record in˺ the unseen, so they copy it ˹for all to see˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question of Access to the Record**

The run of questions reaches its last stage. After asking what they created, what they own, and what they can hear, the verse asks what they can read. *Al-ghayb* is the unseen, the realm of what is hidden from creatures; the verse asks whether they possess it in a form they can transcribe.

The image is administrative. A record exists, and the question is whether they have it in hand and are copying from it. The verb *yaktubūn* is the ordinary verb for writing, and the picture it produces is of people with a document in front of them, taking notes.

The absurdity is intended, and it follows the pattern of the preceding verses. A person who claims to know what God will do, or what the unseen contains, is claiming to have a source. The verse asks him to produce it.

**The Same Two Verses in Al-Qalam**

This verse and the one before it appear again, almost word for word, in Sūrah al-Qalam: or are you asking them for a reward so that they are overburdened by debt, or do they have access to the Record in the unseen, so they copy it? (68:46–47).

The repetition across two Meccan chapters indicates a fixed argument rather than an occasional remark. The two questions belong together: the first removes a financial explanation for the message, the second removes an informational one.

Read together they say that the opponents had neither a grievance about cost nor an alternative source of knowledge. What they had was a position, and the position was unsupported.

**The Unseen Is an Exclusive Domain**

The Qur'an states the rule plainly. Say: none in the heavens and the earth knows the unseen except Allah (27:65). The exception in Sūrah al-Jinn adds the mechanism: He is the Knower of the unseen, and He does not disclose His unseen to anyone, except a messenger He is pleased with (72:26–27).

The exception matters for this verse. There is a way for a human being to know something of the unseen, and it is commission. The people being questioned had no commission, which is why the question about copying has no answer.

The verse therefore does not deny that the unseen can be known. It asks who has been given it, and the answer separates the messenger from everyone else.

**Copying Implies a Document**

The choice of the verb for writing is worth examining. The verse does not ask whether they have been told, or whether they have inferred, or whether they have guessed. It asks whether they are transcribing.

Transcription is a public act with a public product. A person who copies from a record can show the copy, and the copy can be compared with the original. The demand implicit in the verse is therefore a document, and it is the same demand made in verse thirty-eight: let their listener bring a compelling proof.

The Qur'an repeats this demand in the same terms: then bring us your scripture, if what you say is true (37:157). A claim about the unseen that produces no text is, in this Book's terms, no claim at all.

**The Reward Question Answered Elsewhere**

The verse before this one asked whether payment was being demanded, and the Qur'an answers its own question in other chapters. In al-Muʾminūn: or is it because you are asking them for tribute? But the reward of your Lord is best, for He is the Best Provider (23:72). In Yūsuf the statement is flat: you are not asking them for a reward for this Qur'an — it is only a reminder to the whole world (12:104).

The phrase *a reminder to the whole world* is the operative part. A message that costs nothing and addresses everyone is not a commercial product, and the question about payment dissolves on contact with that description.

The pairing of the two questions in both chapters suggests they were the two most common objections, and both are answered by stating facts rather than by argument.

**What They Would Have to Produce**

If the question were answered in the affirmative, the consequences would be severe for their own position. A person who has the record of the unseen knows what will happen, and such a person would not need to wait for the Prophet's death or plot against him.

The verse therefore exposes the inconsistency between their claim and their behaviour. They plotted, they waited, and they negotiated — all activities of people who do not know the outcome. Their conduct contradicted the knowledge their accusations implied.

That contradiction is the argument, and it requires no external evidence. It is drawn from what they did.

**What the Record Contains**

The verse speaks of a record in the unseen, and the Qur'an describes several such records, which is why the question has weight.

There is the register of deeds, laid open on the day and read by the person himself: woe to us, what kind of record is this that leaves out nothing small or large? (18:49). There is the Preserved Tablet in which the revelation itself is kept (85:22). And there is the decree, from which nothing occurs except by measure (15:21).

None of these is accessible to a creature except through a messenger commissioned to convey part of it. The verse asks whether the opponents have bypassed that arrangement, and the question answers itself: they had no text, no commission, and no account of how they obtained either.

The demand behind the question is the demand the Qur'an makes throughout this passage — produce the document — and it was never met.

**The Series Ends Where It Began**

The questions from verse thirty-two to this one cover judgment, creation, ownership, access, offspring, payment, and now knowledge. Each one asks what the opponents have that would justify their rejection, and each answer is nothing.

The last question is the most fundamental. Everything they claimed rested on an assertion about the unseen — that the message was invented, that the day would not come, that their gods had standing. The verse asks for the warrant behind that assertion and finds none.

The next verse turns from what they know to what they intend, and the sūrah's answer to intention is shorter than the question.

---
## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:42
> Or do they intend to scheme ˹against the Prophet˺? Then it is the disbelievers who will fall victim to ˹their˺ schemes

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Turns From Knowing to Plotting**

The previous questions asked what the opponents possessed. This one asks what they intend. *Kayd* is a scheme — a plan designed to work through indirection, so that the target does not see it coming.

The verse takes their plotting seriously enough to name it, and then reverses its direction in a single clause. The schemers will be the ones schemed against.

The reversal is stated as a fact rather than a threat. There is no promise of retaliation, no description of punishment; the verse simply says where the plan lands.

**Plotting Is Answered by Plotting**

The Qur'an describes this reversal repeatedly, and the language is consistent. When the disbelievers conspired to capture, kill, or exile the Prophet ﷺ, they planned, and Allah also planned — and Allah is the best of planners (8:30). The same sentence closes the account of the plot against Jesus: the disbelievers made a plan, but Allah also planned (3:54).

The symmetry of the language is deliberate. Both sides plot; the verse does not deny that. It states that one of the two plans succeeds, and identifies which.

The formulation in al-Anfāl is blunter: Allah frustrates the evil plans of the disbelievers (8:18). The frustration is not a coincidence; it is described as an act.

**They Plot Against Themselves**

The verse says the scheme returns on its makers, and the Qur'an explains why. In every society the most wicked are placed to conspire within it, yet they plot only against themselves, and they fail to perceive it (6:123).

The failure of perception is the key. A plot requires an accurate model of the world, and the plotters in the Qur'an consistently operate on a false one: they assume the message depends on the man, that time is on their side, and that no one sees what they are doing.

The verse in al-Anʿām describes the result as self-inflicted harm that the plotters do not recognise as such. That is what this verse states in a single clause.

**Only God's Planning Is Flawless**

The Qur'an gives the reason for the asymmetry. I only delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless (7:183), and the same sentence appears in al-Qalam (68:45). The word used for flawless describes a plan with no gap in it.

Human planning is partial by nature. It works with incomplete information, uncertain allies, and a future nobody controls. The verse does not say God out-thinks the plotters; it says His plan has no defect, which is a different claim and a stronger one.

The same idea appears in the description of divine speed: Allah is swifter in devising, and the recording angels note whatever is devised (10:21). The comparison is not about cleverness but about completion.

**Satan's Scheme Is Weak**

The Qur'an extends the same assessment to the source of the plotting. Believers fight for the cause of Allah, whereas disbelievers fight for the cause of the Devil — and Satan's schemes are ever weak (4:76).

The word *kayd* is used for both the human plot and the devil's, and the description of weakness is applied to the second in order to characterise the first. A scheme whose origin is weak does not become strong by being adopted.

That assessment is what makes this verse's reversal credible. It is not a threat of overwhelming force against a small plot; it is a statement that the plot was never substantial.

**The Consolation Attached to the Same Idea**

The Qur'an draws a practical conclusion for the Prophet ﷺ from the fact that plots fail. Be patient, for your patience is only with Allah's help. Do not grieve over those who disbelieve, nor be distressed by their schemes (16:127).

The connection between the two statements is causal. Distress about a plot presupposes that the plot might succeed; the verse removes that presupposition and the distress with it.

This is why the command in verse forty-five to leave them can be given at all. Withdrawal is only possible for someone who does not believe his inaction will decide the outcome.

**Scheming as a Substitute for Answering**

The verse's position after twelve unanswered questions is significant. Every question in the preceding section ended without a response, and the challenge to produce a sūrah was never attempted.

Plotting begins where argument stops. A person who can answer does not need to scheme; he answers. The turn to *kayd* is therefore evidence about the argument, and the verse records it as such by placing it at the end of the interrogation.

The Qur'an notes the same sequence elsewhere. When the Quraysh could not meet the message, they demanded miracles instead — a spring, a garden with rivers, the sky falling in pieces, a house of gold (17:90–93). The escalation from objection to demand to plot is a single movement, and this verse names its final stage.

That is why the answer is so brief. A scheme does not require refutation; it requires the statement that it will not work.

**What Happened to the Scheme**

The verse was revealed to a community under active threat, and the outcome is a matter of record. The plot to kill the Prophet ﷺ before the emigration failed; the military campaigns that followed ended at Badr and later at the conquest of Mecca; the men who devised the strategy died or submitted.

None of this required the small Muslim community to out-plan its opponents. The verse's claim is that the plan was already defective, and the events showed the defect.

The verse therefore functions as both a warning and a diagnosis, and the diagnosis is the part the reader is meant to apply: a scheme built on a false model of how things work fails on its own terms.

---
## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:43
> Or do they have a god other than Allah? Glorified is Allah far above what they associate ˹with Him˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Question in the Series**

The interrogation that began at verse thirty-two ends here. Minds, creation, treasuries, a stairway, offspring, payment, access to the unseen, plotting — and now the question underneath all of them: do they have another god?

The verse is the shortest of the series and the most direct. The earlier questions examined capacities; this one examines the object of worship, and the answer determines everything the previous questions were about.

If there is another god, the objections might have standing. If there is not, every objection collapses at once, and the verse's second half states that in the form of praise rather than argument.

**The Question Contains Its Own Answer**

*Am lahum ilāhun ghayru Llāh* — or do they have a god other than Allah? The form is a question, but no answer is possible, because the questioners themselves did not claim to have created or sustained anything.

The Qur'an makes the same point with a conditional that has no fulfilment: had there been in the heavens and the earth gods besides Allah, both would have been ruined (21:22). The ruin is not described as a punishment but as a consequence of the arrangement — two sovereigns produce conflict, and conflict produces disorder.

The observable order of the world is therefore the answer. The verse does not need to develop the argument because the questioners' own experience supplied it.

**Tasbīḥ as the Removal of a Claim**

The second half is *subḥāna Llāh ʿammā yushrikūn*: glorified is Allah above what they associate. The word *subḥān* declares God free of a defect, and the formula is used in the Qur'an precisely where a false attribution has just been named.

The construction is important. It does not argue against the partners; it declares God above them. The distinction is between refutation and exemption, and the verse chooses the second because the attribution is not a claim about God but a claim about the claimants.

The same formula appears after the ascription of daughters (37:159), after the claim of offspring (19:91–92 in effect), and after the association of jinn (6:100). Its function is consistent: the statement is closed, not disputed.

**The Partners Had Names**

The question is not abstract, and the sūrah that follows this one names the objects of the association. Have you considered Lāt and ʿUzzā, and the third one, Manāt as well? (53:19–20). Three named deities, worshipped at shrines the Meccans visited, with an established cult and a priesthood.

The naming matters for the question in this verse. A god is not a philosophical principle here; it is a shrine with a function. The verse asks whether any of those functions amounts to divinity, and the following verses answer that the names themselves were invented: these are mere names that you and your forefathers have made up, a practice Allah has never authorized (53:23).

The test the verse implies is practical. The partners were asked for help, and the record of what they delivered is empty.

**No Partner Can Be Produced**

The Qur'an supplies a test for the claim, and the test is simple. Say: invoke those you claim to be divine besides Him — they do not have the power to undo harm from you or transfer it to someone else (17:56).

The criterion is functional. A god who cannot remove harm or redirect it is not performing the function that makes divinity meaningful to the person worshipping. The verse does not argue about the nature of these beings; it asks what they do.

That is why the question in aṭ-Ṭūr is rhetorical. Do they have a god other than Allah? The answer is available to anyone who has ever prayed to one of the partners and received nothing.

**The Series Reviewed in One Line**

The twelve verses from thirty-two to forty-three form a single argument, and this verse is its conclusion. Do their minds command them? Did they create? Do they hold the treasuries? Have they a stairway? Are they assigning to God what they refuse for themselves? Is payment the obstacle? Can they read the unseen? Will their plot work? And now: is there another god?

Each question removed one possible ground for the rejection. The final question removes the ground that all the others rested on, since every objection assumed a plurality of powers able to decide such matters.

The accumulation is the method. No single question is decisive; the sequence is.

**Why the Questions End With Oneness**

The ordering is instructive. A theological treatise would begin with God's oneness and derive everything from it. The sūrah does the reverse: it examines the opponents' claims one by one and arrives at oneness as the residue.

That order suits an audience that already believed in God and had added partners. The Qur'an's argument with such an audience is rarely about God's existence; it is about what follows from it. Once every alternative source of authority has been examined and found empty, the oneness of God is not a doctrine but a description of what remains.

The verse's placement therefore does argumentative work. It converts a series of negatives into a single positive.

**The Exclamation Ends the Argument**

After this verse the sūrah stops asking questions. The remaining verses describe the day, command patience, and instruct in praise.

The shift is significant. Argument addresses people who might be persuaded; description addresses people who have been left to themselves. The sūrah makes the transition here, and the formula of glorification marks it.

There is also a tone to notice. The verse does not end with a warning. It ends with praise, which is the sūrah's way of saying that the question of God's oneness is settled independently of what anyone does about it.

**What the Verse Leaves Standing**

Nothing of the opponents' position survives it. They have no separate god, no access, no plan, no warrant, and no complaint about cost. The verse states the conclusion in the form of an exclamation because a conclusion of this kind is not information but recognition.

The following verse introduces a hypothetical — if they saw a piece of the sky falling — and the hypothetical exists to show that even direct evidence would not change the position the questions have already dismantled.

---
## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:44
> If they were to see a ˹deadly˺ piece of the sky fall down ˹upon them˺, still they would say, “˹This is just˺ a pile of clouds.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sign They Asked For**

The verse answers a demand the Qur'an records verbatim. The disbelievers said to the Prophet ﷺ: so cause deadly pieces of the sky to fall upon us, if what you say is true (26:187).

The demand was framed as a test. If the messenger is genuine, let him produce the punishment he describes, and the production will prove both the messenger and the message.

The verse accepts the scenario and describes what would happen. The piece of sky falls. It is seen. And the response is that it is a cloud.

**The Explanation Comes Before the Evidence Lands**

The verse's force lies in the sequence. The sign is not refused; it is given, and the explanation is already prepared. *Rakʿun makdūd* — a heaped-up cloud mass — is the ordinary meteorological description of a rain cloud piled high.

That is the diagnosis the verse is making. The problem is not a shortage of evidence but the presence of an interpretation that can absorb any evidence. A falling fragment becomes weather; a miracle becomes magic; a messenger becomes a poet.

The Qur'an states the mechanism elsewhere: even if every sign came to them, they would not believe until they see the painful punishment (10:97). The sign does not fail; the disposition defeats it.

**The Gate of Heaven**

The same point is made with a stronger hypothetical. Even if We opened for them a gate to heaven, through which they continued to ascend, still they would say: our eyes have truly been dazzled — in fact, we must have been bewitched (15:14–15).

The escalation is deliberate. The first scenario is a fragment falling; the second is a person ascending through an opened gate. The second removes any possibility of misperception, since the person is inside the event rather than watching it from a distance.

The response in both cases is the same, and in the second case it is a confession of bewitchment. That is the verse's central claim: the interpretation is chosen before the observation is made.

**The Demand Was Longer Than This**

The request for pieces of the sky was one item in a list, and the full list is recorded in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ. They said they would never believe until a spring gushed from the earth, or a garden of palms and vines with rivers flowing through it, or the sky fell upon them in pieces as he had claimed, or Allah and the angels were brought before them face to face, or he had a house of gold, or he ascended into heaven — and even then they would not believe the ascension unless he brought down a book they could read (17:90–93).

The reply recorded there is a single sentence: glory be to my Lord — am I anything but a human messenger?

The list shows what this verse is answering. The demand was not a genuine test with a stated threshold; it was a moving set of conditions, each one added after the previous. The verse describes the behaviour of people for whom no sign is ever the last one.

**What This Says About Evidence**

The verse is often read as a statement about stubbornness, and it is that, but it also makes a claim about how belief works. Evidence does not produce faith in a person who has decided otherwise; it produces material for the decision already made.

The Qur'an's own account of its audience supports this. The Meccan leaders heard the Qur'an repeatedly, recognised its quality, and still classified it as poetry or sorcery. Their classification was not a failure of perception but a choice with a motive, which verse thirty-two named as transgression.

That is why the sūrah spent twelve verses asking questions rather than presenting signs. Questions address a disposition; signs do not.

**Why the Sign Is Not Given**

There is a mercy in the refusal that the verse implies. In the Qur'an's account of earlier peoples, a sign given to a people who then reject it is followed immediately by destruction. The people of Ṣāliḥ were given the she-camel and hamstrung it; the people of Mūsā were given nine clear signs.

A demand for a sign, once granted and rejected, closes the possibility of repentance. The verse describes what would happen if the demand were met, and the description explains why it is not met.

The people making the demand did not understand what they were asking for. The verse tells them.

**The Verse Prepares the Withdrawal**

The next verse commands the Prophet ﷺ to leave them until the day that strikes them down. That command follows naturally from this one: if evidence will not be used, argument has reached its limit.

The sequence is the sūrah's practical conclusion. The questions have removed every objection, the hypothetical has shown that evidence would not help, and what remains is withdrawal and patience.

There is no despair in the sequence. The command to remind in verse twenty-nine still stands, and the sūrah ends with praise rather than with abandonment. The verse simply records a fact about the people being addressed: they have made a decision that evidence cannot reach.

**A Claim About Language as Much as Belief**

The final detail is the words themselves. A pile of clouds is not a lie in the ordinary sense; it is a description that fits the appearance while excluding the meaning.

That is the form the rejection takes throughout the sūrah — soothsayer, madman, poet, inventor. Each term describes something real and misapplies it. The verse identifies the technique by showing it applied to the most undeniable event imaginable.

The reader is meant to recognise the technique, and recognising it is the practical use of the verse.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:45
> So leave them until they face their Day in which they will be struck dead—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command to Withdraw**

The verse instructs the Prophet ﷺ to leave them, and the same instruction appears at several points in Meccan revelation. Turn away from them, for you will not be blamed (51:54). Turn away from them and wait (32:30). Allah knows what is in their hearts, so turn away from them, caution them, and give them advice that will shake their very souls (4:63).

The repetition shows that withdrawal was a standing instruction rather than a momentary reaction. It was given at different times, in different circumstances, and in different chapters.

The instruction is not indifference. The verse in an-Nisāʾ pairs turning away with cautioning and advising, which are active acts. What is withdrawn is the expectation of persuasion, not the effort of the message.

**Struck Dead: The Word and Its Force**

The verb describes being overcome so completely that a person drops — the state of one who faints, is stunned, or dies. The same root is used for the swooning of Mūsā at the manifestation on the mountain (7:143) and for the collapse of the disbelievers at the first blast.

The image is physical and sudden. These are people who have been talking, plotting, and waiting; the verse describes them as dropped.

The contrast with the previous verses is exact. The waiting they proposed was a strategy of patience; the day that ends it arrives without warning and removes the capacity to continue.

**Two Readings of the Day**

The commentators differ on which day is meant. Some read it as the day of each person's death, when the state of overwhelming arrival comes upon the individual. Others read it as the Day of Resurrection, when all are overcome together.

The two readings are not mutually exclusive, and the Qur'an describes both events with the same imagery. Death is described elsewhere as the arrival of the truth a person had avoided (50:19), and the resurrection as the blast after which people rise.

Weighing them, the resurrection reading fits the immediate context better, since the following verse describes scheming failing and no help arriving, which are features of the general day rather than of an individual death. The death reading remains valid as the first instalment of the same outcome.

**Withdrawal Is Not Abandonment**

The command needs care in reading. Leaving them is not leaving them to destruction by human hands; it is leaving them to the consequences they are generating.

The Qur'an describes those consequences as self-produced. They plot only against themselves, but they fail to perceive it (6:123). The verse in aṭ-Ṭūr says the same thing by describing the day as one in which their scheming will not help them.

That is why the command can be given without moral difficulty. The Prophet ﷺ is not being told to let people perish; he is being told that the mechanism of their perishing is their own conduct, and that his continued argument will not interrupt it.

**What He Was To Do Instead**

The sūrah does not leave the command bare. Verse twenty-nine said: remind, for you are not a soothsayer or a madman. The verses that follow this one instruct patience and praise.

The shape of the instruction is consistent across the Meccan chapters. The Prophet's task is delivery, not outcome: your duty is only to remind; you are not there to compel them (88:21–22). The withdrawal here is from compulsion, not from the work.

That distinction matters for how the verse should be read. It is not a permission to stop caring; it is a description of the limits of what any messenger can do.

**The Patience That Follows**

Three verses later the same address turns positive: be patient with your Lord's decree, for you are truly under Our watchful Eyes (52:48). The withdrawal and the patience belong to one instruction.

A person told to leave a matter alone needs something to do with his attention, and the sūrah supplies it. Patience is not passivity; it is the continuation of the work under conditions that do not reward it.

The verse in an-Naḥl makes the connection explicit: be patient, for your patience is only with Allah's help, and do not grieve over them (16:127). The command to leave them and the command to be patient are the same instruction seen from two sides.

**The Withdrawal Is Bounded by a Date**

The command to leave them is not open-ended, and the verse says so. Leave them *until* they meet their day. The particle *ḥattā* sets a limit on the withdrawal, and the limit is an event rather than a duration.

That matters for how the instruction should be understood. It is not a permanent abandonment and not a surrender; it is a suspension of effort for a defined period, with a defined end.

The same bounded form appears in the parallel commands. Turn away from them and wait (32:30) — waiting has an object, and the object arrives. The verses that follow in this sūrah describe the arrival, which is why the command can be given without the community losing hope.

For the reader the point is practical. There are situations in which continued argument produces nothing, and the verse identifies them and prescribes withdrawal until the situation changes rather than withdrawal indefinitely.

**The Day Named Twice**

The verse names the day and then describes it, and the next verse names it again. The repetition is characteristic of the sūrah, which returned to the day at verse nine and again at verse thirteen.

The effect is to keep the reader oriented in time. Everything in the second half of this chapter is dated: the punishment will come, the day will arrive, the scheming will fail on a specific occasion.

For the audience, the naming was a correction of their own sense of time. They were waiting for the Prophet's death; the verse tells them what day they are actually waiting for.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:46
> the Day their scheming will be of no benefit to them whatsoever, nor will they be helped

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day That Ends Strategy**

The verse describes the day by what stops working. Their scheming will not benefit them, and no help will reach them. Both statements are about resources that function in this world and cease to function there.

Strategy works in the world because the future is open. A plan can change an outcome that has not yet arrived. On the day described here the outcome has arrived, and a plan directed at an outcome that is already present has nothing to act on.

That is the precise sense of the negation. The scheme is not forbidden or punished at that point; it is useless, which is a colder statement than either.

**“Nor Will They Be Helped”**

The second half removes the alternative to strategy, which is assistance. A person whose own plan fails can still be saved by someone else, and the verse closes that route.

The Qur'an states the same closure in several forms. There will be no protector or intercessor other than Allah, and even if every ransom were offered it would not be accepted (6:70). On that day no soul will be of any benefit to another whatsoever (82:19).

The two negations together — no plan and no helper — cover the whole of what a person in danger relies on. Nothing else exists to be relied on, which is why the day is described as a day rather than as an event: it is a condition with no exits.

**The Qur'an's Catalogue of Failed Plots**

The claim that schemes fail is not asserted here for the first time, and the supporting verses are numerous. Allah frustrates the evil plans of the disbelievers (8:18). They planned, but Allah also planned, and Allah is the best of planners (8:30). They plot only against themselves, but they fail to perceive it (6:123). Satan's schemes are ever weak (4:76). Allah is swifter in devising, and the recording angels record whatever is devised (10:21).

The variety of formulations covers different aspects: frustration, reversal, self-harm, weakness, and speed. Taken together they describe a pattern rather than an incident.

The pattern is what makes this verse's statement credible. It is not a prediction about one group of plotters but a description of how plotting fares in this Book's account of the world.

**Why the Parties Are Unequal**

The asymmetry has a stated cause. I only delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless (7:183). The word for flawless describes a plan with nothing missing from it.

A human plan is built on estimates: what the other side will do, when events will occur, who can be trusted. Every one of those estimates can be wrong, and usually several are. The verse does not say God outmanoeuvres the plotters in their own game; it says His arrangement has no gaps for a manoeuvre to exploit.

That is why the day ends strategy rather than defeating it. There is no contest on that day to lose.

**The Verse Describes Two Absences, Not One Punishment**

It is worth separating what the verse says from what it does not say. It does not describe torment here; it describes two things failing to arrive — benefit from their planning, and help from anyone else.

That distinction is important. The Qur'an's accounts of the day are full of description: the Fire, the dragging, the boiling drink. This verse adds none of it. It states only that the resources people rely on will be absent.

The effect is colder than description would be. A person who imagines punishment can prepare himself for pain; a person told that his plans and his allies will simply not function has nothing to prepare.

The two absences cover the whole of human agency. Planning is what a person does alone; help is what he receives from others. Both are removed, and nothing is left between the person and the outcome.

**The Consolation Attached**

The Qur'an draws a practical conclusion from all of this, addressed to the Prophet ﷺ: be patient, for your patience is only with Allah's help. Do not grieve over those who disbelieve, nor be distressed by their schemes (16:127).

The two clauses are connected. Distress about a plot assumes the plot might succeed. Once that assumption is removed, the distress loses its object, and what remains is patience.

This verse supports the command given in the verse before it. Leaving them is possible only for someone who does not believe his own inactivity decides the outcome, and the verse supplies the reason he need not believe it.

**The Verse's Function in the Sūrah**

Verses forty-five and forty-six close the section that began with the withdrawal command. The description of the day is complete: they will be struck down, their planning will fail, and no help will come.

What follows is a change of register. The next verse mentions another torment before that day, and then the sūrah turns entirely to the Prophet ﷺ with instructions for patience and praise.

The arrangement is deliberate. The fate of the opponents is stated, closed, and not returned to. The remaining verses of the chapter are addressed to the person who has work to do.

**The Day When All Authority Is God's**

The second absence has a stated reason elsewhere. On that day no soul will be of any benefit to another whatsoever, for all authority on that day belongs to Allah entirely (82:19).

The clause explains why help cannot arrive. Help in this world comes through delegated authority — a patron, a judge, a kinsman, an ally, each holding some power to act. On that day the delegation ends, and with it the possibility of assistance.

The verse in aṭ-Ṭūr states the result without the explanation, and the two statements together give the full picture. The failure of help is not a refusal but a structural fact about the day.

That is why the Qur'an insists on seeking protection while delegation still operates. The verse in al-Anʿām describes the people who will have no protector or intercessor other than Allah, and who will find every ransom refused (6:70) — a description of the same day from the side of those still able to act.

**A Statement About Time Rather Than Anger**

There is no vindictiveness in the verse, and the absence is worth noticing. It does not describe the plotters being punished for their plots; it describes the plots ceasing to work.

The difference matters for how the warning functions. A punishment can be resented as unfair; the failure of a plan is simply what happens when a plan is built on a false model. The verse states the second, and the reader is left with the model rather than with a threat.

That is the most useful form the warning could take, since a model can be corrected while there is still time.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:47
> Also, the wrongdoers will certainly have another torment before that ˹Day˺, but most of them do not know

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Torment Before the Day**

The verse adds something to the picture the previous verses have drawn. Beyond the punishment of the day there is another, earlier one, and the wrongdoers have it coming.

The phrase *dūna dhālika* — besides that, or short of that — places the second torment on this side of the day. It belongs to the interval, not to the conclusion.

That addition changes the shape of the warning. The previous verses described a single event at the end of time; this verse says the account begins earlier, and that most people do not notice it beginning.

**Two Readings of the Earlier Torment**

The commentators identify it in two ways. The first is the punishment of the grave, the state that begins at death and continues until the resurrection. The second is the afflictions of this life — hardship, loss, fear, and calamity understood as consequences of wrongdoing.

Both readings are supported elsewhere. The grave is described in the Qur'an in terms of a continuing state (14:27 in the sense of the believer's firmness and its opposite), and the lesser torment in this life is stated explicitly: We will certainly make them taste some of the minor torment in this life before the major torment of the Hereafter, so perhaps they will return to the Right Path (32:21).

The two readings are not rivals so much as stages. The verse is unspecific, and the unspecificity allows both to stand.

**Calamity as a Call to Return**

The second reading has a purpose attached to it in the Qur'an's own words: so perhaps they will return (32:21). The affliction is not presented as a verdict but as an invitation.

The mechanism is described in detail elsewhere. When a community was afflicted it humbled itself and called for mercy; then the hardship was replaced with prosperity and the people concluded that their ancestors had experienced the same alternation, and the warning was lost (7:94–95). The Qur'an describes the failure as a failure of reading rather than of experience.

That is what this verse says in its closing clause. The torment arrives; most people do not know what it is.

**“Most of Them Do Not Know”**

The ignorance named here is not about the existence of hardship. Everyone knows hardship. The ignorance is about its meaning — the failure to connect the event with its cause and its purpose.

The Qur'an treats this failure as the central human problem. Affliction is sent, and it produces either return or explanation. The explanation usually takes the form of coincidence, inheritance, or bad luck, and each explanation preserves the person's position while removing the call.

The verse therefore identifies a specific blindness, and it identifies it as widespread: most of them. The minority who read the event correctly are the ones the sūrah has described as afraid among their people.

**Affliction as Expiation for the Believer**

The same events have a different function for the believer, and the distinction is stated in a report from the Prophet ﷺ. Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī and Abū Hurayrah narrated that he said: no fatigue, nor disease, nor sorrow, nor sadness, nor hurt, nor distress befalls a Muslim, even if it were the prick he receives from a thorn, but that Allah expiates some of his sins for that (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5641).

The report makes the smallest possible affliction significant. A thorn prick is not a trial in any meaningful sense, and its inclusion extends the principle to everything.

That extension is what separates the two readings of this verse. The same event is a call to return for one person and an expiation for another, and the difference lies in how the person receives it rather than in the event.

**Affliction Read Correctly**

The sūrah has already shown what reading affliction correctly looks like. The people described in verses twenty-six to twenty-eight were afraid among their families, and their fear was directed at a specific danger: the scorching heat of the Fire.

Their fear produced conduct, and the conduct produced the outcome. When they speak in paradise, they do not describe their hardship; they describe the grace that ended it. The affliction was read as a warning, and the warning was acted on.

That is the contrast with most people. The same events reach both groups, and only one group treats them as information. The verse's closing clause is therefore not a comment on events but on attention — and attention is something a reader can change.

**The Verse Balances the Withdrawal**

The command to leave them in the previous verse could be misread as a statement that nothing happens until the end. This verse corrects that reading: something is already happening, and it is happening now.

The correction has a pastoral purpose. A community told to withdraw from its opponents needs to know that the matter is not suspended. The verse assures them that the account is running.

It also warns the reader against complacency. The torment before the day is not reserved for opponents; it is described as belonging to the wrongdoers, and the category is defined by conduct rather than by camp.

**What the Verse Asks the Reader to Do**

The practical demand is attention. The verse says most people do not know, which implies that knowing is possible and that not knowing is a failure rather than a fate.

The Qur'an's instruction for reading affliction is consistent: it is a signal, and the signal is meant to be answered. The answer in this sūrah is given in the verses that follow — patience, praise, and glorification at the times named.

The sequence is deliberate. A verse about unread calamity is followed by instructions for how to live inside calamity, and the two together form the sūrah's practical teaching.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:48
> So be patient with your Lord’s decree, for you are truly under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes. And glorify the praises of your Lord when you rise

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Address Turns to the Prophet**

The sūrah has moved from oaths to scenes to accusations to questions, and it ends with a direct address. The second person appears here and holds through the final two verses.

The turn is significant. Everything before it was descriptive or interrogative; this is instruction. The chapter that began by swearing on a mountain ends by telling one man what to do in the morning.

The placement is also a statement about audience. The people described as scheming and waiting have been left; the person being spoken to is the one carrying the message, and the instructions are for him.

**“With Your Lord's Decree”**

The object of the patience is *bi-ḥukmi rabbik* — with the judgment or decree of your Lord. The patience is not general endurance; it is endurance of a specific arrangement.

The decree includes the delay, the opposition, and the outcome that has not yet arrived. The Prophet ﷺ is told to be patient not with the behaviour of his opponents but with the shape of events as God has ordered them, which includes the fact that the opponents are still free.

That is a harder instruction than enduring persecution. Persecution can be resisted; a decree can only be accepted. The same formula appears in Sūrah Ṭā Hā: so be patient with what they say (20:130 in the sense of endurance under their speech), and in al-Qalam with an added command not to yield to any evildoer (68:48).

**“Under Our Eyes”**

The phrase *bi-aʿyuninā* — with Our eyes, before Our eyes — is an idiom of protection and attention. It appears in the account of Noah: build the Ark before Our eyes and by Our revelation (11:37), and in the instruction about Mūsā: I have cast upon you a love from Me, and so that you would be brought up under My eye (20:39).

The idiom does two things at once. It states that God sees what is happening, which is a comfort to the person being harmed, and it states that the events are within His supervision, which is a restraint on the person being commanded.

For the Prophet ﷺ in Mecca, both halves mattered. He was under attack and he was under instruction; the phrase covers both.

**The Decree Includes the Delay**

The instruction to be patient with the decree has a specific object in this sūrah. The decree being accepted is not only the fact of opposition; it is the timetable, which includes the fact that the opponents are still free and the day has not arrived.

That is the hardest part of the instruction. A person can endure hostility more easily than he can endure the continuation of hostility without an end in sight. The sūrah has already told him that the day is fixed and that nothing will avert it; it now tells him to accept that the fixing includes a period of waiting.

The parallel verses make the same demand. Be patient with your Lord's decree, and do not yield to any evildoer or staunch disbeliever from among them (76:24). The patience is with the arrangement; the refusal is toward the people.

**Seen by God and Watched by Men**

The phrase *under Our watchful Eyes* has a second edge. The Prophet ﷺ was being watched — by opponents who tracked his movements, by relatives under pressure from the clan, by a city that treated him as a problem.

The verse answers that surveillance with a greater one. Being seen by God is not only protection; it is also a standard. The person who knows he is observed behaves differently in private, and the phrase supplies that awareness at the moment when public support is thinnest.

Both readings belong to the idiom. The Ark was built under God's eyes (11:37) in circumstances where the builder was mocked, and Mūsā was brought up under God's eye (20:39) inside the household of his enemy. In both cases the phrase covers protection and supervision at once.

**Glorify When You Rise**

The second half of the verse gives a practice. *Sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika ḥīna taqūm* — glorify the praise of your Lord when you rise.

The commentators read the rising in three ways: rising in the morning, rising from a gathering, and rising for prayer. The first is supported by the reported practice of the Prophet ﷺ, who is described as saying on waking and on standing: glory be to You, O God, and with Your praise. The third connects it to the formulas of praise recited within the prayer itself.

The readings are compatible, and the practice is simple enough that all three can be performed. The verse attaches praise to a moment of transition — the point at which a person moves from one state to another.

**Why the Moment of Rising**

The choice of moment is instructive. Rising is when a person is least guarded: waking, leaving, beginning. It is also the moment at which the day's concerns arrive.

Attaching praise to that moment sets the direction of the day before the day sets it. The practice is small, requires no time or place, and cannot be postponed on grounds of circumstance.

That fits the sūrah's situation. A community under pressure has few resources, and the verse gives it one that no opponent can remove.

**Patience and Praise as One Instruction**

The two commands are joined in a single verse, and the joining is not accidental. Patience without praise becomes endurance under resentment; praise without patience becomes performance.

The Qur'an pairs them often. Be patient with what they say, and glorify the praises of your Lord before sunrise and before sunset (50:39). The pairing appears again in the final verse of this sūrah.

The pairing also answers the verse before it. A person told that affliction is already occurring and that most people misread it is given the practice by which it can be read correctly.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Practical Conclusion**

Everything in this chapter has been oriented toward a decision, and this verse states the decision in the form of a routine. Be patient with the decree. Know that you are seen. Praise when you rise.

The routine is available to anyone, which is why the instruction extends beyond the Prophet ﷺ in the commentators' reading. The same three elements — patience under a decree, awareness of being seen, praise at transitions — describe the religious life in miniature.

The final verse completes the routine with the hours of the night, and the chapter ends where it began in a sense: on a fixed point, this time not a mountain but a practice.

## Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr 52:49
> And glorify Him during part of the night and at the fading of the stars

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Hours Named**

The final verse completes the routine begun in the verse before it. Two times are named: part of the night, and the fading of the stars.

The first is a duration rather than a moment — some portion of the night, unspecified. The second is a precise point in the darkness, the time when the stars withdraw before the dawn.

The two together cover the whole night with an emphasis on its end. The instruction is not to fill the night but to be present in it, and especially at its turning point.

**Which Prayers the Night Command Covers**

The commentators identify the night glorification with specific prayers. Some read it as the night prayer, ʿishāʾ. Others include the sunset prayer, maghrib, with it. Others take it as a general command to glorify God through the night, which includes voluntary worship.

The parallel verse in Sūrah Qāf gives the same instruction in almost identical words: and glorify Him during part of the night and after the prayers (50:40), which supports the reading of a general practice attached to the obligatory times.

The flexibility of the readings is itself a feature. The verse is not establishing a new obligation but naming a practice that the fixed prayers already contain and that voluntary worship can extend.

**Idbār an-Nujūm: The Fading of the Stars**

The phrase *wa idbāra an-nujūm* means at the turning away of the stars — the moment when they withdraw as the light increases. The word is from a root meaning to depart or to turn the back.

The time indicated is the last part of the night, immediately before dawn. A report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās reads the phrase as indicating the two cycles of prayer performed before the obligatory morning prayer, and the parallel phrase in Sūrah Qāf as indicating the two cycles after the sunset prayer.

That reading is supported by the practice of the Prophet ﷺ, who is reported to have adhered to the two cycles before Fajr more firmly than to any other voluntary prayer (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1163; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 724), and who said of them: the two cycles of Fajr are better than this world and everything in it (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 725).

**Why the Last Part of the Night**

The timing has a logic that the Qur'an states elsewhere. And rise at the last part of the night, offering additional prayers, so your Lord may raise you to a station of praise (17:79). The night prayer is associated with a specific effect on the one who prays it.

The hour itself does part of the work. It is dark, quiet, and unobserved; nothing performed in it is performed for an audience. The Qur'an describes the prayer of the night as more impressive and more suitable for speech (73:6), and the reason given is the absence of the day's preoccupations.

For a community under surveillance and mockery, an act performed before dawn was the one act no opponent could see, comment on, or interrupt.

**The Night in the Qur'an's Devotional Geography**

The night occupies a distinct place in the Qur'an's description of worship, and the descriptions are consistent. The righteous are those who used to sleep only little in the night, and who prayed for forgiveness before dawn (51:17–18). The instruction to rise for part of the night is given as a means to a station of praise (17:79).

The hours before dawn are singled out repeatedly, and the reason is stated in Sūrah al-Muzzammil: worship in the night is more impactful and suitable for recitation (73:6). The impact is attributed to the conditions of the hour rather than to the act alone.

The verse in aṭ-Ṭūr places its emphasis at exactly that point — the fading of the stars, the last stretch before light. It is the same hour the righteous are described as occupying, which connects the sūrah's closing instruction to the portrait of the rewarded given in its middle.

**The Sūrah Ends on Worship**

The chapter began with five oaths and a warning, moved through the Fire and the Gardens, answered twelve accusations, and closed with an instruction about praise.

The ending is a statement about what the chapter was for. The warnings, the scenes, and the questions exist to produce a disposition, and the disposition is expressed in worship. A sūrah that ended on the fate of the deniers would leave the reader with fear; this one leaves him with a schedule.

The same structure appears in the sūrahs around it. Sūrah Qāf ends with the same command and the same two times (50:39–40), and adh-Dhāriyāt ends with an instruction to glorify and to be among those who seek forgiveness (51:17–18).

**The Contrast With the Waiting**

The final verses stand against the accusation recorded earlier in the chapter: we await an ill-fate for him (52:30). The opponents were waiting; the Prophet ﷺ is told to praise.

The contrast is between two ways of occupying time. Waiting is passive and directed at another person's end. Praise is active and directed at God, and it fills the hours with something that does not depend on the outcome being watched.

That is the practical difference the verse draws, and it is available to anyone in the same situation. The night belongs to the one who prays in it, whatever the day brings.

**The Fading Stars and the Chapter's First Oath**

The chapter opened by swearing on a mountain, a book, a house, a sky, and a sea. It closes on stars withdrawing.

Both images are drawn from the visible world, and both mark a limit. The mountain is the fixed point of the earth; the fading stars are the last thing visible before the light takes over. The chapter's beginning and ending are both moments of transition in the created order.

Between them stands everything the sūrah argued: that the order is not self-sustaining, that the day will come, that the objections are empty, and that what remains for a person is patience and praise. The last word of the chapter is a time of day, and the instruction attached to it is the smallest act of worship there is.
