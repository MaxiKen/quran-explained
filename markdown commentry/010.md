# Sūrah Yūnus (Chapter 10) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Late Makkan Wilderness and the Taunt of Signs**

Revealed during the intensely bitter years following the deaths of the Prophet's beloved wife Khadījah and his protective uncle Abū Ṭālib—a period marked by the abortive mission to al-Ṭā'if and unrelenting Qurayshi harassment—Yūnus reflects a context of ideological stagnation. The Makkan idolaters were trapped in a paradox: they marveled that a mortal human from among their own streets had been chosen to receive celestial revelation (*“Is it a wonder to the people that We revealed to a man from among them?”*, 10:2), yet they demanded that the promised divine chastisement be brought upon them instantly if his warnings were genuine. Sūrah Yūnus confronts this obstinacy with majestic philosophical calm. It shows that the Prophet ﷺ has no power to alter the scripture on his own initiative (*“It is not for me to change it of my own accord; I only follow what is revealed to me,”* 10:15) and that the delaying of judgment is not divine impotence, but divine mercy granting humanity time to reflect.

**The Shape and Narrative Arc**

The architecture of Yūnus unfolds in three grand movements. The opening movement (1–30) establishes the divine authority of the *Kitāb al-Ḥakīm* (the Book Rich in Wisdom), drawing evidence from the natural theater: the sun as a radiant lamp (*ḍiyāʾ*), the moon as a measured light (*nūr*), the alternating cycle of day and night, and the provision poured from heaven. It introduces the striking parable of worldly life (verse 24): a lush garden watered by rain that flourishes until its owners imagine they have absolute mastery over it, whereupon divine command strikes it by night or day, leaving it desolate as though it had never bloomed yesterday. The second movement (31–70) engages in a relentless cross-examination of polytheist psychology. It challenges them to identify who sustains them from sky and earth, who created their hearing and sight, and who brings the living from the dead. When they concede that it is Allah, it asks: *“Then how are you turned away?”* It issues the standing literary challenge to produce a single sūrah like it (10:38), exposes their reliance on pure conjecture (*ẓann*), and offers the soothing reassurance that God is a witness over every movement, conversation, and deed. The third movement (71–109) marshals the evidence of sacred history: the defiance of Noah against his mocking elders, the long liberation struggle of Moses and Aaron against Pharaoh and his sorcerers, and the dramatic drowning of Pharaoh in the Red Sea—whereupon Pharaoh tries a deathbed conversion, but is told that repentance is invalid when mortality is breached, and his corpse is preserved as a physical sign for future generations (10:90–92). The climax highlights the people of Jonah (10:98), before concluding with the sovereign rule of belief: *“If your Lord had willed, all those on earth would have believed together. Will you then compel the people until they become believers?”* (10:99).

## Verse 10:1

> Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom

**Letters That Decline to Explain Themselves**

The chapter opens with three letters that form no Arabic word. *Alif. Lām. Rā.* They are spoken separately, not read as a name, and no gloss follows them. Twenty-nine of the Qur'an's 114 chapters begin with such disconnected letters, drawn from only fourteen of the alphabet's letters — half the alphabet, in combinations of one to five. *Alif-Lām-Rā* itself opens five chapters, including this one. The striking feature is the silence that follows. The text does not decode its own initials, and the classical exegetes record readings — an oath, a name of God, a name of the sūrah, an abbreviation of a divine attribute, an opening device to arrest attention — without settling on one. The silence is best understood as deliberate. A book that explained its own mystery would remove the one thing the letters do without assistance: they hold the listener still.

**“These Are the Verses of the Book”**

After the letters comes a demonstrative, and it points at something present. *Tilka āyātu l-kitāb* — these are the verses of the Book — is spoken over a recitation in progress, not over a finished object on a shelf. The word *āyāt* carries both of its senses at once: signs, and verses. The Book is definite — *al-kitāb* — and most commentators take it as the Qur'an as a whole rather than this chapter alone, a reading supported by the identical openings elsewhere: “Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the clear Book” (12:1) and “Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the Book; the clear Quran” (15:1).

**Ḥakīm: A Book That Is Wise and Makes Wise**

The adjective *ḥakīm* is built on the *faʿīl* pattern, and that pattern can be read actively or passively — a thing that has a quality, or a thing that confers it. Both readings are recorded for this word, and both hold. Passively, the Book is wise: its statements are sound, its rulings are apt, its meaning is *muḥkam*, precise and firmly joined, a word formed from the same three-letter root. That precision is the subject of 3:7, where some verses are described as precise and foundational while others are elusive. Actively, the Book makes wise: it is a source of *ḥikmah*, the capacity to place things where they belong. The two readings are not competing so much as sequential — a speech is only capable of making its hearer wise if it is itself coherent. The same description is given of the recitation's standing before God: “And indeed, it is—in the Master Record with Us—highly esteemed, rich in wisdom” (43:4), and of the chapter that follows this one: “˹This is˺ a Book whose verses are well perfected and then fully explained. ˹It is˺ from the One ˹Who is˺ All-Wise, All-Aware” (11:1). Wisdom here is a property of the speech, and by derivation of the One who sent it.

## Verse 10:2

> Is it astonishing to people that We have sent revelation to a man from among themselves, ˹instructing him,˺ “Warn humanity and give good news to the believers that they will have an honourable status with their Lord.”? Yet the disbelievers said, “Indeed, this ˹man˺ is clearly a magician!”

**A Human Messenger Was Always the Only Workable Option**

This objection returns across the Book, and every return exposes the same assumption: a heavenly message must arrive in a heavenly body. “And nothing has prevented people from believing when guidance comes to them except their protest: ‘Has Allah sent a human as a messenger?’” (17:94). The answer is given elsewhere in blunt terms — had an angel been sent, it would have had to appear as a man, and the confusion would have been worse rather than better (6:9). A messenger who cannot eat, tire, grieve, or be tempted cannot be imitated, and a messenger who cannot be imitated cannot guide conduct. Mecca mocked the very feature that made the guidance usable: “What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺?” (25:7). Others wanted a messenger of higher rank, “a great man from ˹one of˺ the two cities” (43:31) — Mecca or Ṭāʾif — which shows that the grievance was status, not species.

**Warn First, Then Give Good News**

The commission is stated in two halves, and the order matters. Warning comes first because the audience is hostile; glad tidings follow because terror is not the aim. The Prophet's own summary of his task is short: “I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God” (18:110). A warner reminds people of an ending they are already walking toward; a bringer of news tells them that this ending has a favourable case. Neither half works alone. Warning by itself produces despair or defiance; good news by itself produces the complacency this sūrah later names as contentment with the present life.

**Qadam Ṣidq: A Standing Earned, Not Merely Given**

The promise to the believers is phrased as “an honourable status”, literally *qadam ṣidq* — a foot of truth. The idiom is ordinary Arabic rather than technical theology: to have a foot somewhere is to have a claim on it, a place where your weight already rests. Commentators have taken the phrase three ways — a rank of precedence with God, a reward from Him, or the Prophet's intercession on the Last Day — and these read better as degrees of one fact than as rivals. What decides the meaning is the word *ṣidq* fixed to the foot. A truthful foot is one that does not slip when tested, so the standing promised is secure rather than merely high. Set beside the intercession verse, “Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?” (2:255), the reward appears as a place granted by permission — and permission is precisely what makes it stable. The believers are promised a footing, and the wording insists the footing will hold.

**“Clearly a Magician” — A Charge with a Long History**

The response to the commission is a slur, and the slur is generic. “Similarly, no messenger came to those before them without being told: ‘A magician or a madman!’” (51:52). Mecca's accusation matches a template older than Mecca: “Now, the pagans are astonished that a warner has come to them from among themselves. And the disbelievers say, ‘This is a magician, a total liar!’” (38:4). The same astonishment, the same word. A charge that fits every prophet fits none of them, and its real function is to close the conversation rather than to test the claim. A variant reading of the final word gives “clear magic” in place of “clear magician”, which turns the accusation from the speaker onto the speech. Either way it is aimed at the human medium: the man is a deceiver, or his book is a trick. Both versions concede that something unprecedented has been said, and both decline to examine it.

## Verse 10:3

> Surely your Lord is Allah Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne, conducting every affair. None can intercede except by His permission. That is Allah—your Lord, so worship Him ˹alone˺. Will you not then be mindful

**Six Days Describe an Order, Not an Effort**

Creation is given a number, and the number carries no suggestion of strain. A measured period is not a struggle; naming six Days says that the making was staged, ordered, and deliberate rather than instantaneous. The Arabic *yawm* denotes a stretch of time as readily as a solar day, and no sun exists yet at this point in the account, so the six cannot be days measured by one. A report in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2789, from Abū Hurayra, fills the sequence with content: the soil on Saturday, the mountains on Sunday, the trees on Monday, the things entailing labour on Tuesday, light on Wednesday, the animals spread on Thursday, and Adam after ʿAṣr on Friday. It names seven acts where the verse names six Days, which is why commentators have weighed it carefully; what it shares with the verse is the shape — creation as a series of distinct acts, finished and then governed. The same pairing of six Days and the Throne appears at 7:54, 11:7, 25:59, 32:4 and 57:4, so this is a fixed formula, not a passing remark.

**Rule Stated as a Continuous Act**

After the Throne comes a present-tense verb, and the shift from past to present is the whole point. *Yudabbiru l-amr*, conducting every affair, describes management that never stops. The Throne is not a resting place after labour; it is the seat of an administration still running. The same sequence occurs where the heavens are raised without visible pillars: “It is Allah Who has raised the heavens without pillars—as you can see— then established Himself on the Throne” (13:2), which then adds that He subjects the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term, and “He conducts the whole affair” (13:2). Creation is followed by command in 7:54 as well, where the day and night overlap in rapid succession and the sun, moon and stars are subjected by His command. To say the world was made and is being run is to deny two errors at once: that God retired from the world after making it, and that the world runs itself. Between those errors sits the doctrine this verse states — a maker who is also a governor, and a governance as continuous as the orbit it directs.

**Intercession Exists Only Where Permission Reaches**

Between the description of rule and the call to worship stands one clause about intermediaries, and its placement is argumentative. “Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?” (2:255). The verse here is identical in meaning, and its force lies in who is limited. Permission does not restrict God; it restricts the intercessor. No one stands in a position to negotiate with the One who conducts every affair, because nothing reaches Him from outside His own order. The point is stated without qualification: “Say, ‘All intercession belongs to Allah ˹alone˺. To Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. Then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.’” (39:44). This does not close the door on intercession granted rather than seized. The Prophet ﷺ said, “My intercession is for the people who committed major sins in my Ummah” (Sunan al-Tirmidhī 2436, from Jābir b. ʿAbdullāh) — an intercession that exists because it was permitted, not because it was claimed. And the One who grants it answers to no one: “He cannot be questioned about what He does, but they will ˹all˺ be questioned” (21:23).

## Verse 10:4

> To Him is your return all together. Allah’s promise is ˹always˺ true. Indeed, He originates the creation then resurrects it so that He may justly reward those who believe and do good. But those who disbelieve will have a boiling drink and a painful punishment for their disbelief

**Origination Then Return: The Argument from the First Making**

The resurrection is not asserted here; it is inferred, and the inference runs from what has already happened. He who begins a thing can resume it, and resumption is the lesser of the two acts. The Book makes the comparison explicit: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being’” (36:79). Elsewhere the reasoning is stated as a matter of degree: “And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him” (30:27). The sceptic's question receives the same answer — “Who will bring us back ˹to life˺?” Say, “The One Who created you the first time” (17:51). Three features of the argument deserve notice. It appeals to the listener's own body rather than to testimony. It treats origination as the harder case, so the conclusion is *all the more*. And it places knowledge at the hinge: the One who made each being knows each being, which is why reassembly is not a problem of memory. Nothing is lost in the first making that could be missing in the second.

**Reward “With Justice” — and More Than Justice**

The purpose clause gives the reason the return exists: *li-yajziya*, so that He may reward. Recompense is not an add-on to resurrection; resurrection exists so that recompense becomes possible, and the reward is specified as just. Two other verses state the same purpose with a different emphasis — “so that He may ˹generously˺ reward those who believe and do good, out of His grace” (30:45) and “So He may reward those who believe and do good. It is they who will have forgiveness and a honourable provision” (34:4). Set beside this verse, a distinction appears that commentators have drawn from it: the believer's reward is described in grace, while the disbeliever's punishment is described in justice. Nobody is treated with less than fairness. Some are treated with more. That asymmetry is not arbitrariness but mercy placed on one side of the ledger, and it explains why the verse names justice for both outcomes and reserves grace for one.

**The Boiling Drink**

The punishment is described with a concrete object, and the object is a drink. *Ḥamīm* is water heated to the point where it scalds, and the Qur'an uses it repeatedly: “garments of Fire will be cut out for them and boiling water will be poured over their heads” (22:19), and “˹They will be˺ in scorching heat and boiling water” (56:42). Choosing a drink is deliberate. Drink answers thirst, the need that cannot be argued with; a drink that worsens what it was supposed to relieve is the sharpest image of a desire that is met by its own opposite. The image also answers the pleasures it replaces. Those who are content with this life and drink deeply of it are given, in the end, a drink that keeps the form of satisfaction and removes the relief. Nothing in the description is arbitrary: the punishment answers the crime in kind, which is the meaning of justice in the previous clause. Between the return and its consequences the verse inserts a short sentence — “Allah's promise is ˹always˺ true” — and grammatically it is an emphatic construction, the noun thrown forward and then confirmed by an accusative of confirmation. The effect is a statement that cannot be hedged: the promise of God is a fact in the same way a completed act is a fact. A promise from a being who conducts every affair (10:3) is not a forecast but a decree, and the verse treats the two as identical.

## Verse 10:5

> He is the One Who made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases, so that you may know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺. Allah did not create all this except for a purpose. He makes the signs clear for people of knowledge

**Stations Ordained So That Time Can Be Counted**

The moon is given *manāzil*, stations — fixed stopping places on a route, the word used for the halts of a caravan. The image is of a journey with appointed halts, and the Qur'an describes the last of them vividly: “As for the moon, We have ordained ˹precise˺ phases for it, until it ends up like an old, curved palm stalk” (36:39). Arab astronomers later formalised these halts as twenty-eight lunar mansions, the *manāzil al-qamar*, marking the moon's nightly position against the fixed stars. The purpose stated here is practical rather than contemplative: “so that you may know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺”. The same purpose appears where the question was asked directly — “They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the phases of the moon. Say, ‘They are a means for people to determine time and pilgrimage’” (2:189). A calendar is not a minor religious convenience. Fasting, pilgrimage, ʿiddah, contracts and debts all run on a reckoned month, and a community without a shared count cannot keep an obligation. The verse treats timekeeping as part of creation's design, not as a human invention layered on top of it.

**Nothing in This Was Made in Vain**

“Allah did not create all this except for a purpose” (10:5) — *bi-l-ḥaqq*, in truth — closes off a reading of the sky as decoration. Truth here carries its ordinary Qur'anic weight: the thing is real, it answers a purpose, and it was not made for play. The same claim appears in the prayer of those who reflect on the heavens and the earth: “Our Lord! You have not created ˹all of˺ this without purpose. Glory be to You!” (3:191). The negation is the operative word. A universe made without purpose would leave the human being with nothing to learn from it, since a sign only signifies if someone intended it. The verse therefore establishes the ground of interpretation before it invites interpretation: the sky is legible because it was written. This is a claim about meaning, not about physics, and it is what makes the next clause possible — signs can be clarified only where signs exist.

**Signs Clarified for a People Who Know**

The verse ends by naming an audience: “He makes the signs clear for people of knowledge” (10:5). The clarification is not withheld from others so much as wasted on those unwilling to look. Knowledge here is the condition of reading, and the Qur'an repeatedly pairs the sky with understanding — “And He has subjected for your benefit the day and the night, the sun and the moon. And the stars have been subjected by His command. Surely in this are signs for those who understand” (16:12). The link between knowledge and observation is stated as a promise elsewhere: “We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth” (41:53). What distinguishes the people of knowledge is not more data but a habit: they look at a regularity and ask after the One who set it. Ignorance, in this verse's terms, is not the absence of information but the refusal to draw the inference that the information invites.

Arabic keeps these apart consistently, and the Qur'an keeps them apart here and at 71:16, where the moon is placed “as a ˹reflected˺ light, and the sun as a ˹radiant˺ lamp” (71:16).

## Verse 10:6

> Surely in the alternation of the day and the night, and in all that Allah has created in the heavens and the earth, there are truly signs for those mindful ˹of Him˺

**The Most Reliable Sign Is the One Nobody Notices**

The verse begins with the plainest phenomenon available to a human being: night replaces day, and day replaces night. Nobody has to travel for it, pay for it, or be taught to see it. The word used is *ikhtilāf* — alternation, mutual replacement — which carries both the difference between the two and the succession of one by the other. The Qur'an returns to this sign constantly, and its recurrence in the sign-lists is a deliberate choice of evidence. “Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth; the alternation of the day and the night; the ships that sail the sea for the benefit of humanity; the rain sent down by Allah from the skies, reviving the earth after its death…” (2:164). The same pairing opens the argument elsewhere: “Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the day and night there are signs for people of reason” (3:190). A sign that repeats twice daily cannot be dismissed as a coincidence, and it cannot be missed by anyone who is awake for it. That is precisely why the verse chooses it. Evidence that can be staged, bought or doubted loses its force within a generation; evidence that arrives twice a day, unannounced and unpaid for, cannot be avoided by anyone willing to look up.

**From One Sign to the Whole Inventory**

After naming the alternation, the verse widens without limit: “and in all that Allah has created in the heavens and the earth” (10:6). The move is from a single observable regularity to the totality of created things, and it changes the character of the claim. The listener is no longer being asked to weigh one argument but to accept that the category of signs has no boundary — every object is included. The longer sign-verse spells out the range: the ships, the rain, the scattered creatures, the shifting winds, the clouds drifting between the heavens and the earth (2:164). The shorter one narrows it to weather and wind: “And ˹in˺ the alternation of the day and the night, the provision sent down from the skies by Allah —reviving the earth after its death—and the shifting of the winds, are signs for people of understanding” (45:5). The logic is cumulative rather than deductive. No single item carries the proof; the sheer number of ordered things does. A person who dismisses one sign has thousands left, and the verse's generality makes that dismissal expensive.

**Signs Addressed to a Specified Reader**

The verse does not say the signs are for everyone in the same way. They are for those mindful of Him — *al-muttaqīn*, the people of *taqwā*. The word denotes a guarded posture: someone who acts as though the outcome of his conduct matters. Elsewhere the audience is described as people of reason — *ulu l-albāb* (3:190) — and elsewhere as people of understanding or insight (45:5; 24:44). The variations are not sloppy but complementary. Reason supplies the capacity; insight supplies the habit of looking; *taqwā* supplies the motive, the sense that what one concludes will have consequences for how one lives. A person with no stake in the answer can examine the sky indefinitely and draw nothing, because nothing turns on the result. The verse therefore makes a claim about the psychology of perception: seeing a sign is not a purely optical event.

## Verse 10:7

> Indeed, those who do not expect to meet Us, being pleased and content with this worldly life, and who are heedless of Our signs

**The Meeting Is the Hinge of the Whole Description**

The verse defines a class of people by a single missing expectation: they do not anticipate meeting God. *Liqāʾ* is an encounter, a face-to-face arrival, and in Qur'anic usage it names the standing of the creature before its Maker on the Day of Resurrection. The absence of that expectation is not presented as an intellectual position but as a disposition, and the rest of the verse shows what follows from it. Elsewhere the same expectation is made the mark of the believer: “those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and to Him they will return” (2:46), and it is made the motive for good conduct: “So whoever hopes for the meeting with their Lord, let them do good deeds” (18:110). Those who reject the idea ask for it to be replaced by spectacle: “Those who do not expect to meet Us say, ‘If only the angels were sent down to us, or we could see our Lord!’” (25:21). An expected meeting governs behaviour in the meantime; a denied meeting leaves the present as the only arena that counts. That is the whole diagnosis in the opening phrase.

**Loving the Meeting, and What the Prophet Meant by It**

The expectation described here has a positive counterpart, and the Prophet ﷺ stated it in one sentence: “Whoever loves to meet Allah, Allah too loves to meet him; and whoever hates to meet Allah, Allah too hates to meet him” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6507, from ʿUbāda b. Al-Ṣāmit). ʿĀʾisha, or one of the Prophet's wives, objected that they dislike death. His answer removes the misunderstanding: the love or hatred in question is not about dying. When a believer's death approaches, he is given the good news of God's pleasure, and nothing becomes dearer to him than what lies ahead; when a disbeliever's death approaches, he is given the news of punishment, and nothing becomes more hateful to him than what lies ahead (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6507). The distinction is exact. Disliking death is human; disliking the meeting is a verdict on a life. The verse describes people in the second condition long before any punishment is mentioned.

**Contentment Is the Symptom, and the Word Is Precise**

The people described are “pleased and content with this worldly life” (10:7), and the second verb is *yaṭmaʾinnūna*, from the root of *iṭmiʾnān* — settled calm. The same root describes the highest state of the believer's heart: “Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort” (13:28). One word carries both meanings, and the object decides which applies. A heart at rest in God is at peace; a heart at rest in the world is settled on something that moves. The verse is not condemning pleasure or comfort as such. It is naming a resting place that cannot hold. Meccan life offered wealth, trade, status and poetry, all of it real and none of it lasting, and the danger described is not enjoying these things but becoming tranquil in them — treating a way-station as an address. That is why the same people are later described as knowing only the outward face of this life: “They ˹only˺ know the worldly affairs of this life, but are ˹totally˺ oblivious to the Hereafter” (30:7).

## Verse 10:8

> they will have the Fire as a home because of what they have committed

**Maʾwā: A Shelter, Not a Sentence of Exile**

The word for what the Fire becomes is *maʾwā* — a shelter, a place of resort, the lodging one turns to when there is nowhere else. The choice is deliberate and ironic. These people made this world their refuge and were tranquil in it; the verse gives them a different lodging of the same grammatical kind. The same word recurs wherever the permanence of the outcome is stressed: “But as for those who are rebellious, the Fire will be their home. Whenever they try to escape from it, they will be forced back into it” (32:20). The detail matters — escape is tried and fails, so the shelter is a shelter in the strict sense: there is no elsewhere. Elsewhere the word carries its sting as a rebuke: “So enter the gates of Hell, to stay there forever. Indeed, what an evil home for the arrogant!” (16:29), and “Then Hell will be their home—what an evil place to rest!” (3:197). A residence is not an event. That is the difference between this punishment and a penalty. One word does the work of a paragraph: it says where these people live, and it says they do not move.

**A Sentence That Waited a Whole Verse to Finish**

Grammatically this verse is a continuation, and its dependence on the one before it is not a stylistic accident. The description of the heedless was left hanging — “Indeed, those who do not expect to meet Us, being pleased and content with this worldly life, and who are heedless of Our signs,” (10:7) — and the outcome arrives here, after a pause long enough to make the listener supply it himself. Arabic does this often, and the effect is to make the conclusion feel inevitable rather than announced. Anyone who heard the first verse could guess the second, and the guess is part of the argument. The delay also has a moral function. The portrait is given in full before the sentence, so the reader is asked to recognise the state before being told the consequence. Recognition first, verdict second. A punishment presented without the portrait would look arbitrary; a portrait presented without the punishment would look sentimental. The two verses do neither.

**“Because of What They Have Committed” — the Language of Earning**

The cause is given in the vocabulary of acquisition: *bi-mā kānū yaksibūn*, on account of what they used to earn. The verb *kasaba* belongs to trade and labour — what a person acquires by his own effort and holds as his gain. Its use here does three things at once. It makes the punishment earned rather than imposed, which answers the charge of injustice before it is made. It makes it continuous rather than occasional, since the imperfect with *kānū* describes a settled practice, not a lapse. And it keeps the commercial metaphor that Meccan listeners would have felt instantly: a life is a transaction, and this is the settlement. The same finality is stated without the metaphor: “They will be in Hell forever. Their punishment will not be lightened, nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺” (2:162). Nothing is added to the account and nothing is discounted. The verse's brevity is itself part of the statement: the reckoning needs no elaboration because the earnings were itemised by the earners themselves.

The same rhetorical method appears in the summary formula used of destroyed peoples: “And who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or reject the truth after it has reached them? Is Hell not a ˹fitting˺ home for the disbelievers?” (29:68).

## Verse 10:9

> Surely those who believe and do good, their Lord will guide them ˹to Paradise˺ through their faith, rivers will flow under their feet in the Gardens of Bliss

**Guidance Given as a Reward, Not Only as a Starting Point**

The verse places guidance after faith rather than before it, and the preposition is the reason: their Lord guides them *bi-īmānihim*, by means of their faith. Guidance in the Qur'an is usually the beginning of the story — the thing that makes belief possible — but here it is stated as something given to those who already believe, which means it is increase rather than origin. The same dynamic is described elsewhere in explicit terms: “He is the One Who sent down serenity upon the hearts of the believers so that they may increase even more in their faith” (48:4). Faith is treated as capable of growth, and growth is supplied by God. The negative case shows what its absence costs: “How will Allah guide a people who chose to disbelieve after they had believed, acknowledged the Messenger to be true, and received clear proofs?” (3:86). The verse therefore states a principle that runs through the whole book: guidance is not a single event at the start of a life but a continuing supply, and it is conditioned on what the recipient does with it. Those who keep faith are guided further; those who abandon it are left to their choice.

**“Under Their Feet” — Rivers and Position**

The image is precise about position. Rivers run *min taḥtihim*, from beneath them, and the preposition has been read with the gardens and with the inhabitants. Either way the picture is of a person above the water rather than beside it, and the commentators connect it with the raised couches on which the people of the Garden sit. The formula recurs throughout the Book and is one of its most repeated promises: “Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow” (2:25). “˹But˺ Allah will surely admit those who believe and do good into Gardens, under which rivers flow, where they will be adorned with bracelets of gold and pearls” (22:23). Water in an Arabian landscape is not ornament but survival, and its abundance marks the difference between a dwelling and a garden. Placing it beneath the inhabitants rather than in front of them makes the abundance total: it surrounds the position they occupy.

**Faith and Deeds Named Together, Never Apart**

The verse's subject is not “those who believe” but “those who believe and do good” (10:9), and the pairing is a fixed formula in the Qur'an. Neither half is treated as enough on its own. Faith without works is a claim; works without faith are, in this book's terms, earnings with no one to receive them. The promise attached to the pairing is consistent across the text — admission, rivers, permanence — and the pairing is what makes the promise conditional rather than automatic. “They will have Gardens under which rivers flow… They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever” (2:25). The verse here adds the one element the others do not: the guidance that carries them there. Belief and action describe what the person did; guidance describes what God does with it. The verse holds both in a single clause, and refuses to let either side of the transaction disappear.

The third is the plainest sense in context, since the sentence ends in the Gardens. But the second is supported by the description of that Day elsewhere, where the believers' light runs before them and on their right and they ask for it to be completed: “Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right. They will say, ‘Our Lord! Perfect our light for us, and forgive us’” (66:8).

The same title appears as a straightforward statement of where the righteous will be: “Indeed, the righteous will be in Gardens and bliss” (52:17).

## Verse 10:10

> in which their prayer will be, “Glory be to You, O Allah!” and their greeting will be, “Peace!” and their closing prayer will be, “All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds!”

**Subḥānaka: The Sentence That Removes Every Defect**

“Glory be to You, O Allah!” (10:10) translates *subḥānaka Allāhumma*, the formula of *tasbīḥ*. Its grammatical force is a declaration of removal: God is far from, and above, whatever is unworthy of Him — deficiency, partner, fatigue, injustice, change of mind. It is the shortest possible statement of transcendence, and it is the speech of everything that exists: “The seven heavens, the earth, and all those in them glorify Him. There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot understand their glorification” (17:44). The people of the Garden therefore join a praise already in progress, and they do it audibly rather than inaudibly. On earth the glorification of things is beyond human comprehension; in the Garden it is the content of human speech. That continuity is the point of the verse's first clause — worship is not interrupted by death but clarified.

**Peace as the Greeting of That Abode**

The second form of speech is *salām*, and it is a greeting rather than a prayer. The word names safety, wholeness, freedom from harm, and as a greeting it means: nothing here will hurt you. It is the greeting the believers exchange and the greeting they receive: “Those who believe and do good will be admitted into Gardens, under which rivers flow—to stay there forever by the Will of their Lord—where they will be greeted with ‘Peace!’” (14:23). The direction from above is stated even more directly: “And ‘Peace!’ will be ˹their˺ greeting from the Merciful Lord” (36:58). At the gate the keepers use it as welcome and verdict together: “Peace be upon you! You have done well, so come in, to stay forever” (39:73), and on the Day of meeting it is what God gives them: “Their greeting on the Day they meet Him will be, ‘Peace!’” (33:44). The Abode itself is named for the greeting — *Dār al-Salām*, the Abode of Peace (10:25) — and the naming explains the promise in the previous verse: a place where the greeting is peace is a place where no defence is needed. The same word is used of the greeting angels bring to the dying who are upright, and of the greeting at the gate, so the believer hears it at the two thresholds of his existence and then hears it continuously inside.

**Two Directions of the Same Word**

*Salām* moves both ways, and the verse benefits from the ambiguity. The people of the Garden greet one another, and they are greeted — by angels, by the keepers of the gates, by God. “There they will never hear any idle talk—only ˹greetings of˺ peace” (19:62), and the righteous are “received with salutations and ˹greetings of˺ peace” (25:75). The absence of idle speech is the negative half of the promise; the greeting is the positive half. Social life in this world is made of talk that carries risk — flattery, argument, gossip, negotiation — and every one of those exists because people want or fear something from each other. Remove the want and the fear and what remains is the greeting. The verse is describing a community in which nothing has to be negotiated, which is a description of peace more exact than any political one.

The final clause names the closing of their speech: “All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds!” The phrase is *al-ḥamdu li-llāhi rabbi l-ʿālamīn*, which every Muslim recites as the opening of the Qur'an's first chapter (1:2).

## Verse 10:11

> If Allah were to hasten evil for people as they wish to hasten good, they would have certainly been doomed. But We leave those who do not expect to meet Us to wander blindly in their defiance

**A Prayer That Was Almost Answered**

The verse answers a request that was actually made. The Quraysh said to the Prophet ﷺ: “O Allah! If this is indeed the truth from You, then rain down stones upon us from the sky or overcome us with a painful punishment” (8:32). That is a conditional prayer for destruction, offered in sarcasm, and the verse takes it literally. If evil were delivered to people at the speed they want good delivered, the prayer would have been granted and the pray-ers destroyed on the spot. The conditional form does the work: the verse does not say God refuses to punish, but that He does not punish on demand. Human impatience runs in one direction — good now — and the divine order does not match it. What looks like indifference is a mismatch between two speeds, and the verse states which of the two is merciful.

**Respite That Looks Like Generosity**

The Book has a technical name for the danger inside this delay. “So leave to Me ˹O Prophet˺ those who reject this message. We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot understand” (68:44), followed by the sentence that names the mechanism: “I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless” (68:45). The mechanism is described in detail elsewhere: “When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair!” (6:44). The gifts keep arriving while the warnings stop feeling urgent, and the recipient reads the silence as approval. This is why the Qur'an forbids treating prosperity as a verdict. The people described here are not being punished yet, and their comfort is not evidence in their favour; it is the space in which they are deciding. That single word is the entire content of a respite.

**Respite Is Not Acquittal**

The delay has a ceiling, and the Prophet ﷺ stated both the ceiling and the severity behind it: “Allah gives respite to the oppressor. When He takes him over, He never releases him.” He then recited: “Such is the seizure of your Lord when He seizes the towns while they are wrongdoing” — the words of 11:102 (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4686, from Abū Mūsā). The hadith supplies exactly what this verse leaves unstated. Time given is not time forgiven. The respite granted to those who do not expect the meeting is the same respite described in that report — long, deliberate, and ending in a seizure that has no release. Between the two statements sits the whole of a human life, which is why the verse is placed where it is: after the description of contentment with this world, and before the history of peoples who were given the same delay and used it the same way. The report is cited here because it fixes the endpoint of the delay, and fixes it as certain.

The verse is describing a policy rather than a capacity, and the policy is stated elsewhere as a settled fact about how respite works: “Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin” (3:178).

The same phrase describes the hypocrites: “Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance” (2:15).

## Verse 10:12

> Whenever someone is touched by hardship, they cry out to Us, whether lying on their side, sitting, or standing. But when We relieve their hardship, they return to their old ways as if they had never cried to Us to remove any hardship! This is how the misdeeds of the transgressors have been made appealing to them

**Three Postures Cover the Whole Range of Suffering**

The verse lists the positions of the body — on the side, sitting, standing — and the list is not decorative. It covers every posture a sufferer occupies: the person too weak to rise, the person who can sit but not walk, the person still moving through his day while carrying a trouble. Sickness, debt and fear do not confine themselves to prayer mats, and the verse is describing prayer that happens wherever pain finds a person. The same three postures appear elsewhere in the Qur'an, but attached to a different kind of person: “˹They are˺ those who remember Allah while standing, sitting, and lying on their sides” (3:191). The parallel is exact and the difference is everything. In one verse the postures describe a life arranged around remembrance; in this one they describe the positions in which a person remembers only when he needs to. The same body, the same words, and two opposite relationships to them. The list is a small piece of realism: it describes where prayer actually happens, without approving the reason it happens there.

**A Cry That Is Sincere and Short**

The call described here is genuine. Nothing in the verse suggests the sufferer is performing. “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Who rescues you from the darkest times on land and at sea? He ˹alone˺ you call upon with humility, openly and secretly’” (6:63). The Qur'an returns to this pattern constantly, and its favourite setting is the ship: “When you are touched with hardship at sea, you ˹totally˺ forget all ˹the gods˺ you ˹normally˺ invoke, except Him. But when He delivers you ˹safely˺ to shore, you turn away. Humankind is ever ungrateful” (17:67). At sea there is no intermediary to appeal to and no ritual to hide behind, and the verse treats that as a revelation of what people actually believe. Sincerity under pressure is easy to produce; the difficulty is keeping it afterwards. Under pressure a person's real theology becomes visible, and the verse treats what is visible there as accurate: the One they call on is the One they believe in.

**Made to Seem Fair**

The final clause uses a passive: their misdeeds have been made appealing to them. Something has done the decorating, and the Qur'an is careful about who. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise” (6:44). God's part is the provision and the respite; the adornment is what the desire does to the object once provision stops being received as a gift. The passive keeps both truths in place — the person is not only tricked, since he chose the direction, and he is not wholly autonomous either, since the appeal grew on him. “This is how” ties it to precedent: the same sequence has run before, in other lives and other peoples, and it produces the same person every time.

The same movement is described with its consequence attached: “When one is touched with hardship, they cry out to their Lord, turning to Him ˹alone˺. But as soon as He showers them with blessings from Him, they ˹totally˺ forget the One they had cried to earlier, and set up equals to Allah” (39:8).

## Verse 10:13

> We surely destroyed ˹other˺ peoples before you when they did wrong, and their messengers came to them with clear proofs but they would not believe! This is how We reward the wicked people

**Two Conditions, Both Required**

The verse states two facts about each destroyed people, and neither is dispensable. They did wrong, and their messengers came to them with clear proofs. Wrongdoing alone does not bring destruction, and a messenger alone does not make a people guilty. The principle is stated as a rule of divine procedure: “Your Lord would never destroy a society until He had sent to its capital a messenger, reciting Our revelations to them. Nor would We ever destroy a society unless its people persisted in wrongdoing” (28:59). The pairing answers two opposite complaints at once. The first is that punishment falls on people who never heard; the verse denies it, since the messenger and the proofs come first.

**The Same Sentence, Repeated as a Formula**

“This is how We reward the wicked people” (10:13) is a fixed formula, and it appears verbatim in the account of the wind that destroyed a whole people: “It destroyed everything by the command of its Lord, leaving nothing visible except their ruins. This is how We reward the wicked people” (46:25). Repetition of the sentence is the point. A formula is precedent; it tells the listener that the previous case governs this one. The word translated as wicked, *mujrimūn*, names people who have committed a crime rather than people who are merely mistaken, and it is the same word used in the previous verse of those whose misdeeds were made appealing to them. The connection is deliberate. The individual described in the verse before — praying under pressure, forgetting under relief — is being placed inside a class that has a documented outcome. What began as a private habit ends as a public ruin. The formula also removes any suggestion that these were exceptional peoples punished for exceptional reasons.

**Ruins Are the Evidence, Not the Decoration**

The destroyed peoples are invoked because their remains are visible, and the Qur'an insists on that visibility: “Many are the societies We have destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, leaving them in total ruin. ˹Many are˺ also the abandoned wells and lofty palaces!” (22:45). The wells and palaces are named because they are what a traveller actually sees — infrastructure built for permanence, standing empty. Meccan caravans passed the ruins of peoples who had thought exactly what Mecca thought. The verse also fixes a time for each case: “Those ˹are the˺ societies We destroyed when they persisted in wrong, and We had set a time for their destruction” (18:59). A set time means the destruction was neither random nor immediate, and it means the delay described in the previous verse had a limit. Precedent, proof, refusal, appointed time, ruin — the verse compresses the entire procedure into one sentence and hands it to the listener as a pattern he can recognise. The listener standing in Mecca is asked to notice which item in that list he recognises. Recognition is the whole of the argument, and it asks nothing of him except honesty about what he has already seen.

The same appeal is made in a longer form: “Have they not seen how many ˹disbelieving˺ peoples We destroyed before them? We had made them more established in the land than you. We sent down abundant rain for them and made rivers flow at their feet. Then We destroyed them for their sins and replaced them with other peoples” (6:6).

The Qur'an says the same thing about the security people feel before the end arrives: “Did the people of those societies feel secure that Our punishment would not come upon them by night while they were asleep?” (7:97).

## Verse 10:14

> Then We made you their successors in the land to see how you would act

**Succession Is a Transfer of Liability**

The verse is short, and every word in it carries weight. *Khulafāʾ* — successors, those who come after and stand in the place of others — is the same word family used where the office is described as a trust: “He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others. He may test you with what He has given you” (6:165). To be a successor is not only to be later. It is to inherit a position with its obligations attached — the land, the means of life in it, and the accountability of the people who held it before. The same idea carries its warning explicitly elsewhere: “He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth. So whoever disbelieves will bear ˹the burden of˺ their own disbelief” (35:39). The verse here states the transfer and nothing else. The peoples destroyed in the previous verse vacated the land; the audience of this sūrah occupies it. What the verse adds is the reason for the transfer, and the reason turns the inheritance into a question.

**The Question the Angels Asked, Now Being Answered**

The word for successor is the word used in the first announcement of humanity's appointment: “˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, ‘I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.’ They asked ˹Allah˺, ‘Will You place in it someone who will spread corruption there and shed blood?’” (2:30). The angels' objection was answered at the time with a sentence rather than a demonstration: “I know what you do not know.” This verse supplies the demonstration. The appointment is followed by history, and history is where the question is settled — not by assertion but by what people actually do with the land they inherit. Read against 2:30, the destruction of the previous peoples and the succession of the next one is the same experiment running again, with a new group given the same position and the same freedom. The verse is therefore a statement about the structure of the world: the earth is handed from one people to the next, and each transfer is a trial.

**A Sentence That Turns History Into an Address**

The subject of the verse is not “them” but “you”. History is not being narrated for interest. The listener is being told that he is inside the pattern, standing on ground vacated by people who were watched in the same way. The Qur'an makes the same move when it explains why fortunes alternate: “We alternate these days ˹of victory and defeat˺ among people so that Allah may reveal the ˹true˺ believers” (3:140). Nothing about the alternation is random, and nothing about the present occupant's position is permanent. The verse is fifteen words long and it removes every ground of complacency a successful people might stand on — strength, wealth, ancestry and duration are all shown to be the same position held by someone else before. Nothing in the sentence is addressed to the past.

Elsewhere the scope is stated with the instrument attached: “And so We have made you ˹believers˺ an upright community so that you may be witnesses over humanity and that the Messenger may be a witness over you” (2:143).

## Verse 10:15

> When Our clear revelations are recited to them, those who do not expect to meet Us say ˹to the Prophet˺, “Bring us a different Quran or make some changes in it.” Say ˹to them˺, “It is not for me to change it on my own. I only follow what is revealed to me. I fear, if I were to disobey my Lord, the punishment of a tremendous Day.”

**What They Wanted Changed**

The demand has a specific content, and the surrounding verses make it plain: what offended the Quraysh was the Qur'an's treatment of their gods. A recitation that condemned al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā and Manāt, that mocked the intercession attributed to them, and that placed every human being alone before a single Lord was intolerable to a city whose standing, trade and pilgrimage economy were bound up with its sanctuary. The request was therefore not for stylistic improvement but for the removal of an offence. This is why the verse connects the demanders to those “who do not expect to meet Us” (10:15) — the same people described at the beginning of the chapter. Their objection is not intellectual. A man who expects no meeting can accept almost any theology that leaves his position intact, and the Qur'an's insistence on the meeting is precisely what he cannot have edited out.

**“It Is Not for Me to Change It on My Own”**

The refusal is stated as a limit on the speaker rather than on the demand. *Mā yakūnu lī an ubaddilahu min tilqāʾi nafsī* — it is not mine to alter it of my own accord. The answer addresses the smaller request and by that same stroke the larger one, since if the messenger cannot amend a verse he plainly cannot produce a replacement book. The same limit is declared in the Prophet's own instructed words: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘I do not say to you that I have Allah's treasuries or know the unseen, nor do I claim to be an angel. I only follow what is revealed to me’” (6:50). The restriction is reinforced by a statement about the source of his speech: “Nor does he speak of his own whims” (53:3). What makes the refusal powerful is that it costs him something. He is not declining an offer he finds distasteful; he is admitting, to a hostile audience, that he has no authority over the very thing they are attacking him for.

**The Refusal Is Itself Evidence**

The inability to edit is an argument, and the interpretive tradition drew it out: a man who authored a book can revise it, and a man who cannot revise it did not author it. The Qur'an makes the point from the other direction when it describes the change that does occur — and attributes it to God: “When We replace a verse with another—and Allah knows best what He reveals—they say, ‘You ˹Muḥammad˺ are just a fabricator’” (16:101). Replacement happens, and it is described as divine action with divine knowledge behind it: “If We ever abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better or similar one” (2:106). The distinction is between revelation revised by its Sender and revelation edited by its carrier. The Prophet's refusal in this verse places him firmly on one side of that line, and his opponents' own demand shows that they had assumed the other.

**“I Only Follow What Is Revealed to Me”**

The positive half of the answer is one clause, and it defines the office. The messenger is a conveyor, not an author, and the same sentence appears in his instructed reply on another occasion: “Say, ‘I am not the first messenger ever sent, nor do I know what will happen to me or you. I only follow what is revealed to me. And I am only sent with a clear warning’” (46:9). The limits stated there are the same: no knowledge of the unseen, no precedence, no ownership. This is not modesty as a rhetorical posture.

## Verse 10:16

> Say, “Had Allah willed, I would not have recited it to you, nor would He have made it known to you. I had lived my whole life among you before this ˹revelation˺. Do you not understand?”

**An Argument Built From the Audience's Own Memory**

The reply takes a different form from the one before it. Instead of a statement about authority, it is an appeal to shared history — and the history belongs to the people being addressed, not to the speaker. They lived with him. They knew his family, his trade, his temper, his habits, the forty years before the first revelation. The verse asks them to consult that knowledge, which is the one body of evidence no outsider can dispute and no argument can manufacture. This is a distinctive feature of the Qur'anic defence of its messenger: it does not offer a biography to be believed but a memory to be checked.

**“Had Allah Willed” — the Speaker Removed From the Sentence**

The first clause makes the recitation depend completely on God's will, twice over: had God willed, I would not have recited it, and He would not have made it known to you. Both halves of the transaction are placed outside the messenger — the delivery and the disclosure. Nothing is left for him to own. The same position is stated in his instructed speech: “I only follow what is revealed to me” (6:50), and in the description of the revelation's origin: “And so We have sent to you ˹O Prophet˺ a revelation by Our command. You did not know of ˹this˺ Book and faith ˹before˺” (42:52). The verse adds a detail that closes the door on the idea of preparation: he did not know the Book before it came. There was no schooling, no teacher, no period of study that could account for the recitation. The will of God is not invoked here as a shield against questioning; it is invoked as the only remaining explanation once the alternatives have been excluded.

**Forty Years in Mecca, Verified by His Enemies**

The claim “I had lived my whole life among you before this” (10:16) is verifiable by the people he is addressing, and the record confirms it. He was born in Mecca, raised in the house of his uncle Abū Ṭālib, known in trade, and called *al-Amīn* — the trustworthy — before he was ever called a prophet. The strongest attestation comes from an opponent. Abū Sufyān b. Ḥarb, then Mecca's leading adversary, was questioned about him by Heraclius, who asked: “Have you ever accused him of telling lies before his claim?” Abū Sufyān answered, “No” — and later admitted that only fear of being exposed by his own companions made him tell the truth (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7). Heraclius drew the inference himself: a man who does not lie about people will not lie about God. An enemy's unwilling testimony is stronger evidence than a follower's praise, and the verse's appeal to shared life rests on exactly that kind of evidence.

## Verse 10:17

> Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His revelations? Indeed, the wicked will never succeed

**A Question With Only One Possible Answer**

The verse opens in a form that Arabic uses when no comparison is intended: *man aẓlamu mimman* — who is more wrongful than the one who… The construction does not invite a search for a worse offender; it announces that none exists. The same formula recurs wherever the Qur'an names the gravest offence, and the offences named are always of this kind: “Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His signs? Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed” (6:21); “Who does more wrong than the one who fabricates lies about Allah when invited to submit ˹to Him˺?” (61:7); “Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah? They will be brought before their Lord, and the witnesses will say, ‘These are the ones who lied against their Lord’” (11:18). The repetition is not stylistic. It marks a settled ranking of wrongs, in which offences against revelation stand above offences against persons, because they corrupt the ground on which every other judgement depends.

**Why a Lie About God Is the Worst of Wrongs**

The ranking has a reason. A lie about a person damages that person's reputation; a lie about God damages the standard by which reputations, laws and duties are judged. It borrows the highest authority in existence for a private purpose, and it makes every listener an accomplice or a victim. The Qur'an describes the fabricator's end in a scene rather than an abstraction: “Who does more wrong than the one who fabricates lies against Allah or claims, ‘I have received revelations!’—although nothing was revealed to them—or the one who says, ‘I can reveal the like of Allah's revelations!’?” (6:93). The verse continues with the angels stretching out their hands at the moment of death, saying: “Give up your souls! Today you will be rewarded with the torment of disgrace for telling lies about Allah.” The disgrace is specified because the offence was a claim to honour.

**Success Means Reaching What You Were Aiming At**

*Falāḥ* is not vague wellbeing. The word denotes arriving at the good one set out for — deliverance, attainment, the opposite of loss. To say the wicked will never succeed is therefore a precise statement: they will not reach the objective, whatever it was. The fabricator sought authority and will not get it; the denier sought to keep his position and will lose it. The Qur'an states the outcome in a scene: “On the Day of Judgment you will see those who lied about Allah with their faces gloomy. Is Hell not a ˹fitting˺ home for the arrogant?” (39:60). The gloom is the visible form of a plan that did not arrive. Negation of *falāḥ* is also permanent in the wording — the particle used makes the failure absolute rather than temporary, which answers the obvious objection that plenty of fabricators prosper for a while. Prosperity is not attainment, and the verse is about attainment.

**Denial Is a Choice, Not a Condition**

The second crime deserves separate attention because it is the more common one. Denying God's revelations does not require inventing anything; it requires only a refusal, and a refusal feels costless at the time. The verse places it beside fabrication precisely to remove that impression. Both are described as wrong of the same magnitude, and both are described as acts rather than states — things a person does, not things that happen to him. The same description is given of those who deny and then confess too late: “They will cry, ‘They have failed us,’ and they will confess against themselves that they were indeed disbelievers” (7:37).

## Verse 10:18

> They worship besides Allah others who can neither harm nor benefit them, and say, “These are our intercessors with Allah.” Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Are you informing Allah of something He does not know in the heavens or the earth? Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate ˹with Him˺!”

**Idols Measured by What They Can Do**

The verse begins with a test rather than an insult. Whatever is worshipped is measured by two capacities: harm and benefit. The idols fail both. The same test is applied to them repeatedly, and always in the same terms: “Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, ‘Should we invoke, other than Allah, those ˹idols˺ which cannot benefit or harm us?’” (6:71). “Do you then worship—instead of Allah—what can neither benefit nor harm you in any way?” (21:66); “They call besides Allah what can neither harm nor benefit them. That is ˹truly˺ the farthest one can stray” (22:12). The argument is empirical rather than theological. Nobody is asked to accept a metaphysical claim; everyone is asked to notice what the object in front of him has never done. Ibrāhīm put the same question in the sharpest form: “How can you worship what you carve ˹with your own hands˺?” (37:95). A thing that cannot act cannot be appealed to, and the verse treats this as self-evident to anyone willing to look.

**The Real Dispute Was About Intermediaries, Not About God**

The second clause reveals what the argument was actually about. The Meccans did not deny God's existence or His creation; they assigned Him intermediaries. “These are our intercessors with Allah” (10:18) is a claim about access, not about divinity, and it explains why the Qur'an's argument are so insistent on this point. The gods named in the sanctuary — “Now, have you considered ˹the idols of˺ Lât and ’Uzza, and the third one, Manât, as well?” (53:19–20) — were understood as means of approach, patrons who could put a case. That is also why the idols are described as unable to harm: the fear they inspired was not of their anger but of their displeasure being provoked by neglect. The verse dismantles the arrangement at its hinge. If the object cannot act, it cannot plead; and if it cannot plead, the worship directed at it buys nothing.

**Intercession Granted Is Not Intercession Claimed**

The verse does not deny that intercession exists. It denies that it can be arranged from below. Ownership is stated in a single sentence: “Say, ‘All intercession belongs to Allah ˹alone˺. To Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth’” (39:44), and the condition is stated with it: “Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?” (2:255). Even the angels operate inside that limit: “They do not intercede except for whom He approves, and they tremble in awe of Him” (21:28). Where intercession occurs, it occurs as a grant. The Prophet ﷺ said, “My intercession is for the people who committed major sins in my Ummah” (Sunan al-Tirmidhī 2436, from Jābir b. ʿAbdullāh) — an intercession conferred, not claimed, and conferred on a person whose standing with God is incomparably different from a carved stone's. The distinction the verse draws is therefore not between intercession and no intercession but between a favour received and a favour assumed.

**Transcendence Stated as the Final Word**

The verse closes with *tasbīḥ*: glorified and exalted is He above what they associate. The formula appears wherever the Qur'an has just described something unworthy of God, and it functions as a correction rather than a comment: “The command of Allah is at hand, so do not hasten it. Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate ˹with Him in worship˺!” (16:1); “Can any of your associategods do any of this? Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate with Him ˹in worship˺!” (30:40). The pairing in the second example is instructive — creation, provision, death and resurrection are listed, then the question is asked, then the glorification follows.

## Verse 10:19

> Humanity was once nothing but a single community ˹of believers˺, but then they differed. Had it not been for a prior decree from your Lord, their differences would have been settled ˹at once˺

**Difference Is Built Into the Arrangement**

The verse does not present the splitting as a failure of the plan. Elsewhere the same fact is stated as something that will continue: “Had your Lord so willed, He would have certainly made humanity one single community ˹of believers˺. They will always ˹choose to˺ differ” (11:118), and “Had Allah willed, He could have easily made you one community ˹of believers˺, but He leaves to stray whoever He wills and guides whoever He wills” (16:93). The conditional form — had He willed — is the key. Uniformity was possible; it was not chosen. A world of compelled agreement would contain no belief, only compliance, and compliance is not what the test measures. “If Allah had willed, succeeding generations would not have fought ˹among themselves˺ after receiving the clear proofs. But they differed—some believed while others disbelieved” (2:253). Difference is the visible cost of a freedom that has to exist for the test to be real. The cost is not trivial, and the verse does not pretend otherwise. Communities split, books are read against one another, and people are harmed by the divisions. What is asserted is that the alternative was not harmony but compulsion.

**The Difference Was Over the Books**

What people divided about is named precisely, and the naming is unflattering to all parties. “And no one disputed the Scriptures except the very people who received them after clear proofs had come to them—out of jealousy” (2:213). The disputes were not between the ignorant and the informed but inside the communities that held the revelation. The same diagnosis appears twice more: “They did not split ˹into sects˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them” (42:14), and “But it was not until knowledge came to them that they differed out of mutual envy” (45:17). In each case the sequence is the same — knowledge arrives, then division follows, and the motive given is rivalry rather than confusion. That is a serious claim about religious history, and it locates the problem in the recipients rather than in the texts. The books were clear; the reading of them was contested, and the contest had a motive. Naming that motive is the verse's sharpest contribution. A difference caused by confusion can be cured by explanation; a difference caused by rivalry cannot, because explanation is not what the parties are after.

**The Prior Decree: Judgment Postponed, Not Cancelled**

The second sentence explains why the disagreement has not already been resolved. A word had gone before from the Lord — a decree already issued — and without it the matter would have been settled on the spot. The decree is the appointed term: judgment is fixed for a time, and until that time the dispute runs. The same conditional appears with the term named explicitly: “Had it not been for a prior decree from your Lord for an appointed term, the matter would have certainly been settled between them ˹at once˺” (42:14). And the settlement is promised with its content: “To Allah you will all return, then He will inform you ˹of the truth˺ regarding your differences” (5:48). The postponement is therefore not indifference. Every contested question in this verse's scope has a scheduled hearing, and the verse tells the listener both that the hearing exists and that it has not happened yet.

## Verse 10:20

> They ask, “Why has no ˹other˺ sign been sent down to him from his Lord?” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹The knowledge of˺ the unseen is with Allah alone. So wait! I too am waiting with you.”

**A Demand for a Sign While the Sign Is Being Recited**

The question is asked of a man in the middle of reciting a book they cannot match. The demand is not for evidence in general but for a particular kind of evidence: something visible, spectacular, and other than the Qur'an. The same question is recorded in the same words elsewhere — “They ask, ‘Why has no ˹other˺ sign been sent down to him from his Lord?’” (6:37) — and the answer there begins by conceding God's power: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Allah certainly has the power to send down a sign’—though most of them do not know” (6:37). The requesters are not confused about whether God can act. They are setting terms. The verse answers them by refusing to negotiate the terms at all, and the refusal is grounded in the messenger's position rather than in God's ability. Nothing here disputes the sincerity of a genuine seeker; the surrounding verses make clear that this is a demand from people who had already decided, and the reply is calibrated to that fact.

**The Signs They Had in Mind**

The Qur'an records what the requests looked like when spelled out. They were material: “They challenge ˹the Prophet˺, ‘We will never believe in you until you cause a spring to gush forth from the earth for us’” (17:90), followed by demands for a garden, a house of gold, and a piece of the sky dropped on them. Similar requests appear at 13:7 — “If only a sign could be sent down to him from his Lord” — and 13:27. The verse explains why granting them would not have produced faith: “If We willed, We could send down upon them a ˹compelling˺ sign from the heavens, leaving their necks bent in ˹utter˺ submission to it” (26:4). A sign of that force would leave the necks bent — submission produced by overwhelm rather than by conviction. That is precisely what the arrangement of the test excludes. The demanders wanted a miracle that would settle the matter; the verse shows that such a miracle would settle it by destroying the conditions under which belief counts as belief.

**The Unseen Belongs to God Alone**

The answer given to the Prophet is a statement about knowledge, not about power. The unseen — *al-ghayb*, everything outside created perception, including the timing and the choice of signs — is God's alone. The same restriction is stated where the whole affair is being closed: “To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the knowledge of what is hidden in the heavens and the earth. And to Him all matters are returned” (11:123), and where the Hour is asked about: “They ask you as if you had full knowledge of it. Say, ‘That knowledge is only with Allah, but most people do not know’” (7:187). The messenger has no key to the treasury of signs, and he cannot schedule a demonstration on request. This is the same limit declared in the verses before it — he cannot alter the recitation, and he cannot produce signs. Both limits follow from one fact: he is a messenger, and a messenger's competence extends only to what he has been given. The answer is therefore no deflection. It states where the decision about signs actually sits, and it leaves the demanders addressing the only party able to grant one.

## Verse 10:21

> When We give people a taste of mercy after being afflicted with a hardship, they swiftly devise plots against Our revelations! Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah is swifter in devising ˹punishment˺. Surely Our messenger-angels record whatever you devise.”

**The Turn Happens at Speed**

The word doing the work here is “swiftly”. The verse does not describe a slow slide back into unbelief; it describes an immediate reversal, and the speed is the offence. The same observation is made in four other places, and each one notes the haste. “But if We give them a taste of prosperity after being touched with adversity, they say, ‘My ills are gone,’ and become totally prideful and boastful” (11:10). “If We give people a taste of mercy, they become prideful ˹because˺ of it. But if they are afflicted with an evil for what their hands have done, they instantly fall into despair” (30:36). “And if We let someone taste a mercy from Us, they become prideful ˹because˺ of it. But when afflicted with evil because of what their hands have done, then one becomes totally ungrateful” (42:48). The pattern is symmetrical and unflattering: hardship produces prayer, relief produces arrogance, and neither produces reflection. The verse is naming a reflex rather than a decision, which is why it can be stated as a general rule about people rather than a report about a few.

**The Drought, the Prayer, and the Rain**

Commentators connect this verse to a specific event: a severe drought that struck Mecca, after which the Quraysh asked the Prophet ﷺ to pray for rain, and rain came in abundance. The request is recorded in the interpretive tradition as an approach made to him during the hardship, and the mercy in this verse is the rain that followed. Mālik narrated that a man entered the mosque on a Friday while the Prophet ﷺ was delivering the khutba and said: “O Allah's Messenger! The livestock are dying and the roads are cut off; so please pray to Allah for rain.” The Prophet raised his hands and said, “O Allah! Bless us with rain!” three times. The following Friday a man came through the same gate asking him to pray for it to stop (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1013).

**God Is Swifter in Devising**

The answer is given in the same vocabulary, which makes it a direct reply rather than a change of subject. They devise; God devises, and His devising is faster. The comparison is stated twice more in the same terms: “And the disbelievers made a plan ˹against Jesus˺, but Allah also planned —and Allah is the best of planners” (3:54), and “Those ˹disbelievers˺ before them ˹secretly˺ planned, but Allah has the ultimate plan. He knows what every soul commits” (13:42). God's devising is understood as His disposal of the outcome — the way events are arranged so that a scheme arrives at a result its author did not intend. It is not a counter-plot in the human sense, since it needs no concealment and no timing. The warning attached to it is about false security: “Did they feel secure against Allah's planning? None would feel secure from Allah's planning except the losers” (7:99). The person who has just been given rain and has begun to explain it away is, in the verse's terms, in exactly that condition.

**The Plotters Are Being Written Down**

The final clause removes the privacy on which plotting depends. The messengers — the angels assigned to record — write down whatever is devised. The same fact is stated in an oath: “while you are certainly observed by vigilant, noble scribes, who know whatever you do” (82:10–12), and “by the hands of angel-scribes, honourable, upright” (80:15–16). A scheme that is recorded is no longer a scheme; it is evidence. The verse therefore answers the plotter on three levels at once. Speed: God's disposal is faster. Secrecy: the recording removes it. Efficacy: the outcome belongs to the One who conducts every affair. Nothing is left of the plot except the entry in the book, and the entry is what the person will be shown.

## Verse 10:22

> He is the One Who enables you to travel through land and sea. And it so happens that you are on ships, sailing with a favourable wind, to the passengers’ delight. Suddenly, the ships are overcome by a gale wind and those on board are overwhelmed by waves from every side, and they assume they are doomed. They cry out to Allah ˹alone˺ in sincere devotion, “If You save us from this, we will certainly be grateful.”

**The Verse Begins With a Benefit, Not a Threat**

The opening is a statement of provision: He carries you across land and sea. The verb carries the sense of being borne by something provided for the purpose — roads, mounts, hulls, sails. The sea is included in the benefaction, and the Qur'an names it as such elsewhere: “And He is the One Who has subjected the sea, so from it you may eat tender seafood and extract ornaments to wear. And you see the ships ploughing their way through it, so you may seek His bounty and give thanks ˹to Him˺” (16:14). The order matters for how the rest of the verse reads. The storm is not presented as the natural condition of the sea; it is an interruption of a system that ordinarily works. A person who begins from the benefit cannot treat the danger as the whole story, and a person who begins from the danger cannot see that he has been carried a thousand times before. The verse starts where gratitude starts.

**The Gale and the Waves From Every Side**

The description of the crisis is exact about direction. The waves come from every side, and the passengers conclude that they are encompassed — surrounded, closed in, with no exit. The same scene appears with a sharper image: “And as soon as they are overwhelmed by waves like mountains, they cry out to Allah ˹alone˺ in sincere devotion. But when He delivers them ˹safely˺ to shore, only some become relatively grateful” (31:32). Waves like mountains is not exaggeration for its own sake; it removes the possibility of swimming, of swimming to shore, of any human response at all. The verse's theological point depends on that totality. When every option has been taken away, what remains is not a strategy but an address — and the verse reports what the address is.

**“In Sincere Devotion” — What the Storm Exposes**

The phrase *mukhliṣīna lahu d-dīn* — devoting religion completely to Him — is the key to the verse. In the storm, devotion is pure. No intermediary is called, no idol is named, no patron is appealed to; the religion of the person aboard is, for those minutes, exactly what monotheism claims it always is. The same observation is made at sea in another place: “If they happen to be aboard a ship ˹caught in a storm˺, they cry out to Allah ˹alone˺ in sincere devotion. But as soon as He delivers them ˹safely˺ to shore, they associate ˹others with Him once again˺” (29:65). The Qur'an treats this as evidence rather than as anecdote.

## Verse 10:23

> But as soon as He rescues them, they transgress in the land unjustly. O humanity! Your transgression is only against your own souls. ˹There is only˺ brief enjoyment in this worldly life, then to Us is your return, and then We will inform you of what you used to do

**The Harm Comes Back on the Doer**

“Your transgression is only against your own souls” (10:23) is a statement of consequence rather than a consolation. It tells the hearer where the injury lands. A transgression appears to gain something — wealth taken, a rival humiliated, an appetite satisfied — and the verse says the gain is not where it looks. The same principle is stated in the general register: “No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another. Then to your Lord is your return, and He will inform you of what you used to do” (39:7). Nobody absorbs the cost on the transgressor's behalf, and nothing external carries it away. The interpretive tradition reads this clause with the next as a single proposition under one vocalisation: your transgression against yourselves is nothing but the enjoyment of this worldly life — the whole of what it buys, and no more. Read that way, the sentence states the exchange rate: a lasting injury for a temporary pleasure.

**Enjoyment, and the Word for It**

The thing gained is called *matāʿ* — an enjoyment, a brief provision for use, the word used of goods that are consumed rather than kept. The Qur'an uses it consistently to mark shortness: “Whatever ˹pleasure˺ you have been given is no more than ˹a fleeting˺ enjoyment and adornment of this worldly life. But what is with Allah is far better and more lasting. Will you not then understand?” (28:60). The comparison is drawn where the audience is asked directly: “Do you prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter? The enjoyment of this worldly life is insignificant compared to that of the Hereafter” (9:38). And the verdict is stated flatly: “Every soul will taste death. And you will only receive your full reward on the Day of Judgment… whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment” (3:185). The word choice does the argument. A *matāʿ* is not a possession; it is something used up, and the person who trades his soul for it has traded a permanent thing for a consumable.

**The Return, and Being Told What One Did**

The verse ends with two futures: a return, and an informing. “Then you will be returned to the Knower of the seen and unseen, and He will inform you of what you used to do” (62:8); “To Allah you will all return, and He will inform you of what you used to do” (5:105). The informing needs explanation, since God already knows. What is promised is not new information for Him but the manifestation of the record to the person — the moment at which a life is shown to the one who lived it, without interpretation or excuse. That is why the formula appears at the end of so many passages about conduct. It closes the case. The verse's structure therefore runs from a specific scene to an universal procedure: a man is saved from drowning, transgresses on the beach, is told that the injury is his own, that the gain is temporary, and that the account will be read back to him. Every clause is a step, and the last one is the reason the earlier ones matter. The verse moves from a beach to a courtroom in four sentences, and it does so without raising its voice, because the procedure it describes does not need emphasis to be certain.

The wider statement of the same fact is stark: “Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people's hands have done, so that Allah may cause them to taste ˹the consequences of˺ some of their deeds and perhaps they might return ˹to the Right Path˺” (30:41).

## Verse 10:24

> The life of this world is just like rain We send down from the sky, producing a mixture of plants which humans and animals consume. Then just as the earth looks its best, perfectly beautified, and its people think they have full control over it, there comes to it Our command by night or by day, so We mow it down as if it never flourished yesterday! This is how We make the signs clear for people who reflect

**A Parable Built From the Ordinary**

The comparison is taken from farming, and every element in it is familiar to the audience: rain, mixed vegetation, grazing animals, a field at its peak. The Qur'an uses this sequence repeatedly as its standard illustration of a life cycle: “Do you not see that Allah sends down rain from the sky—channelling it through streams in the earth—then produces with it crops of various colours, then they dry up and you see them wither, and then He reduces them to chaff? Surely in this is a reminder for people of reason” (39:21). The longer version adds the human element: “This is like rain that causes plants to grow, to the delight of the planters. But later the plants dry up and you see them wither, then they are reduced to chaff” (57:20). Choosing the ordinary is the point. A parable about a rare catastrophe would allow the listener to place himself outside it; a parable about a harvest does not, because everyone has eaten and everyone has seen a field cut.

**“They Think They Have Full Control Over It”**

The delusion is named in a single phrase, and it is the hinge of the parable. The people of the earth conclude that they are capable of it, that they have mastered it, that its produce is theirs by competence. The claim is not spoken aloud in the verse; it is reported as a thought, which makes it harder to disown. Nobody in the field is making an argument against God. He is simply assuming that the system is his. That assumption is what the command destroys. The same delusion is described in the shorter parable: “˹It is˺ like the plants of the earth, thriving when sustained by the rain We send down from the sky. Then they ˹soon˺ turn into chaff scattered by the wind” (18:45). The wind does the work in that version; here it is the command.

**The Command Comes by Night or by Day**

The phrase “by night or by day” is understood as meaning at any moment — the two halves of the clock covering all of it. The timing is not only unknown but indifferent: the destruction does not wait for a convenient hour, and it does not announce itself. Commentators have taken the command here as a violent wind, a flood, or a blast of some kind, and the range is the point; the specific instrument matters less than the fact that it arrives from outside the system the gardener thought he controlled. The general statement of the same fact is shorter and applies to the gardener himself: “Every soul will taste death” (3:185). The field and the farmer share one clock. The parable's power comes from that overlap — a person who has understood the harvest can be made to understand his own life, because both are cut at a time neither of them chose.

## Verse 10:25

> And Allah invites ˹all˺ to the Home of Peace and guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path

**Dār al-Salām: The Name Explains the Place**

The destination is named, and the name is theological. *Salām* is one of God's names — “He is Allah—there is no god except Him: the King, the Most Holy, the AllPerfect, the Source of Serenity” (59:23) — so an abode called the Home of Peace is an abode named after its Owner. The same title is given where the destination is described as a relationship: “They will have the Home of Peace with their Lord, Who will be their Guardian because of what they used to do” (6:127). The phrase “with their Lord” is the operative part. The contrast with the previous two verses is deliberate and complete. The world is a field that is cut at an unknown hour; this is the home of settlement — “O my people! This worldly life is only ˹a fleeting˺ enjoyment, whereas the Hereafter is truly the home of settlement” (40:39). Two abodes are being compared, and the comparison is not between pleasures of different intensity but between a place that ends and a place that does not.

**Peace Means Safety From Ending**

The interpretive tradition draws out the connection between *salām* and *salāmah* — peace and safety — and the link is what gives the name its weight. The inhabitants of Paradise are at peace because they are safe: safe from death, from loss, from decline, from the arrival of a command by night or by day. Nothing in that abode can be taken away, and that is precisely what the previous parable showed the world cannot promise. The description of the abode confirms it: “There they will never hear any idle talk—only ˹greetings of˺ peace. And there they will have their provisions morning and evening” (19:62). Every form of harm that the world distributes — bereavement, sickness, betrayal, decay — is an interruption, and the Home of Peace is defined as the place where interruption does not occur. The name is therefore a statement about permanence, not about mood. The same connection explains why the greeting of that abode is peace and why its inhabitants hear nothing idle: a place safe from ending has no need of defence, negotiation, or reassurance.

**The Straight Path Is What an Invitation Requires**

An invitation without a route is a wish. The second half of the verse supplies the road: the Straight Path. The pairing is standard in the Qur'an, where guidance is consistently described as a path rather than a state, and the messenger's role is described in the same terms: “But We have made it a light, by which We guide whoever We will of Our servants. And you are truly leading ˹all˺ to the Straight Path” (42:52). The path metaphor does specific work here. A path is walkable by ordinary people, it has a direction, and it can be missed — none of which is true of a mood or a feeling. The invitation to the Home of Peace is therefore an invitation to walk somewhere, and the guidance mentioned in the same breath is what makes the walking possible. The verse refuses to separate the destination from the route, which is why the call is universal while the arrival is not.

## Verse 10:26

> Those who do good will have the finest reward and ˹even˺ more. Neither gloom nor disgrace will cover their faces. It is they who will be the residents of Paradise. They will be there forever

**The Finest, and Then More Than the Finest**

The promise is stated in two parts, and the second part is what makes the first remarkable. Those who do good receive *al-ḥusnā* — the finest, the best possible thing — and *ziyādah*, an increase beyond it. A reward described as the best would already be complete; adding to it means the scale is not closed. The same phrase appears where the response to God's call is described: “Those who respond to ˹the call of˺ their Lord will have the finest reward. As for those who do not respond to Him, even if they were to have everything in the world twice over, they would certainly offer it to ransom themselves” (13:18). The comparison inside that verse is worth holding: the finest reward is set against everything in the world doubled, and the doubled world is the losing side. The increase promised here is not a bonus tacked onto a transaction; it is the sign that the transaction is not the right model at all.

**What the Increase Is**

The interpretive tradition identifies the increase with the vision of God in the Hereafter, and the identification rests on a report. Ṣuhayb narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said, explaining this verse: when the people of Paradise have entered Paradise, a caller will call out that they have a promise from God which He wishes to fulfil. They will answer: has He not brightened our faces, admitted us to Paradise, and saved us from the Fire? Then the veil will be lifted, and nothing given to them will be dearer to them than looking at their Lord (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 181). The Qur'an's own words on that Day are two short clauses: “On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, looking at their Lord” (75:22–23). The increase is therefore not an object added to a list of rewards but a change in the kind of thing being given — from what God provides to God Himself being seen. That reading explains why the increase is not itemised. Every other reward in the Qur'an's descriptions of Paradise is a thing — gardens, rivers, silk, fruit — and a list of things can be completed. The increase is kept off the list because it is not a thing, and the verse's silence about its nature is the only way to stop it being counted beside the rest. That silence also explains the verse's next move. A reward beyond description is followed at once by a description of what it does to a face, which is the only way such a reward can be spoken of at all.

**Faces That Are Not Covered**

The negative promise is as precise as the positive one. Neither gloom nor disgrace will cover their faces, and both nouns matter. Gloom is what grief looks like from outside; disgrace is what shame looks like. The faces of these people will carry neither, because they will have nothing to grieve over and nothing to be ashamed of. The Qur'an states the same fact positively: “On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright” (75:22), and it sets the two groups side by side elsewhere: “On that Day some faces will be bright while others gloomy” (3:106), with the bright-faced “in Allah's mercy, where they will remain forever” (3:107). Choosing the face as the site of the promise is deliberate. The face is where a person is known and where his state shows; a promise about the face is a promise about being seen without fear. The negative form is also more informative than a positive one would be. To say that gloom will not cover a face is to say that everything producing gloom has been removed — loss, regret, separation, the sight of what might have been.

## Verse 10:27

> As for those who commit evil, the reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. Humiliation will cover them—with no one to protect them from Allah—as if their faces were covered with patches of the night’s deep darkness. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever

**Proportion, Not Excess**

The verse opens with a rule of measure, and the rule is defensive: the reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. Nothing more. The Qur'an states the same principle with the arithmetic made explicit: “Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold. But whoever comes with a bad deed will be punished for only one. None will be wronged” (6:160), and “The reward of an evil deed is its equivalent. But whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah” (42:40). The asymmetry is deliberate and it runs against the punishment, not in its favour. Good is multiplied; evil is matched. The verse here states the matching side, and it does so before describing the punishment, so that the description arrives inside a declared limit. Stating the rule before the imagery is deliberate. The imagery of punishment is vivid, and a vivid image without a declared limit invites exaggeration. Nothing in what follows exceeds what was earned, and the reader is told this in the first clause rather than being asked to infer it later. The limit also answers an objection the verse anticipates. A punishment exceeding its cause would be injustice, and this sūrah has already said the return exists so that reward and punishment can be rendered justly. Proportion is what makes the outcome defensible.

**Humiliation as a Covering**

The second clause describes what will come over them: *dhilla*, abasement, the state of being brought low. The verb is one of covering, and the covering is not applied to their bodies but to their condition. Humiliation in the Qur'an is the opposite of the arrogance that produced the evil deeds, and it is described in exactly those terms: “On the Day of Judgment you will see those who lied about Allah with their faces gloomy. Is Hell not a ˹fitting˺ home for the arrogant?” (39:60). The gloom on the face is the visible form of a claim that has been withdrawn. A person who acted as though he were beyond reach is now inside a situation he cannot influence, and the covering is that situation made apparent. Nothing external is added to produce it; it is the removal of what he had assumed.

**No Protector Between Them and Allah**

The clause in the middle is the one that answers the theology attacked earlier in this chapter. “With no one to protect them from Allah” (10:27) — no ally, no patron, no intercessor standing between them and the outcome. The same phrase describes the Day in general: “the Day you will ˹try in vain to˺ turn your backs and run away, with no one to protect you from Allah. And whoever Allah leaves to stray will be left with no guide” (40:33). The verse is closing the door the idolaters had opened when they said their gods would plead for them. Intercession exists, but it exists by permission, and permission does not extend to those who assigned partners to the One who grants it. The protection they relied on was not refused at the last moment; it never existed, and the verse states the fact at the moment when the discovery cannot be avoided. The clause also explains why the humiliation is total. A person with an ally is never fully abased, because the possibility of rescue remains; removing the protector is what makes the covering complete.

The same organ carries both outcomes, which is why the Qur'an returns to it: “while ˹other˺ faces will be dusty, cast in gloom” (80:40–41).

---

## Verse 10:28

> ˹Consider˺ the Day We will gather them all together then say to those who associated others ˹with Allah in worship˺, “Stay in your places—you and your associate-gods.” We will separate them from each other, and their associate-gods will say, “It was not us that you worshipped

**The Partners Include More Than Stone**

A partner is anything worshipped alongside God, and the category is wider than idols. It includes human beings followed without warrant, and it includes one's own desire — a point the Qur'an makes twice in the same words: “Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ the one who has taken their own desires as their god? Will you then be a keeper over them?” (25:43), and “Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god? ˹And so˺ Allah left them to stray knowingly” (45:23). The Sufi tradition drew the practical conclusion. ʿAyn al-Quḍāt al-Hamadhānī (d. 525/1131) wrote that if a person wants self-knowledge he must abandon habit-worship, because habit-worship is idol-worship. The observation is sharp: an unexamined routine functions exactly as a god does, receiving the obedience that should be given on evidence. The verse's scene applies to that case as fully as to a carved image. The wider the category, the more readers the verse has. A person who would never bow to a statue may still be governed by an appetite, and the question here is addressed to whatever receives his obedience without examination.

**“It Was Not Us That You Worshipped”**

The denial comes from the objects themselves, and it is total. They do not claim to have been honoured; they deny the relationship altogether. The same disclaimer is stated as a future fact: “If you call upon them, they cannot hear your calls. And if they were to hear, they could not respond to you. On the Day of Judgment they will disown your worship ˹of them˺” (35:14). And the ground of the disclaimer is their emptiness: “Call upon those you claim ˹to be divine˺ besides Allah. They do not have ˹even˺ an atom's weight either in the heavens or the earth, nor do they have any share in ˹governing˺ them. Nor is any of them a helper to Him” (34:22). The denial is not a betrayal in the ordinary sense, since there was never a bond to betray. That is the difference between this denial and a human betrayal: a person can break a bond that existed, while an object can only reveal that none did. The worshippers projected a relationship onto something that had no interior, and the Day simply reports what was always the case.

**Idols Given the Power to Speak**

The scene requires the objects to speak, and the Qur'an addresses the difficulty explicitly. In this world an idol cannot answer: they are described elsewhere as unable to speak, and Ibrāhīm's rebuke turns on it. On the Day, speech is given to things that had none, as part of a wider testimony: “When they reach it, their ears, eyes, and skin will testify against what they used to do. They will ask their skin ˹furiously˺, ‘Why have you testified against us?’ It will say, ‘We have been made to speak by Allah, Who causes all things to speak’” (41:20–21). The answer given by the skin is the answer to the whole scene. The God who made the tongue in the first place can make anything speak, and the testimony of a person's own body is harder to dispute than the testimony of a witness. The idols' denial in this verse is the same kind of speech — produced not by them but by the One who causes things to speak, and produced against the people who used them.

The same Day is described in shorter form elsewhere: “˹Consider˺ the Day We will gather them all together then ask those who associated others ˹with Allah in worship˺, ‘Where are those gods you used to claim?’” (6:22).

## Verse 10:29

> Allah is sufficient as a Witness between each of us that we were totally unaware of your worship.”

**“Allah Is Sufficient as a Witness Between Us”**

The formula is legal language. *Kafā bi-llāhi shahīdan baynanā wa-baynakum* — Allah suffices as a witness between us and you. A witness is someone who saw what happened and can be asked; sufficiency means no further witness is required. The same formula is used where the Prophet is instructed to stake his message on it: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Sufficient is Allah as a Witness between me and you. He ˹fully˺ knows His servants’” (17:96). Applied here, the formula does something unusual: it is spoken by the false gods against their own worshippers. The party being called as witness is the One they were set beside, and His testimony is not about their divinity but about the fact of the worship. The choice of witness is exact. A witness must be independent of both parties, and the One whom the idols were set beside is the only figure in the scene who is neither accuser nor accused. His testimony cannot be dismissed as interested, which is what makes the denial unanswerable. The verse therefore turns the idolaters' own appeal inside out. They had invoked their gods as intermediaries with God; on that Day those gods invoke God as a witness against them.

The content of the testimony is ignorance. And if they were to hear, they could not respond to you. On the Day of Judgment they will disown your worship ˹of them˺” (35:14).

**Satan Makes the Same Speech, With One Difference**

The parallel disclaimer is recorded, and comparing the two is instructive. Satan will say after the judgment: “Indeed, Allah has made you a true promise. I too made you a promise, but I failed you. I did not have any authority over you. I only called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves” (14:22). The idols deny awareness; Satan admits the call. Both disclaim responsibility, and both throw the case back on the one who followed. The difference matters because it locates the guilt precisely. Satan's defence is that he had no authority and used none — only an invitation, which was accepted. The idols' defence is that they were not even there. In neither case does anything remain of the excuse the follower will try to make, and in both cases the closing words are the same in substance: blame yourselves. The comparison also shows what this verse is not doing. It is not excusing the follower on the grounds that he was misled, since the misleader disclaims the power to mislead.

## Verse 10:30

> Then and there every soul will face ˹the consequences of˺ what it had done. They all will be returned to Allah—their True Master. And whatever ˹gods˺ they fabricated will fail them

**Every Soul Faces What It Sent Ahead**

The verb is one of encountering: each soul will find, meet, be confronted by what it did. The deeds are not brought as evidence by a third party; they are present as the person's own doing, which is a stronger position for the judgment and a weaker one for the defence. The same confrontation is described from the individual's side: “The Day every soul will find whatever good it did ˹present˺ before it—as well as whatever evil it did” (3:30). The verse's use of “every soul” is inclusive and deliberate. The scene in the previous verses concerned the idolaters and their gods, but this clause widens to everyone gathered, since the facing of one's deeds is not a punishment reserved for association but the condition of the Day itself. The person who relied on a patron loses the patron; the person who relied on himself is left with the same record. Both meet the same thing. The formulation closes every avenue of comparison. No record is read beside another, and no one's deeds are weighed against someone else's circumstances. The phrase “every soul” closes the last escape too: there is no crowd to hide in and no representative to answer in a person's place.

**“Their True Master”**

The verse names God with a qualifier that only makes sense against the preceding scene: *mawlāhumu l-ḥaqq*, their true Master, Protector, Patron. The word *mawlā* carries exactly the relationship the idolaters had tried to build with their gods — guardianship, support, a claim on help. Adding “the true” says that every other instance of the relation was counterfeit. The same distinction is drawn where the two groups are separated: “That is because Allah is the ˹True˺ Master of the believers, whereas the disbelievers have no master” (47:11). The verse is therefore not merely returning people to God; it is telling them that the One they are returned to is the only patron the relationship ever had. What they thought they had in addition was not an extra support but a substitute for the real one. The verse completes the chapter's earlier argument in this way.

## Verse 10:31

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who provides for you from heaven and earth? Who owns ˹your˺ hearing and sight? Who brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living? And who conducts every affair?” They will ˹surely˺ say, “Allah.” Say, “Will you not then fear ˹Him˺

**Four Questions the Audience Answers Correctly**

The verse is a questionnaire, and its power lies in the answers. The Prophet is instructed to ask four questions and then to report the reply: they will say, Allah. This is not a prediction but a record of what actually happened when the questions were put. The same exchange is reported four more times in the Qur'an, always with the same result. “If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon ˹for your benefit˺, they will certainly say, ‘Allah!’ How can they then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?” (29:61). “And if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will definitely say, ‘Allah!’ Say, ‘Praise be to Allah!’ In fact, most of them do not know” (31:25). The audience is not ignorant of God. They are agreed on the largest part of the case, and the argument proceeds from the agreement rather than against it. That method is the most economical available. A refutation has to establish its own premises; this one borrows them from the people being addressed, so no premise in it can be disputed by them.

**Hearing and Sight as Something Owned by Another**

The second question shifts from the world to the body. Who has charge of your hearing and your sight? The verb is one of sovereignty, and the point is that the faculties are held rather than owned. The Qur'an states the same fact as a benefaction: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘He is the One Who brought you into being and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect. ˹Yet˺ you hardly give any thanks’” (67:23). The warning attached to the faculties is that they can be rendered useless: “And We gave them hearing, sight, and intellect. But neither their hearing, sight, nor intellect were of any benefit to them whatsoever, since they persisted in denying Allah's signs” (46:26). A person who will not use his hearing on the matter it was given for has not lost the faculty; he has wasted it. The question carries a second edge for that reason. It asks who owns the senses, and it implies that the owner may call for an account of how they were spent. The question therefore does double work — it establishes that the senses are given, and it prepares the rebuke for the way they are being used.

**Living From the Dead and Dead From the Living**

The third question is the resurrection argument in its shortest form, and it is drawn from agriculture. The seed is dead matter that produces a living plant; the plant dies and returns to the ground. The Qur'an states the cycle as a fact of observation: “He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And He gives life to the earth after its death. And so will you be brought forth ˹from the grave˺” (30:19), and “Indeed, Allah is the One Who causes seeds and fruit stones to sprout. He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. That is Allah! How can you then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?” (6:95). The argument does not ask anyone to accept a miracle. It asks him to look at a field, where the transition between life and death happens twice a year in both directions, and then to say why the same agent could not perform it once more on a larger scale.

## Verse 10:32

> That is Allah—your True Lord. So what is beyond the truth except falsehood? How can you then be turned away?”

**The Demonstrative Closes the Argument**

The verse begins by pointing. *Dhālikum* — that one — gathers the four answers of the previous verse into a single identification and hands it back to the audience as their own statement. Nothing new is asserted; the audience has already supplied the content. The same formula appears where the questions have just been asked and answered: “That is Allah, your Lord, the Creator of all things. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. How can you then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?” (40:62), and “That is Allah—your Lord! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. ˹He is˺ the Creator of all things, so worship Him ˹alone˺” (6:102). The rhetorical method is consistent: ask, receive the answer, then name the One described. The listener is not being persuaded from outside; he is being held to what he said. The method has a further advantage. A person corrected by his own words cannot claim to have been outmanoeuvred by a cleverer opponent; the correction arrives in his vocabulary and in his order.

**A Question That Leaves No Third Option**

“What is beyond the truth except falsehood?” (10:32) is a statement in the form of a question, and it excludes a middle position. There is no partially true lord, no arrangement in which the truth is supplemented, no graduated scale on which an idol occupies a lower rung of the same order. The verse's logic is deliberately severe, and it is aimed at the specific compromise Mecca had adopted — God as the head of a pantheon, with lesser beings handling access. That arrangement requires a third category between truth and falsehood, and the verse refuses to grant one. The same exclusivity is stated where devotion is defined: “Indeed, sincere devotion is due ˹only˺ to Allah” (39:3). A person who wants a moderate position between monotheism and association is being told that no such position exists to occupy. The severity is not rhetorical. It reflects the structure of the claim. Lordship is not a quality that comes in degrees, so a hierarchy of lords is not a softer monotheism but a different proposition altogether, and the verse declines to treat it as a compromise.

**“How Can You Then Be Turned Away?”**

The closing question is passive, and the passivity is significant. The audience is not asked how they turned away but how they are being turned away — which implies an agent and a direction. The same question closes other passages in which creative power has just been described: “Indeed, Allah is the One Who causes seeds and fruit stones to sprout… That is Allah! How can you then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?” (6:95), and the identical phrase at 35:3 and 40:62. The question is a rebuke aimed at reasoning rather than at information. These people have the facts; they have just answered four questions correctly. What remains unexplained is the movement away from a conclusion they themselves reached, and the verse treats that movement as the thing requiring explanation. Nobody is turned away from the truth by evidence. Something else does the turning. The question also carries bewilderment rather than anger. It is the question of someone who cannot reconstruct the road that led away from a conclusion, because no such road exists. What remains is a preference, and preferences do not answer to argument.

The distinction is drawn explicitly elsewhere: “That is because Allah ˹alone˺ is the Truth and what they invoke besides Him is falsehood, and Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Most High, All-Great” (22:62).

## Verse 10:33

> And so your Lord’s decree has been proven true against the rebellious—that they will never believe

**The Word That Has Come Due**

The verse reports the actualisation of a divine statement. *Ḥaqqat kalimatu rabbik* — the word of your Lord has come true, has been realised, has landed. The verb denotes a debt falling due or a sentence taking effect, and the image is legal rather than emotional. The same formula appears where the outcome of denial is stated as a settled matter: “And so your Lord's decree has been proven true against the disbelievers—that they will be the inmates of the Fire” (40:6). The decree is not described here as a decision made in response to the moment; it is described as something that was already in place and has now reached its term. That is consistent with the Qur'an's wider statement about divine action: “Indeed, We have created everything, perfectly preordained” (54:49). The same register appears in the accounts of destroyed peoples, where the appointed time arrives and the outcome is reported without commentary. Nothing in the verse suggests improvisation. What has happened is that a known outcome has arrived at the people to whom it applied. The legal register is worth noticing because it removes any suggestion of passion. A decree falling due is not an act of anger; it is the operation of a term set earlier, reported in the tone of a clerk rather than a judge.

**What the Decree Consists Of**

The content of the decree is given in the clause that follows: that they will not believe. The subject is specified as the rebellious — *al-fāsiqūn*, those who have departed from the limits, the same class described in the preceding verses as associating partners despite knowing better. The sequence in the passage matters for reading the decree correctly. First came the questions, which the audience answered correctly (10:31). Then came the identification and the exclusion (10:32). Then came the question about how they are being turned away. The decree is stated after all of that, which places it at the end of a process rather than before one. It is not a verdict issued before the evidence; it is the settled description of people who heard the evidence, agreed with it, and walked away from it anyway. The order of the verses makes the sequence impossible to miss: confession, identification, exclusion, then the decree. Nothing stands between the audience's own answer and the outcome except the audience's refusal. That placement distinguishes this decree from fate as the word is usually understood. It is not a sentence handed down before the conduct; it is the description of a conduct already chosen.

## Verse 10:34

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Can any of your associate-gods originate creation and then resurrect it?” Say, “˹Only˺ Allah originates creation and then resurrects it. How can you then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?”

**A Question Answered by Silence**

The question is put to the associate-gods through their worshippers, and no answer comes. The verse does not report a reply as the previous verse did; it moves directly to the Prophet's own statement, which supplies the answer the idols cannot give. The silence is the argument. A being asked whether it can originate creation and then bring it back has only two possible responses, and neither is available to a carved object: it cannot claim the power, and it cannot deny the question, because it cannot speak. The same incapacity is stated where the idols are addressed directly: “If you call upon them, they cannot hear your calls. And if they were to hear, they could not respond to you” (35:14). The verse therefore places the associate-gods in a position where their absence of function is showed by the form of the question itself. The Prophet's own answer, supplied immediately, is also a courtesy. It stops the silence being read as a trick, and it states the position openly so that the audience can disagree with a claim rather than with a pause.

**Origination and Return as One Act**

The two verbs are joined, and the joining is the substance of the argument. Originating creation is the first act; bringing it back is the second, and the Qur'an treats the second as the easier of the two. The claim is stated in the sūrah's own earlier words: “Indeed, He originates the creation then resurrects it so that He may justly reward those who believe and do good” (10:4). The same pairing appears with the inference drawn: “He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And He gives life to the earth after its death. And so will you be brought forth ˹from the grave˺” (30:19). By asking about both acts in one question, the verse closes the escape route of a lesser divinity. A being that could make something but not restore it would be a partial cause; the question allows no partial claim, because the two acts are attributed to the same agent in the same breath. The pairing also answers the objection that resurrection is a different kind of act from creation. It is not; it is the same act performed on the same material, and both belong to whoever performed the first.

**“How Can You Then Be Deluded?”**

The closing question is a fixed formula, and it appears wherever creative power has just been described. “Indeed, Allah is the One Who causes seeds and fruit stones to sprout. He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. That is Allah! How can you then be deluded ˹from the truth˺?” (6:95). The same phrase closes 35:3 and 40:62. Its recurrence is not repetition for emphasis; it marks a consistent interpretive move. Once the creative act is established, the question of how anyone could be diverted becomes the only question left. The diversion is not explained by evidence, since the evidence has been conceded. The formula therefore names the phenomenon the whole passage is investigating — a correct belief accompanied by an inconsistent practice — and leaves it standing as something that requires explanation from the person doing it. The formula's recurrence across the Qur'an shows a settled move rather than a local argument. Wherever creative power is established the question follows, because the inconsistency it names is the same in every case.

## Verse 10:35

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Can any of your associate-gods guide to the truth?” Say, “˹Only˺ Allah guides to the truth.” Who then is more worthy to be followed: the One Who guides to the truth or those who cannot find the way unless guided? What is the matter with you? How do you judge

**Guidance to the Truth Belongs to God Alone**

The answer is given before the audience can respond, and it is exclusive: Allah guides to the truth. The Qur'an states the same exclusivity in the Prophet's instructed speech: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Allah's guidance is the ˹only˺ true guidance. And we are commanded to submit to the Lord of all worlds’” (6:71), and it describes the mechanism: “But We have made it a light, by which We guide whoever We will of Our servants. And you are truly leading ˹all˺ to the Straight Path” (42:52). The object of the guidance is specified as the truth — *al-ḥaqq* — which is the same word used of God in the previous verse. Guidance to the truth is therefore not guidance to a set of preferences; it is direction toward what is actually the case, and only the One who is the truth can supply it. The wording also excludes a common misunderstanding. Guidance to the truth is not persuasion, encouragement, or the supply of motives; it is direction toward what is actually the case, and that requires knowledge of the case.

**“What Is the Matter With You? How Do You Judge?”**

The two closing questions are addressed to the reasoning rather than to the belief. *Famā lakum kayfa taḥkumūn* — what is wrong with you, how do you judge? The rebuke is not that they lack information; the preceding verses established that they have it. It is that their judgment does not follow from what they know. The same rebuke appears where the same inconsistency is described: “And if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will definitely say, ‘Allah!’ Say, ‘Praise be to Allah!’ In fact, most of them do not know” (31:25). The verse treats the failure as a failure of inference. A person who says “Allah” to four questions about creation and then directs his worship elsewhere has made a judgment, and the judgment does not match the answers. Asking how he judges is asking him to show his working, and no working exists. The double question is also a kindness. It lets the hearer notice the gap himself instead of being told about it, and noticing it is the first step toward closing it.

**A Test for Everything That Is Followed**

The criterion the verse supplies applies beyond stone idols. Anything followed is being treated as a guide, and the question is whether it can guide or whether it needs guidance itself. The Qur'an names the most common substitute: “Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ the one who has taken their own desires as their god? Will you then be a keeper over them?” (25:43). A desire is the clearest case of something that cannot guide unless guided — it moves, it changes, it requires direction from something else, and it is followed anyway. Customs, inherited opinions and the opinions of respected people fall under the same test.

## Verse 10:36

> Most of them follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions. ˹And˺ surely assumptions can in no way replace the truth. Allah is indeed All-Knowing of what they do

**Assumption Named as the Thing They Follow**

The verse identifies what the audience is actually following, and the word is *ẓann* — assumption, conjecture, a belief held without knowledge. The verse is not accusing them of stupidity; it is describing an epistemic state. They are not following a showed position or a refuted one. They are following something they have never examined. The same diagnosis is applied to the naming of the gods: “These ˹idols˺ are mere names that you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized. They follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions and whatever ˹their˺ souls desire, although ˹true˺ guidance has already come to them from their Lord” (53:23). The pairing of assumption and desire in that verse is exact, and it explains the mechanism. An unexamined belief is held because it is wanted, and it is wanted because it is comfortable, and it stays unexamined for the same reason. The verse names the state rather than arguing with it, which is the right approach. A person holding an unexamined belief cannot be refuted, because there is no position to refute — only a habit. What can be done is to point at the habit and name it. Naming it is not a small thing either. A habit that has never been looked at cannot be answered, and the first act of examination is to see that there is something there to examine.

## Verse 10:37

> It is not ˹possible˺ for this Quran to have been produced by anyone other than Allah. In fact, it is a confirmation of what came before, and an explanation of the Scripture. It is, without a doubt, from the Lord of all worlds

**A Negative That States a Fact About the Book**

The verse opens with an impossibility. *Mā kāna hādhā l-qurʾānu a yuftarā min dūni llāh* — this Qur'an could not have been fabricated apart from God. The construction is not a prohibition but a denial of possibility, and it is stated before any evidence is offered in the verse itself. The evidence has been accumulating since earlier in the chapter: the messenger cannot edit the recitation (10:15), he lived forty years among his audience before it and never read or wrote (10:16), and the demand that he produce a different book met a refusal grounded in his own lack of authority. This verse draws the conclusion those facts support. The negative is also a claim about the book rather than about the man. What makes fabrication impossible is not the Prophet's honesty alone but the nature of what he brought — a text whose character no human being in that society could have produced. The negative is also a challenge in disguise. To say that fabrication is impossible is to invite the try, and the following verse issues the invitation openly.

**A Confirmation of What Came Before**

The positive half of the verse begins with continuity. The Qur'an confirms the scriptures that preceded it, and the claim is made repeatedly: “Believe in My revelations which confirm your Scriptures. Do not be the first to deny them” (2:41); “He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel” (3:3). The confirmation is not a quotation but a corroboration — the same core teaching about one God, prophecy, judgment and accountability, arriving again in a new language to a new people. The claim is also a testable one in its context. Mecca was not ignorant of the earlier revelations; it traded with Byzantium and Abyssinia and hosted Jews and Christians in the peninsula. A book that contradicted the substance of those scriptures would have been identified quickly, and the verse invites the comparison rather than avoiding it. Confirmation is also a claim about this book's contents. A text that disagreed with the Torah and the Gospel on monotheism, prophecy and judgment would not have survived contact with the communities holding them, and such communities were present in the peninsula.

**An Explanation of the Scripture**

The second description is *tafṣīl al-kitāb* — a detailed exposition of the Book. The phrase is read by most commentators as referring to the Qur'an's own contents: the laws, beliefs and rulings set out in detail, as against a general revelation that requires elaboration. The Qur'an claims the same function where its own status is asserted: “This is the Book! There is no doubt about it—a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺” (2:2). Detail is what makes a revelation usable. A general affirmation of monotheism can be agreed with by almost anyone. A text that specifies how to pray, what to eat, how to divide an inheritance and what to do when a contract is disputed is a different kind of thing, and it is exposed to checking in a way that generalities are not.

## Verse 10:38

> Or do they claim, “He made it up!”? Tell them ˹O Prophet˺, “Produce one sûrah like it then, and seek help from whoever you can—other than Allah—if what you say is true!”

**The Challenge in Its Plainest Form**

The verse takes the accusation seriously and answers it with a test. If the Qur'an was invented by a man, then men can invent something like it, and the test is set at the smallest unit available: one chapter. The same challenge appears in four places with varying scope. “And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true” (2:23). The demand is escalated elsewhere to ten chapters: “Or do they say, ‘He has fabricated this ˹Quran˺!’? Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Produce ten fabricated sûrahs like it and seek help from whoever you can —other than Allah—if what you say is true!’” (11:13). It is escalated again to the whole of creation: “If ˹all˺ humans and jinn were to come together to produce the equivalent of this Quran, they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported each other” (17:88). And it is reduced to its barest form: “Let them then produce something like it, if what they say is true!” (52:34). The variation in scope also removes an objection about difficulty. A challenge tailored to one audience could be dismissed as calibrated; one that ranges from a single chapter to all humanity and the jinn cannot be.

**One Chapter Is the Minimum, and It Is Small**

The unit chosen is significant. A chapter is not a book; the shortest in the Qur'an runs to a few lines. The challenge does not ask for a comparable work, a comparable corpus, or a comparable lifetime of output. It asks for one chapter — which means the claim of fabrication can be tested by anyone who can compose Arabic prose at all. That is a demanding audience in seventh-century Arabia, where poetry was the recognised measure of a man and where compositions were memorised, compared and ranked publicly. The challenge was addressed to people whose professional competence was language, in their own idiom, using their own unit of measurement. No concession was made for difficulty. The unit also has a practical advantage: a chapter is a complete composition with its own structure and closure, so the test concerns a whole discourse rather than a line of eloquence.

## Verse 10:39

> In fact, they ˹hastily˺ rejected the Book without comprehending it and before the fulfilment of its warnings. Similarly, those before them were in denial. See then what was the end of the wrongdoers

**Rejection That Precedes Understanding**

The verse describes a sequence, and the sequence is the diagnosis. The rejection came first; comprehension never arrived. The word *bal* at the opening — in fact, rather — signals a correction of what the audience would say about itself. They would claim to have examined the Qur'an and found it wanting. The verse says they did not examine it. The same observation is attributed to ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭālib in a line the exegetes quote here: people are the enemies of what they do not know. The remark is not an insult but a description of a mechanism. Hostility arrives before knowledge, and once hostility is in place the work of knowing feels like a concession to an opponent. The verse does not soften the description, and it does not need to; the haste is visible in the sequence itself. The mechanism has a second stage.

**“Similarly, Those Before Them Were in Denial”**

The verse places its audience inside a pattern, and the pattern is what makes the warning concrete. Denial is not new; it has a documented history with documented outcomes. The same injunction to look at that history appears repeatedly: “Similar situations came to pass before you, so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers” (3:137); “So We seized him and his soldiers, casting them into the sea. See then what was the end of the wrongdoers!” (28:40); “Have the deniers not travelled through the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them?” (12:109). The comparison is not a threat of identical punishment. It is a statement that the same choice has been made before by people who were stronger and better provided for, and that the outcome is a matter of record rather than of speculation. The comparison also removes the comfort of uniqueness. Every generation assumes its situation is unprecedented; the verse supplies the list of precedents and leaves the reader to find his place in it.

---

## Verse 10:40

> Some of them will ˹eventually˺ believe in it; others will not. And your Lord knows best the corruptors

**A Division Inside One Audience**

The verse reports a split, and the split runs through a single group rather than between two groups. Some of them will believe in it, others will not — the same audience, hearing the same recitation, arriving at opposite outcomes. The commentators read the first class as those among the Quraysh who rejected the message at the time and later accepted it, and the second as those who never would. History bore that out. Men who had argued against the Prophet ﷺ for two decades — including Abū Sufyān b. Ḥarb, whose testimony about him before Heraclius is recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7 — entered Islam at the conquest of Mecca, while others who had heard the same verses from the beginning died without accepting them. The verse's prediction was therefore testable within the lifetime of its audience, and it was tested.

**The Argument Closes Without a Verdict on Individuals**

The verses from 31 to 39 form the chapter's argumentative core — the questions about provision, senses, life and guidance, the identification of the true Lord, the challenge to produce a chapter. This verse closes that section, and it does so without naming anyone as lost. The decree mentioned earlier applied to a class defined by conduct; this verse states that the class has members and that their membership is known. The effect is to leave the argument standing and the individuals open. Nobody in the audience is told that his case is finished, and nobody is told that it is settled in his favour. The verse ends the debate at exactly the point where further argument would be useless, because what remains is not a question of proof. That is the right place to stop. An argument carried past the point of decision ceases to be an argument and becomes pressure, and this sūrah's method throughout has been to state the case and leave the response free.

## Verse 10:41

> If they deny you, then say, “My deeds are mine and your deeds are yours. You are free of what I do and I am free of what you do!”

**A Separation, Not a Curse**

The verse instructs the Prophet ﷺ in what to say if his audience rejects him, and the words contain no threat. There is no invocation of punishment, no prediction of ruin, no appeal to vengeance. What there is, is a declaration of separate responsibility: my deeds belong to me, yours belong to you. The same instruction appears where the Prophet is told how to answer disobedience among his own family: “But if they disobey you, say, ‘I am certainly free of what you do’” (26:216). It becomes the closing line of the shortest sūrah in the Qur'an: “You have your way, and I have my Way” (109:6). The formula is not a withdrawal of concern. It is a statement about the structure of accountability, made at the point where argument has run out, and it is stated without bitterness — which is itself instructive about how a rejected messenger is to speak.

**The Formula of Separate Accounts**

The wording has a settled form across the Qur'an, and the form is legal. “Say, ‘Would you dispute with us about Allah, while He is our Lord and your Lord? We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours’” (2:139). The same clause appears in the Prophet's instructed address to the People of the Scripture: “Allah is our Lord and your Lord. We will be accountable for our deeds and you for yours. There is no ˹need for˺ contention between us. Allah will gather us together ˹for judgment˺” (42:15). Three features recur in every instance. The Lordship is shared, so the dispute is not about which God exists. The accounts are separate, so nobody carries another's case. And the gathering is common, so the separation is temporary rather than final. The verse is therefore not a statement of permanent rupture; it is a statement about who answers for what, made in advance of the hearing.

It does not say the Prophet stops. The separation of accounts is not the end of the mission, and the verses around this one make that plain. The duty is described elsewhere in the narrowest terms: “The Messenger's duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah ˹fully˺ knows what you reveal and what you conceal” (5:99), and “Whether We show you ˹O Prophet˺ some of what We threaten them with, or cause you to die ˹before that˺, your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. Judgment is for Us” (13:40). The declaration in this verse is about responsibility, not about effort. A person who says “your deeds are yours” is not saying “your case does not matter”; he is saying that the case will not be carried by him on the Day, and that no amount of urgency on his part changes that. The distinction matters, because a messenger who believed he carried his audience's account would either coerce them or despair.

**Peace as the Response to Hostility**

The Qur'an supplies the manner in which this separation is to be expressed, and it is specific. Describing those who hear slanderous talk, it says: “they turn away from it, saying, ‘We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. Peace ˹is our only response˺ to you! We want nothing to do with those who act ignorantly’” (28:55). The formula of separate accounts is accompanied by a greeting of peace and a refusal to engage with ignorance on its own terms. That combination is the verse's practical content for anyone who has argued a serious matter to exhaustion.

## Verse 10:42

> Some of them listen to what you say, but can you make the deaf hear even though they do not understand

**Listening Without Hearing**

The verse describes a real situation: there are people present while the Qur'an is being recited, and they are listening. Nothing in the description suggests they are absent or pretending. The problem is not attendance but comprehension, and the Qur'an describes the mechanism elsewhere in physiological language: “There are some of them who ˹pretend to˺ listen to your recitation ˹of the Quran˺, but We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to understand it—and deafness in their ears. Even if they were to see every sign, they still would not believe in them” (6:25).

**A Question About the Prophet's Limits**

The second half is a question addressed to the Prophet, and its expected answer is no. “Can you make the deaf hear, or guide the blind or those clearly astray?” (43:40). The same question recurs with the metaphor extended: “You certainly cannot make the dead hear ˹the truth˺. Nor can you make the deaf hear the call when they turn their backs and walk away” (27:80; 30:52). The rhetorical function is to fix a boundary. The Prophet's competence extends to delivery; it does not extend to producing the response. The verse is not doubting his ability as a speaker — the challenge to produce a chapter like this one rests on exactly that ability — but it distinguishes clearly between addressing a person and changing him. The first is his task and he performs it. The second is not his, and no exertion on his part will supply it.

**Deafness as a Condition of the Will**

The metaphor is not about the body. Nobody in the audience had a hearing impairment, and the verse's force depends on that. The deafness described is a refusal to process, and the Qur'an locates it precisely: it is the hearts in the chests that go blind, not the eyes (22:46). The same verse names the cure for the condition and it is not information — “Have they not travelled throughout the land so their hearts may reason, and their ears may listen?” (22:46). Travel is prescribed because it disrupts habit, and habit is what the deafness consists of. The verse therefore identifies an obstacle that evidence cannot remove. A person who will not process a statement is not a person who lacks the statement, and adding statements to his supply does not address the problem. What addresses it is a change in the disposition, and the verse leaves that where it belongs.

**What the Messenger Can and Cannot Do**

The boundary drawn here is stated elsewhere as a definition of the office. “The Messenger's duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺” (5:99), and the same limitation appears where the outcome is placed completely outside his control: “Whether We show you ˹O Prophet˺ some of what We threaten them with, or cause you to die ˹before that˺, your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. Judgment is for Us” (13:40). The pairing of duty and limitation is what makes the office bearable. A messenger told to deliver and nothing more can perform his task completely in a room where nobody is convinced. A messenger told to produce belief could never succeed, and his failure would be permanent. The verse's question is therefore not a criticism of the Prophet's method; it is the reason his method is delivery rather than persuasion by any means available.

## Verse 10:43

> And some of them look at you, but can you guide the blind even though they cannot see

**Looking Without Seeing**

The verse names the second channel of guidance and reports that it is also blocked. Some of them look at the Prophet — they see him, they have seen him for years, and looking is not the difficulty. The Qur'an states where the difficulty is: “Have they not travelled throughout the land so their hearts may reason, and their ears may listen? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind” (22:46). The organ is functioning and the perception is not occurring. The verse's question — can you guide the blind? — is therefore not about physical sight.

**The Pair: the Heard Word and the Seen Sign**

This verse and the one before it form a matched pair, and the pairing covers the two ways a revelation reaches a person. The message is heard, and the messenger is seen. Guidance arrives through speech and through the visible evidence of the speaker's life and conduct, and the Qur'an treats both as channels: “Nor are the dead and the living equal. Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ makes whoever He wills hear” (35:22). The two verses together close both channels, and the closing is symmetrical — the deaf do not hear the recitation, the blind do not see the sign. That completeness is the point. It is not that one avenue was blocked and another remained open; the audience had every avenue available and used none of them.

**Guidance Cannot Be Transferred**

The question implies an answer about the nature of guidance: it is not something one person can hand to another. The Qur'an states the limit and its exception in the same sentence: “Nor can you lead the blind out of their misguidance. You can make none hear ˹the truth˺ except those who believe in Our revelations, ˹fully˺ submitting ˹to Allah˺” (27:81; 30:53). The exception is not a restriction imposed from outside; it is a description of where the address lands. A person already disposed to submit receives what is said; a person not so disposed does not, and no increase in the clarity of the speech changes that. Guidance is therefore a transaction requiring something from the recipient. The verse's rhetorical question makes the same point in negative form: a guide cannot lead someone who will not take a step, and the blindness described is the refusal to take one.

**The Second Person Is Deliberate**

The question is addressed to the Prophet in the second person: can you guide the blind? The form is significant. A third-person statement — the blind cannot be guided — would be a fact about the audience. A second-person question makes it a fact about the limits of the one asking, and it does so gently. The Prophet is not told that he has failed; he is asked whether a task he was never given could have been performed by anyone. The same form is used where the limits of prophetic power are stated most plainly: “Say, ‘I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the Will of Allah’” (7:188). What the verse accomplishes is a transfer of the burden. The guidance of another person was never in his hands, and the verse says so in the shape of a question he can answer.

## Verse 10:44

> Indeed, Allah does not wrong people in the least, but it is people who wrong themselves

**A Principle Stated in a Single Line**

The verse is one sentence, and it settles a question that the surrounding verses raise. After two verses describing people who cannot be made to hear or see, the natural inference would be that their condition was imposed. The verse forecloses it. God does not wrong people at all; they wrong themselves. The same statement is made with a measure attached: “Indeed, Allah never wrongs ˹anyone˺—even by an atom's weight. And if it is a good deed, He will multiply it many times over and will give a great reward out of His grace” (4:40). The atom's weight is the smallest unit available in the language, and its use is a way of saying that the absence of injustice is not approximate. Nothing is deducted unfairly, nothing is added unfairly, and the asymmetry in that verse runs in the believer's favour: good is multiplied, evil is matched.

**A Formula Repeated Until It Becomes Doctrine**

The statement is not local to this verse. It appears as a refrain, and its repetition marks it as a settled principle rather than a passing claim. On the Day the wrongdoers will be told: “This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation” (3:182; 8:51; 22:10). The same declaration closes a statement about the fixedness of the divine word: “My Word cannot be changed, nor am I unjust to ˹My˺ creation” (50:29), and it accompanies the rule that each deed returns on its doer: “Whoever does good, it is to their own benefit. And whoever does evil, it is to their own loss. Your Lord is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation” (41:46). The formula's placement is consistent: it appears wherever an outcome might look harsh, and it functions as a guarantee that the outcome has been measured. The verse here is the principle in its barest form, without the scene attached.

**Self-Wrong as the Real Category**

The second half of the verse names the actual offence, and the naming shifts the whole discussion. The wrong is not against God, who cannot be diminished by it; it is against the self. The Qur'an explains how this is possible in the verse about the trust: “Indeed, We offered the trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, but they ˹all˺ declined to bear it, being fearful of it. But humanity assumed it, ˹for˺ they are truly wrongful ˹to themselves˺ and ignorant ˹of the consequences˺” (33:72). The trust is responsibility, and the description of humanity in that verse is exactly the pair of qualities that produce self-wrong: boldness in accepting and ignorance of the cost. A person who takes on a responsibility without calculating what it requires will fail it, and the failure lands on him. The verse is therefore not making a theological abstraction. It is describing the ordinary way human beings damage themselves — by undertaking what they do not fully understand.

## Verse 10:45

> On the Day He will gather them, it will be as if they had not stayed ˹in the world˺ except for an hour of a day, ˹as though they were only˺ getting to know one another. Lost indeed will be those who denied the meeting with Allah, and were not ˹rightly˺ guided

**A Lifetime Compressed Into an Hour**

The verse states a fact about how time will be experienced on the Day, and the compression is extreme. A whole life — its ambitions, its accumulation, its arguments — will feel as though it lasted a hour of a day. The same statement is made in several places with slightly different measures. “On the Day they see it, it will be as if they had stayed ˹in the world˺ no more than one evening or its morning” (79:46). “And on the Day the Hour will arrive, the wicked will swear that they did not stay ˹in this world˺ more than an hour. In this way they were always deluded ˹in the world˺” (30:55). The variation in the measures is instructive: a hour, an evening, a morning, ten days, a day. None of them is offered as a calculation; all of them describe the collapse of a duration into something negligible. The verse is not saying the life was short. It is saying it will be experienced as short, and the experience is the fact being reported.

**Getting to Know One Another**

The clause about acquaintance describes a scene of recognition. The gathered people turn to each other and are briefly aware of one another, the way travellers who have shared a road become aware of their companions when the journey ends. The same exchange appears in the questioning on the Day: “They will reply, ‘We remained ˹only˺ a day or part of a day. But ask those who kept count’” (23:113), and the answer given to them: “You only remained for a little while, if only you knew” (23:114). The reference to those who kept count is a small stroke of realism — in the confusion of the gathering, the people appeal to whoever might have kept better track. The verse's mention of acquaintance is therefore not decorative. It describes the social texture of the moment: strangers and enemies and neighbours, all reduced to the same condition and briefly aware of each other in it.

## Verse 10:46

> Whether We show you ˹O Prophet˺ some of what We threaten them with, or cause you to die ˹before that˺, to Us is their return and Allah is a Witness over what they do

**Two Possibilities, One Outcome**

The verse sets out two alternatives and shows that they converge. Either the Prophet lives to see part of what his audience has been threatened with, or he dies before it. In either case the return is to God, and the witnessing is His. The same sentence appears elsewhere with the messenger's duty attached: “So be patient ˹O Prophet˺. Surely Allah's promise is true. Whether We show you some of what We threaten them with, or cause you to die ˹before that˺, to Us they will ˹all˺ be returned” (40:77). With the limit of the office attached: “Whether We show you ˹O Prophet˺ some of what We threaten them with, or cause you to die ˹before that˺, your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. Judgment is for Us” (13:40). The three versions share a structure: an unresolved alternative about timing, and a fixed statement about the destination. The verse's purpose is to remove the timing from the list of things that need settling.

**Some of the Threats Were Fulfilled in His Lifetime**

The first alternative was not hypothetical. The Prophet ﷺ lived to see a portion of what the sūrah's warnings described. The interpretive tradition points to the Battle of Badr, fought in the second year after the migration, where a number of the men who had led the opposition in Mecca were killed and the balance of power in the peninsula changed. Later he entered Mecca itself, and the sanctuary that had been the centre of the opposition came under the authority of the message it had rejected.

**“Allah Is a Witness Over What They Do”**

The verse closes on witnessing, and the word is the one used when the Prophet is instructed to stake his message on it: “Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, ‘Who is the best witness?’ Say, ‘Allah is! He is a Witness between me and you. And this Quran has been revealed to me so that, with it, I may warn you and whoever it reaches’” (6:19). The witnessing described here is not observation from a distance. It is the presence of a party to the transaction, the One who can testify between the messenger and his audience. For a man whose message is being called a fabrication, the fact that the Sender is a witness is the whole of his defence. The verse supplies it in the same breath as the uncertainty about timing, which is a deliberate pairing: the timing may be unknown, and the witnessing is not in doubt.

**The Witness Is Not a Passive Observer**

Witnessing in this verse is not surveillance from a distance. The One who witnesses is the One who will testify, and the testimony is the ground of the judgment. The recording of what is witnessed is described elsewhere in the Qur'an with the same seriousness: “while you are certainly observed by vigilant, noble scribes, who know whatever you do” (82:10–12). The pairing of the witnessing and the return in this verse is therefore deliberate. The return is the hearing, and the witnessing is the evidence presented at it. For the audience being warned, this is the fact that makes the delay survivable and dangerous at the same time: nothing is being lost in the interval.

## Verse 10:47

> And for every community there is a messenger. After their messenger has come, judgment is passed on them in all fairness, and they are not wronged

**Revelation Is Not a Local Event**

The verse makes a claim about the whole of human history: every community has had a messenger. The claim is stated elsewhere with its content included: “We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, ‘Worship Allah and shun false gods.’ But some of them were guided by Allah, while others were destined to stray. So travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers!” (16:36), and again as a general rule: “We have surely sent you with the truth as a deliverer of good news and a warner. There is no community that has not had a warner” (35:24). The scope is universal, and it is one of the Qur'an's major themes. No people is described as abandoned, unaddressed, or outside the range of the message. The verse therefore sets the sūrah's local argument — Mecca, its idols, its demands for signs — inside a history far larger than Mecca, and it makes the Meccan case an instance rather than an exception.

**Judgment Follows the Messenger**

The order of the clauses is a rule of procedure. The messenger comes first, and the judgment comes after: “And We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺” (17:15). The verse removes the most serious objection to divine justice — that people are held to a standard they never received. Nobody is judged on a message that did not reach him, and the arrival of the messenger is what makes the judgment possible. This is also why the verse insists on fairness as a consequence rather than as an aspiration. Once the messenger has come, the judgment is rendered *bi-l-qisṭ*, in all fairness, and the reason it can be fair is that the condition for fairness has been met. The pairing of the messenger and the fairness is therefore not two ideas but one argument: the proof was supplied, so the sentence can be just.

**One Message, Different Laws**

The universality of the messenger does not mean uniformity of the law. Each messenger comes to a particular people in a particular time, and the Qur'an states the consequence plainly: “To each of you We have ordained a code of law and a way of life. If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but His Will is to test you with what He has given ˹each of˺ you” (5:48). The reason for the particularity is also given: “We have not sent a messenger except in the language of his people to clarify ˹the message˺ for them” (14:4). A messenger who addressed humanity in general would address nobody in particular. What is constant across the communities is the core — worship God, avoid false gods — and what varies is the specific code suited to the people receiving it. The verse therefore supports both the unity of revelation and the diversity of religions, and it does so without making either claim at the expense of the other.

## Verse 10:48

> They ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass if what you say is true?”

**A Question That Appears Five Times**

The wording of this question is so fixed that the Qur'an records it in nearly identical form in five separate chapters: “They ask ˹the believers˺, ‘When will this threat come to pass if what you say are true?’” (21:38; 27:71; 34:29; 36:48; 67:25). The repetition is not a copying of phrasing but a record of a demand that kept being made in the same words, in different settings, to different audiences. Its recurrence tells us something about its function. A genuine question changes as the person asking it learns more; this one does not change, because it is not being asked to learn. The stability of the wording is the evidence of its purpose. It is a formula of challenge, repeated because it works socially — it puts the believers on the spot without requiring the asker to examine anything.

**The Form of the Question Assumes Its Answer**

The construction is conditional in a way that rigs the outcome: when will this happen, if what you say is true? The condition invites the listener to notice that it has not happened, and the implied inference is that it will not. The question is therefore not open. It has a preferred answer built into it, and the preferred answer is dismissal. The Qur'an identifies the same posture elsewhere: those who challenge the Prophet to hasten the punishment, when the punishment would certainly have come to them at once had a time not already been set (29:53). The demand is made from a position of assumed safety, and the safety is what makes the demand possible. A person who genuinely believed the threat was real would not ask for its schedule; he would ask what to do about it. The verse records the question without answering it, and the absence of an answer is the answer.

**Why the Prophet Could Not Give a Date**

The question is addressed to believers rather than to the Prophet, and the reason appears in the verse that follows: he has no power over benefit or harm except by God's will. The knowledge of the Hour is not his. The Qur'an states the limit explicitly and instructs him to say so: “They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the Hour, ‘When will it be?’ Say, ‘That knowledge is only with my Lord. He alone will reveal it when the time comes. It is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth and will only take you by surprise.’ They ask you as if you had full knowledge of it. Say, ‘That knowledge is only with Allah, but most people do not know’” (7:187). The final clause diagnoses the question precisely: they ask as though he knew. The expectation behind the demand is that a prophet is a fortune-teller, and the verse refuses the category. A messenger carries a message; the schedule is not part of it.

## Verse 10:49

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the Will of Allah.” For each community there is an appointed term. When their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it

The reply to the demand for a date opens with a statement about the speaker, and the statement is one of complete dependence: I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the will of Allah. The same statement is given at greater length elsewhere: “Say, ‘I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the Will of Allah. If I had known the unknown, I would have benefited myself enormously, and no harm would have ever touched me. I am only a warner and deliverer of good news for those who believe’” (7:188).

**An Appointed Term for Every Community**

The second sentence supplies the framework the question ignored. Every community has an *ajal* — a term, a fixed span. The same statement appears where the destruction of past peoples is described: “For each community there is an appointed term. When their time arrives, they can neither delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it” (7:34). The term is understood both as the natural span of a people and as the point at which a people may be punished before that span ends, and both readings serve the same purpose: the timing is fixed and it is not human property. The wider statement of the principle makes the mercy in it visible: “If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term” (16:61). The delay is not negligence; it is the term running.

**The Term Cannot Be Moved in Either Direction**

The symmetry of the verse is exact: they cannot delay it by a hour, and they cannot advance it. Both directions are closed, and the closure is stated in the same measure — a single hour. The individual case is stated with the same precision: “But Allah never delays a soul when its appointed time comes” (63:11). The double closure is what defeats the demand completely. The people asking for the threat to come sooner are requesting the one thing that no party to the arrangement can supply — not the Prophet, not themselves, not anyone. A term that could be advanced by request would not be a term. The verse therefore answers the question not by refusing it but by showing that it asks for an impossibility, and it does so in language that applies equally to the person asking and to the person being asked.

## Verse 10:50

> Tell them ˹O Prophet˺, “Imagine if His torment were to overcome you by night or day—do the wicked realize what they are ˹really˺ asking Him to hasten

**By Night or By Day**

The phrase is the same one used in the parable of the garden earlier in this chapter, where the command came upon the field by night or by day and left it as though it had never flourished. The two halves of the clock cover all of it, and their function is to remove any hope of preparation. A punishment that arrives at a known hour can be met; one that arrives at any hour cannot. The same idea appears in the question put elsewhere: “Ask, ‘Imagine if Allah's punishment were to overwhelm you with or without warning—who would be destroyed other than the wrongdoers?’” (6:47). The addition of “with or without warning” in that verse makes explicit what this verse implies by naming both halves of the day. There is no time at which the arrival can be avoided by being ready, which is what makes the demand for it so strange.

**“What Are They Asking to Hasten?”**

The closing question is the verse's real content. It does not ask whether the punishment is coming; it asks whether the people demanding it know what it is. The implication is that they do not. A request made in mockery is made without content — the asker has never pictured the thing he is requesting, because picturing it would end the mockery. The Qur'an states the same fact elsewhere with the outcome attached: “They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the punishment. Had it not been for a time already set, the punishment would have certainly come to them ˹at once˺. But it will definitely take them by surprise when they least expect it” (29:53). The surprise is the point. The punishment will arrive in the condition the demanders have been assuming it never would, and the arrival will be as unexpected as it was requested.

## Verse 10:51

> Will you believe in it only after it has overtaken you? Now? But you always wanted to hasten it!”

**Belief That Arrives Too Late to Be Belief**

The verse poses a question whose answer is obvious, and the obviousness is the point. Will you believe only when the punishment has already reached you? The scenario is not hypothetical in the way the previous verse's was; it describes a real possibility with a known outcome. Belief at that point would not be belief in the ordinary sense, because belief requires the option of not believing. Once the threatened thing is present, assent is not a choice but a description of what one sees. The Qur'an states the rule in the language of repentance: “However, repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, ‘Now I repent!’ nor those who die as disbelievers” (4:18). The timing is what invalidates the statement, not the words. The verse therefore identifies a distinction that is easy to lose: the content of a confession and the condition in which it is made are not the same thing.

**Pharaoh as the Documented Case**

The scenario has a precedent in this very chapter, and it is described in detail. As Pharaoh drowned: “I believe that there is no god except that in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am ˹now˺ one of those who submit” (10:90). The words are correct, and the answer he receives is not a denial of their correctness: “Now ˹you believe˺? But you always disobeyed and were one of the corruptors” (10:91). The rebuke is completely about timing. The same word — now — appears in both this verse and Pharaoh's answer, and the echo is deliberate. The case is then closed with a statement about its use: “Today We will preserve your corpse so that you may become an example for those who come after you” (10:92). The precedent is preserved precisely so that later people can see what a correct confession is worth when it arrives after the option has closed.

**What the Demand to Hasten Actually Proved**

The verse's final clause turns the demand back on itself. You wanted to hasten it — and that wanting was evidence. The same demand is recorded in several places, always with the same tone: “Do they ˹really˺ seek to hasten Our torment?” (26:204); “Do they ˹really˺ wish to hasten Our punishment?” (37:176). The reply given elsewhere distinguishes the two groups precisely: “Those who disbelieve in it ˹ask to˺ hasten it ˹mockingly˺. But the believers are fearful of it, knowing that it is the truth” (42:18). The demand to hasten is thus a marker. A person who believes a threat is real does not ask for it sooner; he asks how to avoid it. The verse treats the demand as a confession made in the language of challenge, and the outcome is stated in the same vocabulary: “˹They will be told,˺ ‘Taste your torment! This is what you sought to hasten’” (51:14).

## Verse 10:52

> Then the wrongdoers will be told, “Taste the torment of eternity! Are you not rewarded except for what you used to commit?”

**“Taste” — the Language of Direct Experience**

The sentence begins with a verb of the mouth, and the choice is exact. *Dhūqū* — taste. The demand that produced this moment was a demand to see: when will this threat come to pass? The answer converts seeing into tasting, which is a different relation to a thing completely. Seeing keeps a distance; tasting does not. The same verb is used in the parallel sentence: “˹They will be told,˺ ‘Taste your torment! This is what you sought to hasten’” (51:14), and again where the reason is stated: “So taste ˹the punishment˺ for neglecting the meeting of this Day of yours. We ˹too˺ will certainly neglect you. And taste the torment of eternity for what you used to do!” (32:14). The verb is not metaphorical decoration. It names the transition from a threat discussed at a distance to an experience undergone, and the transition is what the whole passage has been describing.

**“Are You Not Rewarded Except for What You Committed?”**

The second sentence is a question, and it is the defence of the sentence rather than an addition to it. The same formula appears wherever the outcome of evil is stated: “And whoever comes with an evil deed will be hurled face-first into the Fire. Are you rewarded except for what you used to do?” (27:90); and in the exchange between the followers and their leaders: “They will ˹all˺ hide ˹their˺ remorse when they see the torment. And We will put shackles around the necks of the disbelievers. Will they be rewarded except for what they used to do?” (34:33). The question is rhetorical because its answer is not in dispute. Nobody in the scene claims to have been given more than he earned. The formula's function is to state, at the moment of the punishment, that the punishment is a settlement rather than an infliction — and to say it in the hearing of the ones being punished.

**The Vocabulary of Earnings**

The word used for what they committed is from the root of earning, the same commercial vocabulary that runs through this chapter. Deeds are acquisitions; a life is a transaction; the outcome is a settlement of accounts. The Qur'an keeps the metaphor consistent, and the consistency is what makes its moral language coherent. The same image appears in the question asked about the disbelievers: “˹The believers will be asked,˺ ‘Have the disbelievers ˹not˺ been paid back for what they used to do?’” (83:36).

## Verse 10:53

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺, “Is this true?” Say, “Yes, by my Lord! Most certainly it is true! And you will have no escape.”

**A Direct Question and an Unhedged Answer**

The question is short and the answer is shorter. Is this true? Yes. Nothing in the reply qualifies the affirmation, offers a range of interpretations, or refers the matter to a future clarification. The Qur'an's answers are often complex, and where a question is loaded the reply usually dismantles the loading first — as it did with the demand for a date. Here the question is not loaded, or at least it is answered as though it were sincere, and the answer is given in the plainest form available in Arabic: *balā*, yes indeed, the particle used to affirm against a doubt. The verse therefore treats the question as a genuine request for information, and it meets it with an unqualified statement. That is unusual enough to be significant. The Prophet is not being asked to interpret or to persuade; he is being asked whether the warning is real, and he says that it is.

**“You Will Have No Escape”**

The third clause adds what the affirmation implies: the matter cannot be frustrated. The word *muʿjizūn* means those who render something incapable of reaching its object — those who escape, outstrip, or defeat it. The denial is absolute: you will not escape. The same recognition is recorded elsewhere in the strongest terms, in the speech of the jinn who heard the recitation: “˹Now,˺ we truly know that we cannot frustrate Allah on earth, nor can we escape from Him ˹into heaven˺” (72:12). The two directions are named there and both are closed, which is the sense of the phrase here. The clause completes the answer by removing the alternative to acceptance. A warning that could be avoided by flight would not require belief; this one cannot, and the verse says so in the same breath as the affirmation.

## Verse 10:54

> If every wrongdoer were to possess everything in the world, they would surely ransom themselves with it. They will hide ˹their˺ remorse when they see the torment. And they will be judged in all fairness, and none will be wronged

**The Ransom That Cannot Be Paid**

The verse opens with a conditional that is answered in the negative by every parallel passage. If the wrongdoer had all that is on the earth, he would offer it to ransom himself — and the Qur'an states repeatedly that it would not be accepted. “Indeed, if each of those who disbelieve then die as disbelievers were to offer a ransom of enough gold to fill the whole world, it would never be accepted from them” (3:91). “As for the disbelievers, even if they were to possess everything in the world twice over ˹and offer it all˺ to ransom themselves from the punishment of the Day of Judgment, it would never be accepted from them” (5:36). The escalation is deliberate: the whole world, then the whole world doubled. The verse here states the willingness and leaves the refusal to the passages that complete it, which is why it can be so brief. What it establishes is the value the wrongdoer assigns to his own safety at that moment: everything.

**The Wish Extends to Everything a Person Has**

The ransom is not limited to property. The Qur'an describes the wish in its most extreme form: “˹although˺ they will be made to see each other. The wicked will wish to ransom themselves from the punishment of that Day by their children” (70:11), and the passage continues with spouses, brothers, clan and everyone on earth. The sequence moves from wealth to kin to the whole of humanity, and the logic is that of a person who has run out of categories. When everything is available to be given, nothing is being held back, and the verse's point is that the offering has no purchasing power at all.

**Remorse Hidden at the Sight of the Torment**

The second clause describes a specific reaction: they will conceal their remorse when they see the punishment. The concealment is social. Remorse in private is one thing; remorse displayed before the people one misled, or before those one despised, is another. The same detail appears in the exchange between the followers and their leaders: “The lowly will say to the arrogant, ‘No! It was your plotting by day and night’… They will ˹all˺ hide ˹their˺ remorse when they see the torment” (34:33). The hiding is temporary, and the Qur'an records what follows it — an open confession: “They will cry, ‘Our Lord! Our ill-fate took hold of us, so we became a misguided people’” (23:106). The sequence is psychologically exact. Shame arrives first, while the pride that caused the situation is still intact; the confession comes later, when nothing is left to protect.

**Judged in Fairness, and None Wronged**

The final clause returns to the refrain. The judgment is rendered in justice, and nobody is wronged. The pairing is the sūrah's standard guarantee and it appears here at the moment of greatest severity, which is where it is most needed. “Whoever does good, it is to their own benefit. And whoever does evil, it is to their own loss. Your Lord is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation” (41:46); “My Word cannot be changed, nor am I unjust to ˹My˺ creation” (50:29). The placement is the argument. A description of the ransom and the remorse could easily leave the impression of a scene of pure suffering; the clause restores the frame and states that everything occurring in it has been measured. The verse therefore contains both the severity and its justification, and it does not allow either to be read without the other.

## Verse 10:55

> Surely to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth. Surely Allah’s promise is ˹always˺ true, but most of them do not know

**Ownership Stated Before the Promise**

The verse makes two assertions, and the first grounds the second. To God belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth. God's promise is true. The connection between them is not decorative; it is the reason the promise carries weight. A promise is credible in proportion to the promiser's control over what he has promised, and the verse establishes the control before relying on it. Nothing in the heavens or the earth lies outside the ownership being described, so nothing can prevent the fulfilment of what has been promised. The same reasoning appears where a believer answers a sceptical son: “Pity you. Have faith! Surely Allah's promise is true” (46:17). The affirmation there is made in the face of ridicule, and it rests on the same ground — the promiser is not a party who can be prevented.

**The Promise Includes the Punishment**

The context fixes the reference. The verses immediately before have described the ransom, the concealed remorse and the judgment, so the promise in view is the threatened punishment as much as the promised reward. That is worth stating plainly, because the word “promise” in ordinary use suggests something desirable. The Qur'an uses it in both directions, and here it covers the outcome the audience has been mocking. The effect is to remove the last comfort available to them. If the promise were only of reward, a sceptic could accept the reward and doubt the punishment; the verse treats both as equally covered by the same word and the same certainty. The ownership of the heavens and the earth is what makes both halves unavoidable. It is also what makes the promise personal rather than general: the One who owns the arrangement is not waiting on any part of it.

## Verse 10:56

> He ˹is the One Who˺ gives life and causes death, and to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned

**Both Ends of a Life in One Hand**

The verse states two acts and one destination. God gives life; God causes death; to Him all return. The pairing is not two separate claims but one claim about a single domain, and the Qur'an states it in exactly this form wherever authority is at issue. “O humanity! I am Allah's Messenger to you all. To Him ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. He gives life and causes death” (7:158). “Indeed, to Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. He gives life and causes death. And besides Allah you have no guardian or helper” (9:116). In both cases the statement is used to close a question about who has charge. The verse here does the same work at the end of a passage that has been asking who provides, who guides, who originates and who will judge.

**The Argument With the King**

The pairing has a documented use in argument, and the Qur'an records it. “˹Remember˺ when Abraham said, ‘My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death.’ He argued, ‘I too have the power to give life and cause death.’ Abraham challenged ˹him˺, ‘Allah causes the sun to rise from the east. So make it rise from the west.’ And so the disbeliever was dumbstruck” (2:258). The exchange is instructive because the king's claim is not obviously false — rulers do execute and pardon. What Abraham does is shift the claim to a domain the king cannot enter. The verse here makes the same distinction without the dialogue: giving life and causing death belong to the One who does both as a matter of course, and the return belongs to Him as well. No rival can claim the third item, and the third item is what makes the first two decisive.

**Why the Two Acts Belong Together**

Life and death are named as one operation because the Qur'an treats them as a single arrangement with a purpose. “˹He is the One˺ Who created death and life to test which of you is best in deeds. And He is the Almighty, All-Forgiving” (67:2). Death is listed first in that verse, which is unusual and significant — it is the frame within which life has its meaning. The verse here reverses the order to match the sequence a person experiences: given life, then taken in death, then returned. Read with 67:2, the arrangement is a test with a stated object, and the return is the point at which the result is declared. Nothing in the sequence is arbitrary, and that is what the verse asserts in three short clauses.

**The Verse as a Summary of the Chapter**

This verse does for the chapter what 10:55 does for the argument that preceded it. The sūrah has established that God created and conducts, provides and withholds, guides and leaves astray, judges and returns. The verse compresses all of it into the two acts no creature can perform and the destination no creature can avoid. It also supplies the ground on which the earlier promises rest: a return is credible only if the One who receives it is the One who gave the life in the first place. The same logic appears where resurrection is argued from the visible world: “See then the impact of Allah's mercy: how He gives life to the earth after its death! Surely That ˹same Creator˺ can raise the dead” (30:50). The verse states the principle; the earth supplies the demonstration.

The Qur'an draws the practical consequence of this in the context of loss: “Allah makes such thinking a cause of agony in their hearts. It is Allah who gives life and causes death. And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do” (3:156).

## Verse 10:57

> O humanity! Indeed, there has come to you a warning from your Lord, a cure for what is in the hearts, a guide, and a mercy for the believers

**Four Names for One Thing**

The verse describes the revelation with four terms, and each does distinct work. *Mawʿiẓa* — an urging or admonition, a warning that moves the hearer rather than merely informing him. *Shifāʾ* — a cure, the language of medicine applied to the interior. *Hudā* — guidance, direction toward a destination. *Raḥma* — mercy, the quality of what is given rather than its content. The four are not synonyms piled up for effect. A warning addresses the will, a cure addresses a condition, guidance addresses a direction, and mercy addresses the giver. The verse therefore describes the revelation from four angles at once, and the completeness of the description is the point: nothing a person needs from a revelation is left unnamed. The announcement that it “has come to you” is stated as an accomplished fact rather than a promise.

**A Cure for What Is in the Hearts**

The medical term is the verse's most distinctive claim. The object of the cure is specified: what is in the breasts. The interpretive tradition identifies the diseases — ignorance, doubt, hypocrisy and hatred — and the list is diagnostic rather than rhetorical. Each of those is a condition of the interior that produces outward behaviour, and each is treated elsewhere in the Qur'an as an illness rather than as a mere error. The same description is given where the effect is stated with its limit: “We send down the Quran as a healing and mercy for the believers, but it only increases the wrongdoers in loss” (17:82). And again, in the context of a challenge to the audience's capacity to receive it: “Say, ˹O Prophet˺, ‘It is a guide and a healing to the believers. As for those who disbelieve, there is deafness in their ears and blindness to it ˹in their hearts˺’” (41:44). The cure is real and its efficacy depends on the condition of the patient.

The description was not only theological. A group of the Prophet's companions once passed a tribe that refused them hospitality; when that tribe's chief was bitten by a snake — or stung by a scorpion — and nothing else worked, one of the companions recited Sūrat al-Fātiḥa over him and gathered his saliva, and the man was cured. They were given a flock of sheep in payment but would not take it until they had asked the Prophet, who smiled and said, “How do you know that Sūrat al-Fātiḥa is a ruqya? Take it, and assign a share for me” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5736, on the authority of Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī).

**The Last Two Are Specified for the Believers**

Guidance and mercy are qualified: for the believers. The Qur'an makes the same distinction sharply elsewhere: “We have revealed to you the Book as an explanation of all things, a guide, a mercy, and good news for those who ˹fully˺ submit” (16:89).

## Verse 10:58

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “In Allah’s grace and mercy let them rejoice. That is far better than whatever ˹wealth˺ they amass.”

**An Instruction to Rejoice**

The verse commands a feeling, and that is worth pausing on. *Falyafraḥū* — let them rejoice. The imperative form is used, which means rejoicing is being treated as something a person can be directed to do rather than as a mood that arrives unbidden. The Qur'an does not often command emotions, and where it does, the command is attached to an object: rejoice in this, and not in that. The verse therefore assumes two things. First, that joy is directed — it always has an object, and the object can be chosen. Second, that people are in fact rejoicing, and usually at the wrong things. The command is not a call to manufacture an emotion from nothing; it is a correction of the target. Read this way, the verse is practical rather than devotional: it tells a person where to place the rejoicing he already has.

**What the Grace and the Mercy Are**

The objects of rejoicing are named as God's *faḍl* and His *raḥma*, and the interpretive tradition identifies them concretely: the bounty is the Qur'an, and the mercy is Islam — or, in another reading, the Prophet himself, who was sent as a mercy to the worlds. “We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a mercy for the whole world” (21:107). Both identifications are supported by the immediate context, since the preceding verse has just described the revelation as guidance and mercy. The point of naming them is to make the objects specific. Rejoicing is being directed at something that has been given, something that arrived in history, something that can be described and examined. The verse is not commending a general feeling of blessedness; it is pointing at the thing described in the previous verse and saying that this is what deserves the response.

## Verse 10:59

> Ask ˹the pagans, O Prophet˺, “Have you seen that which Allah has sent down for you as a provision, of which you have made some lawful and some unlawful?” Say, “Has Allah given you authorization, or are you fabricating lies against Allah?”

**The Provision Came Down Whole**

The verse begins with what God did, and the wording is precise. He sent down provision — *anzala* — and the interpretive tradition notes that the verb here carries the sense of created and supplied, as in the statement about cattle: “And He produced for you four pairs of cattle” (39:6). The provision is described as having come from God in a complete state. Nothing about it was partial, conditional, or awaiting human completion. The verse's first move is therefore to establish the origin of the thing being discussed, and it does so before raising the charge. The audience is not being accused of inventing the provision; they are being accused of altering its terms after receiving it. That distinction is what gives the question its force.

**Then It Was Divided by Human Decision**

The second clause states what the audience did with it: you made some of it lawful and some unlawful. The practice is documented elsewhere in the Qur'an in concrete detail. “They say, ‘These cattle and crops are reserved—none may eat them except those we permit,’ so they claim. Some other cattle are exempted from labour and others are not slaughtered in Allah's Name—falsely attributing lies to Him. He will repay them for their lies” (6:138). And the named categories are given: “Allah has never ordained the ˹so-called˺ baḥîrah, sâ'ibah, waṣîlah, and ḥâm camels. But the disbelievers just fabricate lies about Allah, and most of them lack understanding” (5:103). The verses describe a system of religious restrictions built around categories God had not established — animals declared sacred, others declared forbidden, others released from work — all attributed to divine command.

**Authorization or Fabrication**

The second command supplies the alternatives, and there are only two. Has God given you permission, or are you fabricating lies against Him? The disjunction is exhaustive. Either the restriction has a divine source, in which case it can be produced, or it does not, in which case it is a lie attributed to God. There is no third option — no category of human religious legislation that stands on its own authority and yet binds conscience. The Qur'an states the prohibition in general terms elsewhere: “Do not falsely declare with your tongues, ‘This is lawful, and that is unlawful,’ ˹only˺ fabricating lies against Allah. Indeed, those who fabricate lies against Allah will never succeed” (16:116). The verse here makes the same point in the form of a question with no available answer.

## Verse 10:60

> What do those who fabricate lies against Allah expect on Judgment Day? Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most of them are ungrateful

**A Question About Expectation**

The verse opens with a question that has no answer, and the absence of an answer is the point. What do those who fabricate lies against God reckon will happen on the Day of Resurrection? The word *ẓann* covers reckoning, supposing and expecting, and the question asks what expectation the fabricators are operating under. Nothing in the preceding verse suggested they had one. They had made things lawful and unlawful in God's name and had not appeared to consider the consequences. The question therefore exposes a gap rather than requesting information. It asks the audience to state the plan, and the inability to state one is the answer. The same method runs through the chapter: a question that cannot be answered reveals the position it was hiding.

**The Bounty and the Ingratitude in One Sentence**

The second sentence is the verse's balance. God is had of bounty toward humanity; most of them are ungrateful. The two clauses are placed side by side without a connecting argument, and the contrast does the work. The formula is a refrain in the Qur'an: “Surely your Lord is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most of them are ungrateful” (27:73); “Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most people are ungrateful” (40:61). The repetition indicates a settled observation rather than a rhetorical flourish. The bounty is stated as a fact about God's dealings, and the ingratitude as a fact about human response, and the verse does not try to reconcile them. The disproportion is the content of the statement.

**Ingratitude as a Failure to Use**

The definition of ingratitude supplied by the interpretive tradition is active rather than emotional. The ungrateful are those who do not employ their intellects to ponder God's signs, who do not follow the call of His messengers, and who do not listen to and benefit from His revealed books. Each item names an use that was not made. The Qur'an describes the scale of what is being declined: “And He has granted you all that you asked Him for. If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never be able to number them. Indeed humankind is truly unfair, ˹totally˺ ungrateful” (14:34). The verse therefore treats ingratitude as a failure of employment rather than a failure of feeling. A person can be grateful in sentiment and ungrateful in practice, and it is the practice that the verse measures.

## Verse 10:61

> There is no activity you may be engaged in ˹O Prophet˺ or portion of the Quran you may be reciting, nor any deed you ˹all˺ may be doing except that We are a Witness over you while doing it. Not ˹even˺ an atom’s weight is hidden from your Lord on earth or in heaven; nor anything smaller or larger than that, but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record

**Three Things Named, One Witness Over All of Them**

The verse lists three items and then puts all of them under a single gaze. What you are occupied with. What you recite of the Qur'an. What you all are doing. The three are not a random selection. The first covers the Prophet's ordinary activity — the tasks, errands and dealings of a life. The second covers the one act that defines his role: the recitation itself. The word for witness is the same one used elsewhere of God's testimony to His own unity — “Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him” (3:18) — and it carries the same weight of formal attestation rather than casual notice.

**The Witness Is Present During the Act**

The temporal clause is the verse's most precise feature. Not after the deed, not when it is reported, not at the reckoning: *while you are engaged in it*. The witnessing is contemporaneous with the action, which changes what the verse is claiming. A record made afterward can be incomplete; a witness present during the act cannot be. The Qur'an makes the same point about the recording itself: “not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺” (50:18), and again in the description of those who are observed by vigilant, noble scribes who know whatever you do (82:10–12). The verse therefore removes the possibility of an interval in which something could escape. There is no private moment, and the verse says so without dramatizing it — simply by placing the witnessing inside the doing.

**The Atom's Weight**

The second sentence turns from activity to measurement. Not an atom's weight — *mithqāl dharra*, the weight of a small ant or of a speck of dust — escapes your Lord, on earth or in the heavens. The phrase is the Qur'an's standard unit of the negligible, and it appears in the sūrah of the earthquake where the reckoning is described: “So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it. And whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it” (99:7–8). The pairing there is exhaustive, and the unit is the smallest thing the language had. The verse here adds the two locations — earth and heaven — which closes the spatial dimension as well as the quantitative one. Nothing is too small to be noticed, and there is no place where the noticing does not reach.

**The Perfect Record**

The verse ends with a book. Everything described is in a *kitāb mubīn* — a clear, manifest, plain Record. The interpretive tradition explains the term as a book God has written in which He has fixed the forms of all knowable things, and the Qur'an uses the same phrase of revelation itself: “There certainly has come to you from Allah a light and a clear Book” (5:15). The double use is deliberate. The record of deeds and the record of guidance are described with the same words, which makes the two inseparable: what is written down will be read against what was sent. The verse therefore does not present the recording as a hostile act of surveillance.

## Verse 10:62

> There will certainly be no fear for the close servants of Allah, nor will they grieve

**Two Negations Facing Two Directions**

The verse removes two things, and they are not the same kind of thing. Fear faces forward; it is the anticipation of something not yet arrived. Grief faces backward; it is the presence of something already lost. Between the two they cover the whole of a person's relation to time — what is coming and what has gone — and the verse declares both absent. The emphatic opening, *alā*, functions as an announcement: take notice. What follows is then stated absolutely, with no qualification about degree, duration or circumstance. The structure is unusual for a promise. Most descriptions of reward describe something positive to be gained; this one describes two burdens to be removed, and the removal is the reward. A person who has neither fear nor grief has nothing left to suffer, and the verse treats that as enough.

**Who the Awliyāʾ Are**

The term translated as close servants is *awliyāʾ*, the plural of *walī*, and it is one of the richest words in the Qur'an's vocabulary of relation. Its root carries nearness, protection, allegiance and succession, and the same word is used of God's relation to believers and of believers' relations to one another. The Qur'an regulates those relations carefully: “Believers should not take disbelievers as guardians instead of the believers—and whoever does so will have nothing to hope for from Allah— unless it is a precaution against their tyranny” (3:28). Here the word is used of God's own friends, and the tradition reads it at two levels.

**What Is Reported About Them**

The tradition preserves a description of this group in the Prophet's words: when they are seen, God is remembered. The description is remarkable because it is completely about effect rather than status. Nothing is claimed about their rank, their knowledge or their miracles; the only test given is what happens to an observer in their presence. That is consistent with the definition that follows in the next verse, which is behavioural rather than honorific. The same concern with nearness to God is developed at length in a ḥadīth qudsī recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6502, on the authority of Abū Hurayra: God says, “Whoever shows hostility to a pious worshipper of Mine, I declare war against him,” and then describes how a servant draws near through the obligatory acts and continues to draw near through supererogatory ones until God loves him. The report grounds the verse's category in practice rather than in title.

## Verse 10:63

> ˹They are˺ those who are faithful and are mindful ˹of Him˺

**A Category Defined in Two Words**

The previous verse announced that God's close servants have no fear and no grief, and it did not say who they are. This verse supplies the definition, and the definition is two words: those who believe and are mindful. *Āmanū* — they believed, they were faithful. *Yattaqūn* — they are mindful, they guard themselves, they keep God's command in view. Nothing else is added. No lineage, no office, no spiritual state, no title, no lineage of teachers, no miracle, no knowledge beyond what belief and mindfulness require. The verse therefore closes the category against every claim that might be made on grounds other than these two. A reader looking for a description of the saints will find here the plainest possible one, and its plainness is the point: the category is public, and anyone can check whether he meets it.

**The Definition Uses the Chapter's Own Vocabulary**

Both terms in the definition have already been at work in this chapter. Belief is what the audience has been accused of lacking, and it is what the messengers demanded from their people. Mindfulness — *taqwā* — is the quality the chapter's parables turn on: the garden cut down belongs to people who were not mindful, and the Home of Peace is prepared for those who are. The definition therefore introduces nothing new. It names the two qualities the chapter has been describing from its opening verses, and it identifies the people who have them as God's friends. That continuity is what makes the definition credible. A category invented at the point of reward would be suspect; a category built from the chapter's own terms is simply the naming of what has already been described.

---

## Verse 10:64

> For them is good news in this worldly life and the Hereafter. There is no change in the promise of Allah. That is ˹truly˺ the ultimate triumph

**Good News in Both Worlds**

The verse extends the promise beyond the Hereafter, and that extension is the verse's distinctive claim. *Bushrā* — good news, glad tidings — is given to them in the life of this world and in the life to come. Most of the Qur'an's promises are deferred, and deferral is what makes faith difficult. This verse states that part of the return is immediate. The believer is not only promised something later; he is given something now, and the something now is of the same kind as the something later. The wording does not specify a quantity or a form, which leaves the claim general enough to cover whatever a believer actually experiences. The verse therefore answers a common objection to religious commitment — that it costs the present for the sake of the future — by denying the premise. The present is not only paid from; it is paid to.

**No Change in the Words of God**

The second sentence is the guarantee, and it is stated as a fact about God rather than as a reassurance to the reader. There is no changing the words of God. The same formula appears elsewhere with the same force: “The Word of your Lord has been perfected in truth and justice. None can change His Words. And He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing” (6:115). And in the context of recitation and reliance: “Recite what has been revealed to you from the Book of your Lord. None can change His Words, nor can you find any refuge besides Him” (18:27). The three passages make the same point from different angles: the words are complete, they cannot be altered, and there is nowhere else to go. Applied here, the formula means that the promise to the friends of God is not contingent on anything they might later do or fail to do, and not subject to revision by anyone.

---

## Verse 10:65

> Do not let their words grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. Surely all honour and power belongs to Allah. He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing

**A Command Addressed to a Feeling**

The verse opens with a prohibition, and the thing prohibited is grief. *Lā yaḥzunka qawluhum* — let not their speech grieve you. The command is addressed to the Prophet, and it concerns not what people do but what they say. The distinction is significant. Harm can be guarded against; speech cannot be prevented, and its effect arrives without permission. The verse therefore addresses the one part of the situation the Prophet cannot control, and it addresses it directly rather than through an argument about why the words do not matter. That is why the instruction is a command rather than a consolation. Consolation would leave the grief in place and argue with it; the command treats the grief as something that can be declined, and it supplies the reason immediately afterward.

**The Same Instruction Given Elsewhere**

The command is not unique to this verse, and the parallel is close. “So do not let their words grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. Indeed, We ˹fully˺ know what they conceal and what they reveal” (36:76). The two passages give the same instruction and support it with the same kind of reason: God knows what is hidden and what is open. The repetition indicates a sustained feature of the Prophet's situation rather than a single incident. He was spoken against continuously, and the Qur'an returns to the subject because the grief was a real and recurring cost of the task. The verse here adds what the other does not: the statement about honour. That addition gives the instruction its ground, and it is why this version of the command is the fuller of the two.

**Why Honour Cannot Be Held by Anyone Else**

The reasoning behind the claim is stated elsewhere in the Book, and it is severe. “And do not invoke any other god with Allah. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. Everything is bound to perish except He Himself. All authority belongs to Him” (28:88). If everything perishes except God, then no lasting honour can belong to anything else, and the honour that creatures hold is temporary by definition. The Qur'an draws the practical conclusion in a verse addressed to those who seek it: “Whoever seeks honour and power, then ˹let them know that˺ all honour and power belongs to Allah. To Him ˹alone˺ good words ascend, and righteous deeds are raised up by Him. As for those who plot evil, they will suffer a severe punishment” (35:10). The verse therefore redirects the search rather than condemning it. Honour is a real thing and it can be sought, but only where it exists.

---

## Verse 10:66

> Certainly to Allah ˹alone˺ belong all those in the heavens and all those on the earth. And what do those who associate others with Allah really follow? They follow nothing but assumptions and do nothing but lie

**The Question Is About Following, Not About Believing**

The verb the verse uses is significant. It does not ask what they worship or what they believe; it asks what they follow — *yattabiʿūna*. The choice shifts the enquiry from opinion to conduct. Association is not presented here as a mistaken idea but as a practice, something a person does with his life, day after day. That framing matters because it makes the charge harder to evade. A person can defend a belief by explaining how he came to hold it; he cannot easily explain what he is following when the thing followed has no existence independent of the following. The verse therefore asks about the object of the practice rather than the content of the creed, and the answer it supplies is that there is no object at all. What remains is the practice itself, sustained by nothing outside the one performing it.

**Following Assumption**

The answer is given in two halves, and the first names the substitute for an object. They follow nothing but assumption — *ẓann*. The word covers conjecture, supposition and the acceptance of something without evidence, and the Qur'an treats it as the characteristic epistemic state of those who reject revelation. The same pairing of assumption and falsehood appears in a warning addressed to the Prophet: “˹O Prophet!˺ If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah's Way. They follow nothing but assumptions and do nothing but lie” (6:116). The formulation is identical, which shows that the verse here is not making a local accusation but applying a general description. Assumption is not a mild word in this vocabulary. It denotes a claim held without support, and holding it is the actual offence.

---

## Verse 10:67

> He is the One Who has made the night for you to rest in and the day bright. Surely in this are signs for people who listen

**Rest and Seeing**

The two halves of the day are assigned two functions, and the functions are complementary. The night is made for rest — *sakan*, the stilling after exertion, the settling of a body and a household. The day is made for seeing — *mubṣir*, bright, giving sight. The first addresses recovery; the second addresses work. Together they describe a complete economy of a life: effort by day, restoration by night, repeated without interruption. The same description appears where the bounty is stated: “It is Allah Who has made the night for you to rest in and the day bright. Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most people are ungrateful” (40:61). And where the alternation is listed among the signs: “And one of His signs is your sleep by night and by day ˹for rest˺ as well as your seeking His bounty ˹in both˺. Surely in this are signs for people who listen” (30:23).

**The Verse Is Built From Two Existing Passages**

There is a formal feature worth recording. The first half of this verse is identical to part of 40:61, and the second half to part of 30:23. The verse is therefore assembled from wordings that already exist elsewhere in the Book. That is not a defect of composition; it is a feature of how the Qur'an builds its arguments. A formulation established in one place is reused in another, carrying its meaning with it. A listener who has heard the sūrah of the Forgiving Believer will recognize the phrase about night and rest; one who has heard the sūrah of the Romans will recognize the phrase about signs for those who listen. The repetition is what makes the recitation cumulative, and it is why the Qur'an can state a complex claim in a single line. A listener who has heard neither still receives the whole argument, because the wording carries its own explanation.

---

## Verse 10:68

> They say, “Allah has offspring.” Glory be to Him! He is the Self-Sufficient. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. You have no proof of this! Do you say about Allah what you do not know

**“Glory Be to Him”**

The first response is the shortest and the most absolute. *Subḥānah* — glory be to Him, far is He above what they say. The phrase is the Qur'an's formula of exclusion, used wherever something is asserted of God that cannot be. It does not argue; it removes. The effect is to place the claim outside the range of what can be said rather than inside the range of what can be disputed. The same formula introduces the correction elsewhere: “They say, ‘Allah has offspring.’ Glory be to Him! In fact, to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth—all are subject to His Will” (2:116). The verse here follows that pattern exactly, and then goes further by naming the attribute that makes the claim impossible.

**The Self-Sufficient**

The name given is *al-ghanī* — the One who is without need, self-enough, requiring nothing from anything. The choice is precise, because offspring exists for a reason: for company, for help, for continuation, for the extension of a line. Every one of those reasons assumes a need. A being who lacks nothing cannot have offspring in the sense the claim intends, since offspring answers a want that does not exist in Him. The name therefore does not merely deny the claim; it explains why the claim is incoherent. The same reasoning appears where the assertion is repeated with its own refutation attached: “O People of the Book! Do not go to extremes about your faith… Allah is only One God. Glory be to Him! He is far above having a son!” (4:171). The name therefore does the work that an argument would otherwise have to do, and it does it in one word.

**“You Have No Proof”**

The fourth response changes register from theology to evidence. *Mā lakum min sulṭān* — you have no authority, no warrant, no proof. The word *sulṭān* is the Qur'an's technical term for demonstrable authorization, and it is invoked repeatedly in the context of association: those who take protectors beside God have no proof (3:151); those who dispute about God after He has been responded to have no proof before Him (42:16). The charge is therefore not that the claim is unpleasant but that it is unsupported. The verse does not ask the audience to produce a better argument; it states that they cannot produce any, which is a different and stronger assertion. A claim about God made without authorization is, on the verse's own terms, not a religious position but a fabrication. That is why the next verse states the consequence in the form of a general rule.

**Speaking About God Without Knowledge**

The final question is the verse's most general statement, and it applies beyond the claim about offspring. Do you say about God what you do not know? The question identifies the underlying offence, which is not the particular content but the manner of asserting it. Religious speech about God requires knowledge, and knowledge requires a source. The chapter has been building toward this point: the division of provision into lawful and unlawful was attacked on the same ground, and the general prohibition is stated elsewhere — “Do not falsely declare with your tongues, ‘This is lawful, and that is unlawful,’ ˹only˺ fabricating lies against Allah” (16:116). The verse therefore converts a theological dispute into a rule about speech, and the rule is one the reader can apply to his own statements.

The Qur'an makes the same point where the claim is stated most bluntly: “They say, ‘The Most Compassionate has offspring.’ You have certainly made an outrageous claim” (19:88–89).

---

## Verse 10:69

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Indeed, those who fabricate lies against Allah will never succeed.”

**Fabrication Is Not Error**

The word used is from the root *f-r-y*, which means to cut, to devise and to invent. It denotes something produced rather than something mistaken. The distinction is important and the Qur'an maintains it carefully. A person who holds a false belief through ignorance is described as unaware; a person who invents religious content is described as fabricating. The first is an epistemic failure, the second an act. The chapter has already used the term where it described the invention of lawful and unlawful categories, and the parallel formulation appears in a general prohibition: “Indeed, those who fabricate lies against Allah will never succeed” (16:116). The identity of the wording across the two chapters shows that this is a fixed charge with a fixed consequence, not a contextual rebuke. The same sentence therefore stands wherever the act occurs.

**“They Will Not Succeed”**

The consequence is stated in the Qur'an's own term for success. *Falāḥ* covers prosperity, deliverance, attainment and the reaching of what one sought. Its negation is therefore comprehensive. A fabricator may gain followers, wealth, position and even temporary religious authority, and the verse does not deny any of that. It denies *falāḥ*, which in the Qur'an's usage always extends beyond this life to the final outcome. The claim is that whatever a fabricator achieves, he does not arrive at the thing he was actually seeking, because the thing he was seeking is God's acceptance and the outcome he gets is the opposite. The verse therefore makes a statement about the end rather than the middle, and it is the end that determines whether anyone succeeded.

---

## Verse 10:70

> ˹It is only˺ a brief enjoyment in this world, then to Us is their return, then We will make them taste the severe punishment for their disbelief

**Tasting**

The verb is the same one used where the punishment was described earlier in the chapter, and the repetition is deliberate. To taste is to experience directly, without intermediary and without distance. The demand that produced the earlier passage was a demand to see; the answer was that they would taste. Here the same verb is applied to the fabricators, and the object is specified as severe punishment. The word for severe — *alīm* — denotes pain that reaches and remains, and the Qur'an uses it consistently of the punishment of those who denied. The verse therefore does not describe a symbolic consequence. It describes an experience, and it uses the vocabulary of the senses to say so, which is the strongest form of description available. Nothing is left to inference.

The final clause states why. *Bi-mā kānū yakfurūn* — for what they used to disbelieve, or because of their disbelief. The attachment of the reason is a consistent feature of the chapter's descriptions of punishment, and it appears in the same form where the wrongdoers are told that they are rewarded only for what they committed. The verse therefore does not present the punishment as arbitrary or as disproportionate. It presents it as the settlement of a specific account, and it names the entry in that account. Disbelief here is not only the absence of belief; it is the act described in the previous verses — inventing speech about God and attributing it to Him. The reason clause closes the case by showing that the punishment matches the offence.

---

## Verse 10:71

> Relate to them ˹O Prophet˺ the story of Noah when he said to his people, “O my People! If my presence and my reminders to you of Allah’s signs are unbearable to you, then ˹know that˺ I have put my trust in Allah. So devise a plot along with your associate-gods—and you do not have to be secretive about your plot—then carry it out against me without delay

**The Command to Relate**

The verse begins with an instruction rather than a statement: relate to them. *Utl* — recite, recount, tell. The chapter turns at this point from argument to narrative, and the turn is commanded rather than incidental. Stories in the Qur'an are not illustration; they are evidence of a different kind. An argument can be disputed, but a recorded outcome cannot, and the outcome is what the story supplies. The command is addressed to the Prophet and directed at the same audience that has been arguing with him, which means the story is not a digression for the believer's benefit. It is the next move in the dispute. The audience is being shown a case identical in structure to their own: a messenger, a people who found him unbearable, and an ending.

**Noah in the Chapter's Sequence**

Noah is the first of the messengers narrated here, and the choice is significant. He is described elsewhere as having remained among his people for a thousand years less fifty (29:14), which makes his case the longest recorded instance of the situation the chapter is describing. The same story is told in several places — in the Heights, in Hūd, in the Believers, in the Poets, in those Ranged in Ranks, in the Moon, and in the sūrah that bears his name. The repetition is not redundancy. Each account selects different parts of the same events, and this one selects the moment of confrontation, because that is what the chapter needs. The Prophet has been told repeatedly that he cannot compel belief; he is now shown a messenger who said so to his people's faces and then invited them to do their worst.

---

## Verse 10:72

> And if you turn away, ˹remember˺ I have never demanded a reward from you ˹for delivering the message˺. My reward is only from Allah. And I have been commanded to be one of those who submit ˹to Allah˺.”

**No Reward Ever Requested**

The central claim is stated absolutely: I have not asked you for any reward. The negation covers the past as well as the present, and it covers every form of payment. This is one of the recognized marks of prophecy in the Qur'an, and it is stated as a criterion rather than as a boast. The sūrah of Yā-Sīn puts it in the form of an instruction to the audience: “Follow those who ask no reward of you, and are ˹rightly˺ guided” (36:21). The two conditions are joined there as they are joined here — no payment, and right guidance — and the joining makes the absence of payment evidential. A person seeking gain from a message will shape it to please those who pay. A person seeking nothing can say what is true regardless of its reception. The claim therefore supports the message rather than merely describing the messenger.

---

## Verse 10:73

> But they still rejected him, so We saved him and those with him in the Ark and made them successors, and drowned those who rejected Our signs. See then what was the end of those who had been warned

**The Drowning in the Same Sentence**

The second part of the response is stated without pause: and We drowned those who denied Our signs. The two clauses are joined by a single conjunction, and the joining is the verse's structure. Salvation and destruction are not two separate events but one event seen from two sides, and the verse refuses to separate them. The same construction appears in the parallel account: “But they rejected him, so We saved him and those with him in the Ark, and drowned those who rejected Our signs. They were certainly a blind people” (7:64). The wording is nearly identical, which shows that this is a fixed formulation rather than an improvised summary. The pairing is what makes the story usable as a precedent: the outcome is not partial, and it is not delayed beyond the term.

**Three Words That End a Millennium**

The verse opens with the shortest possible report: they denied him. *Fa-kadhdhabūhu*. After nine and a half centuries of warning, after the speech recorded in the previous verses, after the challenge to plot openly — the outcome is given in one verb. The compression is not a stylistic choice. It is a judgement on the length of the exchange. A thousand years of argument produce a single word of result, and the verse states that word without comment. The effect is to make the reader feel the disproportion between the effort expended and the response received, which is the disproportion the Prophet was living with. The verse does not moralize about it. It reports it and moves immediately to what God did.

**Salvation and Succession**

The response has two parts, and the first is positive. God saved Noah and those with him in the Ark, and He made them successors — *khulafāʾ*. The word denotes those who come after others and take their place, and the Qur'an uses it in several related senses. It is the word used of the authority placed on earth: “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth” (2:30). It is used of the general human condition: “He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you” (6:165). And it is used of Noah's people specifically, in the address to the ʿĀd: “Remember that He made you successors after the people of Noah and increased you greatly in stature” (7:69). The verse here makes the same point in miniature — the saved do not merely survive; they inherit.

---

## Verse 10:74

> Then after him We sent ˹other˺ messengers to their ˹own˺ people and they came to them with clear proofs. But they would not believe in what they had rejected before. This is how We seal the hearts of the transgressors

**The Messengers Between Noah and Moses**

The verse moves from a single case to a sequence. After Noah, messengers were sent to their own peoples. The tradition identifies them: the pre-Islamic Arabian prophets Hūd, Ṣāliḥ and Shuʿayb, together with the biblical figures Abraham and Lot, among others. The phrase "to their people" is repeated deliberately — each messenger was sent to his own community, in its own language, addressing its own practices. The verse therefore establishes the pattern the chapter asserted earlier: for every community there has been a messenger. What is new here is the temporal ordering. The messengers are not a simultaneous plurality but a succession, each following the last, which makes the history a series of comparable cases rather than a single unusual event.

**The Sealing of Hearts**

The final clause names the mechanism: thus do We seal the hearts of the transgressors. The sealing is a Qur'anic term with a precise meaning. It is described in the sūrah's opening: “Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment” (2:7). The image is of a closure that prevents anything from entering, and the Qur'an uses it of people whose repeated refusal has produced an inability. The verse here states it as a divine act — We seal — which raises the question of whether the sealing is arbitrary. The answer is supplied by the preceding clause. The sealing follows the habituated denial; it is what that denial becomes. The transgression is named as the condition of the sealed, not as an incidental description.

---

## Verse 10:75

> Then after these ˹messengers˺ We sent Moses and Aaron to Pharaoh and his chiefs with Our signs. But they behaved arrogantly and were a wicked people

**Two Messengers Sent Together**

The verse names Moses and Aaron, and the pairing is distinctive. Most of the messengers in the preceding verse are sent singly, each to his own people. Here two are sent to one people, and the Qur'an explains the reason elsewhere: Aaron was given as a support at Moses' own request. The arrangement is significant for what it says about the mission rather than about the men. The task was addressed to a regime, not to a tribe, and it required two voices. The verse therefore treats the pairing as part of the sending rather than as an incidental detail, and it names both men in the same clause so that neither is subordinated to the other in the account.

**To Pharaoh and His Chiefs**

The recipients are named in two groups, and the two groups represent the two levels of power. Pharaoh is the ruler; the chiefs — *al-malaʾ* — are the dignitaries around him, the people of highest social standing in Egyptian society. The verse addresses the mission to both, which means the message was delivered at the top of the structure rather than at its base. That is unusual. Most of the messengers narrated in the Qur'an are sent to their own tribes and speak to their neighbours. Moses and Aaron are sent into a court. The naming of the chiefs alongside Pharaoh also distributes the responsibility: the rejection that follows belongs to the ruling group as a whole, not to one man's obstinacy alone.

The signs are specified as God's own — *bi-āyātinā* — and the possessive matters. They are not Moses' achievements or demonstrations of his skill. The Qur'an enumerates them elsewhere: “We surely gave Moses nine clear signs” (17:101), and the tradition identifies the hand and the staff as the two that appear first. The signs are described as clear proofs in the parallel accounts, and their clarity is what makes the rejection culpable. A messenger who arrives without evidence can be dismissed; one who arrives with demonstrable signs cannot. The verse therefore establishes the evidential basis before describing the response, and the order is the same one used in the previous verse.

**“They Behaved Arrogantly”**

The response is given in one verb: *istakbarū* — they behaved arrogantly, they considered themselves too great. The word is the Qur'an's standard term for the specific failure of those who reject a messenger, and it is used of Pharaoh's court repeatedly: “So We plagued them with floods, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood—all as clear signs, but they persisted in arrogance and were a wicked people” (7:133). The arrogance is not pride in the ordinary sense. It is a refusal grounded in self-assessment — the conviction that accepting the message would be beneath the accepter, and that the accepter's standing would be reduced by the act of receiving it from another. The Qur'an names the consequence of that state directly: “Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled” (40:60). The verse therefore identifies the obstacle as internal rather than intellectual.

---

## Verse 10:76

> When the truth came to them from Us, they said, “This is certainly pure magic!”

**The Source Is Stated Before the Accusation**

The verse opens by fixing where the thing came from. When the truth came to them from Us. The preposition matters, and it is doing argumentative work before the accusation is even reported. The audience is told where the thing came from at the moment they are told that it arrived. The signs are described as God's in the previous verse; here the truth itself is described as coming from Him. The verse therefore establishes the origin of what was rejected in the same breath as the rejection, which makes the accusation that follows a statement about the accusers rather than about the thing accused. Nothing in the verse questions the character of the signs. The question it raises is about the people who named them.

**A Charge Made Against Every Messenger**

The accusation is not peculiar to Pharaoh's court, and the Qur'an records its recurrence across the messengers. Speaking of Jesus: “How I prevented the Children of Israel from harming you when they came to them with clear proofs and the disbelievers among them said, ‘This is nothing but pure magic’” (5:110). And of the Prophet himself: “Yet when the Prophet came to them with clear proofs, they said, ‘This is pure magic’” (61:6). The identical wording in both passages shows that the charge is a standard response rather than a considered finding. It is what is said when a sign cannot be denied and its source cannot be accepted. The verse therefore places Pharaoh's court inside a pattern, and the pattern is what makes the story useful to the audience hearing it.

---

## Verse 10:77

> Moses responded, “Is this what you say about the truth when it has come to you? Is this magic? Magicians will never succeed.”

**A Question Instead of a Denial**

Moses does not answer the accusation with a counter-accusation or with a defence of his own character. He answers it with a question that turns the charge back on the speakers: is this what you say about the truth when it has come to you? The question is not rhetorical in the empty sense; it identifies an inconsistency. The truth has arrived, and the response to it is a label. By putting the two side by side in a single sentence, Moses makes the disproportion visible. Nothing needs to be added to the question, because the contrast does the argument. The verse therefore shows a messenger handling hostile speech in the most economical way available — by asking the speakers to look at their own words.

**“Magicians Will Not Succeed”**

The final clause is a general principle rather than a personal prediction. *Lā yufliḥu s-sāḥirūn* — the magicians do not prosper. The reasoning is supplied by the tradition: their craft is founded on deception and falsehood, and what is founded on deception cannot reach a lasting end. The same statement appears where Moses is instructed before the contest: “Cast what is in your right hand, and it will swallow up what they have made, for what they have made is no more than a magic trick. And magicians can never succeed wherever they go” (20:69). The clause therefore does two things at once. It predicts the outcome of the coming contest, and it states a rule about the craft itself — that its methods exclude the possibility of real success.

---

## Verse 10:78

> They argued, “Have you come to turn us away from the faith of our forefathers so that the two of you may become supreme in the land? We will never believe in you!”

**An Argument From Ancestral Practice**

The reply opens with the charge that appears most often in the Qur'an's accounts of rejected messengers. Have you come to turn us away from what we found our fathers upon? The objection is not that the message is false, incoherent or unsupported. It is that it disrupts an inheritance. The same response is recorded of the Makkan opponents in almost identical terms: “When it is said to them, ‘Come to Allah's revelations and to the Messenger,’ they reply, ‘What we found our forefathers practicing is good enough for us.’ ˹Would they still do so,˺ even if their forefathers had absolutely no knowledge or guidance?” (5:104). The question appended there is the answer the verse here leaves implicit. Ancestral practice is not evidence of correctness; it is evidence of age.

**The Qur'an's Answer to the Ancestral Objection**

The objection is not left standing anywhere in the Book, and the answer is always the same shape. The practice of the forefathers is examined for knowledge rather than for age. The question appended in the parallel passage is blunt: would they still follow, even if their forefathers had absolutely no knowledge or guidance? (5:104). The same question appears where the response is described as following what Satan invites them to (31:21). The argument is therefore not that tradition is worthless but that it is not evidence. A practice inherited from people who did not know is inherited ignorance, and the verse here records the objection in its purest form so that the answer can be applied to it directly.

---

## Verse 10:79

> Pharaoh demanded, “Bring me every skilled magician.”

**The Regime's Response to a Sign**

The verse records Pharaoh's answer to everything that has preceded it, and the answer is not an argument. He does not produce evidence, examine the signs, or address the charge that magicians do not prosper. He summons professionals. The response is administrative rather than intellectual: a problem has appeared, and the state's specialists will handle it. That is the verse's central observation. When a regime cannot answer a claim on its merits, it responds with resources, and the resources are chosen to match the category it has assigned to the claim. Having named the signs as magic, the court now acts on that naming.

The demand is exhaustive: every skilled magician — *kulla sāḥirin ʿalīm*. The two descriptors are joined, and both matter. *Sāḥir* identifies the profession; *ʿalīm* identifies mastery within it, the one who knows the craft deeply. The verse therefore describes a mobilization rather than a request. The same demand appears in the parallel accounts, with the same emphasis on completeness: the chiefs are told “to bring you every clever magician” (7:112), and elsewhere they propose dispatching mobilizers to all the cities “to bring you every skilled magician” (26:36–37). The scale of the mobilization is itself evidence of how seriously the court took the signs, and it contradicts the dismissal implied by the accusation of magic.

**The Court's Confidence and Its Basis**

There is a practical dimension to the demand that the narrative leaves for the reader to supply. Summoning every master magician in the country requires messengers, travel, time and payment, and the parallel accounts record the magicians asking what they will receive if they prevail (7:113). The mobilization is therefore an expensive act of state, undertaken to answer a single man. The disproportion is itself informative. The court is spending real resources to maintain a characterization, and the characterization is what the following verses will remove. The verse thus records the cost of a false explanation before the explanation collapses, which is the pattern the chapter describes wherever wealth and truth are set against each other.

---

## Verse 10:80

> When the magicians came, Moses said to them, “Cast whatever you wish to cast!”

**The Arrival Is Reported in Three Words**

The verse opens with the arrival: when the magicians came. Nothing is said about their number, their preparations or their confidence, although the previous verse has established that every master of the craft in the country was summoned. The compression is deliberate. The narrative has already supplied the scale; it does not need to repeat it. What matters is that the mobilization succeeded and that the confrontation is now unavoidable. The verse therefore moves directly to the exchange that follows, and it does so without any description of the scene, which leaves the reader's attention completely on the words.

The order of speaking is significant. The magicians have arrived at Pharaoh's summons, on Pharaoh's terms, in a contest designed to produce Pharaoh's conclusion — and the first words are Moses'. He does not wait to be challenged, does not respond to a provocation, and does not ask for his turn. He opens the encounter. The parallel accounts record a negotiation about who casts first, and in each of them Moses ends up yielding the first move deliberately: “They said, ‘O Moses! Either you cast, or let us be the first to cast.’ Moses responded, ‘No, you go first’” (20:65–66). The verse here compresses that exchange into a single instruction, and the instruction has the same effect.

**“Whatever You Wish to Cast”**

The wording is unusually open. Moses does not specify what they should produce, does not set conditions, and does not limit the demonstration. *Mā kuntum ṣāniʿīn* — what you were going to make, whatever your craft can do. The openness is the point. A man who feared the outcome would impose restrictions; a man who knows the result will differ from what the court expects has no reason to. The verse therefore records a confidence that is not stated as a claim but showed in the form of the invitation. The same instruction appears verbatim in the parallel account: “Moses said to them, ‘Cast whatever you wish to cast’” (26:43), which shows that this is a fixed formulation rather than an improvised line.

**The Instruction Is Not a Challenge to the Crowd**

It matters that the sentence is addressed to the magicians rather than delivered to the audience. Moses' instruction redirects the encounter to the practitioners themselves, which changes its character. The parallel account records what their craft actually produced — ropes and staffs made to appear as though they were slithering (20:66) — and it is precisely those men who will recognize, within moments, that what they are watching is not of the same kind.

---

## Verse 10:81

> When they did, Moses said, “What you have produced is mere magic, Allah will surely make it useless, for Allah certainly does not set right the work of the corruptors

**The Verdict Arrives Before the Outcome**

The magicians cast, and the first thing that happens is not a miracle but a sentence. Moses names what they have produced before anything has failed. That order is the verse's central feature. A verdict delivered after the event would be a commentary on a visible result; a verdict delivered at the moment of casting is a statement of knowledge. The verse therefore presents Moses as someone who already knows what the craft can and cannot do, and who says so while it is still working. The audience watching has no way to confirm the claim at the moment it is made, which is precisely why the claim carries weight when the confirmation follows.

**Nullification Promised, Not Performed by Moses**

The second clause assigns the action to God. *Sayfaʿaluhu llāh* — Allah will bring it to nothing, will nullify it. Moses does not claim to undo the craft himself, and he does not describe a contest of power between two practitioners. The nullification is divine, and the verb used elsewhere for the same event is explicit about the mechanism: “Cast what is in your right hand, and it will swallow up what they have made, for what they have made is no more than a magic trick. And magicians can never succeed wherever they go” (20:69). The verse therefore keeps the roles distinct: the magicians produce, Moses names, and God nullifies. Nothing in the sequence makes Moses a competitor.

---

## Verse 10:82

> And Allah establishes the truth by His Words—even to the dismay of the wicked.”

**Truth Established by Words**

The verse states the mechanism by which the truth prevails, and the mechanism is speech. *Yuḥiqqu llāhu l-ḥaqqa bi-kalimātihi* — Allah establishes the truth by His words. Not by superior force, not by a larger army, and not by the magicians being outperformed. The words in view are the promises made to Moses, and the tradition identifies them with the assurance given elsewhere: “We will certainly strengthen your arm through your brother, and grant you both authority, so they will not be able to harm you. ˹Go˺ with Our signs. You and your followers will prevail” (28:35). The verse therefore describes how a promise operates in history: what God has said becomes what happens, and the becoming is the establishment. The truth was already true before the contest; what the contest did was make it apparent.

**“Even Though the Criminals Dislike It”**

The final clause names a reaction and dismisses it as irrelevant. *Wa-law kariha l-mujrimūn* — though the criminals hate it. The dislike is real and it is stated without minimization. What the clause denies is any causal connection between the dislike and the outcome. The truth is established regardless of how it is received, and the verse treats the reception as a fact about the receivers rather than about the truth. That distinction is the chapter's recurring concern. A message does not become false because it is resented, and it does not become true because it is popular. The verse states the principle in six words, at the moment when the resentment is at its maximum.

---

## Verse 10:83

> But no one believed in Moses except a few youths of his people, while fearing that Pharaoh and their own chiefs might persecute them. And certainly Pharaoh was a tyrant in the land, and he was truly a transgressor

**The Outcome of the Contest Was Not Conversion**

The verse opens with a fact that the preceding narrative does not prepare the reader for. The magicians prostrated; the crowd saw what happened; and almost nobody believed. The verse states the result without softening it: none believed in Moses except a few. After the clearest demonstration recorded in the story, the number of believers is given as a remnant. The verse therefore refuses to let the miracle do the work that miracles are assumed to do. Evidence was produced, and evidence did not produce belief. That is the chapter's central claim about the relation between signs and faith, and this verse supplies its hardest instance.

The phrase translated as a few youths is *dhurriyya*, which denotes offspring or progeny, and its reference is disputed. The pronoun can attach to Moses or to Pharaoh. On the first reading, the believers were a small number of young Israelite men. On the second, they included people attached to Pharaoh's own court, among them his wife, whose belief is recorded elsewhere: “And Allah has set forth an example for those who believe: the wife of Pharaoh. She pleaded, ‘My Lord! Build me a house with You in Paradise, save me from Pharaoh and his evil deeds, and save me from the wrongdoing people’” (66:11). The second reading is less likely on the grammar but stronger on the evidence, since it accounts for believers inside the regime. Both readings agree on the number: few.

**“A Tyrant in the Land”**

The description of Pharaoh is given in two terms, and the first concerns height. *ʿĀlin fī l-arḍ* — exalted, elevated in the land. The elevation is not metaphorical. It describes a man who raised himself above the people he ruled, and the Qur'an records the point it reached: “But he denied and disobeyed. Then he turned his back, striving. Then he gathered ˹his people˺ and called out, saying, ‘I am your lord, the most high!’” (79:21–24). The verse here states the same fact in three words. The elevation is what made the fear rational, and the verse supplies it so that the believers' fear is understood as a response to a real situation rather than as weakness.

---

## Verse 10:84

> Moses said, “O my people! If you do believe in Allah and submit ˹to His Will˺, then put your trust in Him.”

**Two Conditions and One Command**

The verse is a single sentence with a precise logical shape. If you believe in God — and if you are submitting — then put your trust in Him. The two conditions are joined, and the command follows both. The structure is not redundant. Belief and submission are distinct: the first is a conviction about God, the second is a posture toward His will. A person can hold the conviction without the posture, and the verse requires both before issuing the command. The command itself is the third element, and it is the only thing the verse asks for. Nothing is demanded about the outcome, the timetable or the means of deliverance.

The verse treats trust as something that follows from belief rather than as a separate virtue. That ordering is significant. Trust — *tawakkul* — is the consignment of an affair to the One believed in, and it assumes the belief. A person who does not believe that God governs cannot entrust anything to Him. The verse therefore presents trust as the practical form of belief under pressure. It is what belief looks like when the situation is dangerous, and the situation here is as dangerous as the story gets: a small group inside a terror state, facing a ruler who has claimed divinity.

---

## Verse 10:85

> They replied, “In Allah we trust. Our Lord! Do not subject us to the persecution of the oppressive people

**The Reply Matches the Command**

The verse records an immediate response, and the response is the exact thing that was asked for. Moses said: put your trust in God. They said: in God we trust. Nothing is added, nothing is negotiated, and no condition is attached. The correspondence between the command and the reply is complete, and the verse gives it in the shortest possible exchange. That economy is meaningful. The previous verse raised a question about whether trust follows automatically from belief; this verse shows a group producing it on demand, in one sentence, in the middle of a danger that the verse itself goes on to describe.

**“Do Not Make Us a Trial”**

The word used is *fitna*, which covers trial, temptation, testing and the state of being made an occasion of error for others. The request is unusual, and its direction is worth noting. They are not asking mainly to be spared; they are asking not to be made into a temptation for the oppressors. The concern is that their suffering will be used as evidence against the message — that the oppressors will say the believers were punished because their cause was false. The same concern appears in the prayer recorded elsewhere: “Our Lord! Do not make us a target for the wrongdoing people” (60:5). The request therefore concerns the meaning of their fate rather than only its pain.

---

## Verse 10:86

> and deliver us by Your mercy from the disbelieving people.”

**The Prayer Completes Itself in One Clause**

The petition begun in the previous verse is finished here, and the completion is brief. Deliver us by Your mercy from the disbelieving people. Two requests were made across the two verses: not to be made a trial for the wrongdoers, and to be delivered from them. The second is the more obvious of the two, and it is stated in the plainest available terms. The verse therefore ends the prayer with the thing the believers actually want, after having first named the thing they most feared. The order is instructive — the concern about meaning precedes the concern about safety — and the completion returns to safety without retracting the first request.

The single qualifier in the petition is decisive. *Bi-raḥmatika* — by Your mercy. The believers do not ask to be delivered because they deserve it, because they are many, or because their case is strong. They ask on the ground of mercy, which is a ground that requires nothing from them. The choice is exact for a group that has just been described as a frightened remnant. They have no leverage and they claim none. The verse therefore records a petition with no basis except the character of the One addressed, and that is the only basis available to anyone in their position. Mercy is invoked precisely because merit cannot be.

---

## Verse 10:87

> We revealed to Moses and his brother, “Appoint houses for your people in Egypt. Turn these houses into places of worship, establish prayer, and give good news to the believers!”

**Houses Designated in Egypt**

The verse records an instruction with a specific practical content. Appoint houses for your people in Egypt — choose dwellings, settle them, fix a place. The command is not spiritual in the first instance; it is about location. A group under threat needs a place, and the verse begins by providing one. The instruction is addressed to a community that has no temple, no public sanctuary and no right of assembly, and it responds to that condition with a directive about buildings. The verse therefore treats the material arrangement of a religious community as a matter of revelation rather than of expediency.

The second clause converts the dwellings. *Wa-jʿalū buyūtakum qibla* — make your houses places of worship, or, on another reading, orient them toward prayer. The tradition offers two explanations, and they are not mutually exclusive. The first: Pharaoh had destroyed the Israelites' places of worship, so God instructed Moses and Aaron to select dwellings that would function as mosques, allowing the community to re-establish its devotional duties. The second: out of fear of persecution, the Israelites were permitted to establish prayer spaces inside their own homes. Both readings describe the same situation from different angles — public worship had become impossible, and private worship was made enough. The verse supplies the legal ground for praying at home when the mosque is not available.

---

## Verse 10:88

> Moses prayed, “Our Lord! You have granted Pharaoh and his chiefs luxuries and riches in this worldly life, ˹which they abused˺ to lead people astray from Your Way! Our Lord, destroy their riches and harden their hearts so that they will not believe until they see the painful punishment.”

**The Prayer Opens With an Observation**

Moses does not begin with a request. He begins by stating a fact: You have given Pharaoh and his chiefs adornment and wealth in the life of this world. The two nouns are distinct — *zīna*, ornament and display, and *amwāl*, wealth and property — and together they cover both the appearance and the substance of the regime's power. The observation is addressed to God as an acknowledgment rather than a complaint. Nothing in the wording questions the giving; the giving is stated as God's act. What the prayer goes on to dispute is the use to which the gift has been put, and the distinction between the two is what makes the request that follows intelligible.

**An Alternative Reading of the Purpose Clause**

The first request uses a verb of obliteration. *Iṭmis ʿalā amwālihim* — efface their wealth, wipe it out, blot it away. The word denotes the removal of a thing's form so that it no longer appears as what it was. The request is therefore not for redistribution or reduction but for erasure. Its object is the very thing identified in the observation: the adornment and property that gave the regime its persuasive power. The prayer is internally consistent — the instrument of misguidance is the thing asked to be removed — and it asks for removal rather than for the punishment of the people holding it.

---

## Verse 10:89

> Allah responded ˹to Moses and Aaron˺, “Your prayer is answered! So be steadfast and do not follow the way of those who do not know.”

**The Answer Is Immediate and Explicit**

The verse reports a response in the clearest terms available. *Qad ujībat daʿwatukumā* — your prayer has been answered. The particle *qad* with the past tense indicates a completed fact: the answer has already been given, not merely promised. Nothing is deferred, no condition is attached, and no part of the prayer is refused. The verse therefore settles the petition of the previous verse in a single clause, and it settles it before describing what follows. The reader is told that the request succeeded before being told what to do with the success.

The verb and the pronoun are both dual, and the dual is significant. Your prayer — the prayer of the two of you. The verse confirms what the previous verse's content implied: Aaron joined in the supplication. The tradition notes this explicitly, since the prayer is recorded in Moses' words but answered to both. The detail matters because it maintains the pairing that runs through the whole account. Two were sent, two were given the instruction about houses, two prayed, and two are now addressed. The mission is consistently a joint one, and the verse preserves that fact in its grammar rather than in a separate statement.

---

## Verse 10:90

> We brought the Children of Israel across the sea. Then Pharaoh and his soldiers pursued them unjustly and oppressively. But as Pharaoh was drowning, he cried out, “I believe that there is no god except that in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am ˹now˺ one of those who submit.”

**The Pursuit Described With Two Adverbs**

The second sentence characterizes the pursuit before reporting its end. Pharaoh and his soldiers pursued them *baghyan wa-ʿadwan* — in injustice and in aggression, or in envy and enmity. The two terms cover both the groundless nature of the act and the hostility behind it. The characterization is placed before the drowning so that the reader understands what is being ended. The pursuit is not a military operation with a rationale; it is described as excess and aggression, which is consistent with the description of Pharaoh two verses earlier as exalted in the land and among the transgressors. The verse therefore connects the pursuit to the character already established.

The timing is the verse's entire subject. The confession is not made when the sea opens, not when the pursuit begins, and not when the water closes. It is made when drowning has overtaken him. The verse states the moment precisely — *adrakahu l-gharaq*, the drowning reached him — and then gives the words. The placement is what gives the confession its meaning. A statement of belief made before the danger would have been a choice; made inside it, it is a report of what is now unavoidable. The verse therefore records the words in full, because the full wording is what makes the timing visible.

---

## Verse 10:91

> ˹He was told,˺ “Now ˹you believe˺? But you always disobeyed and were one of the corruptors

**“But You Always Disobeyed”**

The second clause supplies the record. *Wa-qad ʿaṣayta qablu* — you disobeyed before. The particle *qad* with the past tense marks a completed fact, and *qablu* fixes it in the past: before this moment, before the water, before the pursuit. The clause is therefore not an accusation of a new offence. It is a reference to a history that the confession does not address. The verse places the past and the present side by side and lets the contrast do the work. A life of disobedience is not undone by a sentence spoken when the alternative has been removed, and the verse states the life rather than arguing the point.

**What the Verse Says About Repentance**

The Qur'an states the rule explicitly elsewhere, and the rule is what this verse shows. “However, repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, ‘Now I repent!’ nor those who die as disbelievers” (4:18). The wording is almost identical to the word the verse uses, and the correspondence shows that the verse is not describing an exception but applying a general principle. Repentance has a window, and the window closes when the choice closes. The verse therefore does something the rule alone cannot do: it shows a man inside the closed window saying the right words, and it records what those words are worth.

---

## Verse 10:92

> Today We will preserve your corpse so that you may become an example for those who come after you. And surely most people are heedless of Our examples!”

**A Rescue That Is Not a Rescue**

The verse opens with the verb of salvation. *Fa-l-yawma nunajjīka bi-badanka* — today We will save you in your body. The word is the same one used of the deliverance of the Children of Israel, and its application to Pharaoh is the verse's central device. He is saved, and the saving is limited to his corpse. Nothing else of him is preserved — not his life, not his kingdom, not his claim. The verse therefore uses the language of rescue to describe the most complete defeat in the account, and the contrast between the word and the thing is what gives the sentence its force.

The tradition explains the mechanism. The bodies of Pharaoh's soldiers sank; his did not. His body was preserved on a raised surface so that it would be visible, and its visibility is the stated purpose. The preservation is therefore not a honour and not a mercy. It is the making of an exhibit. A body lost at sea is forgotten within a generation; a body displayed becomes a fact that can be pointed at. The verse specifies the medium of the sign with unusual precision, and the precision indicates that the physical fact matters — that the sign is intended to be seen rather than merely reported.

---

## Verse 10:93

> Indeed, We settled the Children of Israel in a blessed land, and granted them good, lawful provisions. They did not differ until knowledge came to them. Surely your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their differences

**A Settled Dwelling in a Blessed Land**

The verse begins with the fulfilment. The people who crossed the sea are settled, and the settlement is described with two qualifiers: it is a sure dwelling, and the land is blessed. The tradition identifies the location as a comely dwelling in Palestine or Syria. The word for settled conveys the placing of a people in a dwelling that suits them, and the blessing attached to the land indicates that the provision is built into the place rather than added to it. The verse therefore describes a completed act of favour: a people delivered from a terror state and given a land capable of sustaining them.

**Why Knowledge Produces Difference**

The mechanism is worth stating plainly. Before the Torah there was less to disagree about; after it there was a text, a law, and a body of determinations that could be interpreted in more than one way. Difference arises where there is something determinate to differ over. The verse therefore describes a real feature of religious life rather than a failure of the community. The same observation appears elsewhere: “Mankind was one community. Then Allah raised up the prophets as bearers of good news and as warners. And He revealed with them the Scripture in truth to judge between people about their differences. Yet none differed about it except those who were given it—after clear proofs had come to them—out of mutual jealousy” (2:213). The verse here makes the same point in fewer words.

---

## Verse 10:94

> If you ˹O Prophet˺ are in doubt about ˹these stories˺ that We have revealed to you, then ask those who read the Scripture before you. The truth has certainly come to you from your Lord, so do not be one of those who doubt

**A Conditional Addressed to the Prophet**

The verse opens with a conditional whose condition is not expected to be met. If you are in doubt about what We have sent down to you. The tradition is explicit that the Prophet was not in fact in doubt, and it offers two readings of the address: the intended audience is the disbelievers, or it is people generally. Both readings preserve the literal form while locating the instruction elsewhere. The verse therefore uses the second person to reach a wider audience, a device the Qur'an employs at several points. The effect is that the instruction is stated in its strongest form — addressed to the messenger himself — so that it cannot be treated as a concession for the weak.

The instruction names a specific group and a specific action. Ask those who recite the Book before you — the Jews and Christians, who recite the Torah and the Gospel. The verse therefore treats the earlier scriptures as a witness to the account just narrated. The stories of Moses, Pharaoh and the crossing are not new material; they exist in prior revelation, and the people who hold that revelation can confirm it. The same point is made elsewhere: “Those to whom We gave the Scripture know it as they know their own sons” (2:146). The verse therefore does not ask for belief on the basis of the Prophet's word alone. It points to an independent corpus that can be consulted.

---

## Verse 10:95

> and do not be one of those who deny Allah’s signs or you will be one of the losers

**“Or You Will Be One of the Losers”**

The consequence is stated in the chapter's recurring commercial vocabulary. *Fa-takūna mina l-khāsirīn* — you will be among the losers. The word denotes one who has lost in a transaction, whose capital has gone and whose account is in deficit. The chapter has used the same imagery throughout: deeds as earnings, lives as transactions, the settlement at the return. The verse therefore states the consequence in the terms the chapter has established, and it states it as a certainty rather than a risk. The particle *fa-* with the subjunctive makes the loss a direct result of the denial, not a possible outcome of it.

**The Verse Completes a Single Sentence**

The chapter has spent ninety-four verses persuading. It has argued from creation, provision, the senses, the alternation of night and day, the history of the messengers and the fate of those who rejected them. This verse stops arguing and forbids. The shift is significant. There comes a point in any argument where further evidence does not change the outcome, because the obstacle is no longer informational. The verse identifies that point and responds to it appropriately. The reader who has received the account, the witness and the source is not lacking information; he is being told what to do with what he has, and the telling is a command rather than an invitation.

---

## Verse 10:96

> Indeed, those against whom Allah’s decree ˹of torment˺ is justified will not believe—

**The Word That Has Come Due**

The verse names a decree and describes its status. *Ḥaqqat ʿalayhim kalimatu rabbika* — the word of your Lord has come due upon them, has been confirmed against them. The verb *ḥaqqa* denotes something that has become fixed, established and unavoidable; it is the same verb used where the chapter earlier described the word coming due upon those who wronged. The "word" here is God's stated determination, and the verse treats it as a fact rather than as a threat. It has already occurred in the sense that matters: the determination is settled, and what remains is its appearance in time.

The chapter has described the same phenomenon earlier, in different language. After the messengers who came with clear proofs, it states: thus do We seal the hearts of the transgressors. The sealing and the decree coming due are two descriptions of one condition, and both are given as divine acts following human conduct. The verse here states it more starkly, and the starkness is what requires the qualification in the next verse. Read together, the passages describe a process rather than a selection: refusal repeated until it becomes incapacity, and incapacity confirmed as a fact.

---

## Verse 10:97

> even if every sign were to come to them—until they see the painful punishment

**Every Sign, and Still No Belief**

The verse supplies the condition that the previous verse left open, and the condition is exhaustive. Even if every sign came to them. The wording admits no remainder — not one sign is excluded, not one kind of evidence is held back. The verse therefore forecloses the most common objection to the preceding statement: that the people in question were not given enough. They are described as receiving everything available, and the result is unchanged. The chapter has been building toward this claim since its opening verses, where the sufficiency of the signs in creation and revelation was established. This verse extends the sufficiency to its maximum and states the outcome anyway.

**Belief Born of Despair**

The tradition offers two readings of what happens at that moment. On the first, belief arises out of despair as the person faces impending doom — the case of Pharaoh, described in this very chapter. On the second, the reference is to belief at the time of punishment in the Hereafter, when everyone will "believe" in the sense of seeing what was described. Both readings agree on the essential point: the belief that arrives at that moment is not the belief the messengers asked for. It is a recognition compelled by the thing recognized, and it carries none of the weight that free belief carries. The verse therefore describes a real event and denies its value in the same statement.

---

## Verse 10:98

> If only there had been a society which believed ˹before seeing the torment˺ and, therefore, benefited from its belief, like the people of Jonah. When they believed, We lifted from them the torment of disgrace in this world and allowed them enjoyment for a while

**A Wish Expressed About History**

The verse opens with a particle of unfulfilled wish. *Fa-lawlā kānat qarya āmanat* — if only there had been a town that believed. The construction expresses something desired and absent, and its use here is striking. The verse is not stating a rule but lamenting an absence: across the whole recorded history of the warned communities, one exception exists. The framing therefore makes the exception visible. A reader who encounters the verse understands immediately that what follows is unusual, and the unusualness is the point of the verse rather than an incidental detail.

The people of Jonah are named, and they are the only community in the Qur'an described as believing before the punishment arrived and being spared for it. Their story is given elsewhere in brief: Jonah was sent to a hundred thousand or more, he left them in anger, they were warned, and they believed. The account in those Ranged in Ranks records the outcome: “So they believed, and We let them enjoy ˹life˺ for a while” (37:148). The verse here states the same sequence with the additional detail that the punishment was lifted from them in this world, which is the distinctive feature of the case. No other warned community is described as receiving that reversal.

---

## Verse 10:99

> Had your Lord so willed ˹O Prophet˺, all ˹people˺ on earth would have certainly believed, every single one of them! Would you then force people to become believers

**Free Choice as the Condition of Belief**

The tradition draws the theological consequence directly. Compulsion was available and was not used; what was willed instead is that people arrive at belief on their own. The Qur'an states the same principle in the form of an offer: “So let whoever wills take a way to his Lord” (73:19), and again in the sūrah of Man, where the identical wording is repeated. The verse therefore grounds the mission in the freedom of the one addressed. A messenger is sent to people who can refuse, and the possibility of refusal is not a defect in the arrangement but the condition that makes acceptance meaningful.

There is a second function in the verse, and it concerns the one addressed. The chapter has described a messenger whose people refuse him, whose mission produces a handful of believers, and whose warnings are answered with mockery. The verse tells him that the outcome was never in his control, and that its absence is not a failure on his part. God could have produced unanimity and did not; the Prophet cannot produce it and is not asked to. The verse therefore removes the burden of the result from the messenger completely and leaves him with the delivery, which is the only part of the task that was ever his to perform.

---

## Verse 10:100

> It is not for any soul to believe except by Allah’s leave, and He will bring His wrath upon those who are unmindful

**A Statement From the Tradition**

The early Sufi figure Dhū'l-Nūn al-Miṣrī, who died in the middle of the third Islamic century, put the same point in a compressed formula preserved in al-Qushayrī's treatise. Its sense is that his knowledge of his Lord reached him from his Lord, and that without that giving there would have been no knowing. The formulation keeps both halves of the verse intact: the act of knowing belongs to the servant, and its possibility is a gift. The statement is not a denial of effort. It denies only that effort by itself produces the result, which is the same denial the verse makes in the form of a condition.

The second clause names a consequence. *Wa-yajʿalu r-rijsa ʿalā lladhīna lā yaʿqilūn* — He places filth upon those who do not use their reason. The word *rijs* denotes impurity and defilement, and the commentators identify it here either with divine anger or with the penalty that follows it. Both identifications are recorded, and they are compatible: the impurity is the state, and what it brings is its expression. The verse therefore describes a defilement that is placed rather than merely incurred, which continues the theme of the previous clause. What is granted to one group is placed upon another, and in both cases the agent is the same.

## Verse 10:101

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Consider all that is in the heavens and the earth!” Yet neither signs nor warners are of any benefit to those who refuse to believe

**The Limit Stated in the Same Verse**

What follows is a negation, and it is unqualified. Neither signs nor warners benefit a people who do not believe. The verse does not say the benefit is small, delayed or uncertain. It says there is none. The statement is severe, and it is placed immediately after the command to look, which produces a striking contrast: look at everything, and know that looking will not help those who have already decided. The verse therefore states both the availability of the evidence and the futility of adding to it, in two clauses, without attempting to reconcile them. The reconciliation lies in the distinction between those who have not yet decided and those who have.

**The Verse Is Not Addressed to the Refusers**

The command is short, and the tradition's gloss on it is demanding. To look is not to glance. The Qur'an describes the failure it is guarding against: “How many signs in the heavens and the earth do they pass by with indifference!” (12:105). The image is of people walking past evidence daily without registering it. The same concern appears where reflection is commanded explicitly: “Have they ever reflected on the wonders of the heavens and the earth, and everything Allah has created, and that perhaps their end is near? So what message after this ˹Quran˺ would they believe in?” (7:185). The verse therefore asks for attention rather than information, and attention is the one thing the audience controls.

## Verse 10:102

> Are they waiting for ˹anything˺ except the same torments that befell those before them? Say, “Keep waiting then! I too am waiting with you.”

**The Question Leaves Only One Option**

The verse opens with a question that eliminates alternatives. Are they waiting for anything except the like of the days of those who passed away before them? The phrase "the days of those before them" is a Qur'anic idiom for the events that overtook earlier communities — the punishments that fell on Noah's people, on the messengers' opponents, on Pharaoh's court. The question asserts that nothing else remains on the list. Every other possibility has been exhausted by the preceding argument: the signs have been given, the warners have come, the demand for a date has been answered. What is left is the outcome those communities received, and the verse asks whether that is what is being waited for.

**“I Too Am Waiting With You”**

The formulation is not unique, and the parallel is close enough to be instructive. “Are they awaiting the coming of the angels, or your Lord ˹Himself˺, or some of your Lord's ˹major˺ signs? On the Day your Lord's signs arrive, belief will not benefit those who did not believe earlier or those who did no good through their faith. Say, ‘Keep waiting! We too are waiting’” (6:158). The two passages share the command and the shared waiting, and the longer version supplies what the shorter one leaves implicit: the arrival of the signs closes the period in which belief has value. The verse here therefore carries that meaning with it, and it does so without stating it, because the chapter has already stated it in the account of Pharaoh.

## Verse 10:103

> Then We saved Our messengers and those who believed. For it is Our duty to save the believers

**A Present Tense Describing the Past**

There is a grammatical feature in this verse that carries meaning. The verb "We save" is in the present form, and the events it describes are past — the deliverance of Noah, of the messengers after him, of Moses and those with him. The tradition notes the point explicitly: the present tense refers back to the days of those who passed away before, and it means "then We saved." The effect of the tense is to make the past action available as a pattern. A past event narrated in the past tense belongs to history; the same event stated in the present tense belongs to a rule. The verse therefore does not merely report what happened. It states what happens, and the difference between those two is the whole content of the verse.

**The Promise Applied to the Present Community**

The tradition reads the verse as addressing the immediate situation: God will deliver the Prophet and his followers from the persecution of the Makkans, as He delivered earlier messengers and their followers from their persecutors. The application is not an extension of the verse but its intended reference. The same assurance is given elsewhere in explicit terms: “We certainly help Our messengers and the believers, ˹both˺ in this worldly life and on the Day the witnesses will stand forth ˹for testimony˺” (40:51). The two passages cover the same ground, and together they place the deliverance in both worlds. The verse here states it in the shortest form, as an obligation already incurred.

## Verse 10:104

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O humanity! If you are in doubt of my faith, then ˹know that˺ I do not worship those ˹idols˺ you worship instead of Allah. But I worship Allah, Who has the power to cause your death. And I have been commanded, ‘Be one of the believers,’

**“Who Will Take Your Souls”**

The affirmation carries a qualifier, and the qualifier is the verse's sharpest feature. I worship God — the One who will take your souls. The clause is not a general description of divinity. It is a specific statement about the audience's own end, and it is placed inside the declaration of worship so that the two cannot be separated. The tradition explains the purpose: the God the Prophet worships is the One whom people meet after death and who will inform them of what they used to do, whereas the gods of the audience have no role beyond this life. The qualifier therefore converts a theological statement into a personal one. The audience is told which of the two objects of worship will be present at their death.

**“I Have Been Commanded to Be Among the Believers”**

The final clause is a report of a command, and its wording is modest. The Prophet does not say he is the first of the believers, the greatest, or their leader. He says he has been commanded to be among them. The same formulation appears elsewhere: “And I am commanded to be the first of those who submit ˹to His Will˺” (39:12), and in a context where the command is paired with a prohibition: “Say, ‘I have been commanded to be the first to submit and not be one of the polytheists’” (6:14). The verse here uses the more inclusive form, and the inclusiveness is significant. The messenger places himself inside the community of believers rather than above it, which is the same self-description Noah gave in the account narrated earlier in this chapter.

## Verse 10:105

> and, ‘Be steadfast in faith in all uprightness, and do not be one of the polytheists,’

**The Command Continues Inside a Quotation**

The verse opens with a conjunction and continues the report begun in the previous one. The Prophet has said that he has been commanded; this verse supplies the next item in the list of commands. The nested quotation is preserved, and the nesting matters. What the reader receives is not a narration about a command but the command itself, quoted in the form in which it was given. The effect is to place the reader inside the address rather than outside it. He is not being told what the Prophet was told; he is being told the thing. That is why the verse can be so short and still function as instruction rather than as report.

**“Set Your Face”**

The idiom in the command is physical. To set the face toward something is to orient the whole person toward it — the direction of the body determines the direction of the attention, and the Qur'an uses the image where total commitment is required. The phrase occurs in several places with the same force, and it always denotes an undivided orientation rather than a preference. A person whose face is set toward something is not simultaneously facing elsewhere, and the idiom therefore excludes partial adherence. The verse's command is not to believe in a general sense but to orient, which is a claim about the arrangement of a life rather than about the contents of a mind.

## Verse 10:106

> and ‘Do not invoke, instead of Allah, what can neither benefit nor harm you—for if you do, then you will certainly be one of the wrongdoers,’

**A Prohibition Against Invocation**

The verse forbids a specific act, and the act is invocation. *Lā tadʿu* — do not call upon. The verb covers supplication, appeal and the addressing of a request, and it is the primary act of worship in the Qur'an's vocabulary. The prohibition therefore goes to the centre of religious practice rather than to its edges. It is not a restriction on a ritual detail; it is a statement about whom a person addresses when he needs something. The verse places that question at the head of the creed it is building, and it answers it by exclusion before the next verse answers it by affirmation.

The objects of the prohibition are defined by a test, and the test is functional. Whatever can neither benefit you nor harm you is not to be invoked. The formulation appears repeatedly in the Qur'an, and it is always used as a decisive criterion. “Say, ˹O Prophet˺, ‘How can you worship besides Allah those who can neither harm nor benefit you? And Allah ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing’” (5:76). And in the longer form: “Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, ‘Should we invoke, other than Allah, those ˹idols˺ which cannot benefit or harm us, and turn back to disbelief after Allah has guided us?’” (6:71). The verse here uses the same test in the shortest available form, as part of a command rather than as a question.

## Verse 10:107

> and ‘If Allah touches you with harm, none can undo it except Him. And if He intends good for you, none can withhold His bounty. He grants it to whoever He wills of His servants. And He is the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.’”

**“He Grants It to Whomever He Wills”**

The third clause concerns the distribution of the good. He causes it to fall upon whomever He wills among His servants. The statement is about selection, and it is placed after the statement about power rather than before it. The order matters: the verse first establishes that no one can withhold the bounty, and then states that its arrival is determined by God's will. The same point is made elsewhere in a similar form, where provision is described as given to whom God wills without reckoning. The verse therefore does not present the distribution as arbitrary; it presents it as belonging to the same hand that controls the removal of harm, which is the only hand the preceding clauses have established.

The verse ends with forgiveness and mercy, and the placement of those two names is significant. Everything before them has been about power — harm none can remove, good none can withhold, bounty given to whom He wills. A verse ending there would leave the reader with a description of unqualified power. The two names convert the description. The One who holds both directions is described as forgiving and merciful, which tells the reader what the power is exercised by. The verse therefore does not soften its claim about sovereignty; it identifies the character of the sovereign, and it places that identification last so that it is what remains.

## Verse 10:108

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O humanity! The truth has surely come to you from your Lord. So whoever chooses to be guided, it is only for their own good. And whoever chooses to stray, it is only to their own loss. And I am not a keeper over you.”

**Guidance Benefits the One Guided**

The first consequence is stated with an emphasis that is easy to miss. Whoever is guided is guided only for his own soul. The restriction — only — is the point. The benefit does not accrue to the messenger, to the community as an institution, or to God. It belongs to the person guided. The same formulation appears elsewhere with the same restriction: “So whoever chooses to be guided, it is for their own good. And whoever chooses to stray, it is only to their own loss. You are not a keeper over them” (39:41). The verse therefore removes any suggestion that acceptance of the message is a favour conferred on its bearer. It is a gain retained completely by the one who gains it.

**“I Am Not a Keeper Over You”**

The final clause defines the messenger's role by exclusion. *Wa-mā anā ʿalaykum bi-wakīl* — I am not a guardian, trustee or keeper over you. The same statement appears elsewhere in the Qur'an, and each occurrence marks a limit on the mission. The Prophet is not a warder over his people; he is a bearer of good news and a warner. The verse therefore states what the messenger cannot do, and the statement is a release rather than a complaint. He is not responsible for the outcome of his audience's choice, and the verse says so in the first person, in his own voice, at the end of a chapter that has described his inability to guide from its opening verses.

## Verse 10:109

> And follow what is revealed to you, and be patient until Allah passes His judgment. For He is the Best of Judges

**Two Commands Close the Chapter**

The final verse contains two imperatives and one statement, and the arrangement is the chapter's conclusion in miniature. Follow what has been revealed to you. Be patient until God judges. He is the best of judges. The first command concerns action, the second concerns duration, and the statement supplies the ground for both. Nothing in the verse is addressed to the audience. The closing words of the chapter are spoken to the messenger alone, which is a significant way to end. A hundred and eight verses have been directed outward — to humanity, to the opponents, to the readers. The last one turns inward, and it leaves the messenger with a task rather than with an argument.

**“Follow What Is Revealed to You”**

The command is general, and its generality is the point. No specific injunction is named, no particular ruling is cited. The instruction is to follow the revelation as a whole, and the same command appears elsewhere in the same terms: “˹O Prophet!˺ Follow what is revealed to you from your Lord—there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and turn away from the polytheists” (6:106). And again: “Follow what is revealed to you from your Lord. Surely Allah is All-Aware of what you ˹all˺ do” (33:2). The verse here gives the command in its shortest form, without the accompanying prohibition, because the prohibitions have already been stated in the creed quoted in the preceding verses.

The second command specifies its own limit. Be patient — until God renders His judgment. The patience is therefore not open-ended in principle, even though its duration is unknown. The verse states that there is an endpoint and that it belongs to God, and both facts are what make the command bearable. The same instruction is given elsewhere with its difficulty acknowledged: “Be patient ˹O Prophet˺, for your patience is only with Allah's help. Do not grieve over those ˹who disbelieve˺, nor be distressed by their schemes” (16:127). The verse here supplies the reason patience is possible: the judgment will come, and it will not be rendered by the parties involved.

**“He Is the Best of Judges”**

The tradition reads the command to be patient as containing a promise: God would help the Prophet overcome his enemies and grant him victory. The reading is supported by the structure. A command to wait for a judgment, followed by a statement that the judge is the best of judges, implies an outcome rather than merely an ending. The same assurance is given elsewhere in explicit form: “Indeed, messengers before you were rejected but patiently endured rejection and persecution until Our help came to them. And Allah's promise ˹to help˺ is never broken” (6:34). The verse therefore does more than instruct; it indicates what the waiting is waiting for.
