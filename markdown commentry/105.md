# Sūrah al-Fīl (Chapter 105) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary
## Sūrah al-Fīl 105:1

> Have you not seen ˹O Prophet˺ how your Lord dealt with the Army of the Elephant

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Already Knows Its Answer**

The sūrah opens not with a statement but with a question, and the kind of question matters. "Have you not seen" is a form the Book uses when the answer is assumed to be already in the hearer's possession; the point is not to inform but to make the hearer act on what he knows. The addressee is the Prophet, but the question passes straight through him to the Makkans, who had grown up within earshot of this story. Nobody in the audience needed the facts. The facts were the common property of the city. What was lacking was the conclusion, and the question is aimed at that gap. The same opening question appears in the sūrah that turns on the one who denies the Judgment, so the device works as a standing instrument of the Book: pose the known, and demand the unrecognized from the one who knows it.

**An Army Named for Its Single Elephant**

The translation renders the Arabic ahl al-fīl as "the Army of the Elephant," and the singular is deliberate. The sūrah names an entire expedition after one of its animals, the way a desert people name things after what strikes the eye. A force that filled the roads of the Hijaz is referred to in the Book as "the people of the elephant," as if the whole host were subordinate to the beast that led it. Early accounts give the number of elephants as several, and the later ones say eight or twelve, but the Qur'anic title keeps the single. The reduction is a piece of the argument: the story is told through its one visible sign, and the sign will prove enough. Whatever the size of the army, its identity in the memory of the Arabs was fixed by one image, and the sūrah takes that image and makes it the name of the event.

**A Governor With a Programme for the Pilgrimage**

The man at the head of the expedition was Abrahah, an Abyssinian who held Yemen as governor and was known in the accounts by the scar on his face, al-Ashram, "the one with the split lip." His project was not conquest for its own sake. According to the tradition received in the Islamic sources, he had raised a great church at Sanʿāʾ with the intention of drawing the pilgrims of Arabia away from the Kaʿbah, and when the caravans of worshippers kept coming to Makkah instead, he marched on the city with the largest army Arabia had then seen. The expedition is traditionally dated to the year 570 CE, the year the Prophet was born, which is why the epoch acquired the name "the Year of the Elephant." The sūrah does not tell any of this. Its audience already had it, and the verse works like a cross-reference that does not need a footnote.

**The Manner Is the Whole Story**

The Arabic question is a question of manner: how did your Lord deal with them? The answer, which fills the rest of the sūrah, is a chain of participles: He sent birds, the birds pelted, and the army was left like straw. The structure of the five verses is therefore a single sentence in two movements: two questions, and then one unbroken statement of what happened. The phrase "your Lord" does the work of the whole first verse. It names the actor before the action is described, so that the manner of the dealing cannot be read as military failure, bad weather, or disease on the march. The agency is established in the question itself, and the remaining verses supply only the details. This is the sūrah's central claim in miniature: the event is not a battle lost but a dealing done, and the dealer is the Lord of the addressee.

**The Lesson the City Owed the Story**

The Makkans had been the survivors of the event. The army meant to level the sanctuary went home in ruins, and the city and its people stood unharmed. The question of the verse asks them to read that outcome correctly. If a host with the mightiest piece of war equipment Arabia had then seen could be reduced to straw at the gates of the sanctuary, the protection of the sanctuary could not be a matter of walls, of numbers, or of the goodwill of any tribe. It had a source, and the source is named in the opening question: the Lord. The Book states the same principle in a plainer shape elsewhere, in the account of the safe haven that stands while the people around it are snatched away (29:67). The first verse of al-Fīl is that principle posed as a question, waiting for the city to answer with its faith.

**What the Question Withholds**

The first verse withholds the manner, and the withholding is its method. A question that states its answer in the same breath is a statement dressed as a question; this one keeps the answer for the four verses that follow. The tension between the question and the withheld answer is the space in which the sūrah works: the audience holds the image of the army, and the sūrah holds the image of the dealing, and the meeting of the two is the lesson. The verse is therefore not the introduction to the story; it is the frame of the story, and the frame is shaped so that nothing inside it can be read apart from the Lord who is named in it. The whole of al-Fīl stands on this first line.

## Sūrah al-Fīl 105:2

> Did He not frustrate their scheme

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Question Names the First Answer**

The first verse asked how the Lord dealt with the army. This verse narrows the question to one particular form of dealing: He made their scheme go to waste. The Arabic verb behind "frustrate" carries the sense of rendering void, of turning a plan into its opposite, and the nuance is the point. The scheme did not merely fail; it was inverted. Every step that had been taken to destroy the sanctuary was taken, in the accounting of the verse, toward the sanctuary's preservation. The repetition of the question form matches the first verse, and the pair together forms one complete argument: consider the manner, and recognize whose hand performed it. The two questions are not two stories; they are the same story asked twice, the second time with the name of the answer already standing inside it.

**A Plot Allowed to Ripen Into Its Own Trap**

The commentaries are agreed on the shape of the reversal, and they differ on nothing essential: God did not interrupt the expedition. He allowed it to be formed, financed, marched, and deployed, and only then did the dealing take place. That allowance is the hard part of the verse, because it means the schemers were not cut off in their planning; they were given the full space of their plan and watched it come to nothing. The Book states the principle in two other places with an identical phrase. When the disbelievers conspired against the Prophet, "They planned, but Allah also planned. And Allah is the best of planners" (8:30), and in the same voice: "And the disbelievers made a plan ˹against Jesus˺, but Allah also planned —and Allah is the best of planners" (3:54). The plotting of the disbelievers "was only in vain" (40:25). The verse of al-Fīl is the instance the later verses treat as the rule.

**The Beast That Would Not March on the City**

The earliest histories preserve a detail that gives the frustration a face. When the army reached the edge of the sanctuary and the lead elephant was driven toward Makkah, the animal went down on its knees and refused to advance. Beating it did not help. Turned to any other road it would rise and walk; turned back to the city it knelt again, and no whips could move it. The story is told in the same terms by the early biographers, and it has the quality of a report that came out of the city itself. Whether one reads it as a literal account of the beast's refusal or as the tradition's way of saying that the expedition's terror stopped at the boundary, the function of the story is clear: the instrument of the scheme was inert at the exact moment the scheme needed it, and the refusal happened before any bird crossed the sky.

**Two Ways to Read a Failure**

Any army that goes home in ruins can be read two ways. The first reading is secular: the campaign failed because of logistics, of terrain, of disease on the march, and the lesson for the Quraysh is that no foreign power can be trusted to respect the sanctuary. The second reading is the one the verse forces: the failure was an act, with an actor, and the actor is the same "He" of the opening. The verse leaves no room for a third reading in which the event is luck or accident, because its grammar is the grammar of an intentional deed performed by a named Lord. For the Makkans the stakes of the choice were practical. If the first reading were true, their safety depended on the moods of empires. If the second were true, it depended on a Guardian who had just demonstrated, in a single night's dealing, that he could undo the greatest force on the Peninsula. The sūrah leaves the choice open in the form of a question, but the question is loaded.

**What "Their Scheme" Really Was**

The phrase "their scheme" does not describe a military plan; it describes a project with a destination. The aim was not the spoils of Makkah or the submission of its people. It was the redirection of the pilgrimage, the replacement of one centre of devotion with another, the transfer of the religious gravity of Arabia from the House to a building raised in the north. The frustration of the scheme is therefore larger than the destruction of an army. It is the rejection, in a single event, of a plan to move the faith of a people by force. The sanctuary had been a point of attraction before the expedition, and it remained one after; the arrow of the pilgrimage bent around the assault and kept its course. The short question of the verse carries the whole of that outcome in the word "scheme," because the scheme was a plan for the hearts of the pilgrims, and that is the thing that was frustrated.

**A Question That Prepares the Answer**

The second question is also a hinge. Everything said so far has been about what happened to the army; the following verses turn to what the answer was made of — birds, stones, clay. The pair of questions therefore divides the sūrah in two: the first half establishes the fact and the agency, the second half describes the instrument. A listener who kept the two questions in mind could not miss the shape of what follows, which is a statement that answers both at once: the manner of the dealing was a swarm, and the frustration of the scheme was complete. The questions do the preparatory work, and the statement that comes after them lands as the fulfillment of a demand the audience had already made on itself.

## Sūrah al-Fīl 105:3

> For He sent against them flocks of birds

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shift From Question to Statement**

The third verse changes the register of the sūrah. The first two verses were questions, and the audience was asked to recall. This verse simply states: He sent. The conjunction that opens it marks the transition, and the verb is a bare past tense with no hedge, no report, no "it is said." The dealing is no longer a matter of memory to be retrieved; it is an act declared. "Against them" fixes the direction: the send was aimed at the army, not at the city, not at the sanctuary, not at the pilgrims who had gathered around the House. The precision of the direction is the first feature of the judgment, and the remaining verses will show that it was carried through to the last detail. The audience hears the change in key and understands that the explanation of the manner, which the first verse demanded, has at last begun.

**A Word the Book Uses Only Once**

The translation says "flocks of birds," and the Arabic word for it, abābīl, is one of the rarest in the entire vocabulary of the Book. It is used in this single verse and nowhere else, and its morphological form carries the sense of successive groups, of things that come in rows, one wave after another. The exegetes recorded the range of readings: groups, a multitude, a succession of bands, or a swarm that arrives from every direction at once. Whatever the exact shade, the word refuses the picture of a single flight. It is not a flock in the ordinary sense but a layered, renewing presence in the sky — a first band, and another, and another, so that the attack has no visible end. The Book, which chooses its words with an economy the whole sūrah displays, spent a unique word on the instrument, and the uniqueness is itself a statement about the instrument.

**Where the Birds Came From**

The accounts that surround the verse fill in what the verse leaves open. The majority of the early exegetes held that the birds were sent from the sea, so that the army found the sky supplied from the one direction from which it had expected nothing. The species named in the reports range from the small to the large, swallows and herons among them, and the detail that matters is not the species but the number: a sky full, in rows, in the phrase of the word itself. The army that had crossed the desert in columns met an opposition that had no column and no camp and no supply train. It could not be negotiated with, because it had no leader; it could not be routed, because it was not a formation; it could not be feared in the ordinary way, because it was made of the smallest living things the soldiers knew. The send was measured not against the army's strength but against its ability to answer.

**The Instrument Chosen for Its Unlikeliness**

The disproportion between the army and the birds is the second great contrast of the sūrah, after the elephant and the straw that will close it. A host with the war equipment of an empire is answered with the commonest creature of the countryside. The point is not that birds are strong; the point is that the power which sent them is not measured on the scale on which armies are measured. The birds needed no discipline, no food, no quarter; they could not desert, and they could not be bribed. They are the purest form of an instrument, because they have no will of their own that could fail the purpose of the send. When the dealing of a Lord is performed through a creature that has no stake in the outcome, the lesson the event teaches is undiluted: the victory belongs entirely to the one who sent, and nothing in the instrument can claim a share of it.

**Against Them, and Not Against the City**

The direction "against them" deserves a second look, because the sūrah is careful that the blow falls on the army and on no one else. The city was spared, the sanctuary was untouched, the people who had gathered around the House went home unharmed. A judgment aimed at a city would have used fire, or siege, or the ruin of its wells. A judgment aimed at an army used the sky. The distinction is not incidental to the story; it is the story. The message of the event, repeated in the sūrah that follows, is that the protection of the sanctuary and the destruction of its enemies are two acts of the same hand, and the hand can tell them apart. The birds were sent with that discrimination in them, and the verses that follow will show how completely the discrimination was carried out.

**The Stage Set for the Stones**

The verse ends mid-action. The birds have been sent, and the sentence does not yet say what they carried. The next verse supplies the payload, and the gap between the two verses is a deliberate pause, the space in which the audience holds the image of the filled sky before it is given its contents. The sūrah thus builds its mechanism in two stages: first the agents, then their weapons. The effect of the division is to make the second stage land harder, because the first stage has already established that the sending was real, directed, and aimed. By the time the stones are named, they are no longer a detail of the story but its evidence, and the evidence is weighed against a claim that no army could have answered.

## Sūrah al-Fīl 105:4

> that pelted them with stones of baked clay

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Cheapest Weapon in the World**

The verb of the verse is a verb of pelting, of striking with thrown stones, and the object is a stone. Against the most expensive force Arabia had then assembled — trained riders, war equipment, an army that had crossed a peninsula — the Book records that the answer was a pebble. The disproportion returns in its sharpest form: the mightiest army in the recent memory of the Arabs was undone by the most common object on the ground. A stone cannot be forged, cannot be stored, cannot be taxed; it is the property of the earth and of no one. The judgment is performed with the material that belongs to no power, which is the last reason the victory cannot be claimed by any party in the story. The weapon was so cheap that no ledger of the age could even record it as a cost, and the undoing of the expedition is entered in the sūrah at zero price.

**Sijjīl: The Name of the Stone**

The word for the stones, sijjīl, means a hard, baked clay, a fired or hardened earth, and the translation renders it "stones of baked clay." Some of the linguists who commented on the verse connected the word to the two Persian elements for stone and for clay, sang and jīl, so that the name itself carries its description, a stone that is really a piece of hard clay. If that etymology is accepted, the word is a borrowed term that the Book naturalized, a foreign material absorbed into the vocabulary of the event. The size is given by the surrounding accounts: each stone was about the size of a lentil or a chickpea, small enough to be carried by a small bird and heavy enough to do the work. The detail of the size matters, because it fixes the picture: not a meteor and not a hail of boulders, but a rain of small hard pellets, each one a complete instrument.

**The Weight of a Small Stone**

The reports that the verse occasioned are unanimous in one fact: every soldier on whom one of the stones fell was destroyed. The deaths were not uniform in their manner. Some accounts say the blow was immediate, and others that the struck men lingered for days, crawling along the road back to Yemen, dying slowly as the wounds worked. The variety in the reports does not touch the uniformity of the outcome: the stone, once it hit, did its work. What is striking is the ratio. A pellet of baked clay, the weight of a few grams, carried enough of the dealing to end a man. The sūrah does not say the army was out-fought. It says the army was pelted, and the verb is the quiet one, the verb of children's games, used here for the undoing of an empire's expedition.

**The Substance from Which Men Are Made**

There is a layer under the story that the sūrah does not spell out but the Book spells out elsewhere. Man is created from clay: "We created man from clay" is the account in the sūrah of Nūḥ (15:26), and the story of the clay that becomes a living creature is one of the standing images of the Book. If that image is held against this verse, the pelting takes on a second reading. The judgment against the army is performed with the substance of the body, the same matter that composes the men being struck. The earth, which was formed into the creature, is sent back against the creature who would storm the sanctuary. The sūrah does not draw the line; it only names the material. The line is drawn by the reader who knows the Book's other stories of clay, and the effect is to make the judgment total: it uses against the invaders nothing that is foreign to their own composition.

**Precision That Spared the City**

The stones fell on the army, and on the army alone, so far as the event is reported. The sanctuary was not pelted; the city was not pelted; the pilgrims who had come to the House did not report a single stone on the ground around them. A pelting of that kind, at that density, with that effect on everything it touched, and with nothing touched but the column of the army, is not a weather event. It is a targeting. The discrimination is the second feature of the judgment, after the direction stated in the previous verse, "against them." The hand that sent the stones could have sent them anywhere, and the place where they fell is the argument. The same hand that had protected the sanctuary through the night of the assault is the hand that performed the pelting, and the two acts are one act seen from two sides.

**The Army After the Pelting**

The fourth verse is the point of no return in the sūrah. Before it, the army existed as a threat; after it, it exists as a body to be disposed of. The final verse will describe that disposition, and the present verse is the hinge on which the story turns from military event to image of ruin. The word "pelted" is a past tense, completed, with no condition attached. The dealing is over. What remains for the sūrah to say is only the shape of what is left, and the shape, as the last verse will show, is the least honored thing in the vocabulary of the desert, the residue of a meal the animals have finished. The verse completes the mechanism of the sūrah and withholds its conclusion. The conclusion, the shape of what is left, belongs to the last verse, and the withholding is the sūrah's final rhetorical move: the audience is left standing in the aftermath, with the birds gone and the stones spent, holding only the image of the army after the dealing. That image will be the last thing the sūrah shows them, and it will be the thing they carry home.

## Sūrah al-Fīl 105:5

> leaving them like chewed up straw

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Image of the Sūrah**

The sūrah closes with a simile, and the simile is its verdict. The army is left "like chewed up straw," and the Arabic, ʿaṣfan maʾkūlan, means the residue of something the animals have eaten, the remains of straw or chaff after the creature's teeth have passed over it. It is not straw that lies whole in the field; it is straw that has already been consumed and rejected, the dregs of a meal. The translation's "chewed up" carries the same mark: the value has already been taken out of the material, and what is left is only the shape of it. The mightiest army of the recent history of Arabia is compared, in the last word of the sūrah, to the most worthless remnant an Arab eye ever rested on. The comparison is not an exaggeration. It is the figure the event had earned, and the Book gives it without an adjective.

**From the Greatest Sign to the Least Remnant**

The structure of the five verses is a single descent. The first verse names the army by its greatest sign, the elephant, the largest animal in the horizon of the audience. The last verse names it by its least remnant, the chewed straw, the smallest object of no value in the same horizon. Between the two, the sūrah moves through the dealing: the frustration of the scheme, the sending of the birds, the pelting with the clay. The whole arc is a reduction, and the reduction is the argument. Power that can be reduced to straw in a single dealing is not power in the sense the army claimed for itself. The sūrah does not say the army was destroyed; it says what it was like after, and the likeness does the work of the eulogy it withholds. The Book's other destructions are matched to their peoples in the same way. The army of Pharaoh is taken in the water, and the people of the wind are taken in the wind, and each host meets the element that answers its claim. The army of the elephant, which claimed the mightiest single instrument of war, meets the most numerous and the lowest, the swarm that no commander can muster and no treasury can buy. The matching is the justice: the measure of the destruction is the measure of the claim.

**A Name Preserved, a Claim Erased**

The event did not erase the army from the memory of the Arabs; it erased the army from the rank of threats. The sūrah itself is proof, because the sūrah is named for the event, and the name has survived for a thousand years in the recitation of the Book. What was destroyed was not the story but the claim: the claim that this force could stand against the sanctuary, that the pilgrimage could be moved by the point of a spear, that the religion of a people was a matter of military supply. The straw that is left is the story with the claim removed from it. The Makkans told the story to their children, and in the telling, the army was already straw, a thing that had happened, not a thing that could happen again.

**The Year That Took Its Name From the Dealing**

The event became a coordinate of time. The year in which it happened, traditionally the year 570 CE, is the year the Arabs named after the elephant, and it is the year in which the Prophet was born. A chronology in which a generation's landmark is a divine reversal is a chronology that has taken the lesson into its bones: the city's time is measured not by its caravans or its alliances but by the dealing that protected it. The sūrah's first question — how did your Lord deal with them? — is answered, in the long view, by the calendar itself. The answer is so permanent that the year, not the army, is what survives, and the survival is the point the sūrah makes in its last simile.

**The Governor Did Not Outlive the Campaign**

The accounts that follow the sūrah record what became of the man who led the expedition. Abrahah did not return to a life of power. The narratives say he survived the march home for a short time only, and that he died in Yemen, the province he had governed, the project he had come to ruin left unfinished and unrepaired. The fate of the governor completes the pattern of the sūrah: the instrument, the host, and the hand are all disposed of, and the sanctuary stands. Nothing in the expedition's apparatus outlived the dealing. The sūrah does not mention the death, because its last word, the straw, already contains it. The return itself became a story told along the road. The army that had marched in columns came back as a line of the dying, and every camp between Makkah and Yemen heard what had happened before the survivors reached it. The news traveled faster than the men, and by the time the governor died, the event had already become the thing the Arabs told one another about the sanctuary.

**The Last Word and the Next Sūrah**

The sūrah ends on the straw, and the sūrah that follows turns at once to the Quraysh, the people who were spared. The connection is not an accident of arrangement. The lesson of the dealing is addressed to the survivors, and the next sūrah opens with the list of the favors that the dealing secured. The two sūrahs form a single movement: the first shows the hand, the second names the ones the hand protected and asks them for the worship that belongs to the hand. The straw of this verse is the ground on which that address stands, and the address will follow in the verses that come next.
