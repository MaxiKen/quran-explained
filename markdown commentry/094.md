# Sūrah ash-Sharḥ (Chapter 94) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:1

> Have We not uplifted your heart for you ˹O Prophet˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Question, Asked Twice Over**

This chapter opens with the same grammatical form that closed the argument of the chapter before it. The negative rhetorical question — have We not? — states a fact and requires acknowledgment, and it was used three times in succession for the shelter given to an orphan, the guidance given to one without a path, and the sufficiency given to a man with dependents. The fourth instance begins a new chapter and turns inward. What was described in the previous sūrah were conditions of a life: no father, no scripture, no means. What is described here is a state of the person himself. The heart is named first, before any burden or any honour, and the naming is the chapter's central claim. Everything God did for this man was done to a heart that had first been opened. Asking the same question twice over is a way of insisting rather than of repeating. A reader who encounters the doubled form understands that the answer is meant to be beyond doubt, and the doubling is what supplies the insistence without adding new content.

**Sharḥ al-Ṣadr: The Opening of the Breast**

The word *sharḥ* denotes an opening, an expansion, a widening of something that was closed. Applied to the breast, it describes the removal of constriction: the capacity to take in what previously could not be held. The Qur'an uses the same image for the fundamental religious state. Guidance is described as an opening and misguidance as a closing: "Whoever Allah wills to guide, He opens their heart to Islam. But whoever He wills to leave astray, He makes their chest tight and constricted as if they were climbing up into the sky" (6:125). The comparison is physical and unflattering — the constricted chest is likened to a man struggling upward through thin air — and it fixes the meaning of the term. An expanded breast is the condition of receiving; a constricted one is the condition of refusal. This verse claims the first state as a gift already given.

**Mūsā Asked for the Same Thing**

The request appears in the Qur'an in the mouth of another messenger, at the moment of his commissioning. Mūsā's first words on being sent are a prayer for capacity: "My Lord! Uplift my heart for me, and make my task easy, and remove the impediment from my tongue, so people may understand my speech" (20:25–28). Three requests, and the first is for the heart. The order is instructive. Before asking for an easier task or a clearer tongue, the messenger asks to be able to carry what he has been given. The verse in this chapter announces that the request has been answered for another messenger without his having to ask it. The gift precedes the mission here rather than following the call, which is why the chapter can list it as an accomplished fact rather than as a petition. Mūsā asked for the same thing, and the parallel is what gives the request its dignity. A reader who learns that an earlier prophet sought an opened chest sees that the request is not a sign of weakness but part of the prophetic office. The comparison is honourable rather than diminishing.

**The Burden of Insult and the Chest That Bears It**

One strand of the exegetical tradition connects the opening of the breast with the endurance required to bear mockery. The Qur'an describes that pressure on the Prophet in direct terms: "We certainly know that your heart is truly distressed by what they say" (15:97). The distress is acknowledged as fact, and the answer to it in this chapter is not a promise that the talk will stop but a statement that the heart has been widened. The distinction is the practical core of the verse. Nothing in the Prophet's circumstances had improved when the taunts were made; what had changed was his capacity. A widened chest does not make the insult smaller. It makes the room larger, and the difference between the two is the difference between a man crushed by opposition and a man who can keep working inside it. The burden of insult is named alongside the chest because the two belong together. A reader who has been spoken about knows that the weight is carried in the body, and the verse describes the relief in the same register. The physical language is not metaphor but accurate description.

**A Gift Described in Physical Language**

The tradition preserves accounts of an actual opening. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records from Anas ibn Mālik that the angels came to the Prophet, took him to Zamzam, opened his chest, washed his heart with the water of Zamzam, and restored it (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 162). A fuller version of the same report describes the removal of a clot identified as the portion of Satan, and the filling of the heart with faith and wisdom. The commentators who cite these reports read the verse as an allusion to that event; others take it as a description of the heart being made a vessel for revelation. The two readings are not rivals so much as descriptions of the same fact at different levels. What both preserve is the insistence that the expansion was done to the Prophet rather than achieved by him, and that insistence is what the verse's grammar already carries. Describing a gift in physical language makes it something a person can recognise in himself. A reader who has felt his capacity widen after a period of pressure has experienced what the verse describes, and the recognition is what makes the promise credible rather than merely hopeful.

**Why the Heart Comes Before the Honour**

The chapter's list runs from the inward to the outward: the heart opened, the burden lifted, the renown raised. The order is a judgement. A man with an elevated name and a constricted chest is in a worse position than the reverse, because the honour becomes a weight he cannot carry and a target he cannot ignore. The revelation's ordering says that the capacity precedes the position. It also answers an objection that was being raised in Mecca at the time, which was that this messenger had nothing to recommend him in worldly terms. The chapter concedes the worldly terms and then lists what was given instead, beginning with the one gift that cannot be seen and cannot be taken away. A reader who measures a life by its visible standing will miss the first item entirely, and the first item is the one that made the rest possible.

**The Opening and the Interval That Preceded It**

The occasion of this chapter and the one before it is the same, and the connection explains the gift named here. Revelation had paused; the pause had been read by an opponent as abandonment; the previous sūrah answered the reading. This chapter continues the answer by describing what the interval produced. Some commentators connect the opening of the breast with the relief that came after the solitude of the mountain cave where the revelations began, and others with the ending of the interruption itself. Both readings locate the gift at the point where the Prophet's capacity was most tested. A man waiting for a revelation that does not come is in a state that requires a particular kind of strength: the strength to keep the obligation without the reassurance. The verse claims that the strength was supplied, and it claims it as an act performed on him rather than a quality he possessed. The interval did not test whether he had an expanded heart; it happened after the expansion, and the expansion is what carried him through it.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:2

> relieved you of the burden

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Weight Named Without Being Described**

The verse says that a burden was lifted and does not say what the burden was. The Arabic is *wiṣr*, a load heavy enough to require effort to carry, and the word appears elsewhere in the Qur'an for the loads people will bear on the Day of Judgment. Its use here is deliberately unspecific, and the exegetes have filled the space with several candidates: the weight of sin, the weight of the prophetic office, the weight of a society's confusion and idolatry, the weight of the insults being endured. Each reading has support, and the verse does not decide between them. The unspecificity is a feature. A burden that is named can be argued about; a burden that is acknowledged can only be confirmed by the one who carried it. The verse asks the Prophet to confirm, and the confirmation is the point of the question form. Naming a weight without describing it leaves the identification to the person carrying it. A reader knows what his own burden is, and the verse does not need to specify it in order to reach him. The generality is what allows the statement to apply to anyone under strain.

**The Forgiveness Reading and Its Textual Ground**

The reading most widely held in the tradition is that the burden lifted was the burden of sin, and the ground for it is a verse revealed later at al-Ḥudaybiyah: "so that Allah may forgive you for your past and future shortcomings, perfect His favour upon you, guide you along the Straight Path" (48:2). The wording there is close enough that the exegetes connected the two passages, and the connection is sound in method: the Qur'an explains itself. On this reading the relief announced in this chapter is the removal of a liability, and the removal is total rather than partial, since the later verse covers both what came before and what was to come. The reading also fits the chapter's structure. A burden of that kind is invisible to other people and crushing to the one who carries it, which is why it is listed second, after the opening of the heart and before the raising of the name.

**The Office Reading and Why It Also Fits**

A second reading takes the burden as the prophetic task itself. On this view the relief is not from sin but from the impossibility of the assignment: a man with no scripture, no army and no standing sent to a city that intended to kill him. The chapter supports this reading by what it says next, since the following verses move directly to the promise that ease accompanies hardship. A task made bearable is a burden lifted, and the Qur'an describes the same lightening elsewhere in legislative terms: "Allah intends ease for you, not hardship" (2:185). The two readings are not mutually exclusive. A messenger can be relieved of a liability and have his office lightened in the same act, and the verse's refusal to specify allows both to stand. What it excludes is a reading in which nothing real was removed, since the verb is a verb of taking away.

**What Lifting a Burden Is Not**

The verse does not say that the work ended, or that the opposition stopped, or that the task became easy in the ordinary sense. It says a load was taken off. The distinction matters for anyone who reads the verse as a promise that religious commitment removes difficulty from a life. The Prophet's difficulties continued after this chapter was revealed; they intensified. What changed was the weight he carried while facing them. The Qur'an's consistent teaching is that the demand remains and the capacity is supplied, which is a different arrangement from the removal of the demand. A reader who expects the second arrangement will be disappointed by his own experience; a reader who understands the first will recognise in this verse a description of what he has already been given. Lifting a burden is not the same as removing the work. A reader who expects to be relieved of his obligations has misread the verse; what is removed is the weight that made them unbearable. The distinction is what keeps the promise honest.

**The Burden of a Society's Confusion**

A third strand of interpretation reads the burden as the state of Arabia itself — the ignorance, the tribal warfare, the practices the revelation set out to abolish. On this reading the lifting is not complete in the Prophet's lifetime but is begun through him, and the burden is transferred from a people who carried it unknowingly to a revelation that addressed it directly. The reading has the advantage of connecting the verse to the rest of the chapter's purpose, which is to describe what God did through this messenger rather than only what He did for him. Its weakness is grammatical: the burden in the verse is attached to the Prophet in the second person, not to a community. The stronger reading keeps the burden personal, and it is the one the following verse confirms by describing the weight as one that pressed on his back. The burden of a society's confusion is heavier than any personal difficulty, and the verse is addressed to a man carrying it. A reader who has tried to explain a truth to people who do not want it understands the specific exhaustion described here, and the understanding is the beginning of the consolation.

**Relief Stated as a Completed Act**

The grammar of the verse is perfective. The burden has been lifted, not will be. That is a striking claim to make to a man whose circumstances had not visibly improved, and it is the same rhetorical method the previous chapter used with its three questions. The completed act is asserted in a present that does not yet show it, and the assertion asks for trust in the unseen arrangement rather than for evidence in the visible one. The chapter that follows this statement will explain how the arrangement works — that ease travels with hardship rather than after it — and the explanation is what makes the perfective tense intelligible. A relief that has already been accomplished is not contradicted by continuing difficulty, because the relief was never about the difficulty. It was about the load. Stating the relief as a completed act is what distinguishes this verse from a hope. A reader is not told that his burden may be lifted; he is told that it was. The tense is doing the work, and the work is to place the relief in the past where it cannot be lost.

**Why the Burden Is Named After the Opening**

The order of the chapter's first three verses is a claim about sequence. The heart is opened first, and the burden is lifted second. A reader who reverses the order will expect the removal of a difficulty to produce relief, and will be puzzled when it does not. The chapter says the opposite: capacity comes before removal. A man given the capacity can carry what remains after the load is reduced; a man without it is crushed by what is left. The ordering also explains why the burden could be lifted at all. Some weights cannot be taken off a person without destroying him — a responsibility, an office, a knowledge of what his society is doing — and such weights are borne rather than discarded. The verse describes a lifting, and the previous verse supplies the condition that made the lifting survivable. The two belong together, and the chapter's arrangement keeps them in the right order.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:3

> which weighed so heavily on your back

**Expanded Commentary**

**Anqaḍa: A Weight That Makes the Back Speak**

The verb in this verse is unusually concrete. *Anqaḍa* describes a load so heavy that it makes a sound — the creaking of a pack-saddle under a burden, or the cracking of a joint under strain. The exegetes note the sense precisely: something pushing down so hard that the back makes a noise. The word choice removes any possibility of reading the burden as a metaphor for mild discomfort. This was a weight that produced an audible response in the body carrying it. The Qur'an rarely uses physical language of this kind for spiritual states, and when it does, the effect is to insist that the state was real. The Prophet's burden was not an abstraction he reflected on. It pressed on him, and the verse records the pressure with a verb drawn from the loading of animals. A weight that makes the back speak is an image drawn from ordinary labour, and the reader recognises it immediately. The verse is not describing an unusual suffering but an ordinary one carried to the point of damage, and the ordinariness is what makes the description apply so widely.

**The Back as the Seat of Carrying**

The specification of the back is significant. A burden borne in the hands can be set down; a burden on the back is carried wherever the carrier goes. It cannot be put aside for rest without help, and it shapes the carrier's posture. The image therefore describes a condition rather than a task. Whatever the burden was — a liability, an office, the state of a people — it is described as something that accompanied the Prophet through his days rather than as something he picked up at intervals. That description fits the office reading particularly well. Prophethood is not a role that can be laid aside in the evening, and the verse's choice of the back rather than the hands says so without stating it. The reader is given the posture of a man walking under a load, and the next chapter's promise of ease is addressed to that posture.

**Why the Description Is So Physical**

The Qur'an's consolations to this Prophet are consistently embodied. The heart is opened; the back is relieved; the name is raised. None of the three is described in purely interior terms, and the pattern is deliberate. A man under sustained pressure needs more than a proposition; he needs his condition named in a language that matches how it feels. The verse supplies that naming. It does not say that the difficulty was small, or that it was deserved, or that it was a lesson. It says that the load was heavy enough to make a sound, and that it has been taken off. The acknowledgment is itself part of the relief, because a burden that is not acknowledged by anyone becomes harder to carry than the same burden that is seen. The chapter opens by seeing it. The physical description is chosen because the experience is physical. A reader who has been told that his distress is spiritual or imaginary has not been consoled; the verse describes a back and a weight, and the concreteness is what makes the relief intelligible.

**The Two Readings of What Was Lifted**

The commentators divide on the object, and the division follows the ambiguity of the previous verse. Some read the burden as the weight of prophethood itself, lightened by the arrival of revelation and the growth of a community to carry it with him. Others read it as the burden of ignorance and confusion that afflicted Arabia, lifted through the revelation of God's commands. The first reading keeps the burden personal, which the grammar supports, since the load is described as resting on his back. The second makes it collective, which the historical outcome supports, since the revelation did transform the peninsula. The stronger reading is the personal one, and the collective effect can be understood as its consequence. A messenger relieved of a load he could not carry is thereby able to carry a message to people who cannot carry themselves. The two readings of what was lifted are both worth holding. A reader who takes the narrower reading receives a statement about a past event; the wider reading gives him something applicable to himself. The verse supports both, and the support is why it has been read in both ways.

**The Verse Against a Theology of Merit**

A weight that has to be lifted is a weight the carrier could not lift himself. The verse forecloses any reading of the Prophet's achievement as self-made. He did not expand his own heart, lighten his own back or raise his own name; all three are attributed to God in the perfective, and all three are put to him as questions requiring acknowledgment. The method is the same one the previous chapter used with the orphan, the unguided man and the poor man. Nothing is credited to the recipient, and the absence of credit is not a slight but the ground of the consolation. A man who achieved his own standing can lose it; a man whose standing was given depends on the Giver, and the Giver has just described what He did. The verse is therefore a statement about God's action disguised as a question about a back. Set against a theology of merit, the verse is a statement about grace. A reader who believes that relief is earned has been shown a man relieved before he had accomplished anything, and the sequence defeats the belief. The verse is doing theological work in the language of a body.

**The Relief and the Work That Continued**

The lifting of the burden did not end the work, and the chapter makes that clear by what it says next. The promise of ease with hardship, repeated twice, only makes sense if hardship remains. The relief described here is therefore not the removal of a condition but the removal of a weight within it. The distinction is the practical teaching of the verse. A person does not wait for the load to disappear before he can stand upright; he is given the capacity to stand under it, and the capacity arrives before the standing. That is the sequence of this chapter: the heart opened, the back relieved, the name raised, and then the command to exert oneself. Nothing in the sequence suggests rest. The relief is what makes exertion possible, and it is described in the body's own language so that no one mistakes it for a mood.

**A Weight He Never Complained About**

The verse records something the Prophet did not say. The tradition preserves no complaint from him about the weight he carried, and the Qur'an's own testimony is that the distress caused by his opponents' talk was known to God rather than reported by him: "We certainly know that your heart is truly distressed by what they say" (15:97). The knowledge is attributed to God, not to a statement by the Prophet. The verse in this chapter continues that pattern. The burden is described by God, in terms more graphic than any the Prophet used, and the description is put to him as a question he can only confirm. A reader who has carried something heavy without telling anyone will recognise the situation. The acknowledgment comes from outside, and the acknowledgment is part of the relief. The chapter performs the function that other people usually fail to perform: it names the load accurately, and it names it as lifted.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:4

> and elevated your renown for you

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Name Raised Where Names Were Everything**

In the society this chapter addressed, a man's name was his capital. Lineage determined protection, reputation determined marriage and alliance, and a man without a name had no standing to defend himself with. The verse announces that this particular name has been raised, and the announcement is made to the man whose name was, at the time, being dragged through the streets of Mecca. He was called a poet, a sorcerer, a madman and a liar. The claim that his renown had been elevated was therefore not a description of his public standing but a statement about a different register of fame, one that the people insulting him could not see and could not prevent. The verse does not dispute their insults. It states, in the perfective tense, that the elevation has already happened. A name raised where names were everything is a gift the reader can measure. In a society organised by lineage and reputation, the raising of a name was not a courtesy but a change of standing, and the verse describes it in those terms.

**Raised by Being Joined to the Name of God**

The mechanism of the elevation is specific, and the exegetes identify it correctly. The Prophet's title — Messenger of Allah — attaches his name to the divine name, and the attachment is permanent. It is spoken in the testimony of faith, which is recited on entering the religion; it is recited in the call to prayer, five times a day, in every Muslim settlement; and it is repeated in the greeting at the end of each unit of prayer. No other name in history is spoken as often in as many places. The Qur'an's own wording connects the two names in a single command: "Indeed, Allah showers His blessings upon the Prophet, and His angels pray for him. O believers! Invoke Allah's blessings upon him, and salute him with worthy greetings of peace" (33:56). The verse places God's own action first, then the angels', then the believers'. The renown described in this chapter is that sequence in operation.

**A Renown That Cannot Be Withdrawn**

The elevation has a structural feature worth noticing. A reputation built on achievement can collapse when the achievement is forgotten; a reputation built on being named in worship lasts as long as the worship does. The Prophet's name is not preserved by historians or monuments but by a daily act performed by people who have never heard his voice. The verse's promise is therefore not a prediction about fame in the ordinary sense, which the Qur'an elsewhere treats as worthless. It is a statement about a name embedded in a practice, and the practice is commanded: "Invoke Allah's blessings upon him". The command converts the renown from an accident of history into an obligation of worship. Every person who prays performs the elevation, and none of them can stop. A renown that cannot be withdrawn is what distinguishes this gift from ordinary reputation. A reader who has seen a reputation destroyed knows that most renown is fragile; the verse describes one that is not, and the description is the reason it is worth mentioning.

**The Reading That Looks Backward to Earlier Scriptures**

Some commentators read the verse as referring to the mention of the Prophet in the scriptures that came before the Qur'an. The reading has textual support, since the Qur'an describes him as one whom the people of the earlier Book recognise: they find him written with them in their own texts. On this reading the elevation is not only forward-looking; it reaches back into the history of revelation and places this messenger inside a line that his opponents did not control. The claim would have been provocative in Mecca, where the Jews and Christians of Arabia were known to possess books the Quraysh did not have. A name recorded in those books is a name that predates the insults. The reading is secondary to the worship-based one, but it reinforces the same point: the renown was not conferred by public opinion.

**Why Renown Comes Last in the List**

The chapter's order is inward to outward, and the placement of renown at the end is a judgement about value. The heart opened and the burden lifted are gifts to the person; the name raised is a gift about the person, visible to others and dependent on them for its performance. Placing it last subordinates it to the first two. A man with an opened heart and a lightened back can carry a great name; a man with a great name and a constricted heart is crushed by it. The Qur'an's ordering says which matters, and it says so without stating the comparison. The reader who wants the fourth gift without the first two has misread the list, and the chapter's structure is arranged to make that misreading difficult. Renown comes last because it is the least of the three gifts and the one most easily mistaken for the greatest. A reader who reads the list in order sees the chest, the burden and then the name, and the order tells him which of the three matters most.

**The Question Form and Its Demand**

Like the verses before it, this one is a question, and the question requires an answer. To say yes is to accept that the elevation happened, that it happened by God's action, and that it happened before anyone could see it. The last of those is the hardest, and it is the one the occasion of the chapter makes urgent. A man being publicly humiliated is being told that his name has been raised, and the two facts are simultaneously true. The chapter holds them together without reconciling them in visible terms. It asks for acknowledgment of the unseen fact while the visible one is still in force, and the acknowledgment is the act of faith the verse is designed to produce. The name was raised; the insults continued; both statements are true, and the second one ends. The question form carries a demand. A reader who is asked whether his name has not been raised is being asked to acknowledge it, and the acknowledgement is the response the verse seeks. The form is not decorative; it requires an answer.

**A Name Raised in a City That Refused It**

The claim of this verse was made when its addressee had a following of a few dozen people and an organised opposition behind him. The Meccan objection to his claim is recorded in the Qur'an in the objectors' own words: "If only this Quran was revealed to a great man from ˹one of˺ the two cities!" (43:31). The objection was that renown belongs to men of standing, and that this man had none. The verse answers without argument, by stating that the renown has already been conferred by a party the objectors did not recognise. The two claims were held simultaneously for years, and the visible one was losing. What the chapter asserts is that the invisible register is the one that counts, and that the verdict in it had already been delivered. The reader who measures a name by its current reception is measuring in the register that the objectors used, and the verse tells him that the other register exists and has already been filled in.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:5

> So, surely with hardship comes ease

**Expanded Commentary**

**With, Not After**

The preposition in this verse carries the whole promise. *Maʿa* means with, alongside, in company with — not after, not instead of, and not in place of. The ease is not positioned at the far end of the difficulty, waiting for the difficulty to finish. It travels with it. The distinction is not a nicety; it changes what a person in hardship should expect. A promise of ease after hardship sets up a waiting period in which nothing improves and the sufferer has no reason to look for relief. A promise of ease with hardship tells him that something in his present condition is already working in his favour, even where he cannot see it. The exegetes have pressed this point, and the grammatical fact is on their side: the verse does not use a preposition of sequence. With, not after, is the distinction the verse turns on. A reader waiting for the hardship to end before the ease begins has misread the preposition, and the misreading is what produces the despair the verse is meant to cure.

**One Definite Hardship, Ease Without a Limit**

The Arabic article does further work. The hardship is *al-ʿusr*, definite — one specific, identifiable difficulty. The ease is *yusran*, indefinite, without the article, which in Arabic leaves the quantity and the identity open. The construction therefore sets a single defined hardship against ease that is undefined and repeatable. The early exegetes drew the arithmetic out plainly, and Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have put it in a sentence: one hardship will never overcome two eases. Whether the second ease is the one repeated in the next verse or a further instance, the grammatical asymmetry supports the conclusion. A named, bounded difficulty is being matched by a relief that has no fixed limit, and the mismatch is in the sufferer's favour. One definite hardship against ease without a limit is an unequal pairing, and the inequality is deliberate. A reader who counts his difficulties and finds them many should note that the article makes the hardship singular and the ease indefinite. The grammar is the consolation.

**The Same Promise Stated Elsewhere**

The verse belongs to a consistent pattern in the revelation. The legislation on maintenance after divorce ends with the same assurance in sequence form: "Allah does not require of any soul beyond what He has given it. After hardship, Allah will bring about ease" (65:7). The ruling on fasting states the intention behind the whole legislative scheme: "Allah intends ease for you, not hardship" (2:185). The first of these places ease after hardship; this verse places it alongside. The two formulations are complementary rather than contradictory, and together they describe a single arrangement: the difficulty has a limit, the relief does not, and the relief is already in motion while the difficulty lasts. A reader who holds only one of the two formulations will either wait passively or expect an immediate end; the pair gives him both the patience and the expectation. Stating the same promise elsewhere confirms that it is a rule rather than a personal reassurance. A reader who finds the pairing repeated in another chapter understands that he is reading a settled principle, and the repetition is what allows him to apply it to a case the verse never mentions.

**A Promise Stated as a Law**

The particles in this verse are emphatic, and the sentence is phrased as a general truth rather than as a personal assurance. It does not say that ease will come to you; it says that with hardship comes ease, in the same register as a statement about how the world works. The formulation matters for its reach. A personal promise can be doubted by someone who feels excluded from it; a law applies to everyone inside its terms, including the person who does not believe it. The chapter has been addressing the Prophet directly up to this point — your heart, your burden, your name — and now it steps back and states a rule. The step back is deliberate. The consolation is being made available to anyone who will read the verse, and it is made available by being stated as a fact about hardship rather than as a favour to one man. A promise stated as a law reads differently from a promise made to one person. A reader who treats the verse as law expects the pairing to hold in his own life, and the expectation is what the phrasing invites. The verse is not comforting a man; it is describing how things are arranged.

**The Answer to the Burden Just Described**

The verse follows immediately on the account of a weight that made the back creak, and the connection is the point of the chapter's middle. A man has just been told that his load was lifted, and he is then told that ease accompanies hardship. The two statements together describe how the lifting works. It is not that the load disappears; it is that relief is present in the same moment as the weight. The chapter's structure makes this reading hard to avoid. The gift list ends with the name raised, and the next sentence generalises the mechanism by which the gifts operated. Nothing in the Prophet's external circumstances had changed when the burden was described as lifted, and nothing changes when ease is promised. What the verse supplies is the reason a lifted burden and continuing difficulty can coexist. The answer to the burden just described is immediate, and the immediacy is the chapter's method. A reader who has been told about a weight on his back is told in the next breath that ease accompanies it, and the proximity of the two statements is what makes the consolation usable.

**What the Verse Does Not Promise**

The promise assumes hardship. It does not predict its absence, and it does not offer a technique for avoiding it. The verse is addressed to a man inside a difficulty, and it speaks to that condition rather than about escaping it. That limitation is what makes the promise credible. A guarantee that the faithful will not suffer would be contradicted by the biography of every messenger, including the one being addressed here, whose life after this chapter contained the death of his wife and uncle, the loss of his home, three major battles and a wound to his face. The verse does not promise a life without hardship. It promises company within it, and company is what a person under a load actually needs. What the verse does not promise is the removal of difficulty, and the omission is honest. A reader who expects a life without hardship will read the verse as broken when the hardship continues; the verse is about accompaniment rather than substitution.

**Expecting the Ease**

A promise of this kind produces a discipline. The person who believes that ease is present with his hardship looks for it, and looking changes what he notices. The alternative — a conviction that the difficulty is the whole of the situation — produces the opposite behaviour: withdrawal, resentment, and the reading of every neutral event as confirmation. The Qur'an treats the expectation itself as part of the arrangement, since a soul that expects nothing cannot receive anything. The verse is therefore not only a statement about the world but an instruction about attention. It tells the reader what to look for while the load is still on his back, and the looking is the first form the ease takes. Expecting the ease is part of enduring the hardship, and the verse makes expectation an act rather than a mood. A reader who has stopped looking for relief has stopped doing something the verse requires of him, and the requirement is within his control.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:6

> Surely with ˹that˺ hardship comes ˹more˺ ease

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Sentence That Is Not a Copy**

The verse repeats the one before it with almost identical words, and the repetition has been read as a problem by readers who expect every sentence to add information. It adds something else: force. Arabic achieves emphasis by restatement, and the Qur'an uses the device where the point is one a listener is unlikely to accept on first hearing. A man under a heavy load does not readily believe that relief is present with his suffering; he believes it, if at all, after hearing it twice. The second sentence therefore performs a function the first could not. It signals that the speaker knows the listener's resistance and has addressed it, and it converts an assertion into an insistence. The chapter's central promise is the one place in this short sūrah where repetition occurs, which marks it as the claim most in need of support.

**The Article That Points Back**

The second verse refers to *that* hardship — the same one, previously named, now with the definite article repeated. The reference is not to a new difficulty but to the identical difficulty already mentioned, and the ease is attached to it again. The construction produces a specific picture: one hardship, with ease on either side of it. The early exegetical tradition states the resulting arithmetic in a form attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās and repeated by the commentators: a single hardship will never overcome two eases. The saying is not a Prophetic report with a chain and a number; it is an exegetical gloss, and its value lies in the reading it records rather than in its authority. What it captures is the plain sense of the two verses taken together — the difficulty is counted once and the relief twice. The article that points back is what makes the second mention a repetition rather than a new item. A reader who notices the definite form understands that the same ease is being spoken of, and the understanding is what carries the argument of the verse.

**Why Ease Is Named Twice and Hardship Once**

The asymmetry is the content of the pair. If the two verses were symmetrical — a hardship and an ease, then another hardship and another ease — the message would be a balanced alternation, and the sufferer would have no ground for confidence. The actual structure is unbalanced in his favour. One named difficulty is surrounded by relief mentioned twice, and the second mention carries the indefinite noun again, leaving the quantity of the relief open. The grammatical effect is cumulative: the hardship is bounded and identified, the ease is repeated and unbounded. A reader who counts the words finds the answer to his situation in the counting, which is presumably why the verse invites it. Naming ease twice and hardship once is the arithmetic of the verse, and the arithmetic is the comfort. A reader who weighs the two mentions against each other finds the balance unequal in his favour, and the inequality is stated in the grammar rather than asserted in commentary.

**The Two Eases Read as Two Worlds**

A common exegetical reading takes the first ease as relief in this life and the second as the reward of the next. On that reading the pair describes the whole span of a believer's experience: something that makes the present bearable, and something that settles the account afterwards. The reading fits the Qur'an's consistent two-term structure, in which every present condition is completed by a future one. It also explains why the second verse was needed. A promise of relief in this life can fail to materialise in the form the sufferer expected, and a promise limited to this life would leave him exposed. The second ease covers the case in which the first is partial. The reading does not exclude the simpler one — that the repetition is emphasis — and both can stand together without contradiction. Reading the two eases as two worlds widens the promise beyond this life. A reader for whom the first ease has not arrived has been told that a second exists, and the second is not subject to the conditions of the first. The reading is traditional and it is generous.

**What the Repetition Costs the Reader**

Repetition of this kind is a demand on the listener rather than a gift to him. It says that the statement will not be softened, qualified or explained away, and that the listener's discomfort with it is expected. A man under a load often resents being told that relief is present, because the claim seems to contradict his experience. The verse does not engage the resentment; it repeats the claim in the face of it. The method is characteristic of the consolation offered in these two chapters. Neither argues with the sufferer's perception nor denies his pain. Both state the arrangement twice and leave the sufferer to reconcile his perception with it, which is the work of patience. The reader who wants the verses to explain his hardship will not find the explanation; he will find the promise, stated twice.

**Patience as the Condition of the Promise**

The ease promised here is not detached from conduct. The same assurance in the sūrah of Divorce is attached to a rule about capacity: "Allah does not require of any soul beyond what He has given it. After hardship, Allah will bring about ease" (65:7). The pairing of capacity and relief is instructive. What is required of a person is limited to what he has been given, and the relief follows the requirement rather than replacing it. The chapter makes the same arrangement in its final verses, which command exertion after the promise of ease has been given. The sequence is consistent: assurance first, effort second, and the assurance is what makes the effort sustainable. A reader who takes the two verses as permission to stop has read them against the chapter that contains them. Patience is the condition of the promise, and the verse does not conceal it. A reader who treats the ease as automatic has removed the requirement; the promise is given to a person who continues while waiting, and the continuing is his part.

**The Arithmetic Against Despair**

The two verses invite counting, and the counting is a discipline rather than a game. A person in hardship who has been told twice that ease accompanies it has been given something to do with his attention: he can look for the two eases against the one hardship. The search is not a denial of the difficulty. It is a way of holding the difficulty in proportion while it lasts. The alternative is the arithmetic despair performs automatically, which counts the hardship, multiplies it by the days it has already lasted, and treats the product as the whole of the situation. The chapter's structure opposes that calculation with a simpler one: one named difficulty, relief mentioned twice, and the second mention left unbounded. A reader who works the two sums finds that they cannot both be right, and the verse tells him which one is. The insistence of the repetition is what makes the second sum available at the moment the first one feels most convincing.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:7

> So once you have fulfilled ˹your duty˺, strive ˹in devotion˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command in the Middle of a Consolation**

Six verses have described gifts and promised relief, and then the chapter turns to a demand. The transition is abrupt, and the abruptness is the teaching. Consolation in this revelation is not a resting place; it is a preparation for work. A reader who expects the promise of ease to conclude the chapter in comfort is told, in the seventh verse, that the ease exists so that exertion can continue. The conjunction at the start of the verse ties the command to what precedes it: because your heart was opened, your burden lifted, your name raised and ease guaranteed, therefore strive. The gifts are the ground of the demand rather than its reward, and the order reverses the assumption most readers bring to a chapter of consolation. A command placed in the middle of a consolation changes the character of both. A reader who is being comforted is also being set to work, and the combination prevents the consolation from becoming an excuse. The verse interrupts comfort with duty on purpose.

**Faraghta: Free, Finished, Relieved**

The verb *faraghta* covers a range of related states, and the exegetes have exploited the range. It can mean to be free from an occupation, to have finished a task, to be relieved of a burden, or to be emptied out and spent. Four readings follow. When you are free from your obligatory worship, occupy yourself with supererogatory prayer. When you are free from worldly affairs, pray. When you have been relieved of the burden described in the earlier verses, exert yourself. When you are spent, toil. Each reading is grammatically available, and they converge on a single point: the verse describes a transition from one kind of effort to another, never a transition from effort to rest. Whichever sense is preferred, the interval in which nothing is required does not appear. Being free, finished and relieved are three senses of one word, and the reader benefits from holding all three. A person who has completed a task is available for the next, and the availability is what the command presupposes. The verse assumes a man who has just been set down.

**Naṣab: Toil That Costs Something**

The verb commanded is *nṣab*, from a root denoting labour, fatigue and the wearing of the body. It is not a gentle word. The Qur'an uses it elsewhere for exhausting effort, and it carries the sense of work that leaves a mark on the one who does it. The command therefore asks for tiredness rather than for attendance. A man who performs his devotions without cost has not been addressed by this verse. The choice of verb is consistent with the chapter's physical language throughout — a heart opened, a back that creaked under a weight — and it completes that language by naming the response: toil. The chapter that described a burden lifted ends by requiring effort, and the effort is described in the same bodily terms as the burden. Toil that costs something is what is being commanded, and the cost is not incidental. A reader who has been given relief is not being invited to rest in it; he is being directed toward effort that will tire him. The verse pairs the gift with the demand in the same breath.

**One Act Follows Another**

The structure of the sentence is sequential: when you have finished this, do that. The sequence is the discipline the verse establishes. There is no interval between the completion of one duty and the beginning of the next, and the absence of an interval is not an accident of phrasing but the content of the command. A religious life organised around transitions is a life in which no period is unclaimed. The Qur'an describes the same arrangement where it commands the Prophet to rise in the later part of the night for additional prayer, "so your Lord may raise you to a station of praise" (17:79). The obligatory prayer is completed and the supererogatory prayer begins; the day's work is completed and the night's worship begins. The verse in this chapter states the principle behind that arrangement.

**The Strongest Reading and Its Support**

Of the four readings, the one with the most support is the first: when the obligatory devotions are finished, turn to the supererogatory. It fits the verb's ordinary sense of being free from an occupation, and it fits the Prophet's practice as recorded in the tradition, in which voluntary prayer filled the hours between the appointed prayers. The reading also gives the verse a practical application that the others lack. A reader knows when he has finished an obligatory act, and he can be told what to do next; he cannot easily determine when he is "spent" or when he has been "relieved". The alternative readings are not excluded — the verb genuinely carries them — but the practical reading is the one that makes the verse actionable, and the chapter's commands are directed at action.

**What the Command Assumes**

The verse assumes that a servant is never without a task. That assumption is the opposite of the common expectation that devotion aims at rest, and it is stated here without argument. The command does not promise that the exertion will end, and it does not describe a final state in which nothing is required. What it describes is a life in which the completion of one duty is the signal for the next. The assumption explains the chapter's structure as well as its content. The promise of ease with hardship is not a promise of eventual inactivity; it is the provision that makes continuous exertion possible. A man who expects rest at the end of his duties will find the verse unintelligible; a man who understands the arrangement will read it as a description of how his time is to be spent.

**A Command Addressed to One Man and Meant for Many**

The verse is in the second person singular, and its immediate addressee is the Prophet. The command, however, describes a pattern rather than a personal assignment, and the Muslim tradition has read it as a rule for anyone who prays. The exegetes apply it explicitly to the community: when you have finished your obligatory worship, occupy yourself with what is voluntary; when you have finished your worldly work, turn to prayer. The extension is legitimate because the verse states a principle about transitions rather than a specific instruction about a specific hour. The reader who finishes a prayer and reaches for his phone has not broken a rule stated in words; he has declined the arrangement this verse describes, and the arrangement is the substance of the command. Addressed to one man and meant for many, the verse travels. A reader who is not the addressee still finds himself described: finished with one thing, and required to stand up for another. The generality of the command is what has kept it in use.

## Sūrah al-Sharḥ 94:8

> turning to your Lord ˹alone˺ with hope

**Expanded Commentary**

**Raghba: Longing Pointed in One Direction**

The final command uses a verb of desire. *Raghiba* means to want, to long for, to turn toward something with appetite, and the preposition attached to it fixes the direction: to your Lord. The verse therefore does not command an action but a want. It tells the reader where his wanting is to be aimed, and the aiming is exclusive. The command completes the exertion of the previous verse by supplying its motive. Toil without a direction is drudgery, and a direction without toil is fantasy; the chapter's last two verses join them. The order is significant: the command to strive comes first, and the command about desire second, which suggests that the desire is formed and corrected by the exertion rather than waiting to be felt before the work begins. Longing pointed in one direction is what the verse requires, and the direction is the whole of it. A reader whose desire is divided among several objects has not yet done what is asked, and the asking is not about the strength of the longing but about where it is aimed.

**Not to the World, and Not to People**

The exclusivity of the direction is the point of the command, and the Qur'an states the contrast elsewhere in a single line: "˹Always˺ remember the Name of your Lord, and devote yourself to Him wholeheartedly" (73:8). The adverb in that verse — wholeheartedly, to the exclusion of others — makes explicit what this verse implies with its preposition. A desire aimed at the world produces the behaviour the revelation criticises in the sūrah of the City, where a man boasts of what he has destroyed and resents the expenditure. A desire aimed at other people produces performance, in which the act is shaped by its audience. The verse closes both possibilities by naming one object. The reader is not asked to reduce his wanting; he is asked to redirect it, and the redirection is what makes the exertion of the previous verse worth anything.

**Hope as the Posture of Worship**

The noun implied in the command is hope or longing, and its presence matters. Worship performed in terror produces compliance without attachment; worship performed in hope produces effort that can be sustained. The chapter has spent six verses establishing grounds for hope — an opened heart, a lifted burden, a raised name, ease guaranteed twice — and the final command spends them. The reader is told to want God because he has been given reasons to expect good from Him. That sequence is characteristic of the Qur'an's method of consolation: the assurance comes first, and the demand follows from it rather than preceding it. A man who has been told that his burden is gone can desire the One who removed it; a man still under an unacknowledged weight cannot, and the chapter knows it. Hope as the posture of worship is what the verse establishes, and the establishment matters for the reader's practice. A person who approaches without hope has not adopted the posture described here, and the verse makes the posture part of the act rather than an addition to it.

**The Ending of a Chapter That Began With a Burden**

The arc of this sūrah is short and complete. A heart is opened; a weight that made the back creak is lifted; a name is raised; hardship is met with ease, stated twice; the servant is commanded to exert himself; and the chapter ends with his desire pointed at his Lord. Nothing in that sequence is incidental. Each item prepares the next, and the last item is the only one that describes the servant's own contribution. Everything before it was done to him. What he contributes is his wanting, and the chapter ends on that contribution, which is the smallest thing he has and the only thing he can give. The placement is a judgement about what worship ultimately consists in. The ending of a chapter that began with a burden is what makes the movement visible. A reader who has followed the eight verses has been taken from a weight on his back to a desire pointed at God, and the distance between those two points is the chapter's argument.

**Effort and Desire Held Together**

The two commands are inseparable, and separating them produces two familiar failures. Exertion without directed desire becomes routine: the acts are performed and the person is unchanged. Desire without exertion becomes sentiment: the feeling is sincere and nothing is done. The chapter places the two in adjacent verses with a conjunction between them, and the arrangement is deliberate. The toil of the seventh verse is to be performed by a person whose wanting is aimed at God, and the wanting of the eighth verse is to be expressed in toil. Neither verse can be obeyed without the other, and the reader who keeps one and drops the other has not kept either. The pair is the practical summary of the chapter, and it follows the consolation rather than replacing it. Effort and desire held together is the state the verse describes, and neither is sufficient alone. A reader who toils without longing is labouring; one who longs without toiling is wishing. The verse joins them, and the joining is the practical instruction of the chapter's last line.

**A Chapter That Ends Facing God**

The last word of the sūrah is the Lord. The sentence ends where the desire is pointed, and the ending leaves the reader in that direction rather than returning him to his circumstances. Nothing in the final verse refers back to the insults, the interval in revelation, the poverty or the opposition. The chapter's last statement is about orientation, and orientation is what survives a change of circumstances. A man whose desire is fixed on his difficulties will be governed by them; a man whose desire is fixed elsewhere can endure them. The verse supplies that fixation in five words, and it does so after the grounds for it have been listed. The reader who has followed the chapter arrives at the command already persuaded, and the command asks him for the only thing the chapter has not already given him.

**What the Reader Keeps From the Chapter**

Eight verses leave a reader with three things, and they are worth stating plainly. The first is a description of what God does before He demands anything: He opens the heart, removes the load, and raises the standing of the one He sends. The second is a rule about difficulty — ease travels with it, and the relief is mentioned twice against one hardship. The third is a pair of commands: exert yourself when one duty ends, and point your wanting at your Lord alone. Nothing in the chapter is speculative, and nothing in it requires a reader to be in the Prophet's position in order to use it. The consolation is transferable because the gifts it describes are of a kind that are still being given, and the commands are addressed to anyone who prays. The reader who closes the chapter with those three things has taken what it was written to give, and the last of them — the direction of his wanting — is the one that determines whether the other two hold.
