# Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā (Chapter 93) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Morning Brightness and the Consolation of the Orphan**

Aḍ-Ḍuḥā, "The Morning Brightness" or "The Early Morning Hours," is the ninety-third chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 11 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with two tender, atmospheric oaths: *“By the morning brightness (*wa-l-ḍuḥā*), and by the night when it grows still and covers in darkness (*wa-l-layli idhā sajā*)—your Lord has not forsaken you, [O Muḥammad], nor has He become detested!”* (93:1–3). In the emotional biography of the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ, Sūrah aḍ-Ḍuḥā holds a peerless, tearfully cherished station: it descended as an intimate, celestial embrace after an agonizing period of silence, reassuring a broken-hearted Prophet that he was neither abandoned nor unloved. In traditional Qur'anic recitation dating back to the Companions, it is from Sūrah aḍ-Ḍuḥā through the end of the Qur'an that reciters utter the celebratory takbīr (*Allāhu Akbar!*) after each chapter, commemorating the joyful resumption of divine revelation. In eleven luminous lines, Sūrah aḍ-Ḍuḥā moves from personal reassurance to the prophetic biography, and from gratitude for divine gifts to the moral imperative of protecting the orphan and the beggar.

**The Structural Architecture and the Memory of the Wards**

The architecture of Sūrah aḍ-Ḍuḥā moves across three distinct, deeply personal movements:
1. The Celestial Reassurance (1–5): Swears by the morning light and the still night that God has not abandoned the Prophet. It delivers the profound comparative perspective of verse 4: *“And the Hereafter is better for you than the first [life],”* followed by the grand prophetic promise: *“And your Lord will surely give you, and you will be well-pleased.”*
2. The Three Historical Gifts (6–8): Directs the Prophet's memory to his own vulnerable childhood, reminding him of three miraculous interventions of divine grace: - *“Did He not find you an orphan and give [you] refuge?”* (born fatherless, losing his mother Āminah at age six and grandfather ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib at age eight, yet divinely protected). - *“And He found you lost / wandering in search of truth and guided [you]?”* - *“And He found you poor and made [you] self-sufficient?”* (enriched by contentment, trade, and the noble companionship of Khadījah).
3. The Three Ethical Imperatives (9–11): Derives from these three divine favors three foundational moral obligations: - *“So as for the orphan, do not oppress him / treat him with harshness!”* - *“And as for the petitioner / beggar, do not repel him!”* - *“And as for the favor of your Lord, proclaim it (*fa-ḥaddith*)!”*

The historical occasion of aḍ-Ḍuḥā is documented in authentic collections (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4950 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1797) by Jundab ibn ʿAbdillāh.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:1

> By the morning sunlight

**An Oath on Light at the Moment Light Was Questioned**

The sūrah opens with an oath on the brightest hour of the day, and the occasion explains the choice. Revelation had paused. The reports differ on the length of the interval — twelve days, forty, fifty — but they agree that the pause was long enough to be noticed and to be mocked. One taunt recorded in the tradition is blunt: "O Muhammad, it seems to me your companion has forsaken you." An oath sworn on the returning light of morning is a direct answer to that taunt. The accusation was that the source of illumination had gone out. The reply begins by swearing on the daily return of light, as though to say that the thing which appears most reliably in the created order is being used to guarantee a promise about the uncreated One. Nothing in the verse argues the point. The oath simply places the reader in the forenoon and asks him to look.

**Ḍuḥā: A Word With a Fixed Hour**

*Al-ḍuḥā* is not a general word for light. It names a specific period: the forenoon, from the rising and spreading of the sun until it approaches its zenith. Arabic distinguishes this hour from the first flash of sunrise and from the full glare of midday, and the precision is part of the word's effect. It is the hour when the day is established but not yet at its peak, when light is complete without being oppressive. The same word gives its name to the supererogatory prayer performed in the late morning, which the Muslim tradition has kept as a distinct act of worship. When the Qur'an swears by *al-ḍuḥā*, it swears by a hour that its hearers could identify from their own day, and that they would associate with a settled, ordinary brightness rather than with anything spectacular. The fixed hour is what makes the word useful in an oath. A reader knows when the forenoon arrives, and the arrival is not negotiated by anyone. The verse chooses a moment that cannot be delayed, disputed or missed, and the reliability of the moment is the basis of the swearing.

**Why the Qur'an Swears by Times of Day**

This oath belongs to a family. The sun and its brightness open the sūrah that follows (91:1); the night when it covers opens the one after that (92:1); time itself is sworn by in the shortest chapter of the Book (103:1). The pattern is deliberate, and it does specific work. Swearing by a time of day is not decoration; it makes duration itself a witness. A period of the day is something every hearer passes through, cannot avoid, and cannot alter. To swear by the forenoon is to invoke a fact that is experienced rather than argued. It also places the promise that follows inside ordinary time. The consolation this sūrah offers is not located in a miraculous interruption of the day but in the day's own reliability, which is a more durable ground for trust than a wonder would be.

**The Morning Hour in the History of This Revelation**

The forenoon has a place in the story this sūrah addresses. The revelations had begun in a cave on the mountain above Mecca, in seclusion, and had continued intermittently for years. The pause that provoked the taunt was not the first interval, but it was the one that produced a public claim about its meaning. An oath on the morning light therefore touches the whole history of the revelation at once: the light that first came, the intervals in which it did not, and the return that the oath takes for granted. The sūrah does not narrate any of this. It assumes it, which is characteristic of the Meccan chapters' method — they address a hearer who is inside the events rather than a reader who needs them explained. The morning hour has a place in the history of this revelation that the reader should not miss. The chapter was sent to a man who had been waiting, and the hour sworn by is the hour in which waiting ends. The choice of the object is therefore not decorative but exact.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:2

> and the night when it falls still

**Sajā: The Night at Rest**

The second object of the oath is the night qualified by *sajā*, a verb whose root carries the sense of covering, settling and becoming still. The exegetes have read it in three closely related ways: the night as it darkens and covers the earth, the night as it settles over the world, and the night as it extends and lengthens. All three describe the same phenomenon from a different angle, and none of them makes the night hostile. The word chosen is not the word for a threatening darkness. It is the word for a darkness that has come to rest. That choice matters for a sūrah whose purpose is consolation. The night sworn by here is the night of quiet, when work stops and the world goes still, and the oath treats it as something worthy of being sworn on rather than as something to be endured.

**A Pair, Not an Opposition**

The two oaths are joined, and the joining is not a contrast. Light and stillness are the two states of the day's cycle, and the sūrah swears by both without preferring one. This is characteristic of Qur'anic oath pairs, which typically present complementary features of a single created order rather than rivals within it. The pairing also does something specific in this context. The taunt that provoked the sūrah concerned an absence — a gap in revelation, a night in which the Prophet did not rise for his vigil. By swearing on the night as well as on the morning, the text refuses to treat the interval as evidence against God. The night is part of the design and is honoured with an oath alongside the light. An absence within a pattern is not a break in the pattern, and the two terms of the oath say so before any argument is made.

**The Night as a Time of Worship**

Far from being a dead hour, the night is the time this revelation assigns to the highest form of voluntary prayer. The command is explicit: "And rise at ˹the last˺ part of the night, offering additional prayers, so your Lord may raise you to a station of praise" (17:79). The night vigil is where the Prophet's standing was built, and the reports of the occasion of this sūrah turn on exactly that practice: he fell ill, missed the vigil for two or three nights, and the absence of his prayer was read by an observer as the absence of his God. Swearing by the night therefore touches the raw point of the accusation. The hour that was cited as evidence of abandonment is itself made the object of a divine oath, which is a way of saying that the hour belongs to God and always did. Night as a time of worship is not a denial of rest but a description of a use. The reader who prays in the dark is doing something the verse recognises, and the recognition is what makes the oath more than a description of scenery. The hour has a function, and the function is named.

**The Alternation Itself Is the Sign**

Swearing by night and morning together invokes a cycle, and the cycle is one of the Qur'an's most frequently cited evidences. "He created the heavens and the earth for a purpose. He wraps the night around the day, and wraps the day around the night" (39:5). The image of wrapping is precise: neither term destroys the other, and each gives way to the other in turn. The alternation is what makes the pattern trustworthy, and it is what makes an interval inside the pattern meaningful. A night that never ended would be a catastrophe; a night that ends is a design. The sūrah's two oaths therefore carry an argument in their structure. They present the reader with a system in which absence and presence alternate by rule, and they invite him to apply the same reading to the interval that provoked the taunt. The revelation pauses and returns as the night gives way to the forenoon. Nothing about the pattern has broken, and the oath says so before the promise is made.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:3

> Your Lord ˹O Prophet˺ has not abandoned you, nor has He become hateful ˹of you˺

**The Sentence the Oath Was Taken For**

Two oaths have been sworn, and now the text delivers what was sworn to. In the grammar of Arabic oaths the sworn statement is the point of the whole construction, and everything before it exists to raise its weight. The statement is short and personal: your Lord has not taken leave of you, and He has not come to loathe you. It answers a specific public claim, and it does so without naming the claim or the claimant. The absence of any reference to the taunt is deliberate. A rebuttal that quoted the insult would have preserved it; a rebuttal that simply states the opposite removes it. The verse is phrased as a fact about God rather than as a response to a person, and that framing gives it a reach far beyond the occasion that produced it.

**Two Verbs, Two Fears**

The verse negates two things, and the two are not synonyms. *Waddaʿa* carries the sense of taking leave, of bidding farewell — the departure of someone who was present and is now gone. *Qalā* carries aversion, the turning of affection into dislike. Together they cover the two ways a person can lose a relationship: by distance and by disaffection. The taunt had implied both. A pause in revelation looks like departure; a pause after years of intimacy looks like withdrawal of favour. The verse denies each in turn, and the denial of the second is the more important, because distance can be survived where loathing cannot. A man can endure the absence of a friend who still loves him; he cannot endure the presence of one who has come to detest him. The verse removes the second fear first in importance and last in order.

**The Verified Occasion**

The context is recorded in the most authoritative collection. Jundub ibn Sufyān reported that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ became ill and did not rise for the night prayer for two or three nights, and that a woman — identified in the tradition as the wife of Abū Lahab — came and said, "O Muhammad, I think your Satan has forsaken you, for I have not seen him with you for two or three nights." On that, the sūrah was revealed: "By the morning sunlight, and the night when it falls still! Your Lord ˹O Prophet˺ has not abandoned you, nor has He become hateful ˹of you˺" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4950). A second report from Jundub al-Bajalī preserves a shorter form of the same exchange, in which a woman remarks that his companion has delayed in coming to him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4951). The two reports agree on the essential facts: an illness, a missed vigil, a taunt about abandonment, and a revelation in answer.

**The Object That Is Not Stated**

The Arabic of the second clause is elliptical. The verb "He has not loathed" appears without an explicit object, and the second person that the translation supplies is understood rather than written. The exegetes note the possibility, and it is worth taking seriously. A verb of loathing left without an object can be read as a statement about the divine nature rather than about a particular case: loathing of this kind is not something God does. On that reading the verse answers more than the taunt. It removes the category. The Prophet is not only assured that he personally has not been rejected; he is told that the relation between God and His servants is not one in which disgust operates as the taunt imagined. The general reading does not weaken the personal consolation. It grounds it. Leaving the object unstated widens the sentence. A reader who supplies his own case finds it covered, and the widening is not vagueness but generosity. The verse could have named what was not abandoned, and by not naming it, it includes everything a person might fear had been.

**The Interval as a Test Rather Than a Verdict**

A gap in favour is one of the hardest experiences for a believer to interpret, and the Qur'an addresses the difficulty directly elsewhere: "Do you think you will be admitted into Paradise without being tested like those before you? They were afflicted with suffering and adversity and were so ˹violently˺ shaken that ˹even˺ the Messenger and the believers with him cried out, ‘When will Allah’s help come?’" (2:214). The verse ends with the assurance that help is near, but its first half establishes the rule: testing is the condition of the path, not a sign that the path has failed. Read against that rule, the interval in revelation takes on a different meaning. It was not evidence of withdrawal but an instance of the shaking described in the verse, and it fell on the Prophet before it fell on anyone else. The denial in this chapter — He has not taken leave of you, He has not come to loathe you — is therefore not a special exemption granted to one man. It is the correct interpretation of an experience that every serious believer will meet, and the chapter supplies the interpretation at the moment it is needed.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:4

> And the next life is certainly far better for you than this one

**A Comparison, Not a Dismissal**

The verse does not say that this life is worthless. It says that what comes is better, and the comparative form is doing precise work. A statement that dismisses the world entirely would leave the reader with nothing to weigh; a comparison asks him to rank two things he already has in front of him. The ranking is stated with emphasis — "certainly far better" — and it is addressed to a man whose present circumstances were, at that moment, difficult. The verse is therefore not a philosophical claim about the nature of existence but a personal reassurance with a stated direction: things get better, and the better place is the one ahead. The consolation lies in the direction of travel rather than in any denial of the present. The comparison is between a later and an earlier state, and the direction is what matters. A reader who has been measuring his present against his past is measuring in the wrong direction, and the verse reverses the measurement without arguing with him about it.

**The Ranking the Revelation Makes Everywhere**

This comparison is one of the most repeated judgements in the Qur'an, and its consistency is what gives this verse its weight. Worldly enjoyment is described as the adornment of the present life, with the lasting good deeds "far better with your Lord in reward and in hope" (18:46). The pleasures of this world are catalogued and then relativised: "These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination" (3:14). The longer treatment is harsher still, describing the life of this world as play, amusement, luxury and competition, and likening it to rain whose plants delight the sowers before they wither and are reduced to chaff (57:20). Set against that catalogue, the promise in this verse is the mild form of a standard claim. What is unusual here is not the ranking but the person to whom it is addressed and the warmth of the address.

**Why the Promise Is Personal**

The verse says "better for you", not better in general. The specificity matters, because the Prophet's situation was not that of a man enjoying this world and needing to be warned against it. He was poor, mocked, bereaved of supporters, and living under threat. For such a man, the statement that the next life is better is not a warning but a compensation. The Qur'an's warnings about worldly attachment are addressed to people who have something to be attached to; its promises of a better abode are addressed to people who have lost. This verse belongs to the second category, and the difference in tone is audible. Nothing here asks the Prophet to give anything up. It tells him what he will be given. The promise is personal because the distress was personal. A general assurance would not have reached a man who felt abandoned individually, and the verse therefore addresses him by position rather than by category. The reader who feels singled out in his difficulty has been answered in the same terms.

**A Promise That Requires Something of the Reader**

The comparison is not automatic in its application to others. The Qur'an states the condition elsewhere in plain terms: "But whoever desires the Hereafter and strives for it accordingly, and is a ˹true˺ believer, it is they whose striving will be appreciated" (17:19), and the opposite case is described in the verse before it, where the one who desires this fleeting world alone is given a portion of it and then destined for the Fire (17:18). The promise made here to the Prophet is unconditional because his standing is what it is; the general rule that governs everyone else attaches the better abode to desire and effort. A reader who hears the verse as a guarantee for himself without the striving has taken the wrong lesson from it. The chapter's consolation is addressed to a man whose life was the striving.

**The Same Promise Made to Those Who Lost Everything**

The verse is addressed to one man, but the promise belongs to a class, and the Qur'an states it for the class in explicit terms. Of those who left their homes under persecution, it says: "We will surely bless them with a good home in this world. But the reward of the Hereafter is far better, if only they knew" (16:41). The structure of that verse matches this one exactly: a real good in this life, and a better good beyond it. The comparison is not a depreciation of the first term. The emigrants were promised a good home here, and the promise was kept; the better abode is added, not substituted. Where the pious are asked what their Lord has revealed, the answer is "All the best!", and the verse continues: "For those who do good in this world, there is goodness. But far better is the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter" (16:30). The ranking is therefore consistent across the revelation, and this chapter states it in its most personal form. A man who has been mocked for having nothing is told that what he is moving toward outweighs what he has lost, and the same statement is made to everyone who has been stripped for the sake of the message.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:5

> And ˹surely˺ your Lord will give so much to you that you will be pleased

**A Promise Measured by Satisfaction**

The verse sets an unusual standard for a divine gift. It does not name what will be given, or how much, or in what form. It names the receiver's state at the end of it: you will be pleased. The measure of the giving is therefore not quantity but satisfaction, and satisfaction is a state only the receiver can report. That formulation removes any possibility of the promise being judged inadequate by an outside standard. A man given a specified reward can compare it with what others received; a man promised that he will be content has been promised something that cannot fall short. The verse is the most generous formulation available, because it makes the receiver's own satisfaction the term of the contract. Measuring the promise by satisfaction rather than by amount is what makes it impossible to disappoint. A reader who expects a specific quantity can be short-changed; a person promised satisfaction cannot be, because satisfaction is the end of wanting. The verse chooses the measure deliberately.

**Certainty in the Future Tense**

Two particles do the work here. The emphatic particle at the start of the sentence asserts the fact, and the future marker places it ahead. Together they produce a statement that is certain in substance and deferred in time. The deferral matters for the occasion. The taunt that provoked the chapter was about the present: the missing nights, the interval, the visible absence of favour. A promise located entirely in the present could not answer it, because the present was exactly what looked empty. By placing the giving in the future with certainty attached, the verse accepts the appearance of the present and overrules it. Nothing is claimed about the current moment except that it is not the last word. The assurance is therefore honest: it does not pretend the present is abundant, and it does not leave the present as the final measure. The future tense carries certainty rather than speculation, and the reader should note the difference. A statement about what will be given is not a hope expressed by the speaker; it is an announcement about a settled matter. The verse uses the tense because the matter is settled.

**What the Giving Includes**

The exegetes have connected this promise with the rest of the revelation's gifts to the Prophet, and the connections are reasonable. The sūrah of Abundance states the same fact in its own terms: "Indeed, We have granted you ˹O Prophet˺ abundant goodness" (108:1). The elevation of his renown is announced in the chapter that follows this one, where he is reminded that his mention has been raised (94:4). The command to send blessings on him is grounded in a divine precedent: "Indeed, Allah showers His blessings upon the Prophet, and His angels pray for him" (33:56). None of these exhausts the promise, and the tradition has read into it the widest possible content, including the acceptance of his intercession for his community. The openness of the verse is what allows all of these readings, and the openness is not a defect. A promise that named one gift would have excluded the others.

**Contentment as the True Form of Wealth**

The verse treats satisfaction as a good in itself, and the Qur'an makes the same point wherever it discusses wealth. The tranquil soul of the sūrah of the Dawn is addressed in a settled relation rather than an accumulated one: "Return to your Lord, well pleased ˹with Him˺ and well pleasing ˹to Him˺" (89:28). The reward of the righteous is described in the same double term — "Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him" (98:8). Pleasure in these verses is mutual and complete, and it is presented as the endpoint rather than as a mood. The promise in this verse belongs to that economy. What the Prophet will be given is not a larger portion of the same things he lacks but a state in which lack is over. That is a different kind of gift, and it is the only one that answers the taunt completely.

**The Promise Redefines What Favour Looks Like**

The taunt measured favour by what could be seen: revelations arriving, nights of prayer performed, a companion present. On that measure the interval was evidence of loss. The promise in this verse changes the measure rather than disputing the observation. What will be given is described by its effect on the receiver, not by its visibility to observers, which means a person watching from outside cannot assess it. The shift is the answer to the accusation. A favour that can only be measured from within cannot be refuted by an outsider's count of visible signs. The chapter has already made the same move with the oath: it swore on the light and the still night, features of the world that both parties see, and then stated a fact about a relation that neither party can inspect. Between the two moves the taunt is left without a standard to appeal to. It counted what was absent; the verse describes what will be present, and describes it in terms the counter cannot reach.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:6

> Did He not find you as an orphan then sheltered you

**A Question That Is a Record**

The chapter turns from promise to memory, and the form it chooses is the negative rhetorical question. *A-lam yajidka yatīman fa-āwā* — did He not find you an orphan and give you shelter? Three such questions follow in succession, and each one records a state the Prophet was in and a change God made in it. The question form does specific work. It cannot be answered with a denial, since the facts are the Prophet's own biography, and it cannot be answered with a claim of credit, since the change is attributed to God in the same breath. What the form produces is acknowledgment. Nothing else in the chapter could establish that combination as efficiently.

**The Facts Behind the Question**

The biography is precise and was known to the first audience. ʿAbdullāh, the Prophet's father, died while his mother Āmina was still pregnant, so he was born without a father. Āmina died when he was six. His grandfather ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib took charge of him and died when the boy was eight, after which his uncle Abū Ṭālib assumed responsibility for him and protected him through the years of persecution. Later he found support in his marriage to Khadījah, who was his first believer and his staunchest supporter when the revelations began. Every one of these facts was public knowledge in Mecca. The verse does not need to narrate them; it names the condition — orphan — and the audience supplies the history. That economy is characteristic of Meccan revelation, which addresses people inside the events. The facts behind the question are the ones the addressee lived through. A reader who knows them understands that the reminder is not theoretical but drawn from the man's own history. The verse uses biography rather than argument, and biography is harder to dispute.

**Āwā: Shelter Means a Place to Be**

The verb translated as sheltered is *āwā*, from a root meaning to lodge, to house, to give refuge. It does not mean merely to help or to provide for; it means to give someone a place. An orphan's problem is not only material. He has no one whose house is his, no one whose standing covers him, no one to speak for him in a dispute. The verb addresses that specific lack. God is named as the one who sheltered, and the human agents through whom the shelter came — a grandfather, an uncle, a wife — do not diminish the attribution. The Qur'an consistently describes provision that arrives through people as provision from God, and this verse is a clear case. The shelter was real and it had human hands in it, and it is credited to the One who arranged those hands.

**The Legislation That Follows From the Memory**

The Qur'an's treatment of orphans is among its most detailed social legislation, and it reads as an extension of this verse. The community is instructed on the orphan women deprived of their rights and on standing up for orphans generally (4:127). The rule on property is stated twice with almost identical wording: "And do not come near the wealth of the orphan—unless intending to enhance it—until they attain maturity" (6:152), and the same prohibition with the same exception in 17:34. The exception is narrow and specific — management that improves the child's estate — and the deadline is fixed. A revelation that began by reminding its messenger that he had been an orphan went on to build a legal framework around the child without a father, and the connection between the memory and the law is not incidental.

**Born Into a City That Had Just Been Saved**

The orphanhood named in this verse has a date, and the date is memorable to the first audience. The Prophet was born in the year of the Elephant, the year in which an invading army was destroyed outside Mecca. The Qur'an devotes a chapter to the event and puts it to its hearer as a question: "Have you not seen ˹O Prophet˺ how your Lord dealt with the Army of the Elephant?" (105:1), ending with the invaders "like chewed up straw" (105:5). The child born into that year lost his father before he was born, and the city that received him had just been defended without a hand raised by its inhabitants. The juxtaposition is the kind the Qur'an likes: protection on the largest scale, and inside it a single child with no one to protect him. The verse about being found as an orphan and sheltered belongs to that setting. The shelter came to a boy whose city had been saved by a power that had no need of the city's strength, and the boy grew up to tell its people that the same power had taken charge of him.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:7

> Did He not find you unguided then guided you

**A Word That Requires Precision**

The verse says that God found the Prophet *ḍāllan* and guided him, and the word has caused more exegetical care than any other in the chapter. The root covers a range of related states: to be lost, to stray, to err, to be unaware of a road or of a matter. Applied to a prophet, the term has to be read with attention, because the Qur'an elsewhere describes its messengers as chosen and protected. The exegetes have therefore worked carefully on the sense intended here, and the range of their readings is instructive. What they agree on is that the verse describes a real change, not a rhetorical flourish: something was missing and God supplied it. The disagreement is about what was missing. Precision matters because the word can be read in more than one direction, and the reader who settles for a loose reading loses the force of the verse. The chapter is describing a condition of not knowing and then being shown, and both halves of that description have to be held firmly.

**The Strongest Reading: Without a Revealed Path**

The reading with the most textual support is that the Prophet had no revealed law or scripture before the Qur'an. The Qur'an states this in terms that leave little room for doubt: "And so We have sent to you ˹O Prophet˺ a revelation by Our command. You did not know of ˹this˺ Book and faith ˹before˺" (42:52). The same fact is put to him directly: "You never expected this Book to be revealed to you, but ˹it came˺ only ˹as˺ a mercy from your Lord" (28:86). Where the story of Yūsuf is introduced, the reminder is repeated: "We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ the best of stories through Our revelation of this Quran, though before this you were totally unaware ˹of them˺" (12:3). On this reading the guidance given is the revelation itself, and the state described is the absence of a divine path rather than a moral failure. The reading fits the verse's context, which is a list of gifts, and it is consistent with the Qur'an's account of a prophet who did not worship idols and did not follow a scripture because none had reached him.

**The Readings That Shrink the Verse**

Two other interpretations survive in the tradition, and both reduce the statement to an anecdote. One takes the verse as a reference to a time when the Prophet was lost in the streets of Mecca as a child and was guided home. The other describes a journey on which his mount strayed from the road and he was set right. Neither reading is impossible in itself, but both leave the verse without theological content, and both sit oddly in a list whose other two items are shelter from orphanhood and sufficiency after need. A list of that kind describes conditions of a life, not incidents on a road. The stronger reading is the one that treats the guidance as the revelation, and it has the advantage of being stated explicitly elsewhere in the Book rather than inferred from a report. The readings that shrink the verse are worth setting out because a reader who encounters them later will otherwise be unsettled. Knowing that a narrower reading exists, and knowing why the wider one is transmitted, allows him to hold the text without anxiety about its stability.

**The Pattern in the Lives of Other Messengers**

The state described here is not unique to the Prophet. Messengers in the Qur'an are consistently called from an ordinary condition rather than from a prepared one. Mūsā received his commission while travelling, without warning, and his prayer on receiving it was for capacity rather than for knowledge he already had: "My Lord! Uplift my heart for me, and make my task easy, and remove the impediment from my tongue" (20:25–27). The pattern is deliberate. A messenger who arrives with a revealed book has not authored it, and the absence of prior acquaintance with the book is the proof. The verse in this chapter places the Prophet inside that pattern, and the placement answers an objection his opponents actually raised: that he had learned the Qur'an from somewhere. He had not, and the verse says so in the shortest possible form.

**Guidance Given to One and Through Him to Many**

The guidance named in this verse does not end with the person guided. The same verse that records the Prophet's prior ignorance of the Book describes its effect: "But We have made it a light, by which We guide whoever We will of Our servants. And you are truly leading ˹all˺ to the Straight Path" (42:52). The gift is transmitted, and the transmission is the point of it. The prayer of Abraham for the city describes the same structure: "Raise from among them a messenger who will recite to them Your revelations, teach them the Book and wisdom, and purify them" (2:129), and the answer to that prayer is described as a favour already bestowed — a messenger "reciting to them His revelations, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom. For indeed they had previously been clearly astray" (3:164). The parallel between the two statements is exact. A people were astray and were taught; a man was without a path and was given one. The verse in this chapter is the personal form of a public fact, and it explains why the reminder is worth making. What God did for one man became the direction of a community.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:8

> And did He not find you needy then satisfied your needs

**ʿĀʾil: Poverty With a Household**

The third reminder names a specific kind of want. The word *ʿāʾil* does not denote poverty in the abstract; it describes a man who has dependents and insufficient means to support them. The term carries the weight of responsibility as well as the absence of resources, and that combination is what makes the condition acute. A man without means and without dependents is poor; a man without means whose family eats what he can find is in a different predicament. The verse names the second. The choice is precise, and it fits the biography: the Prophet was not merely short of money in his early life, he was supporting a household in a city where standing followed wealth. Poverty with a household is a harder condition than poverty alone, and the word captures it. A reader who has dependants and nothing to give them knows the specific weight the verse is naming. The precision is what makes the reminder land rather than merely register.

**Sufficiency as the Ground of Generosity**

The reminder of sufficiency is followed immediately by commands about orphans and beggars, and the connection is not accidental. A man who has been given enough is positioned to give, and the verse supplies the premise for the demand. The Qur'an makes the same move elsewhere, where the righteous are described as those "who give food—despite their desire for it—to the poor, the orphan, and the captive" (76:8). Sufficiency in this chapter is not a reward to be enjoyed but a capacity to be used. The Prophet's own experience of need is what qualifies him to legislate for the needy, and his experience of sufficiency is what obliges him to do so. Both halves of the verse are necessary to the commands that follow. Sufficiency is stated first because it is the ground of what follows. A reader who has been made sufficient is in a position to be generous, and the verse establishes the position before drawing the consequence. The order is not incidental; it is the structure of the argument.

**Poverty in the Social Order of Mecca**

The verse touches a sore point in its original setting. The Prophet's lack of wealth was one of the objections raised against his claim, and the Qur'an records the objection in the words of those who made it: they asked why the revelation had not come down to a great man from one of the two cities (43:31). The assumption underneath the objection was that divine favour is recognisable in material standing. This verse answers that assumption with a biography. God found the man poor and made him sufficient, which means the poverty was not evidence against him and the sufficiency is not a credential either. Both states were conditions arranged by God, and neither is a measure of standing. For an audience that ranked people by property, that claim was a direct attack on the ranking itself.

**Sufficiency Is a Test With Its Own Temptation**

The sūrah of the Dawn describes the two reactions to divine provision with surgical accuracy: "Now, whenever a human being is tested by their Lord through ˹His˺ generosity and blessings, they boast, ‘My Lord has ˹deservedly˺ honoured me!’ But when He tests them by limiting their provision, they protest, ‘My Lord has ˹undeservedly˺ humiliated me!’" (89:15–16). Both reactions are wrong in the same way. Each reads provision as a verdict on the person receiving it — abundance as a certificate of merit, scarcity as a sentence of disgrace. The verse in this chapter refuses both readings by attributing the change to God without attaching a judgement to either state. He found the Prophet needy and made him sufficient, and neither condition said anything about his standing. The sufficiency is therefore a gift with a warning attached. It can be received as provision or misread as merit, and the misreading is the one that produces the boast the sūrah of the City quotes. The reminder is phrased as a question for that reason: an acknowledgment leaves no room for the boast.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:9

> So do not oppress the orphan

**Taqhar: Do Not Overwhelm Him**

The prohibition uses a verb of force. *Qahara* means to subdue, to overpower, to treat someone as beneath you because you can. The translation "oppress" captures the sense, but the Arabic is more physical than abstract: it describes the act of pressing down on someone who cannot press back. The orphan is named first among the three commands that close this chapter because he is the person most exposed to exactly that treatment. He has no father to answer for him, no standing to threaten a wrongdoer, and no means to seek redress. A verb of overpowering is therefore the precise word for the risk he runs. The verse does not forbid a list of specific injuries; it forbids the posture from which injuries follow. Overwhelming an orphan is possible without striking him, and the verse is addressing that possibility. A reader who has never raised his hand at a child may still have used his position to silence one. The word covers the use of advantage, and the coverage is what makes the command difficult.

**The Meccan Context of the Command**

The command was delivered into a society with a documented practice of absorbing the property of the fatherless. The Qur'an indicts that practice in the sūrah revealed shortly before this one: "Absolutely not! In fact, you are not ˹even˺ gracious to the orphan, nor do you urge one another to feed the poor" (89:17–18). The same indictment appears in the chapter that identifies the denier of the Judgment by his treatment of the child without a father: "That is the one who repulses the orphan" (107:2). Against that background the prohibition here is not a general call to kindness but a specific correction of a social habit. The orphan in Mecca was a person whose estate could be managed, used and diminished by those closest to him, and the verse addresses the men who did it. The Meccan context matters because the command was given before there was a community to enforce it. A reader who supposes that such an instruction depends on institutions has misread the setting; it was addressed to individuals in a city where orphans had no protection at all.

**The Verified Promise to the One Who Cares**

The positive counterpart of this prohibition is recorded in the most authoritative collection. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī preserves the Prophet's saying: "I and the caretaker of an orphan will be like this in Paradise," and he joined his index and middle fingers with a small space between them. The gesture is the content of the report. Nearness in Paradise is measured by the nearness of two fingers, and the person placed beside the Prophet is not a martyr or a scholar but a man who looks after a child who has lost his father. The report sets the standard for the conduct this verse protects. The prohibition against overwhelming the orphan is the minimum; the promise describes the maximum, and the distance between them is the range in which the Muslim household is expected to operate. The verified promise is what makes the command sustainable. A reader asked to spend time and attention on a child with no return in view needs to know that the return exists, and the tradition supplies it without qualification. The promise is the support the command requires.

**Manner Before Property**

The verse forbids overpowering, which is broader than theft. A man can leave an orphan's property intact and still overwhelm him — with contempt, with refusal, with the tone used at the door, with the sense that he is a burden. The Qur'an legislates the property separately and in detail, twice, with the same wording and the same narrow exception for management that improves the estate (6:152; 17:34). This verse legislates the manner. The distinction is important, because the property can be audited and the manner cannot. Only the person doing it knows whether the orphan in his house is treated as a guest or as a cost. The verse reaches that knowledge, and it reaches it with a verb of force rather than a rule of accounting. Manner comes before property, and the ordering is deliberate. A reader who gives money to an orphan and then speaks to him harshly has not fulfilled the command; the verse puts the treatment first because the treatment is what the child experiences. The sequence is the instruction.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:10

> nor repulse the beggar

**Tanhar: The Sharp Rebuff**

The verb prohibited here is *nahara*, which denotes driving someone away with severity. It is stronger than refusing. A refusal can be polite; a *nahr* is a rebuke, a raised voice, a gesture of dismissal. The verse therefore does not command that every request be granted. It commands that no requester be driven off. The distinction is precise and practical, because it addresses the part of the transaction that costs nothing. A man may have nothing to give; he always has a manner in which to say so. The prohibition targets the manner, and it does so with a verb that describes an act rather than a feeling. Nobody is required to feel warm toward a persistent beggar; everyone is required not to snap at him. The sharp rebuff is a single word, and the word describes a tone rather than an action. A reader who has turned someone away with a clipped sentence has performed exactly what is forbidden, and the specificity of the term is what makes the prohibition applicable to ordinary speech.

**The Second Reading: The Seeker of Knowledge**

Those who read *sāʾil* as the questioner about religion find support in the chapter's own conclusion, which commands the proclamation of God's blessings. A revelation that ends by requiring its messenger to speak publicly about what he has been given cannot intend to silence those who come asking about it. The two verses frame a single duty: speak of the blessing, and do not drive away the one who asks. Between them lies the whole task of conveying a message. The Prophet's own practice as recorded in the tradition is consistent with this reading, since he answered questioners at length and rebuked those who turned people away. The wider reading therefore does not displace the narrower one; it extends the same prohibition into the area where the chapter's final command operates.

**Manner Is Regulated Where Amount Is Not**

The Qur'an legislates the manner of giving with the same seriousness it applies to the amount, and the two sets of rules point in the same direction. Charity followed by reminders of generosity or by hurtful words is compared to a barren rock with a film of soil that a heavy rain washes bare, leaving nothing behind (2:264). The verse that states the positive case ties the reward to the absence of exactly that behaviour: those "who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah and do not follow their charity with reminders of their generosity or hurtful words—they will get their reward from their Lord" (2:262). Both verses concern transactions in which something is actually given. This chapter's command concerns the case in which nothing is given at all, and it holds the giver to the same standard. A man with nothing in his hand is still forbidden the sharp word. The prohibition is therefore not a lesser form of the charity rules but their extension into the only situation where a poor man can be tested, which is the situation he is in most of the time.

## Sūrah al-Ḍuḥā 93:11

> And proclaim the blessings of your Lord

**Ḥaddith: Say It Out Loud**

The final command uses a verb of speech. *Ḥaddith* comes from the root of narrating and recounting, and it denotes telling something to others rather than acknowledging it privately. The command is therefore not a call to inner gratitude but to public statement. The distinction matters. A man can be grateful in silence and remain, so far as anyone knows, ungrateful; the verse requires the gratitude to be audible. The object of the verb is the blessing of your Lord — singular, definite, and comprehensive. Whatever the specific favour, it is to be recounted. The chapter that began with a taunt ends with an instruction to speak, and the instruction is the answer to the taunt: what God has done is not to be hidden. Saying it out loud is the act the verse requires, and the requirement is unusual. A reader who has been grateful privately has not yet done what is asked; the gratitude is to be declared. The chapter ends on a public act, and the publicity is the point.

**Proclamation as the Form of Gratitude**

The Qur'an treats gratitude as an act with words in it. The promise attached to it is explicit: "If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful, surely My punishment is severe" (14:7). The alternative to gratitude is not mere silence but ingratitude, and ingratitude has a named form elsewhere in the Book: "They are aware of Allah's favours, but still deny them. And most of them are ˹truly˺ ungrateful" (16:83). Awareness without acknowledgment is the state the verse condemns, and proclamation is the act that breaks it. Sulaymān's response to a marvel placed before him supplies the model: "This is by the grace of my Lord to test me whether I am grateful or ungrateful" (27:40). The test is passed by saying so out loud. Proclamation is the form gratitude takes here, and the choice tells the reader something about the chapter's purpose. A private feeling would have required no command; a public statement requires courage and serves others. The verse asks for the harder form because the harder form is the useful one.

**The Command Against Concealment**

Speaking about a blessing is the reverse of a failure the Qur'an condemns in strong terms. Those "who hide the clear proofs and guidance that We have revealed— after We made it clear for humanity in the Book—will be condemned by Allah and ˹all˺ those who condemn" (2:159). The covenant taken from the people of the Scripture required them "to make it known to people and not hide it", and the indictment that follows is that they traded it for a fleeting gain (3:187). Against that background the command in this verse is not a mild encouragement to be positive. It belongs to a category of duty: what has been given from God is to be declared, and concealment is a breach. For the Prophet, whose blessing was a revelation, the duty was absolute; for anyone else, it applies in proportion to what has been received.

**The Chapter Turns Outward at the End**

The structure of the sūrah makes the final command the completion of the consolation rather than an appendix to it. The chapter opened with the Prophet accused of being abandoned and moved through three reminders of what he had been given. A consolation could have ended there, with the assurance complete and the case closed. Instead it ends with an instruction to speak about the gifts. The reason is that a private consolation does not answer a public taunt. The claim that God had left him was made in the open, and it is answered in the open by a man recounting what God has done for him. The proclamation is the public record of the private assurance, and it is the form in which the answer reaches the people who raised the question.

**Proclaiming the Blessing Without Displaying the Self**

There is an apparent tension between this command and the Qur'an's preference for hidden charity, and resolving it clarifies both. The verse on giving states plainly that "to give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you" (2:271), and the condemnation of reminders and hurtful words in 2:264 targets the giver who needs an audience. The proclamation commanded here is of a different object. What is to be recounted is the blessing of your Lord — what God has done — not the generosity of the speaker. A man who says that God sheltered him when he was an orphan has named a favour; a man who says how much he has given has named himself. The distinction is fine in practice and decisive in principle, and the chapter keeps it by attaching the command to God's name rather than to the speaker's. Proclamation of this kind increases gratitude in the speaker and knowledge in the hearer, and it costs neither party anything. Display of the self does the opposite, and it is what the charity rules exist to prevent.
