# Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt (Chapter 79) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:1

> By those ˹angels˺ stripping out ˹evil souls˺ harshly

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sūrah Opens on the Night-Shift of the Unseen**

The new sūrah begins with an oath, and its swearing stands in the unseen: *wan-nāziʿāti gharqā* — by those who wrest out violently, plunging deep. The reader is pulled instantly into the machinery that works every minute in every land while men sleep: the departure of souls. The name of the sūrah is taken from this word — Nāziʿāt — and it announces in its first breath that the following chapter will deal with the great transitions: the taking out of the world and the calling back into it.

Where the earlier oath-sūrahs swore by visible courses — winds dispatched in succession — this sūrah swears by operative workers: beings who perform their task upon the sons of Adam at the rate of every passing hour. An oath is an attestation before the court: God summons witnesses to vouch for the truth He is about to state. The witnesses raised here are those who cannot be refuted by any hearer — because they are already, at the moment of hearing, attending upon somebody's last breath.

**Gharqā: The Forceful Extraction**

The manner-word is chosen with brutal precision: *gharqā* — plungingly, with submerging violence, the way a diver plunges deep to drag up what has sunk and clings. The extraction described, on the dominant reading of the commentators — preserved in the tafsīrs of al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī — is that of the souls of the deniers and the wicked: they're pulled out in violence because they do not wish to come out, bound as they are to the clay and to the years of accumulated selfhood. The soul faces, at its removal, the very accounting it spent a lifetime denying; its reluctance is the reluctance of the condemned brought to the summons.

The continuation-passages of the Book draw the same scene's agony for us with fearsome directness: *“He reigns supreme over all of His creation, and sends recording-angels, watching over you. When death comes to any of you, Our angels take their soul, never neglecting this duty”* (6:61); and harsher still: *“Then how ˹horrible˺ will it be when the angels take their souls, beating their faces and backs!”* (47:27). The gharqā-oath names in one word what those verses depict in full.

**The Party-Scenes Already on the Record**

The individual case, the Book personalizes to the dauntless listener: *“Say: Your soul will be taken by the Angel of Death, who is in charge of you. Then to your Lord you will ˹all˺ be returned”* (32:11). Every soul has its taker commissioned against it; the extraction-teams of this oath are not a poetic unknown but a standing civil service of the unseen. That the taking is guaranteed is the oath's sobriety — for what God swears by exists; oaths are sworn upon what is.

The first oath therefore stands faith-ward: live in reverence of the minute. Whether a man presently believes or disbelieves, his extractors are pre-commissioned; his body's loosening-day is entered in some register of theirs; and the fierceness of the ripping will be proportioned by the file the organ of the self has been making during the pulse-years. The oath plants the reader at his own death's threshold in advance and instructs him: something is already coming for your soul on assignment; the only variable still under your authorship is the manner of its welcome.

**The Alternative Reading: Stars Swept Out of Their Courses**

The classical ledger preserves a second reading across these oath-lines, and this first line carries it as well as the rest: angels or stars? — the extracted things, on the second track, are celestial bodies: stars plunging down their courses, wrenched out of their settings at the world's unmaking (per al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī's transmission of the astral interpretation). The vocabulary truly runs in both directions — *nazaʿa* applies to drawing bows, setting off things, forcing loose — and the early generations of readers abstained from closing a door on it.

The two early masters of the science of tafsīr each gave their posture toward the variance. Al-Ṭabarī judged the oath capable of covering everything that matches the described traits: God swears by the angels, the stars, whatever performs the style of work named — the oath's truth is distributed among all its witnesses. Al-Rāzī advised the disciplined epistemology: we cannot assert which is intended, but we can say that the readings are all possible — and he further offered a spiritual register, reading the whole passage as the stages of a heart's return to God from all besides Him. The oath-verses, on every recorded interpretation, certify the sworn content: the Day whose coming quakes all things.

**Why the Oath Itself Is Already a Proof**

Consider why an oath by soul-taking offers evidence for the resurrection it swears to. The extraction whose description appalls the sceptics is already empirical: men die every minute, in every city — the gharqā-process is currently operating, continuously, in all parts of the world, as verifiable to the sceptic as tomorrow. The oath-Verse directs the hearer's attention to the standing fact: the Master of this extraction-system, whose removal-team daily un-bodies thousands, is being asked whether He can perform a *return*-procedure compounded of the same elements. The questioner of the restoration is reminded that his doubt confronts a system He never questions — one that operates now, at industrial scale, without obstacle.

The oath's grammar — by those doing this work — also binds the coming Day to a working staff: the resurrection the sūrah is about to describe is not rhetorical metaphysics but logistics for a workforce whose résumé the oath has just read. Those so occupied nightly will be, at the blast, occupants of a renewed appointment. Sworn content follows: the quaking hour — but beforehand the declension of extraction must complete its pair: for the wicked, the wresting-out; for the opposite file — the word of extreme gentleness, reserved for the oath that comes next.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:2

> and those pulling out ˹good souls˺ gently

**Expanded Commentary**

**Nashṭ: The Silk-Draw of the Compliant Soul**

The counter-oath: *wan-nāshiṭāti nashṭā* — and by those who draw out smoothly, undraw the knot, pull with perfect ease. *Nashṭ*, from the root of unbinding and smooth-withdrawing — as one looses a tie, or as a bucket is drawn sweetly up from the well without grating on the stones — names the antithetic extraction to the preceding violence: the soul that *wants* its exit, that glides toward the summons it had longed for during life. The commentators' reading matches the first verse's: on one extraction, the souls of the defiant are wrested; on this one, the souls of the believing, loved ones of God are drawn free in the silk-manner described.

The pairing is now complete as jurisprudence: God didn't swear by one extraction-style but by both — the harsh and the easy. Two deaths, one species, sworn by in sequence; and it's the contrast that is the doctrine. The Day to which the oath testifies will measure out to every person exactly the outcome their extraction-previews: the manner of the soul's departure is the carbon copy of the manner of its judgment.

**Two Autopsies of the Same Hour**

Set beside the book's two death-scene panels. The removal-team at the bed of the defiant: *“When death comes to any of you, Our angels take their soul”* (6:61) — neutral idiom in the general case — with the horror-idiom for the specific: *beating their faces and backs* (47:27). And then the other panel, the one answering this verse's gentleness: *“those whose souls the angels take while they are virtuous, saying ˹to them˺: Peace be upon you! Enter Paradise for what you used to do”* (16:32).

Consider the furniture of the second scene: speaking, greeting, announcing — the extraction-team itself delivers the first good news: *Enter Paradise for what you used to do* — at the very moment the world believes a man is lost. The nashṭ-oath draws that entire prospect under its one manner-word: drawn out sweetly because greeted at the draw, released into the address of peace. The two bed-ends, across fifty years of acquaintance, looked identical from the ward's door; at the removal, they divide into the two verses of this oath, forever.

**Ease as the First Coin of the Reward**

The tradition has long lingered over the consolation of this ease-verse, for it pictures the reward's arriving at the earliest possible instant: not in the garden first — the garden does not open to view until later — but at the very moment of departure. The ease is the first payment instalment. The pulled-out soul discovers already, in the manner of its drawing, which file it belongs to, before ever seeing the plain or the books: the gentleness of extraction is personalized pre-notification.

And from the living side, the verse draws its own prescription: work toward your draw. The believer-reading tradition at the sūrah instructs: the manner of the extraction is authored across across the manner of the life — acts, repentance, integrity of the covenant — every day contributing a fibre to what will become either slipperiness toward the Lord of mercy, or barbs and root-lock in the clay. Your soul's contact-surface with this world is being polished or hooked, by you, at each decision; and the engineers who will work at its removal are summoned then by the file, not by the body.

**The Knight-Manner and the Courteous Host**

The two oaths also deserve their scene-scale: the same Book that shows the beating-of-faces extraction elsewhere (47:27) shows the courteous escort of another file (16:32: greeted, informed) — and the imagery carries a lesson the traditions never tired of repeating: your reception at the removal is dressed to the colour of your purpose. God's dispatch packages arrive labelled according to the receiving-file: anger-attendants noor mercy-attendants, matched-sets from the same host's staff. The moral weight of house-keeping the Book assigns to the extraction-scene: as though the denier's whole life of making his soul unwelcome to God were rehearsed in the violence of its removal, and the believer's life of making his soul longed-for by its Lord rehearsed in the drawing's ease.

**How the Living Are Meant to Hear the Oath-Pair**

For the street-sceptics of Mecca the paired oaths carried a forensic pointedness: their assemblies jesting about bones and decay would be standing inside one of these two verses when the jesting season expired — because, empirically, they were all headed for a bed in which one of the two manners of extraction would be performed upon them. Joining in the sarcasm was therefore a form of docketing: each sneer weighed its owner's own extraction toward *gharqā*. The oath-pair operates under exactly the same logic as the whole sūrah: since the two styles must come, one is to be avoided and one is to be prepared for.

And it rehearses the later verse of the same chapter, at the end of the great two-file division (79:37-41): transgressor and preferred-world on one side; awed-of-the-Standing, restrained-of-desire on the other. The patient who has read the chapter to its end returns to the oath-pair and sees it pre-stated in figures: v1 corresponds to the first file, v2 to the second — the oaths already sorting mankind before the sorting is named.

**After Death, the Sea of Missions**

The oath-chain continues upward from the deathbed to the deep traffic: the next verse swears by the gliders, swimmers through the courses of the unseen on God's errands — the extraction-teams being only one visible node in a workforce that fills the space between heaven and earth with motion.

The swearing is compassionate in what it quietly states: the unseen is *not empty*. The sceptic's conception got the region above the roof of the sky in perfect idleness; God replies by oaths over a full workforce at full speed: drawing, gliding, racing, managing. Death-methods were merely the first offices named; the traffic continues, five oaths deep, until the chain arrives at the world's own managers — and then at last delivers, in one burst, the sworn content for which all this witness-bringing was performed.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:3

> and those gliding ˹through heavens˺ swiftly

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Oath Lifts From the Deathbed to the Deep**

After the two death-extractions, the chain of witnesses moves into the open between-worlds: *was-sābiḥāti sabḥā* — and by those who glide, swimming smoothly. *Sabḥ* is swimming/gliding — movement through a yielding medium with serene swiftness. On the dominant reading, the gliders are the angels in their ceaseless translocation between heaven and earth on God's assignments; on the secondary track the same word images the great stars swimming the courses of the sky. Both readings preserve the same higher meaning of this oath, as the coming sections will show: the world is *not static*; obedience is in *motion*; the invisible and visible agencies of God are *travelling* at all hours on the decree of the Monarch the sceptics were asked about.

The placing is deliberate: from the bedsides of dying men the view lifts through what they could never see — the hosts crossing the abyss above them — while the dying and their watchers imagined the region stood idle and void. God's oath declares the space above the roofs to be thoroughfare; the sūrah's transit-vocabulary covers the whole traffic of decree from the beds to the heights.

**Angel-Errands: The Thoroughfare of Command**

On the angel-reading, *sabḥ* paints the purity of their movement: gliding without wading — no resistance in the medium, because obedience has made them frictionless. The Book describes a similar saturation of the space-between with mission-body: *“He sends down the angels with revelation by His command to whoever He wills of His servants”* (16:2) — down-links, up-links, escorts, recorders, examiner-squads at grave-side, bodyguard-units at the faithful, extraction-teams at the beds — the content of the heavenly working-day assembled from verses across the Book.

Their glide-speed is itself the signature of their engineering: instant compliance — stated for the collectors of the Fire also: angels *“who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded”* (66:6). There is no packet-lag in the heavenly workforce: command and completion lie so close the movement looks like swimming. The oath swears by this: whatever offices your case requires, nothing has been delayed in deployment — not your warders, not your sustenance-couriers, not, at the appointed hours, your extractors.

**The Star-Track Alternative: The Silent Swimming of the Sky**

On the second recorded reading of the oath, the swimmers are the suns and stars themselves, gliding in their assigned paths — verses in perfect consonance with the Book's elsewhere-declared portrait of celestial traffic: *“Each is travelling in an orbit of their own”* (36:40). The sky, on that reading, is a sea: billions of great lights swimming without collision, without revolt from their lanes, in courses that have not once quitted the schedule.

Which puts the oath-work of the two tracks on the same doctrinal line: the celestial bodies and the angels are both obedient travellers in God's courses — one track luminous, one track personal — and either is sufficient proof for the style of oath given: the cosmos is *run*, not random; the movers move within a sovereignty so exact that its traffic never collides. Whether the listener's imagination reads angels or reads stars, the outcome is identical: *the tracks are held; the Manager is up; the plan is current.*

**Sabḥ and Tasbīḥ: Swimming as Glorification in Motion**

The root-family of the oath-word carries a second native meaning — tasbīḥ: declaring God's glory above all defect — and Arabic literature hears the resonance without prompting. Set the two senses together: the heavenly obedients *glide* and their gliding is itself *glorification*: continuous motion as continuous worship. The oath's vocabulary is stacked: the same letters that describe the stroke across the deep name the doxology of the heights — God's agents' movement being worship performed as orbit.

The lesson for the human hearer: movement can be worship, or it can be drift. Man was given locomotion precisely that he might convert it to the travel of the gliders: to pray is to swim through the hours toward the next appointed station; to repent is to *glide back* against the current the world's tides push with; the whole soul-language of the Qur'an's road-imagery — path to the Lord — borrows this same waterway the oath names. The believer's *traffic* is to be coded like the angels': swimming, doxological, on-assignment, on-file.

**A Space Reported Full, Against the Sceptic's Vacuum**

Look at what the oath sequence has now assembled by verse three: extraction-teams at every bedside, a glider-traffic filling the middle-region — and, in the next two oaths, racers and managers above them. The heavenly count in the transcribed register is so far: two crews at individual deathpoints, one migration-host between earth and heaven, and two higher offices still to come. The Book's miracle is that all this activity the scholar can neither photograph nor falsify remains permanently verifiable at the only point every auditor eventually visits: his own removal.

The warnings continuing below the oath-chain are, therefore, not delegate-of-gossip about a far-off possibility but the landscaping of a route whose traffic is presently underway: the sūrah does not ask you to believe in a world you cannot reach; it tells you in five strokes that the insisting-about-traffic is the realism — and the sceptic's *empty-heaven* postulate is the unverifiable one. The blast of verse six will shake all visible reality; the shaking itself will be performed by workers already swimming this route — the rājifah and the rādifah are *appointments in the logs* of the very crews the oaths are naming.

**And Then the Fourth Lane: The Front of the Race**

From serene swimming the oath-census rises to velocity: the front-runners, outstrippers — motion over again, but now in its highest gear: the kind that never allows a command to age for even a second before it is routing downward. The gliders' composure becomes the racers' urgency — for some decrees in God's kingdom cannot wait, and there exists a staff built explicitly for no-delay.

The reader of these oaths is meant to be gaining altitude with each stroke: the first verse placed him at his own deathbed; the third has him out in deep space where commands move as swimmers, and the next places him at the starting-line where the commands themselves are despatched. By the fifth oath the logistics-tour will be complete; and then, in order, the delivery: *yawma tarjufu r-rājifah*: the sworn truth, worth summoning all this heavenly testimony to certify, arrives at verse six as the quaking Blast: the sūrah's thesis, certified by the whole invisible civil service.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:4

> and those taking the lead vigorously

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sābiqāt: The Front-Runners of the Decree**

Fourth oath: *fas-sābiqāti sabqā* — and by those who race out ahead, outstripping. *Sabq* is sprinting-leadership: the competition's front edge, where the race is decided. On the leading celestial-personnel readings, these are the angels who outrun all rivals in carrying out what must not wait — those commissioned with the fastest leg of the government of affairs. On the astral track, the image stands for the celestial runners keeping their racing-courses day by day, outrunning one another in the lanes the Book swears by elsewhere (77:1's sent-forths; 36:40's travel-in-orbit).

The neat symmetry across the oath-verses should not be missed: v3 gliding; v4 racing: the archive of the heavens' traffic catalogues every velocity from the smooth to the desperate. God's accommodating recitation teaches us by a ladder: there are deliveries that glide serenely on a schedule — the rain-accounts of the season — and there are deliveries that must arrive *without an instant's slipping* — the soul at the attack of the extraction; the command at the moment the Fire is called to attention; the rising-blast when the Hour strikes.

**The Race of Command-Execution**

Reports, lessons and the picturesque hadith of the tradition always insist on the angelic rule-of-the-first-second: commands from above do not queue. The attribute-compilation elsewhere from the Fire's commissioners is total: *“who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded”* (66:6). Which makes the racing-image comprehensible as character, not merely physics: the front-runners' sprint is the shape of *unhesitated obedience*, lag-freedom as moral perfection now freighted as oath-testimony for the truth of God's Day.

The oaths of racing are thus *conduct*-lessons embedded in a cosmic register: the staff of the heavens models the response-traits the human part of the kingdom is summoned to imitate: no rear-guard in God's service; no putting the command in diagonal stack behind the delights. The Qur'an's motival threnody *sābiqū* — run the race — appears across its calling-pages: the human counterpart of the celestial lane.

**Putting Mankind in the Same Vocabulary**

The Book is explicit that the racing-frame intends human lanes too: about repentance it says *“And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth”* (3:133); about destiny it specifies the station-name: *“and the foremost ˹in faith˺ will be the foremost ˹in Paradise˺. They are the ones nearest ˹to Allah˺”* (56:10-11). Front-running — al-sābiqūn — is the eternal class-name of the elect-of-the-elect: the racing-vocabulary passed down from the oaths into the market of human choice.

So the fourth oath stands pastoral as well as evidential: there is competition in God's economy, and its unit is *speed at the good*. The markets of Mecca, eloquent in racing to profit, and the councils, expert in racing to obtain favours of the strong, are addressed by a sūrah whose fourth line tells them the race that has God as finishing-post is decided by who takes the *lead now*, before the trumpet's burst halts entries. The sābiqāt of verse 4 are the licensed pacesetters of the only heat whose prize is nearness.

**Velocity as the Oath's Evidential Weight**

Attend also to how the multi-speed constancy of the oaths makes the witness irrefutable: you are being sworn — by motion at every rate — that everything in God's creation is operational at every scale simultaneously. Scepticism rests on the picture of heaven as a clergy in recess; the reply is motion-photography: extraction, gliding, racing — personnel swarming the paths between the command and its object at every order of speed. The tedious waiting of the abstract deist is answered by the velocity-table of the actual Governor.

The great race of the coming Day is prepared inside this oath, too: the second blast — the rādifah — is the Day's own out-stripping leg, the one conjunction of events squarely of racing character, pursuing the first with no interval long enough to evade. *Tatbaʿuhā* (79:7) — followed closely — will be the Blast-side analogue of *sabqā*: the same commitment of the same kingdom to never be late at the one appointment the whole sūrah exists to swear to.

**Racing Toward the File's Own Frontiers**

The commentators, working within the two-track system of these oaths, also transmit the spiritual reading in which the front-runners are spirits pressing forth from every lodged thing toward light: al-Rāzī's sequence of the five oaths as five stations of the returning heart — wrested from attachment to what-besides-God, drawn with zeal, then gliding in the ocean of sovereignty, then outstripping the ranks of travellers, and finally reaching the office of conducting affairs — the frontier where human degrees graze the angelic. The tradition tolerates the isharī reading alongside the literal ones since both serve the swearing.

The two tracks are, finally, not contradictory but perspective-related: the outer reading of this oath gives the skies and the squads; the inner reading gives the soul’s own itinerary — and the sūrah's thesis-need, which is immediate in verse 6, is served by both. Whether the soul imagines Gabriel's hosts or its own ascent, the message is convergent: *things are arriving ahead* — and the direction of everything in God's kingdom is toward the one rendezvous of the end the sūrah has sworn its five oaths to deliver.

**One Office Remains From the Register**

The catalogue of the unseen workforce is nearly complete: one desk remains — the most secret of the five: the *conductors of the affair* — the management-verse, where the oath-census discloses that nothing in creation — not rainfall, not kingdom-rise, not a wedding or a funeral — is unattended at the governing level. All that the four first verses described in transit and exertion reduces to a single administrative verb in the fifth: *mudabbirāt*.

At the end of the fifth oath, explicitly with the verse that completes the census of your governors, God delivers the sworn content with an arabic jawbreaking-grammar: *yawma tarjufu* — the Day the Quaking-One quakes. The whole labour-catalogue was the preface; the promulgation is the trembling of one word: the rājifah — which the next verse announces, and with it the sūrah's own movement out of oath and into warning.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:5

> and those conducting affairs ˹obediently˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mudabbirāt: The Management of Everything**

The census of witnesses completes itself at the highest desk: *fal-mudabbirāti amrā* — and by those who conduct the affair, administer the business, run the command. *Tadbīr* is management from behind — the directing-hand that sits at the stern of a matter and steers it. The mudabbirāt are the angels of governance: the delegated officers through whom the minute-to-minute administration of heaven-and-earth actually operates — rain, wind, provision, the rise-dates and fall-dates of kings, the wombs turning, and the extraction-ticketing of a hundred thousand souls a day.

Their existence places the final weight on the oath-chain: the sceptic's world had imagined itself self-running; the five oaths answer in sequence: beings are extracting your souls, beings are gliding on God's errands, beings are racing ahead with His decrees — and beings are *conducting the affair*. The cosmos is an administered regime from the viewer's own bedposts upward. The fourth and fifth verses stand to the administration-side of the same invisible personnel; the first two stood to the fate-side at the bedside; and the fifth seals it: *all of it is run* — with the departments extending from one's own pulse to the furthest star.

**The Tadbīr-Vocabulary Runs Through the Whole Book**

The sūrah's own term is established doctrine, stamped repeatedly across the scripture: *“Surely your Lord is Allah Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne, conducting every affair”* (10:3). The creation-side and management-side thereby belong to one Builder's continuous tenure: the world was not abandoned after its six-Days architecture. And for the bureaucratic circuit that moves between the senses: *“He conducts every affair from the heavens to the earth, then it all ascends to Him on a Day whose length is a thousand years by your counting”* (32:5).

What exactly is being *conducted*? The word *amr* — affair/command/decree — covers, in the Book's usage, both the legislative and executive directions of things: the command coming down and the case-file going up. The fifth verse's oath gathers the total traffic: decision-descent and report-ascent. The angels of governance circulate the whole turn: from above, water and command; from below, deeds and souls — the eternal accounting of the world's course run between the two vertical directions.

**Delegated Service With the Hand Above the Hands**

The management-verse settles a theological worry that attends all doctrine of delegated angels: does delegation mean consigning the world's keys out of the Throne's reach? The Book's own statement kills the thought within its own verses: the conductors act by His command (10:3: *conducting every affair*, said of God Himself with the tadbīr-verb); the agents are officers under ceaseless review. Their conduct is His provision of conduct for Himself — He is the Conducting One through every officer He appoints, exactly as He is the Reckoner through every scribe He stations at each soul's shoulder.

The believer draws the deepest comfort of the chapter from precisely this mathematic: the management exists yet remains unitary. For the troubled of the world, besieged by the sense of headlessness in things, the fifth verse is the direct antidote: *no* moment of yours is unadministered; every affairs-file is before an officer, and behind every officer the Conducting One is present and looking at His own board of officers. Even ruses are conducted and contained: *“they schemed, but Allah also schemed. And Allah is the best of schemers”* (3:54's echo at 8:30's similar) — the enemy's own conspiracies are staffed files in God's management.

**Decree-Traffic in Its Grandest Example**

The tradition furnishes the office's most luminous night: *“That night the angels and the ˹holy˺ spirit descend, by the permission of their Lord, for every ˹decreed˺ matter”* (97:4) — the Night of Decree — when the year's management-files are routed. The fifth oath thus points to a calendar of operations: the management is on schedule — annually-itemized for the world, continuously-running at the bedsides of the dying, instantaneously-executed at the commands that cannot slip.

Which gives the oath's sworn object, coming at verse six, its full awe: even *the greatest administrative file of all* — the end-time of the created system — is on someone's desk, dated, appointed, known. The world's closure is a managed term: not a crack that chaos opens when sovereigns look away, but a scheduled management-decision inside the same ledger that holds tonight's rainfall and tomorrow's bread. The whole terrified vision of the world's ending amounts, in the fifth oath's register, to the arrival of one especially great file at its marked hour, in a regime that has never missed any of them.

**Five Oaths Reviewed as One Sworn Syllabus**

Consider now what the complete census has asserted. Verse one: the extraction-teams exist and the harsh extraction is real. Verse two: the gentle extraction is real, in the identical machinery. Verses three and four: the transitional traffic between heaven and earth is multicursanted, multi-velocity. Verse five: there exists an entire management-layer conducting every affair. Each sworn statement attested by the standing-work of the personnel named; and the whole catalogue of certainties functions as the witness-bench summoned for the main case.

That case — lying exactly at the next verse — could not have a stronger preparation: you accepted Me at every office — watcher, extractor, courier, racer, manager — accept Me at the last office, which is the most precisely scheduled of them all: the Day the Quaking-One quakes. The swearing, in other words, was the taking of the hearer through every aspect of his life which already *runs by God's staff* without his objection, before demanding the one belief about the future whose staff he has not yet seen work: the resurrection-rising.

**The Exclamation of the Oath-Chain's End**

The verse closes with the customary exclamation-mark of the translators — deserved this time above most verses of the register: the chain that began at a deathbed stands, by verse five, presiding over the management of all affairs, so that when God's promise of the quaking Day comes, it comes on the authority of a catalogue of heavenly competences the hearer himself already believes and benefits from daily. The enjambment-terror of the wait is over.

The sūrah now shifts its key: no more oaths, no more by-lines of the witness-bench — the case for which the bench was summoned. *Yawma tarjufu r-rājifah*: the day the quaking thing quakes — the sworn content itself, for the delivery of which the entire invisible civil service was called as witness — arriving with the same whiplash that all God's sworn contents in this Book land with: long oath, short sentence. The following verse delivers it in six Arabic words.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:6

> ˹Consider˺ the Day ˹when˺ the quaking Blast will come to pass

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sworn Content Lands: Ar-Rājifah**

After five oaths of the unseen workforce, the swearing resolves into its content: *yawma tarjufu r-rājifah* — the Day the Quaking One quakes. The construction is typical of the Book: the certificate is brief, the oath long. All five intakes — extraction-teams, gliders, racers, managers — were called against a single proposition: that a certain hour of shaking will come, and with a certain follower hard behind it. The witnesses' labour resumes, in that sentence, the reader's whole attention: this is what the census was called to certify.

The form of the content carries a second elegance: the thing sworn to is stated in event-grammar, not date-grammar. God does not say *when* in the calendar; He says *what* — the day the Quaker quakes. The whole subsequent combat between the sūrah and the street over *when* (vv42-44) is already prefigured in this reticence: the Day is certified as a mode — quaking — and deliberately kept silent as an appointment-descriptor, because its date belongs to the Keeper only.

**Rājifah: The Name From the Mode of Destruction**

The event is named, as this Book's Day-names go, after its operational effect: *rājifah*, from *rajfa* — violent shaking, shuddering, convulsion — the participle-entity: the Quaker. The first shake is the one that ends the world's standing order: the Firm one, the certain one — quivering of the entire created horizon. In the heavenly linguistics of the hour, being-shaken comes before every re-form: the quaking of the world is the first movement of its re-assembly — and so it is given first place here, as the very event-name of the ending-stage.

The parallel map of the two blasts fills in the content: *“The Trumpet will be blown and all those in the heavens and all those on the earth will fall dead, except those Allah wills ˹to spare˺. Then it will be blown again and they will rise up at once, looking on”* (39:68). The rājifah stands on the same line as the first blast — the shake whose reach ends every presently-beating heart and extant fixture, at the appointed instant — excepting only such as God wills, before the second.

**Six Words as Cinema**

In the Arabic, the astonished economy of the verse is worth accounting: a temporal clause, a verb, a subject — the Day / quakes / the Quaker. The reader's film runs: the earth under its present population suddenly *unreliable*; the furniture of habit shaken as a drunk shakes a rug; hearts leaping to throats in one synchronous global beat (79:8's close detail). The teaching of style: the greatest event in creation's biography fits grammatically into a fragment the size of a farewell — and events of that scale resist larger syntax precisely because no amplification adds anything.

The scene-word, al-rājifah, further tells the trajectory of the sūrah downward: this verse shakes the world; the verse after it describes the follower-blast; verses 8-9 move in upon the hearts and eyes — the quake is first cosmological, then finally cardiac. The camera-work of these six verses (6-11) is one continuous zoom: universe → particular organ (heart) → particular organ (eye) → then back to the Street, where the sneer of the undisturbed is still warm: vv10-11.

**Oath Answer to the Sceptic's Arsenal**

The oaths, whose benches sat through verses 1-5, are now actually *used*: the sworn content is precisely where the audience's scepticism was directed — the raising, the Day, the grave's return. God swears His own testimony over the exact item the Meccan street jested at — and He swore, pointedly, *by His own administered system*: managers and racers whose every operation the listener benefits from daily. If the management is trusted for the water-supply, the management is crowned for the deadline.

The street had long asked for dramatic proof: then here is the programme — a death-shake, then an assembling-blast — delivered as a formal notice, certified by the workers on shift. What the sworn content lacks, visibly, is only persuasion-work: after the certificate, no demonstration-argument follows — the sūrah lets the certificate stand, moves into detail-mode (hearts, eyes), and then quotes the scoff (vv10-11) — the Book alternating, as ever, its two registers.

**The Quake Selects and Preserves**

The blast-language in the parallel includes the mercy-clause: *except those Allah wills* — and thereby contains the believer's answer to the panic reading of the rājifah: the shaking does not mean the unqualified termination of the story, only of the *form*. A quaking sown on purposes of God preserves what He wills in it: His own are shaken, but with the way out and the way up already pre-set — as the shaken prophet-ships of history (Noah's, Jonah's) found the shake to be vehicle, not sentence.

Devotionally, the verse installs an endurance-trait: since the blast must come, the strand of the believer's meditation runs — do not locate your storey in the fixed-things of this floor. Everything shakeable is slated to be shaken; and the wise hand is the one that lets go of the banisters of the world before the world asserts its shakeability. Those who fear the Standing have, thereby, the least re-arrangement to do in that day, because their security-lines run to the Quaker Himself, and not to anything quakeable.

**The Follower on Its Heels**

The sworn content continues into the next verse without syntactical pause: a follower riding immediately at the rājifah's heels — the second blast, which the parallel lays out as rise-and-stand (39:68). The pair (rājifah + rādifah) compose the complete sequence: death-shake, then assembly-shake — Book language for the total re-set of history: between them, no empire, no speech, no securities; after them, nothing but the court assembled.

A verse-idle count shows the scale: sworn content consumed in barely a verse and a half; then the camera to the heart (79:8): because the point of all this cosmology is moral — something happens *inside people* when the Quaker quakes — so the sūrah spends its next nomination on what the quaking shakes loose in the hearts of the despising spectators of the street, the same hearts that make light of the summons all their street-lives long.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:7

> followed by a second Blast

**Expanded Commentary**

**Tatbaʿuhā r-Rādifah: A Rider on the First Blast's Heels**

The sworn content is a two-stroke engine: *tatbaʿuhā r-rādifah* — followed by the Following-One. The noun carries its velocity in its root: *radf* is riding directly behind, pursuit without measurable daylight between; the *rādifah* is the Following-One by title: the second blast that pursues the first with the closeness of a pursuing rider. Just as the first is named by what it does (quakes), the follower is named by *when it comes* — immediately afterward: not delayed, not negotiated; riding at the rājifah's heels.

The profile in the parallel is again the programme: *“Then it will be blown again and they will rise up at once, looking on ˹in anticipation˺”* (39:68). Between the two blasts the created order experiences the stillness of the ending — the interval between Quake and Following — until, with the Follower, the assembly suddenly stands looking back at the plain: the collected humanity recreated as witnesses of the appointment-keeping of God.

**The Interval: The World's Silent Midwife**

The reports of the tradition reserve for the interval a particular dread. One transmission counts the span between the two blasts as forty — with the transmitter himself leaving the measure unspecified (days, months, years — the ambiguity belongs to the received text) [UNVERIFIED]. Not its length but its character is the lesson: the universe lies motionless between the end-blast and the assembly-blast — nobody, nothing, no speech, no sound but the awaiting; and then — regenerated as if by a second genesis — mankind rises out of its graves as the earth splits, rushing: *“˹Beware of˺ the Day the earth will split open, letting them rush forth. That will be an easy gathering for Us”* (50:44).

The interval's function, whatever its units, is manifested from the structure itself: a wholly dead interval *demonstrates* the hanging nature of everything: after death, no further work, no development beyond the blast that quakes — the pre-eternity and the post-eternity touch each other across a silence as wide as God commands. The reader of the interval prepares himself by becoming acquainted, now, with the silence-side: he is taught by the structure to expect nothing outside God's hand, since the world's second birth comes, after the pause, from the same power that quaked its first.

**The Labour-Division of the Two Blasts**

The two names assign the division of work simply: the rājifah — Shaker — ends the process of the world; the rādifah — Pursuer — ends the refuge-state of the grave. The second is precisely the blast the sceptics' whole itinerary of objection blocked at: their thesis ran that once the clay had swallowed a man, no process could reverse the setting-in of decay. The follower's blast stands on record as the opposite force — grave-release — staged one pursuit-rider behind the earth-shaking decree: between quaking and following, the whole career of death, from decomposition on, is traversed and reversed.

God's naming already shows the two as *one operation in two strokes*: rādifah pursues rājifah as the pull follows the push. The sceptic's imagined difficulty — reassembly after distance of time and condition — is thereby stamped as a single recorded task of one Governor, from quake to stand. *“It will only take one Blast, then at once they will all be brought before Us”* (36:53) is said from the same family of sūrahs about the second stroke's speed: not a sequence of evolutions but a single convulsive instant delivering mankind back to the summons.

**Why Two Blasts Rather Than One**

The tradition's reflection also asks why the event was not one blast encompassing both functions. The traditional wisdom gives one line of answer: the staging portrays *deliberate reuse*: God is not discoverable in a single world-shake but in the chain quake→silence→following; the resembled process of the interval — like the sleep of 39:42's daily sequence (take, hold, release) — teaches, once again, the same custody at every level of the world: personal nights and cosmic nights both run take-return-take-return (39:42). The two-blast schema embeds proof in structure: the God of the nightly double-taking is the God of the final double-blow.

The theatrical punctuation is also exact: the rādifah possesses the effect-quality the first lacks. After the first — no audience; after the second — hearers emerging upward, eyes open (79:8-9), hearts pounding. The second blast's proper designation as *Pursuer* tells last-minute arrivals its character: there is a noise coming that nothing can duck, because it is made specifically *to find the missing*: the dead are pursued into the assembly by the rider-blast across any distance death could ever establish.

**The Streets' Jest Answered in Two Noms**

The twin blasted names answer, by this point, the Meccan sneer of the following verses comprehensively: *will we be restored to our former state?* — the sūrah has already, three verses earlier, named the two-part instrument: rājifah to end the state, rādifah to restore it — the restoration archived in the name of the second blast itself. In God's diction, the answer to the sneer of *can that really be?* is the name of the officer assigned to the job.

And in the believer's ear, the twin designations double as daily comfort-certificates: the pursuer of the resurrection cannot be outrun; its appointment is already in the logs (79:5's tadbīr-census). Among the racing offices (79:4's sābiqāt), the rādifah is the race's own finishing-gun: command arrives, the dead rise, and the world's second-shift opens. The machinery of vv1-5 that was questioned in the oath now turns to the blasts as its assigned course; and having been shown the sweep, the sūrah descends into the cardiac detail of the spectators of the terror: the dauntless street, suddenly at the moment of re-seeing — the heart, trembling.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:8

> ˹The deniers’˺ hearts on that Day will be trembling ˹in horror˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qulūbun Wājifah: The First Organ Shot of the Day**

The sworn content begins to deliver its details at the level of the insured organ: *qulūbun yawmaʾidhin wājifah* — hearts on that Day fluttering. *Wajafa* is the palpitation of extreme fright: the heart punching its walls in the attempt to escape the ribcage, running on fumes of adrenaline with no direction of escape left. After five angel-motion oaths and two cosmic blasts, the camera of the sūrah lands finally on the human heart — and finds exactly what its owner would neither have predicted nor priced in during the street-season: the organ that argued so loudly in Mecca's squares quivering against its own bones.

The choice of heart is precision-work. In the Book's anthropology, the qalb is the *office of takdhīb*: the deciding-chamber of denial, where the reports were processed in jest, where excuses were manufactured, where the day's logic organized itself away from the fact. Now, with the fact quaked and following, that same organ is shown as the first fall-out site of the realized appointment: denial's seat is terror's first landing-strip. The verse is the completion of the justice performed inside each single body by the risen blast.

**The Reversal of Street-Physiognomy**

Read against the street scenes with which this sūrah-family is full, the physiognomic detail is pitiless: these are the men whose hearts were so calm that they could jest about decayed bones (79:10-11); whose hearts were easy enough at the hearing of the recitation that they could go home to their wine; whose hearts felt certain enough of their supremacy to persecute the weak in public. Now the sequence of the day opens, and the first catalogue-item of the condemned is *their fluttering hearts* — the inner climate so altered that their old easy pulse is unimaginable.

The parallel account of their condition is delivered with satirical precision from the same sūrah-clan: emerging from their graves, *“The disbelievers will cry: This is a difficult Day!”* (54:8) — felt-difficulty marking the disbelievers' own proclamation of the exact thing they used to dismiss as a care-fantasy of the believers. The wajf of the condemned is thus not merely their emotion but their *testimony*: the heart bears witness by its knocking that the earlier confidence had been hubris; the flutter is the truth-at-last arriving home in its own organ.

**Trembling Identified as the Day's Early Verdict-Form**

What's instructive is that the trembling is described as *present at the standing* of the Day — before the questioning, before the records, before the verdicts proper. The effect of the blast alone — the mere occurrence of the sworn event they had dismissed — already reduces the interior states of the people of falsehood to flutter. Punishment begins, in this Book's repeated doctrine, at realization: knowing-and-quivering are already a form of doom before any sentence is addressed.

The corollary in the believer-ward direction is necessarily installed: the option exists of anticipating the trembling, in salvific form, while it is still available in its salutary currency. Divine-fear now is self-chosen flutter — respect, remorse, wajf in the night-prayers, the dread of the Standing — and the Book consistently values that form of awakening-heart as one of the salvation-routes (79:40). The organ's hard choice discovers itself in vv8 vs v40: the hearts that quake by unwilling fright there, or by awe before the Lord here.

**The Heart as the First Fig Leaf Removed**

Considering what the trembling *discloses*: denial had always depended on exterior confidence's masking of interior unease — one sneered with friends because the group-heart kept warm. But at the day of the Persuer, both the group and its warmth are gone: every fluttering heart is an *isolated* witness — the door of the self closed upon its own pounding — the swarm-identity of denials dissolved into individual chests, one by one, in rows. The emergence emptied of companions converts social confidence into solitary sein-unto-God.

The swarm at the graves matters at this point: *“With eyes downcast, they will come forth from the graves as if they were swarming locusts”* (54:7) — locusts, scattered and multiplied beyond counting, none supporting any other — the swarm's form now stressing each isolate's solitude amid the many. The Day's party is full of people and utterly unconsoling: racing, pressing mass without one shared heart. In that condition, the fluttering starts independently in each — heart by heart by heart.

**The Wajf and the Christian-Forgiveness Minutes**

Of the heart's flutter the tradition also observes: hearts, in the end, are God's indicators — the organ, practically physical, that reports what the court administrates. One can describe the trembling by its sequence, remembering that at the tables of the recitation in Mecca these hearts had multiple chances to make their wajf early, since each family-patented sūrah told them of their ending — the warning-books' point was specifically *to make the heart flutter at the right time* — quaking it in reading so that it need not quake at the final. The recitation was therefore a flutter-service; it ran in the evenings; attendance was open.

Now the blast shakes; where flutter had been offered as repentance it arrives as fate. The flutter-state comes to the same hearts; only the *quality* has shifted on schedule — from the trembling of choice into the trembling of sentence. Hearts on that Day, flutter: design; then; and the difference across the divide is only whether the flutter was accepted Sooner or forced upon them *then*.

**The Eyes Are Next Among the Disclosures**

The sūrah's description moves on one organ further downward to the outer gate of the same confession: after the heart of the denier quivers, his eye is downcast — the confession climbing from the interior chamber into the countenance. The next verse's *downcast* is the outer sign of this trembling: once the heart has started its flutter, the face cannot hold the old masterful profile: it sinks into the humbled angle that the Book terms khushūʿ.

The same matching of organ-on-organ links the verses as paired description — as if to say: the old human city had an inside (heart) and an outside (face), and at the blast both sides betray the same denial-era: the inside pumping terror in darkness, the outside hooded in shame — the personnel of the old sabotage laid bare by the recitation of their own organs. The next verse gives the outside image its own line — because the record demands that their eyes, which saw the whole God-evidence (vv27-33), announce that they see too late.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:9

> with their eyes downcast

**Expanded Commentary**

**Abṣāruhā Khāshiʿah: The Gaze Returns to Its Owners**

The disclosure continues with the exterior organ: *abṣāruhā khāshiʿah* — their eyes downcast. *Khushūʿ* is the humbled, lowered surrender of the bearing: the sag of all self-importance out of the bearing — and it is here attributed to the eye itself, the feature in which, more than any other, mastery resides in the human face. The eyes that scanned construction-wordily in the days of argument, that rolled up in the mimicry of the worshipper's posture of prayer, that appraised the world as a shop-window — those same eyes now drop.

The downcast state is not offered merely as poetic colour: after the quake and the pursuer, with hearts already undergoing their flutter, the countenance's lowering is the announced exterior of the leading-class-now-led. The parallel emergence scene gives the face its swarm-context: *“With eyes downcast, they will come forth from the graves as if they were swarming locusts”* (54:7) — emergence plus downcast eyes — the same pairing the sūrah gives here: hearts trembling (79:8), eyes downcast (79:9). The body has turned into the record.

**What the Eye Is Confessing By Its Lowering**

The tradition's jurisprudence of punishments-by-instrument reads the face as readable biography: the eyes were used in service of the disbelieving programme — scanning the signs of the world without their gratitude-glance, scanning warning-verses with the classifier's smirk, scanning weaker men with the persecution's cold estimate. At the Day, the progressive justice of the limbs gives the organ its echo-state: eyes that did not learn to lower with reverence in the world, learn it now at the Day, in shame — the lowered look finally accomplished by the same eyes, at the cost of everything.

Which rests the alternative explicitly alongside: the posture's prayer-version is available in the interval; prostration's *khushūʿ* is the free anticipation of this compelled one. In prayer, the eyes *lower themselves* — the face bowed as worship, while worship is still answerable. The sister-sūrah's disbursement of the inverse theme (they would not bow) sharpened the formula: those invited to bow and refusing will find their spines, their eyes and their profiles carrying the Day's own curvature by compulsion later.

**The Selective Waiving of Shame**

Feeling too the specific completeness of these verses as the deniers' panel: the sūrah gives them two organ-panels — hearts, eyes — and the hearts and eyes in the Book are the twin fronts where the audit of the world is first visibly registered. The panels are assigned to *the deniers'* public roll — considered, for the God's-wary thus open, the alternative panels (witness the parallel: *“On that Day ˹other˺ faces will be glowing with bliss”* (88:8) — the face of the blessed, opposite-attributed) — the trembling and lowering stands thus in precise contraposition to the radiant-looking files of the saved.

The phrase *abṣāruhā* (their eyes) with the feminine offer action also serves the grammatical note — eyes of the hearts, an attribution with an older interpretation-report: each heart's eye; the two organs share the root of seeing. There is intertextual humor in the completed portrait: *(their)* eyes, feminine-plural marked as metaphorical vision-organs too, and in some of the commentatorial opinion *abṣāruha* = the very notable phrase's ownership spreading over the heart's eye as much as over the face's — the lowered, humbled vision across every faculty of recognition the condemned possessed.

**The Body Serves the Proceedings as Exhibit A**

In the old life, the body's features betrayed nothing: the denier was a moving screen of confidence — his chin, his profile, his appraising look were the props of his power over the small. In the new proceedings, his own organs depose against him: the heart knocks out the sentence before it is issued; the eyes prosecute by their lowered angle, unable to hold level with the great plain where everyone stands in rows, awaited. The sūrah family's legislated justice — your actions testifying through your limbs — is on vivid display (41:20: ears, eyes and skins as witnesses; 36:65 hands speak).

And the particular instrument-role of the eye is hugely fit: the Day is a seeing-Day — *Hellfire will be displayed for all to see* at v36 — their eyes that used to flick wilfully past the evidence will now be forced to keep down under the pressure of all that they at last *see*. From the eyes of the condemned, averted in shame, to the eyes of all, lifted at the Fire unveiled: sight's two movements in the sūrah are its frame's two movements — humiliation of the false eyes, display of the real.

**Scenes of Khushūʿ in the Same Register**

The downcast state is a word-property carried consistently by the Day-panels of the Book: the emergence-scene in Qamar's downcast-locusts (54:7), here at the day of the rājifah, and later in the same family — the disbeliever's face downcast under *dhilla*, humiliation, the sūrahs of punishment use the same face-word consistently as the signature of the vanquished posture. The depiction is consistent also because it is the *inverse* of its worship-form: God had trained, in prayer and Qur'an-listening, the same face-state as peace and closeness — there is no new punishment-day invention but the old posture re-issued by sentence.

Devotionally then: learn the khushūʿ of the eye early, says the verse to its listeners, since *your own eyes will learn it by compulsion* at the hour of the swarm; and the believer has his pre-rehearsal institutionalized five-prostrations a day: the humble face is the glory-face of the worshipper. In that framework, worship is literally the daily training of the face for either Day: the face that lowers itself voluntarily all its prayer-life is untroubled at the collect plain because its khushūʿ is already its habit, not its exposure.

**The Camera Pulls Back to the Sneer**

From the heart's flutter and the eye's fall, the sūrah abruptly cuts back, at verse 10, to the old calender — the street-scene — and plays, verbatim, the deniers' sneer from the easy days: *Will we really be restored...after decayed bones?* The control-chaos of the alternation is deliberate: verses 6-9 have run the Day's arrival at organ-resolution; verse 10 returns the audience to the present, where the same men, organs still dignified by daylight, are doing the sneer whose answer God has already filmed.

The contrast lives on a rubric: when the heart flutters (79:8), the tongue is done sneering; and when the eyes drop (79:9), the wit is done mocking. The verses that follow the cut-away let the sneer hang in the ear against the two panels just shown — as if the sūrah had prefaced the joke with its punchline-punishment in advance. Verse 10 and onward take up the sneer's text — and verses 13-14 will answer it with the strongest monosyllable of the chapter.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:10

> ˹But now˺ they ask ˹mockingly˺, “Will we really be restored to our former state

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Cuts Back to the Street**

Having shown the physiognomy of the Day at the verges of the blast — hearts fluttering, eyes dropping — the sūrah jolts its audience back into the arrogant present tense: *yaqūlūna a-innā la-mardūdūna fī l-ḥāfirah* — they say: Shall we really be returned to our former state? The mock-question is quoted verbatim. In terms of cinema: the defeat-documentary is played first (vv6-9); the sneer is then replayed from the street-days when the sneer was still *possible* — and by that ordering, the sneer is delivered pre-falsified.

The quoted construction *a-innā la-* carries its own smirk-mark in grammatical shape: a question whose form already performs astonished incredulity — *is it the case that we...?* — with the solemn *la-* emphatic embedded in it, worn inside-out as parody of solemnity. The doubters mimic the cadence of a serious question while filling it with the sneer; the mimicry has become so famous to the tradition that the words are usually described by the commentators with the diagnostic tag: said *in mockery and denial*.

**Ḥāfirah: The Word for the First Trampled Ground**

The street's own picked word adds its colour: *al-ḥāfirah* — the former state, the first place — though the philological depth is worth pausing over: from *ḥafara*, to dig or trample, *ḥāfirah* is the ground of the hoof-track, the first trodden dance-floor, the original rut. To ask *shall we be returned to the ḥāfirah* is to use, in sneering, a very precise picture: being forced back down the same track one's life has already stamped-out, re-gathered to the first hollow of footprints as livestock are brought back round a paddock.

The lexical layer makes the ridicule all the more damning — and all the more durable in the recitation: the witty term they styled themselves with is *better than they knew*: restoration to the hoof-track of origin is exactly scriptural resurrection, described in self-mockery by its own objectors. The word-knowledge the Book trains consists precisely in finding God's truths inside the enemy's own vocabulary: the ḥāfirah they sneer at is precisely clay-of-origin returned-to, Adam-dust retrieved.

**The Bones Follow as Exhibit in the Next Breath**

The sneer is in two instalments, the first the forehead-exclamation of incredulity — shall we? — and the next verse completes it as its ground in the flesh: *after we have become decayed bones?* The two questions are the classical objection's unimproved form across the whole Book: the standard sceptical exhibit of the desert-clubs: *“When we are reduced to bones and ashes, will we really be raised as a new creation?”* (17:49), and the older complaint paraphrase: *“Who will revive ˹these˺ bones when they have decayed?”* (36:78).

The argument, as the commentators classify it, is an error of *extent* masquerading as an argument of principle: the sneerers are not saying God cannot — they're saying the distance between states *looks too far*: current dust and statistic-ending bones, against some projected reconstituted gathering; their doubt trained on the *span*, not on the Governor's assets. Which is precisely why the Qur'an's response-vocabulary keeps pointing back to the first manufacture: the one who produced the first span produces the second with less demand on His capabilities than the first quotidianly enjoys (36:79 follows).

**Certified Mockery: Why the Sūrah Preserves the Sneer**

It is a peculiar sublimity of the Book's method that it prints the sneers of its opponents with the exactness of sworn evidence — right down to their grammar's micro-particles of parody. The street's line is preserved, attributed to its native venue, and allowed to remain in the eternal playlist — because every preserved sneer now functions already as *their own signed record of dereliction*, lodged in the same recitation that warned them. At the Day the sneer re-sounds in the archive as witness for the prosecution.

Any future claimant, too, of Islam's manifestos is served notice by the format: the mockery-files of the first audience were not censored because God's order of battle tolerates quoting them; answer-truth never needs muzzle. If the sneer is preserved for age after age, the preservation is, in itself, a deterrent: it shows every later sneer-writer that his witticisms stand a good chance of entering that same record unedited — placed permanently next to the organs at their final flutter-and-drop (79:8-9).

**The Prophet's Ear as the First Recipient of the Sneer**

The quoted sneer went, in the first days of revelation, to the Prophet — Meccan groups asking each other aloud, at hearing-distance from his lodging, intending the words to reach. The sūrah's venue of material is the ordinary recollection of the preceding chapter-family: the street, the councils, the taunting neighbours — because the mission lived the material, and the ear of the sūrah's first address holder was lined with these phrases, repeated at night, stylized as evening amusement.

The pastoral consecutive of printing it: consolation and strategy both. Consolation: God stores faithfully what His servants field for His sake, and writes it in His record; strategy: the sneer is answered neither by retaliation nor by muttering refusal but by the court's own count: verse 13 — the one-blast token — delivered without commentary, with the simplest physics of all: *it is one call — and they are all on the surface.* The sneer's long tail of argument is refuted in half a verse. Verses 13-14 will arrive precisely there.

**The Sarcastic Footnote: A Return Would Be a Loss for Us**

Inside the same sneer-episode, at verse 12, the assembled mockers already add their punchline: *if true, it's our loss* — meaning with heavy double-edge both: (a) *the so-called Day is, if real, bad economics for us* — and (b) *the claim is a losing proposition*. The second-sense jeer on *loss* (khasārah) carries the whole club's bet that resurrection is nonsense — the word the Book has otherwise reserved for the doom-registers. The sūrah's next verses will put the same noun under the Fire's own accounting, where loss is spelled in plumbing terms.

The reader now completes the sneer's closing section by adding its kicker, then receives the answer. The alternation deserves one account-final note: *it is itself the doctrinal model*: description of the Day's physiology first (79:6-9), then the sneer's print (79:10-12), then the retort's brevity (79:13-14): denier-questions outlasted by one-word-fact answers; seven centuries of ink expended in debate on their sneer; the answer still, in the sūrah's economics, one blast — *zajrah*.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:11

> even after we have been reduced to decayed bones?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Objection's Exhibit A Comes Out**

The sneer completes its second instalment: *a-idhā kunnā ʿiẓāman nakhirah* — What, after we have become decayed bones? The quotation's exhibit is held aloft: bones — *ʿiẓām*, the skeletal remnant — decayed, *nakhirah*, rotted through to hollowness. The street's argument in its whole form now stands on the record: the extremity of decomposition is presented as self-evident veto of any return. The visible end of every man in this desert-climate of burial was bleached fragments; the club holds those fragments up and asks: *them? Again?*

The strategic instinct of the sneer is worth classifying: it selects for itself the *latest* possible state of matter — not sickness, not death's first days, not the still-recognizable corpse — but bone hollowed by years. By faking the argument at its longest-span imagining, the objectors purport to have exhausted God's difficulty-scale: if at that state no return is conceptually purchasable, then at no state it is. The Book's parallel transcripts of the very same exhibit — from different mouths in the same Book — confirm the classical status of the objection: *“When we are reduced to bones and ashes, will we really be raised as a new creation?”* (17:49) is said by one assembly; *“Who will revive ˹these˺ bones when they have decayed?”* (36:78) by another.

**Nakhirah: The Rottenness Word, Chosen for Maximum Distance**

The adjective deserves its own slow-read: *nakhirah*, from *nakhira* — decayed, hollowed, rotten to crumbling — the bone when its marrow is dust and its cortex porous, the bone that collapses at the touch into powder. It is the extremity-word, the complete-undoing word: the bone-objects have passed out of any resemblance to structure; and the objection claims: structure was the pre-condition of life; structure is gone; therefore life must be gone.

One heraldic point of the philology: the noun for the process-*decay* here is itself from a root denoting hollowing — a cavity-word. The longer the bone lies, the more it converts from *mass* to *shape of former mass*; and it is the condition of *shape-of-former* that is philosophically significant: the sceptic's exhibit contains, etched in the form of the bone itself, the entire memory of the living structure — as though the bones were themselves a prepared *mould*, a pattern kept in storage at the Recorder's loan-library (the word *registered* at 78:29).

**First-Creation Is the Standing Reply-Template**

The Book's own rebuttal-vocabulary breathes the same air on each occasion: *“Say: They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being”* (36:79); and again, in the street-exchange: when they ask *who will bring us back?*, the commanded answer is — *“The One Who created you the first time”* (17:51). The argument's rules are repeated unchanged: always answer the second-production question with the record of the first.

The form of the template is beautifully stable: production-from-nothing/semin-drop is the established case; production from dust-of-structure is the easier disputand-case. Any recording-laboratory that hauled a first human out of nonbeing, and raised entire living worlds round him, has already performed the harder part of the current problem (79:27's comparison will make precisely this point with the sky). The singularity-answer of verse 13 arrives after at exactly the scale of the operation: not process-duration but one shout.

**Decay Is Not Disorder: The Decomposition Doctrine**

The worth of the sneer-creative inversion is the following: the sneer treats decay as the *collapse* of God's system — matter falling where administration stops — while in fact decay is a perfectly staffed process: decomposition cycles, mineral returns, in-grown orders of the grave-soil — all in the tadbīr-table of affairs (79:5). Administered processes run the dismantling; and if the dismantling needed administration — every bacterium moving under mandate — the re-assembly sits equally in the budget of the very same workforce.

Were decay the end of governance, the sceptic's case would hold — there'd be no witness left. But witness: the bone still *exists* — thus kept in existence; the dust *remembers* its lay; the grave's chemistry obeys its codes. The fact that anything at all is still present *to be discussed* is itself the survival of custody. Nothing in the world has ever been allowed to vanish past God's audit-system; what remains — bone, dust, memory, file — is sufficient for the Register that keeps count of every atom to raise the former pattern to the summons.

**Every Sceptic Is a Prior Counter-Example**

These questions, in the Book's most elementary apologetics, answer themselves by their askers: each sceptic *is* the already-raised from a more complete nothing than any grave. The Book states it directly in the address of the same class: *“Was there not a time ˹before˺ when man was nothing ˹worth mentioning˺?”* (76:1). Between unmentionable-nothing and decayed bone there lies more distance in the sceptic's favor than between bone and re-emanation; the first gap bridged daily — every birth says it — without a single protest from the streets.

And it is not only first-clothing between nothing and Adam — each living questioner is, at the hour of his sneer, the *latest standing model of resurrection-powered flesh*: his body was rebuilt cell by cell on molecular pathways he never willed. The one who could convert menstrual liquid into a fresh Adam (77:20-22, the exact parallel passages) is not rationally to be pressed about dust; the sneer's question-begging lives only by the speaker consigning his own case-history into forgetfulness the moment he pronounces it.

**The Sneer Moves to Its Punchline**

The two-component sneer complete — incredulous *shall we?* plus the bones-exhibit — the assemblies capped it off with their failing-jest: *that would be a losing return* — delivered with the merchant-smirk that closed Meccan wit. Verse 12 holds the punchline; the next verse answers by contract, on loss's own ledger-form.

And the positioning is worth noting: the sneer is interrupted by God, mid-anecdote, with a two-word palindrome of the two parties' states — verses 13 and 14 — one shout given and one rising announced — after which the sūrah proceeds to its long worked example of Moses, without returning to finish the street-conversation. The sneer was not worth correcting as an argument; it was worth answering as an event. The sūrah puts one Blast against their two verses, then proceeds to the record of the biggest denier in history to show the Day itself *has precedents*.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:12

> Adding, “Then such a return would be a ˹total˺ loss ˹for us˺!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Punchline of the Assembly**

The sneer closes with its own joke: *qālū tilka idhan karratun khāsirah* — they said: that, then, would be a losing comeback! The words are chosen from the vocabulary of commerce: *karrah* is the return, the comeback-expedition, the second sally; *khāsirah* the losing one. Their jest is the economics-banter of the club: *a second run of us — at these raw-material prices (decayed bones) — is bad business, that's all.* The Qur'an keeps the punchline preserved with the clownish flavor still on it: the twice-dead mocking revival's price-scheme.

The commentators have read the sentence in both its sarcastic registers: as jest about absurdity — *that would surely be a sorry business* — and, graver, as part-defiant acknowledgement: *since the revival you promise holds only our exposure, our revival would, if anything, be our loss* — an early confession that they knew their file does not survive audit (the reading that al-Qurṭubī and others consider: their own conditional knowledge of being ruined if it were true). The two readings are compatible and, taken together, yield the full psychology: they joked truthfully — without intending the truth.

**Words That Come True on Their Speakers' Heads**

The sneer-formula displays a phenomenon the Book follows with watchful irony across all its ages: the denier pronouncing, without knowing it, his own exact verdict. On their lips *khāsirah* was dismissive wit; in the reality of the Day it is a ledger-heading — one of the accountancy-terms of the afterlife-file — *loss*, *losers*, *clear loss* (22:11): *“losing this world and the Hereafter. That is ˹truly˺ the clearest loss”* — the real khāsirah-line defined by God in His own register of sentences.

The Book, ever vigilant for such boomerang-utterances, does not fail to record them: phrases the street-mockers flung upward as jokes land as statutes; the backfire of the words returns upon their very authors with the sureness of the orbit the oath-ocean of stars keeps (79:3). Verse 12's sneer spends centuries as recited text read aloud at prayers; and with every recitation the word khāsirah tolls again with its Day-verdict sound against its first wit-users: yes — *a losing return*: the karrah will be khāsirah for *them*; only their punctuation was wrong.

**Karrah: The Comeback as a Campaign Analogy**

Now consider the word *karrah* more carefully: it images the Day as a *second campaign* — a sally, a cavalry-wheel returning to the field after withdrawal: the military-sporting figure natural to a culture of raid-and-return. The sneerer picks the most life-affirming word possible for the notion of Return — comeback as endeavour — and dismisses it only on the economics: not the coming-back (which his imagination *could* picture) but the *value* of it at costed prices of dusty bone. The sneer therefore contains its own naive visualization: the street *could* imagine an after-thrust; they simply valued it as a bad venture.

The exchange shows the whole texture of their epistemology: valuation-set categories before revelation-set ones; merchant-accounting imposed on the Kingdom. The Book's pervasive critique of the merchant-mind's misapplied currency lies into play at once: the cash-register they consult for the resurrection is not provided in the Kingdom's specie; God's own ledger-terms are nakhl-trade only for earthly trade (35:29's *hope-dealing that does not bust*), while His resurrection-office has never needed any cost-posting at their register at all.

**The Merchant Mind Reading Eternity as Trade**

The Meccan clubs, who lived by caravans and credit, had one reckoning-table: profit and loss — and it became a supremely easy mental step for them to read *everything*, afterlife-claims too, as venture-pricing. The sarcasm tilts at the Messenger precisely along this corporate grain: *Muhammad is pitching a post-death comeback-investment; our accountants say it doesn't pay.* The spiritual illness tracked here is not even belief-capacity; it is *metric-confusion*: instruments calibrated for goods-and-services applied to the Kingdom.

God's own answer-traditions stop such metric-fusions at the root. Loss really exists in the Book's ledger — but its registers are elsewhere: *“Shall we inform you of who will lose the most deeds? ˹They are˺ those whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life, while they think they are doing good!”* (18:103-104) — loss as *spent labors that evaporate*; and *the clear loss* in the waverer's file (22:11). A comeback's khāsirah status is a verdict assignable only after the files compile — and their files were precisely the ones coming up short at the audit (78:27-29, the parallel).

**Judged From the Day-Files the Sneer Pre-Falsifies Itself**

The sneer's own verse-number system (vv10-12) completes the pre-falsification begun by vv6-9's preview of their hearts and eyes: the assembly that sarcastically coins *karrah khāsirah* has already been shown, narrative-first, on the receiving end of that exact expression at Day-minutes: multiplied, downcast, trembling. The joke is run after the subject was already filmed for them; the sūrah never hides its reversals; its reversals are planted in advance of the very mockery.

Deep preaching hides in the sequence: the sneer-board will someday be sitting before the real karrah, and the same men will be making the silent ledger entry personally, not as satire — *total loss for us* — since the assembled re-telling in vv8-9 is their file's own preview. The recitation, by the mere art of order reversal, warns all future sneerers: *word carefully; your jokes are admissible.*

**The Answer Is a Noun-Phrase and a Particle**

Verse 13, with this sneer still in the ear, delivers the divine counter-statement: no argument, no itemized correction, no essay — the whole rebuttal is a three-element sentence meanwhile: *only* one single blast — which the next verse extends by four words. The majestic economy of the answer performs the sneer's deflation more efficiently than any paragraph could: vast question elaborated in two verses (vv10-11), punchline in verse 12 — and the answer, bracketed, one shout plus emergence (vv13-14). The sneering crowd receives its verdict in the form most like itself: compressed wit — only true.

The replay is fully assembled now: sneers at ready, answer at hand — and the sūrah will not pause over the Jehu-dog-eaten assembly: it goes on to the archive of the great prior tyrant and his warner, since the street needed not argument only but precedent-anatomy. The one-shout clause of verse 13 is the pivot-line of the entire chapter, its smallest verse on top of which the whole weight of the sūrah is balanced — a study in how much truth can be transported in the fewest possible syllables.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:13

> But indeed, it will take only one ˹mighty˺ Blast

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Counter-Sentence Is Five Words Long**

The divine retort arrives: *fa-innamā hiya zajratun wāḥidah* — But it is only a single Blast. *Fa-innamā* is the corrective-exhaustive particle — *but no, this is all that it is* — a formula that caps the dispute with the answering register's total of one word-token. The sentence's full design: one long sneer-file (three verses) against one five-word assertion (this verse). God's courtroom style of reply to the wit of the club is itself doctrine in literary form: the sneer was verbose; the truth is brief; and the brevity carries the same unarguability as a verdict.

The answering mode is not explanation but *count*: the one number they had not thought to doubt — the unit, one — entering as the full answer to the logistics-objection. For what is at stake in the sneers was unit-counting: how many dead, over how many ages, across how many lands? The sneer's engine was *mass*; the reply strips the mass away entirely and whispers *wāḥidah*: one. The logistics of the Day of Decision do not scale with the crowds of the dead; they scale with the commands of the Commander, and the commands come in single unites.

**Zajrah: The Shout That Herds History**

The chosen noun has animal-management in its blood: *zajrah*, from *zajara* — to drive, rebuke, scatter with a shout, to startle into motion with a cry — is the word of the herdsman's yell across a herd at dusk; also the word of the single commanding shout that sends a whole field into ordered movement. Not *ḥadīth*, not *qawl*, not pronouncement-esay: a *zajrah* — a shout without a speech, a sound-word carrying signal, not syntax. The Resurrection-Summons is imaged, by the choice of this noun, as one shepherd-call across the grave-fields of the entire species.

The image is at once lightening and alarming. Lightening: re-assembly as call-response, not engineering; the dead do not need re-building — they need *hearing*, and the shout reaches them. Alarming: a zajrah brooks no bargaining — the herd moves at the cry, bleat or no bleat. Both nuances stand in the sūrah's own voice: the believer hears his Lord's summon-love in the single shout; the denier hears the cry that tolerates no petition for adjournment. The answer of verses 13-14 is deliberately both: mercy of ease — dread of obligation.

**Wāḥidah: The Arithmetic That Dissolves the Objection**

Set the sneer-formula and the answer alongside: their sneer ran — how could countless rotten instances be reconstructed; the retort — a single summons. The mass never entered the spec. The whole objection apparatus of the sceptic's arithmetic (too many, too decomposed, too dispersed) is bypassed by a number that cannot be outnumbered: one blast, one call, one command — indivisible, unopposable, un-fatiguable against any quotient of dead. The dead's multitude is irrelevant to a summons uttered once and obeyed instantly by everything.

The arithmetic also echoes earlier demonstrations in the Book: *“It will only take one Blast, then at once they will all be brought before Us”* (36:53), an exact parallel; and the gathering's ease: *“That will be an easy gathering for Us”* (50:44). The tradition's whole mass-handling theme — chaos, numbers, distances of age and geography — dissolves into this single unit: God's instruction-set to the cosmos executes in one line; the season of fusion simply *happens*, as an assignment routed instantly by the speeding mudabbūrāt of vv3-5 whose pace the oaths have already certified.

**The Physics of a Zajrah-World**

Understand the text's physics: *fa-innamā hiya* — *it*, the unfinished affair pointing up from the sneers — *is nothing other than one blast*. The world-ending and world-building sequences are hereby disclosed as *not an operation*: no disassembly-crews of the old, no rebuild-factories of the new. Just *zajrah, wāḥidah*, shout once. That such economy of means should manage the total overturn of history is the Book's doctrine of godhead, stated in the immediate environment of this verse's twin: His command to a thing when He wills it is only to say *Be — and it is* (36:82, closing the same argument as 36:53 and 36:79).

The sceptical imagination of Mecca — schooled in desert praxis and merchant cost-accounts — could not resist cost-accounting even God's acts: *resurrection* sounded expensive. The verse makes the project free: no crew months, no material procurement — nothing but a decree at zero work-hours, and the grave-soil opens. Men count heavy work by expenditure of time and men; God's work-columns, by contrast, list only *kun*. The budget-line of the Last Day is a singleton.

**The Rhythm Said to Carry the Omen**

Rhetorically, the verse is the sharp center of the sūrah: it is simultaneously (a) answer to the sneer, (b) preview of the rādifah described two verses before (79:7's follower, named; this verse describes *its unit character*), (c) proof-text of the Day's ease for God, and (d) stylistic shock-device: the fifth-oath tension created across the opening suddenly discharged in five words. Recitation-conscious readers across the centuries have felt the verse land like the rājifah itself: short, total, irrefutable.

There is a further register in so short a verdict: it is memorable to the point of self-tolling. A verse of five words moves into the memory without effort — exactly as the oaths designed it — so that the recitation listener carries the chapter's rebuttal-of-sneers with him ready-armed into any room of talk: the bones? the dust? — one blast. The Book constructs, within its lush long evidence-passages, these instant-transfer sentences, portable by the weakest reader, deployable against the strongest sceptic; and the zajrah wāḥidah is one of its most recited examples.

**And Instantly the Surfaces**

The near-verse continues the single motion with the particle of instantaneity: *fa-idhā hum bis-sāhirah* — *and behold: they, at the surface* (79:14). Between blast and emergence there is no narrative gap; the grammar squeezes out any time-amount between summons and emergence: *fa-idhā* — and-behold — is the Book's chapter-crumbling connector for immediate succession. The shout is spoken; the shout arrives; and they stand on the surface of the world.

The pair (13-14) answers both wings of the sneer: *restored to former state?* — yes, by one call (13); *after decayed bones?* — yes, instantly at surface (14). Argument completed, refutation filed. The sūrah proceeds now — with the refutation secure — into its detailed worked-history of how one great messenger dealt with one great denier, and what happened to the denier: the story of Moses and Pharaoh, the ancient precedent of the same sneer played out to its full conclusion in time and tide — vv15-26.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:14

> and at once they will be above ground

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-Idhā Hum: And — Behold — They**

Between the blast and the surface there stands only the instantaneous-particle: *fa-idhā* — and then, behold; the so-and particle of surprise-and-swift-succession in Arabic, the same little camera-cut the Book uses when a thing follows at once on another. Nothing is narrated between: no struggle, no procession, no slow reconstruction. The shout sounds, and — *hum* — they: the pronoun of all the dead. Then the state, all at once: *bis-sāhirah* — aboveground, on the surface-level of the world.

The verse's three-unit composition is the Day's first logistics-report: pronoun, prepositional-state. You are to imagine the switch: graves filled with their one-time residents; shout; pronoun alone standing wherever the surface is. Gradualism is not Qur'anic eschatology: the Book will not extend the event to soothe the reader's picture-making. God does not render the scene slow-motion; He delivers it with the raw cut of because: *fa-idhā hum* — and — suddenly — they.

**Ṣāhirah: The Surface-Name, Two Registers Rich**

The noun *as-sāhirah* itself is attention-worthy: the open surface of the earth — the top-soil stratum, the land's flat face; related to *ṣaḥrāʾ* — the wide open plain — and carrying, in some of the transmitted comments, an overlay from the brother-word for wakefulness: suddenly they are *awn-the-surface*, as men flung up out of sleep to the surface of waking. The majority of the readers keep the first: the level face of the earth: the equalized meeting-ground.

Both whisper truth, both names belong to the event's signature: after the blast, the dead are on the *surface* of the ground that a few hours before was their *cover* (78:10's night-garment image lent across as motif) — and they are *awake*, clear of the grave's sleep as a man surfaces out of sleep into a morning. The two overlays support the sūrah's doctrine: the Day is not exotic mystery; it is the *surfacing* of everything that had been briefly on another leveling-form — graves, dust, sleep, cover — now reversed by God's command as instantly as the cover was lowered.

**Their Own Sneer-Word Gets Answered in Twin Vocabulary**

The surface-name's interlock with the sneerers' own hoof-track word is the chapter's most delicious lexical structure. They had said, sneering: *restored to our ḥāfirah* (79:10) — the *hoof-beaten track*, trampled ground, the rut. God answers, declarative: *bis-sāhirah* (79:14) — on the *surface-plain* — both words built on the ḥ-f/ṣ-ḥ image-families of trodden flat ground, with the two nouns functioning as word-twin: they sneered *to the track*; He asserts *to the flat* — and the echo better in Arabic ears than in any later translation: the mocked ḥāfirah is replaced with God's ṣāhirah, the exact *picture* flipped from sarcasm into fact.

The lexical justice deepens one more level: their sneer chose a word from the vocabulary of *digging* (ḥ-f-r), and God's assertive chose a root carrying *surface-and-sight* — from bury-dig to surface-sight; the two words, one digging down in ridicule, one surfacing in truth, are mirror-forms of exactly the same semantic field of ground: the sneer itself was execratively close to the fact, needing only the morpheme-flip of being true.

**The Verse and Its Many Ghosts: The Pouring-Forth Scenes**

The surface's emergence is described across the Book with a moving swarm of images that flesh out the pronoun *hum*. From the split earth: *“the Day the earth will split open, letting them rush forth”* (50:44) — rush=forth-from-split; with locust-simile: *“With eyes downcast, they will come forth from the graves as if they were swarming locusts, rushing towards the caller”* (54:7-8) — headed by the Caller, swarming by inevitability; with the second-blast name: *“then—behold!—they will rush from the graves to their Lord”* (36:51).

Bracket these emergence-vocabularies together: split-open, swarming, rushing, hurrying-*toward*, *before Us*, *to their Lord*: one thing made clear at several swirlings of idiom: emergence is (i) universally massed — the pronoun is crowds of the species — and (ii) pull-directed — the swarm moves *toward* the summons, not randomly: the Day's surface is a magnetized plain with the Caller at its hub. The sneerer's difficulty with re-assembly recurs as the supreme hope and dread of the scene: no dead are unaccounted; all arrive.

**Easy for Us: The Lord's Own Footnote**

The accompanying commentary that the Book itself appends to the split-earth scene is the sentence the sceptic's whole accountancy needed to hear: *“That will be an easy gathering for Us”* (50:44). Easy — *yasīr* — light, cheap, no strain on the Operator. The sceptic's cost-accounting returns invalid: the logistics the doubters found prohibitive register anywhere at the divine board? Elsewhere the Book's doxology-word is stronger still: *Your creation and your resurrection are but as a single soul* — the bookkeeping simplification that simply exposes how little mankind's magnitude matters inside His arithmetic.

God-launched tasks are also, by definition, already-equipped: the workforce certified at the oaths (79:1-5) is standing; the weather of the day (79:6's rājifah and follower-blast) is already named; the surface (sāhirah) is already mapped. What remains to be performed by God is the syllable: *zajrah wāḥidah* — a summons *once*, and the prior verse's instantaneous transfer has delivered the entire species to the surface. The logistics-problem on which the deniers raised their sneers collapses into the divine voice's pronunciation-time.

**The Scene Shifts, but the Registers Close**

The rebuttal of sneers is now formally complete: sneer (79:10), exhibit (79:11), jest-punch (79:12); one blast (79:13), one surface-emergence (79:14). God's own deliverance against the sneer needed two verses: a noun and a particle. The sūrah's next move is pedagogical: no concluding benediction on the answer — instead an immediate turn to the story-record, by God's voice to His Prophet: *Has the story of Moses reached you?* — a question that at once changes the sūrah's register from street-reply to archive-lecture.

The turn is an eloquent dismissal: the sneerers were not subsequently engaged — their objection was handled at its own ephemerality-level; what follows is for the persistent listener who asks for more than one-blast-syllogism: a full precedent from the human record, fully told, with characters, dialogue and end — how the greatest earthly sovereign sneered with a wit they could only envy, and what flooded after that sneer: water in this life, fire in the next. The Moses-block occupies the next twelve verses, and it begins with God's own question of memory — directly to His Messenger — about a call in a dark valley.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:15

> Has the story of Moses reached you ˹O Prophet˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hal Atāka: God's Question-Opening to His Messenger**

The sūrah, the sneers now answered, pivots: *hal atāka ḥadīthu mūsā* — has the story of Moses come to you? The particle *hal atāka* is a rhetorical address-form whose answer is already assured — it has indeed reached the Prophet; it is in fact being delivered at that very moment — and the form is beloved in the Book for opening scenes of huge consequence (its twin marking Sinai-scale events: has the story of the overwhelming Hour come, has the story of the honored guests come). The Prophet is being *seated* — gently interrogated as a hearer before being commissioned as conveyor — and the audience behind him, listening, is seated with him by the same question.

The tenderness of the mode deserves noting: God did not command *listen!*; God *asks*, has the report reached you — as one approaches a friend through what he loves. The relation opened by the question-form is one of tuition and intimacy: a story is being told, with a teacher's pleasure and a learner's seat — and this divine pedagogy, deployed between the Prophet and his Lord, is over-heard by the whole later congregation of readers who come to the sūrah's pupils' bench. The answer moves through the next twelve verses.

**Why Moses Now, After the Sneer?**

The sneer-scene just completed (vv10-14) demanded two modes of proof that argument could not provide: (a) *precedent* — this had happened before, to a man of God and a great tyrant, in the full view of recorded history; and (b) *working-out* — how the sneer-theme plays out when pursued to its final sentence: mockery at first, denial of signs, cries of pride over the multitudes, then seizure. The Moses-Pharaoh archive answers both: the Meccan sceptics could study, person for person, their own originals, and could measure how their sneers would read beside the famous words of the one in the Nile-valley.

The mapping is tabulated by the sūrah's later summary-verse: *“Surely in this is a lesson for whoever stands in awe”* (79:26) — the Pharaoh-file is a pedagogic assignment for the khashy-owner — and the figures match panel-wise: Moses as the prophet-model of the Meccan Messenger's own situation (a weak warner against a settled regime), Pharaoh as the ceiling of *ṭughyān*, supremacy at human maximum, sneer at professional grade. The two-man case was chosen because it contains *every* moral magnitude that the Meccan street's quarrel could ever produce, magnified.

**The Story as the Sūrah's Central Hinge**

Structurally the Moses episode (vv15-26) is the sūrah's pivot — the hinge on which the chapter's two great wings turn. Before it: oath-chain (1-5), blast-program (6-9), sneer-refuted (10-14). After it: the cosmic demonstration-tour (27-33), then the two estates at the Great Calamity (34-41), then the Hour-question (42-46). The story's placement is mediatorial: after the logic of resurrection's possibility is settled by one verse (the sajrah), the narrative *humanizes* it — here are the human costs and courses of that doctrine, as lived-out by two particular men in a valley and a court.

The sūrah's method here teaches the habit of the whole Book: propositional weight is followed by *rehearsed illustration*. God argues at parables' cost: case-study on actual files, not only proofs. The sneer was argued-for in five words; the sneer's biography in a Meccan club is narrated in twelve verses through the archive of Egypt; the slow way is chosen where instruction needs impersonation. Listeners convinced at v13 still needed to walk through Egypt, just as the believers' hearts must be trained on models even after their reasons are settled.

**Question-Grammar and the Ranks of the Telling**

The *hal atāka* formula performs an important etiquette too: the account is marked as *ḥadīth* — a report, a story-item — to be received at the level of historic received-news, not of myth-fabulation. The Book insists its story-genre: it gives annals, not legends; annals for teaching, not romance for wonder. The word ḥadīth used for its openers signals the recounted material's standing as transmitted reality, having reached the Prophet by revelation as a stable screed of the world's earlier traffic.

The question-form also sets the knowledge-hierarchy in plain view: the Prophet is told by asking — knowledge descending upon him through this gracious interrogative — while his auditors are, by the same text, installed in the eaves of the lesson. Fiction and allegory need no such framing of provenance; the question-form is itself an index of the verse's genre: God's recollection, released to His own messenger in question-shape, as pristine, verifiable, and address-form at a living seat — the pattern of the whole Meccan storytelling in the Book.

**A Prophet's Mission Mapped Onto Its Prototype**

For the Meccan Messenger, the personal-office of the story is plain: you have effects against supremacy — here is its anatomy-in-power. Each of the coming verse-details pre-addresses the Prophet's execution: the commissioning word *Go*; the charge *for he has transgressed*; the tender question *would you purify yourself*; the guide-to-your-Lord offer; the showing of the great sign; the predictable denial; the final seizure — the whole course of missionary-vs-tyrant experience pre-run in the record so that God's messenger would not meet any state-of-the-question unrehearsed.

The station-counsel of this storytelling is pressed elsewhere in the Book: the accounts of the earlier prophets are systematically presented as the Prophet's courage-course — each story a resting-stop of certainty for the warner's task. For the audience, the parallel instruction is: your own course after the sneer has an authorized carrier-route, pioneered by Moses before you, from the valley through Egypt's court to the sea's split and the tower's drowning; *their fire and your fire* — the book will even show you what the sneer-holders did when they were asking the same kind of sneering-questions about the afterlife, and how their story ended.

**Following the Question Into the Valley**

The story begins, with God's own pacing, at the first utterance: the valley of Ṭuwa — a named coordinate in the dark night of Midian — where a voice broke over a man tending sheep at the far side of the wilderness, and asked him to leave his shoes. The next verse is the beginning of the call; the verse after is the commissioning — *Go to Pharaoh* — and then the most courteous evangelism ever delivered to a tyrant enters the record (79:18-19), followed by the sign, the sneer, the pride-summation, and the seizure.

Anticipation is the question's real work: with *hal atāka*, the Prophet's whole attention — and through his, ours — is gathered around the teller. The sūrah has already advanced the answer to the sneer by one blast-sentence; now it will *run the experiment* with Pharaoh before the eyes of every Meccan doubter; and it begins at Moses' election-night as deliberately as a court-file is opened: named place, named time — God's voice. The next verse speaks the first line of the call.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:16

> His Lord called him in the sacred valley of Ṭuwa

**Expanded Commentary**

**Idh Nādāhu Rabbuhu: The Night the Silence Broke**

The narrative opens at genesis-moment: *idh nādāhu rabbuhu* — when his Lord called him. Moses, at this point of his own record, is a fugitive-shepherd in Midian: a decade gone from the palace of Egypt, a dead Egyptian overseer in his wake, children and flocks about him in the desert, his name in Egypt's wanted files permanently open. On this particular night, a man of no portfolio sees at the far side of the valley a fire. And a voice comes out of it.

The phrasing keeps the intimacy without extravagance: God *called him* — nādā, address by name, a caller calling a called — a direct address across the dark. Stories of prophecy in the Book keep this form faithfully: not gradual atmospheres of inspiration but a definable call at a definable time in a definable land — the origin of every prophet's mission is a *When*. The preposition is discreet: *his Lord* called — the possessive binding ruler to ruled in the most tender direction — before the commissioning verb arrives as *Go*, in the next verse.

**Bil-Wādi l-Muqaddasi Ṭuwā: A Coordinate of Holiness**

The location carries its lexical marker sacred throughout its own diction: *bil-wādi l-muqaddasi ṭuwā* — in the sacred valley, named Ṭuwā. The place-name is given — a real valley, a real spot of earth — and its quality is given: *al-muqaddas* — sanctified. The Book's signature verse in the fuller Sinai-chapter sets the same scene pinned on both identifications: *“It is truly I. I am your Lord! So take off your sandals, for you are in the sacred valley of Ṭuwa”* (20:12). God names the valley's sacredness at the moment of calling, as He names the addressee.

The sacredness of the valley belongs to its own teaching-category of place: sanctity conferred upon a tract by God's address to a man upon it, not by structures set up on it or invocations inherited about it. A valley, uninhabited except by flocks, in a wilderness of late-night traveler-fear: earth-quality acts when God's voice intersects with it. Meccans, whose attachment to sanctified-zoned nationality ran through the physical Kaaba-haram, received the teaching with particular application: the address sanctifies the tract — God is not confined in stone-zones; a desert valley can carry, upon a night, the whole cosmic court.

**Sandals Off: The Protocol of Address**

The fuller account's accompanying ordinance names the required posture: *take off your sandals* (20:12). Sandals-off is the primitive-archaic protocol of approach to the holy: leather, the traveler's carryover from the road's dung and dust, is shed before the voice of God enters the man's ears; the feet walk on sanctified soil bare. Etiquette institutions begin — none so finely as prophetic protocols — around the address-event itself, since greeting God in ordinary road-gear is a contradiction in terms.

In the pious register, the shedding-of-pair has always been heard as first obedience-lesson: before Moses learns creed, command or challenge, he learns service-posture — kneeling in the presence; and the removal of footwear at the sanctuary's door is the oldest disciplinary movement of Islam's prayer-architecture: the ground of prayer is Ṭuwā-adjacent wherever it stands; and the sanctuary's scent travels from the valley's night across the ages to every mosque's rug-fringe in the world where somebody daily leaves his sandals at an appointed door.

**The Pairing of Exile and Election**

The story's hidden poetics place the election scene exactly at the lowest station of the selected man: an exile from home, a man tending another man's flocks for unspecified years, homesick for a mother, cut off from every credential Egypt seems to confer. God's choice arrives after the erasing is finished: the voice calls the man in whom the world has expunged all other accredited claims — so that *what the voice gives* in the next verse would be seen to be, solely, what God gives. Election at the valley was framed by this matter of timing: after selfhood's mediocre years, not before.

The lesson with abiding standing for the audiences of prophets: God's appointments do not wait upon the recipient's accreditations. In the same breath-pattern, the report of the Meccan Messenger's own calling — a meditating man in the cave-rooms of Ḥirāʾ, far from the court-quotas of Quraysh — is recognizable in the peacetime glances of the valley scene: the voice arrives at the wildernesser, not at the committee-chamber. The sovereign worried about who should speak to power while He is speaking: the shepherd at the fire, not the grandees scheming in the capital.

**The Voice Answering the Solitary Night**

Reputations of the tradition are heavy on the moment's qualitative texture — how alone a man stands, night-desert, family camp down the slope, fire-of-strangers in the distance, sheep in shadow. The address God makes to his servant at the valley is exactly an address into abandonment: none of his people standing by; the only audience commerce the valley could bring: absence and night. The scene-product of so much silencing is the sūrah-standard of call-purity: a soul stripped, in darkness ready to hear, suddenly addressed, *your Lord* calling.

Devotionally the tradition met it everywhere with the same theology-echo: you find God's voices in abandonment-stations, not in crowded declarations. Meditating retreats and wilderness-places are stated as the eyries of call-bearing throughout history; and the pious practice of Islam's earliest community, removing itself at regular rhythm into the cave for meditation, enacted this exact scene-meaning — reach the silence shield first, where the calling conversation can occur; for the voice of the heavens is native to quietness.

**The Commission Drops in the Next Line**

God called him in the valley; and the call, in the very next verse, clocks out as commission: *Go to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺* (79:17). The initiatory call of Moses is thus characterized by its brief sanctification: address, then immediate, no ropes between: appointment uttered in sequence into the same valley-night. Between *your Lord called* and *Go* there comes no exceptional apparatus, no initiatory priesthood: plain appointment, hard target.

The compressed descending-line, call → commission, has been read by the tradition as the textbook-Bible of the mission's whole ethos: the caller is not invited to linger at the sanctified; sanctification is a pass-through to service; the valley does not become a home, only the fire's post-office. Moses, who walked up the slope a shepherd, walks down an envoy; and the valley stands behind him as one dark site in a theology of every man's sites of call: you are called at night; you walk out *sent*. The sūrah of the great summons has arrived at its statement of commission — *Go to Pharaoh* — which is the next verse's exact phrase.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:17

> ˹commanding,˺ “Go to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Idhhab: Two Letters That Found a Mission**

The call resolves into commission: *idhhab ilā firʿawn* — Go to Pharaoh. The imperative *idhhab* — Go — is the Book-historical ignition-verb of prophecy: the calls of the earlier messengers — Nouh, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ — organize themselves around this same mobilizing form, because prophetic careers in the Book commence as *movements*, not as opinions. The commission is short by design: no manual, no staff, no diplomatic cover; the man who came up the valley out of exile-shepherding is ordered: the target is the world's strongest throne — Go.

The fierceness of the send resides precisely in the simplicity: Go. Against the scale of Pharaoh's military-state, the command reads as implausible deportment — one exile versus an empire — but the commission has its own completed title: *your Lord* called him (79:16). Obedience to such a command depends only on the relationship named at the ledger preceding: where the caller is *your Lord*, the size of the commanded-target is already answered at the sentence's first syllable. God's messengers measure assignments by the Sender, not by the address.

**Ilā Firʿawn: The Target Named Without Cushions**

To Pharaoh — by name. No litotes cushioning the direction of travel, no surrounding explanation; the sūrah compresses the entire earth's power-blockage into a proper noun. Prophecy-school doctrine reads nouns of this kind as the address of the tyranny-theme's personification: 'Firʿawn' in the Book's usage is not merely an Egyptian throne-name but the standing address for power unhinged into self-deification. God's commission is deliberately aimed at the top of the power-chain; no delegated official, no chamber-committee — the ministry to *the* tyrant himself.

This naming-grammar is acted as therapy for the Messenger's own street: the Quraysh-leaders listening to this sūrah heard *Firʿawn* in their own flesh — the strong-man title of their own resistance — and the sūrah-story proceeds in fourteen verses to the end of Pharaoh inside the ocean and the afterlife. Prophetic stories in Meccan sūrahs perform their mapping first by *parallel noun*: each denier heard at once his name transcribed inside the ancient names — *I am your lord, the most high* entering Meccan ears from Egyptian mouths with Meccan inflections still on it (79:24).

**Innahu Ṭaghā: The Docket Against Him, One Verb**

One verb forms the whole charge: *innahu ṭaghā* — he has indeed transgressed, overflowed, exceeded. *Ṭaghā* is the flood-verb: a delineated watercourse breaking banks, overflowing its proper route, expanding out beyond its channel into lands on which it has no claim. God's biography of the strongest ruler on earth condenses to this single water-verdict: overflow. All of Fire-risk analysis later in the Book, all of the Day-catastrophe the sūrah now opens at its end-verses, will classify its transgressors under this exact root — the ṭāghīn-file (78:22) is named from the same ṭ-gh-y stock as Pharaoh's docket.

What was Pharaoh's ṭughyān constituted of? The accounts elsewhere in detail: self-deification (I am your lord most high), claim over the Israelites' lives and limbs as property, mockery of the sacred sign, statecraft-of-theaters, ayat-denial, and state-terror. The Book's choice to reduce the catalogue to *ṭaghā* instructs: all named oppressions are surface-forms of the one hydrological sin — the self exceeded its created banks. *Tyranny* in God's registry is overflow — the little human taken-for-whatever-it-wants, over the limit of everybody else.

**Why God Sends to the Vainest Man on Earth**

The commission's second doctrine: God does not quash tyranny by displacement from outside; God begins the quash *by delivering engagement*: Go first — come *to him* — with word. Pharaoh's destruction arrives only after Pharaoh's refusal; the mission runs with prepared aggressivity on both the gatekeepers and the chances. The principle: the destruction of tyrants preceded by the delivery of the call; engagement before sentence — the sunnah of God running even toward the peak of human arrogance.

The speech-manner commanded is recorded in the parallel: *“Speak to him gently, so perhaps he may be mindful ˹of Me˺ or fearful”* (20:44) — *qawlān layyinan* — gentle speech, since even a man who drowned a generation of male children is given courtesy before the exit-genocide is wrought. The tradition since Moses has carried this commission as assignment-universal: call, gently, to the tyrants; invite with would-you's (79:18 uses one); then watch the record they make themselves. God's final difference with the tyrant always shows *first a calling*, and it is the calling that justifies the entire scaffolding of the seize that comes after.

**The Prophet's Own Assignment Standing Mirrored in This Line**

The Meccan carrier of this sūrah received the dossier-parallel at the moment it was most needed: the regime of Quraysh sneering in assembly; a single un-backed warner addressing men whose tribal economy held Arabia's keys. The archive-sentence issued to him by revelation: *Go* is a historic verb — the Prophet was bidden precisely as Moses was bidden. And the doctrine-of-commission stands: *innahu ṭaghā* — the charge verdict fits every overflowing princeling in Mecca too, by class (Abū Jahl, al-Walīd's rounds of arrogance being ṭughyān-in-spectacles-of-silk rather than in crozier and tower).

Both Messenger and street are being equipped by the Moses-file to see, one after the other: the call is unilateral in Looks-valuable; the tyrant's overflow is unilateral in reality; the warner's obedient *Go* is all that stands between a call delivered and a tyrant seized. The commission's brevity stations the future: that the arrival of the sword-of-drowning and-arresting belongs to the aftermaths of the call refused — and the state of the street at this instant (warning already given, mockery current) is precisely the Pharaoh-station *before* the overtaking.

**The Call Proceeds Next From Hell-fire Command to Would-You Manners**

The story's beauty shows in what the commission does with the envoy once Go has been obeyed: the gentlest interrogative of all is placed into the mouth of a rebel-exile at the court of *the most high*: would you, pray, like to purify yourself? Verse 18 carries that phrasing; verse 19 undertakes the actual offer — to convey Pharaoh, personally, to his real Lord — full-of-hope delivery, the most courteous gospel-lining spoken in the Book's memory to the summit of ṭughyān.

From idhhab to hal laka, the sequence is also the divine speech-machinery graduated across address-classes from command to invitation: Moses' *Go* is a command, Pharaoh's *would you* an invitation. God's mouth and Moses' run the whole address-range in the two verses, between servant and tyrant: no matter whom a sermonizer addresses — self or stones, shepherds or sovereigns — the speech of faith designs itself to reach the relation its object actually possesses. The would-you verse issues the offer next.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:18

> And say, ‘Would you ˹be willing to˺ purify yourself

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Courteous Line Ever Spoken to Tyranny**

Here is the actual script of the Prophet's address to Pharaoh, and it begins with a question the Book has placed at the summit of courtesy: *fa-qūl hal laka ilā an tazakkā* — and say: Would you be inclined to purification? *Hal laka* — do you have — is Arabic's least coercive question-portal: the question asks the other if *he* has any share in the following purpose — if he *has it in him*, so to speak, to partake of what is to be offered. At the court of the world's most violent monarch, God's messenger is told to open with: *would you have...?*

The phrasing is God's own pedagogy. The colossal distance between the two men — barefoot exile, throne-sovereign — is deliberately archived in the text with courtesy-of-manner inversely proportional to the power held, so that every follower of the calling reads the doctrine plainly: prophetic speech is not sized on threat-vector but on the addressee's would-be wellness. The crewed speech-order issued with it — *speak to him gently* (20:44) — completes the doctrine: gentle speech for the harsh-chinned, *would-you* for the insolence-crowned, from the mouths of people answered by no other shield than *their Lord* (79:16).

**Tazakkā: The Verb of Purification That Also Means Growth**

The offer-verb placed within the courtesy-frame is a double-registered treasure: *tazakkā* — to be purified, or to grow in purity — carries in the same root-stock the two meanings Islam's whole vocabulary keeps deliberately entangled: purification (tazkiyya) and growth-in-goodness (zakā). The offered thing t h e tyrant is asked whether he inclines to is thus no loss-program: purification-through-growth — removing filth by *addition*: what God asks the overflow-man to incline-to is a project of becoming *larger in the purified direction*, a re-skilling of what his passions were spent on.

The tzadd's pragmatics, at the court of Pharaoh's achievement-show — his cities, his grain-silos, his temples — addressed his identity directly: you have spent so much on construction; would you wish to grow in purity? The offer does not seek mercy; it *flips the self-investment habit* of the builder-emperor onto the one field he had never sown; and his refusal-to-incline (79:21) will be narrated as the specific failure of this exact offer.

**The Frame Itself Indicts the Frame He Will Choose**

The would-you form was chosen absolutely deliberately against the refusal it would meet: God instructs His envoy to ask — *would you have any inclination* — so that when Pharaoh does not, his *disinclination* is what the record seals. Purification was never refused in the sūrah; inclination was. The docketing of the ancient tyrant's condemnation thereby stands as a docket of refusals-of-offers, not of punishments: had God begun with fire, He would have left the answer open; had He begun with the command, the manner of the refusal would not so closely register the truth that he *could* have complied and yet did not.

The would-you form, moreover, turns out to be the forensic meat of the whole exchange: *hal laka* invites attention to the presence/absence of a share-will: the matter is thereby posed as one of appetite — and verses 21-22 are going to recount his reply as disclosed in appetite too: he *chose* what to want; he *willed* the supremacy-scene. God's form of address thus leaves the whole transaction on the man's own valuation report, a matter in which nothing distracts from his own disclosed preferences.

**Purification as the Anti-Ṭughyān**

Against the charge-verb already on his docket — ṭaghā, overflowing — the offered verb has exactly the counter-role: purification is the channel's re-banking. The flood-nature is taken hold-of and confined to its proper river-guise; the overflow self is being *reduced* (washed down to its channel). And the offer is offered as *becoming* (an tazakkā) — a movement-verb: an inclination-toward, not a state — Pharaoh is asked only for the first leaning of the inclination, not for the finished purification: an incremental entry.

Against the sūrah's own final pair (vv 37-41), the offered tazakkā has yet another demonstrable field: *transgressors and world-preferrers* on the one side; those who *feared* and *restrained the soul's desires* on the other. The tyrant's purifying-choice at one decisional point would have enlisted him, by the sūrah's own nomenclature, with the Good file; and his refusal reads out as the very ṭughyān-dayfile the Day's judges register. The sūrah's closing division renames in full the alternative set out at the valley-offer: he was asked to be tazakkā; he answered with denial; the Day splits on the difference itself.

**The Gentle-Speech Decree and What It Teaches the Warners**

God's recorded command completes the manner: *“Speak to him gently, so perhaps he may be mindful or fearful”* (20:44) — qawl layyin, soft speech, with the two hope-hinges — perhaps-mindful, perhaps-fearful — left openly hedged: God knows beforehand what the response will be, yet has instructed gentleness. Hence gentleness is not the strategy of expediency (it pays on all outcomes, this one included); it is the courtly manner of the plain truth; and the lesson of the entire archive is that God's ambassadorial office has no register of telling that coarsens at the addressee's coarseness.

Every reader of the exchange is thereby taught the warner's posture: inclination offered first to grandeur as gentleness — pure remedy, pure offering — until the verdicts turn; this Meccan Messenger's first decade, then, under insults and shoving, is exactly the era of the gentle-offer; then — and only then — the archives' flood. The calibrations of the Sūrah were live: to the embattled audience, these verses described what God's method had been to the greatest strength in history, and what Bon's response-form of its regime was; to the Messenger, how long and how patiently the sunnah of patience runs before the sea folds over the horsemen.

**And the Offer's Inference Is the Next Line: To Your Lord**

The purification-Greeting is not even the whole of the courtesy: the envoy proceeds at once to the transport-offer: let me *guide you* — the sentence of verse 19 — to your Lord, so that you will fear. The sequence is psychologically done: (a) would you incline-to purification? (b) I myself am your transport to Him; (c) the result is awe: from the court's enslaving to an offered chaperon to the Throne — the most intimate manner-spell of all the Book's offers.

In Egypt's court, the would-you has never been completed — the refusal is next (v21) — but in the sūrah's Meccan copy, at the moment of its recitation, the would-you of verse 19 was being reopened through every Meccan ear simultaneously. The gentle-offer is by design still open to every unconquered ṭughyān-address the recitation reaches. God repeats His valley-script through His later messengers unto the last assembly of the daying world — the phrased courtesy is live — and Pharaoh's answer in the file simply remains the text for what happens when a man who could have inclined, *did not incline*.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:19

> and let me guide you to your Lord so that you will be in awe ˹of Him˺?’”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Wa-Ahdiyaka: The Offer of Personal Transport**

The offer extends to the transit itself: *wa-ahdiyaka ilā rabbika* — and I shall guide you myself to your Lord. The delicate word of the shell is *ahdiyaka*: I — the shepherd standing before you — will be your guide, your *hādī*; not maps to self-navigation but companion-conveyance. At the Pharaoh's court, whose appurtenances sported the sacerdotal literate classes of the ancient world — priests, magicians, god-houses of all Nile culture — the offer of *personal conveyance*, barefoot-prophet style, was the most unlikely detail imaginable; and in it, its credible sincerity is attested.

Notice also what the prophet's guidance-offer stipulates: no intermediate class, no credentials, no gatekeeping — the condemned of the land (Israel is working in mud-ditches near the palace walls) is bid to act as the King's guide; God reverse-ranks the ranks in one transitive verb. The offer is invaluable as the read-out of the Qur'anic office of the Messenger: deliverer, not supervisor; escort, not controller; the guided one himself passes his hand onward to who will take it.

**Ilā Rabbika: To YOUR Lord — The Pronoun of the Offended**

Three syllables install the redemption's axis: *ilā rabbika* — to your Lord. The possessive is deliberate and theologically loaded: even Pharaoh has a *rabb*; even at the apex of self-overreach, the Pharaoh is *owned* by someone with the possessive right; and the prophet's diction confines the right to God: your Lord — not your despot, not you yourself: the addressee, to be guided-to, is already *his*. In the sūrah's own near-wording, the coming verse of the court-climax (79:24) will have Pharaoh cry, *I am your lord, the most high* — and the diction of verse 19 stands directly against it as the contended noun of the story: *rabb* — theirs in claim, His in fact.

The sūrah's doctrine-of-lordship is thus quietly played out across the episode in competitives: Shay-analysis of the scene shows the dispute is not at gods-theology in abstract (Egypt was heavily populated with god-shapes) but over *who* claims the lordly-ownership of Pharaoh himself — and verse 19's "your Lord" stands in the prophet's wording as the fixed, gentle, undefeated form of the polemic. At every rejection the principle never shifts: your Lord, always — as the whole Book everywhere to the worst addressing keeps the pronoun: the file of *rabbika* remains in force against even the most high.

**Fa-Takhshā: The Offer's Destination Is Awe**

And the purpose-clause at the close of the offer is not beatitude hereafter and not deliverance from Israel's oppression alone, but — *fa-takhshā* — so that you will be in awe. The offer's destination is an interior state: *khawf/khashya* — awedness, reverence, fear-before-greatness. The order of the argument tells us where ṭughyān lives: the tyrant's disease sits in *the absence of awe* — no fear makes overflow easy, and the prophet's remediative itinerary for the overflow-man runs precisely through the arousal of awe (would you purify yourself → I will guide you to your Lord → you will learn to be in awe).

And awe acts across all the Book as the parent of all tags-closing virtues: at the sūrah's own closing explicitly, the file-division marks the two kinds of men by the presence or absence of this awe: *those who were in awe of standing before their Lord* (79:40) gets the garden-estate; the *ṭāghūn* and *world-preferrers* get the fire-estate (79:37-39). The offer to Pharaoh was thus literally an offer at the exact station of the final fork: *learn awe now* — and the next verses map his disinclination to it into his permanent placement at the other estate.

**The Humanness of the Offer Method**

The offered itinerary constitutes a complete spirituality, in three stages: purification-inclination (v18), guided transport (v19a), attained awe (v19b). Bear in mind: the itinerary God commands toward the palace of Egypt in His scripture is the itinerary for every human court to come — the same pattern is the sūrah's address-craft to Mecca's clubs. Wherever a man stands on the ṭughyān-ladder — a chief in purple or a run-of-street denier — his remedy-rung is the same three steps: incline toward purity, accept the guide, reach the awe.

The sequence also instructs the self-convert in particular: the same formula works first-person. *Hal laka* can be said to a self; the would-you can be stared-in-the-mirror: *would you incline toward your own purification* — while the guide-function recurs in recitation itself — the Book is its believers' ahdiyaka, personal transport to the real rabb. And awe — the terminus — is not posthumously reserved for the Day-scene only; the taste-of-khashya is available during the interval, in the halls of prayer and night-watching, precisely where the file-making of the day-file had its hands at work.

**The Refusal Is Already in the Next Verse — and the Sūrah Forgives the Effort Anyway**

It should be limpid from the whole record what God's pre-knowledge of Pharaoh's response was; the gentle-offer was issued *knowing* the answer. That is one of the Book's most chilling pedagogy-points: calls given-knowing-their-answers are not gambles; they are *establishments*. To hold out the purification-offer to the destined rejecter is God's declared way of full-establishing the argument against him (hujja) so that the tyrant's seize-verse (79:25) and its closing moral at 79:26 will be complete: the lesson against the awe-worthy needs the offer to have come first.

The Book keeps this pattern throughout: offers reach the greats refused-takers with full divine knowledge of their response; prophets are ordered the *perhaps* — speak gently so perhaps he may be mindful (20:44) — a perhaps from the All-Knowing's own prescription, issued to us as instruction in hope: divine speech operates in *perhaps* not because God is uncertain but because the office of the warners must be run on the probabilities as experienced — and the warner must call without ever being taught beforehand which addressee will decline. Pharaoh answered in the negative; another, somewhere along the call's recitation down the ages, might answer in the affirmative: *perhaps* — God's mercy of the *perhaps* keeps every would-you open.

**And to Complete the Offer, the Sign Will Be Shown**

The call's accompaniment arrives next with its visible flank: the great sign (79:20). Gentle-speech + offered guidance + awe-destination is God's court-protocol as far as its speech-side goes; then the evidence-side of the same protocol: the staff, the hand — the sign that turns dependent-men away from its full interior need, as every audience returns thereafter either with awe or with self-summitry's claim.

The sūrah speeds over the sign to the sequel deliberately: no sign-name is even given in these verses, and the response itself is described by twin verbs so short the ancient reader may have blinked — denied, disobeyed (v21). God's reasoning moves quickly over evidence-production to moral production, since the sūrah's case is the response-psychology of the ṭāghī (given-world-preference) — the same psychology whose sneer by the street of Mecca was composed in verses 10-11. The sign will be shown (v20), the sneer will follow (v21), and the hubris will be summoned at its blasphemous peak (vv .23-24) — until the seizure, *for the first and the last* (v25), makes the demonstration of the afterlife's court complete.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:20

> Then Moses showed him the great sign

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-Arāhu: The Evidence Is Staged**

The call completed its speech-side; now its evidence-side proceeds: *fa-arāhu l-āyata l-kubrā* — and he showed him the great sign. The showing-verb is deliberate: *arā* is display, presentation, the making-visible: the sign is not told about or argued; it is *held up*, in the court, in full view of the throne and its jurists. Prophecy's method is established in the one verb: invitation first (vv17-19), display next — the warner's gentleness is then backed by the Sender's demonstration.

The article's superlative carries the whole weight of the choice: *al-kubrā* — the *greatest*: among the signs of that appointment, one towers as the supreme exhibit. The commentators, on the identity of the referred sign, settle into the two great bands of the early tradition: the staff become a manifest serpent — the living one writhing over the court's polished pavements — and/or the hand drawn out shining-bright from the robe, white with the strange skin-light to all present. On either identity or on both together, the sūrah's point is identical: whatever *the great sign* was, it qualified as finality-grade evidence.

**Why the Sūrah Does Not Even Name the Sign**

Strikingly, this sūrah does not say *what* the sign was — it passes over the identity with the greatest of ease, by name only: the great sign. The reticence is wholly in keeping with the sūrah's purpose: these verses are not a rehearsal of the Exodus-miracle-cycle (the Book has other chapters full of the snake and the sea); they are an anatomy of *response*. The identity of the sign is irrelevant to the instructive schema being built: great sign → shown → denial — the skeleton requires only the bones of a superlative *Āyah*, not its zoology.

The reticence also performs the same service as elsewhere in the Book's story-pedagogy: it universalizes the Pharaoh-schema. Any people who witnessed a maximum-grade sign and answered in any of the Pharaoh-modes are *in the story*; the leaving-unnamed of the exhibit hands the disk over to each generation to certify their own exhibits. For Mecca, the recitation itself was the live great-sign — the Book in their faces, Gaza-and-Himyar talk; and the parallel pronoun-structure of the sneer-scenes (vv10-11) has already laid the mould.

**A Sign Whole: Mercy on the Second Circuit**

Why God equips his servants with signs at all gets its formulation from the story-shape under study: the prophetic appeals through reason alone had been exhausted (vv17-19); the infirm will to incline-purify having been exposed (hal laka), the mercy-circuit does not shut but rotates into demonstration. The sign is thus a *second kindness*: after the words fail to land, evidence increases. The sequence answers the common modern charge, that God authored confrontation impatiently: the mission ran its rungs: call, offer, invitation, sign — only then the response filed.

The second circuit of this mercy also instructs every preacher and debater along the road: the Book's method against denial includes visibly provided attestations — and since the prophetic age, God's attestation-strategies for His word include answered prayers, pattern-perfect scriptures, pattern-miracles in living encounters, and residue-evidences hidden in historiography; the general sunnah of the great-sign remains: God shows, men trespass or believe, files assemble. At the sūrah's own era, the assembled announcement (vv27-33) is itself the Meccans' great-sign catalogue — sky built, earth spread, water milked from the pressed clouds (the same vocabulary as ch.78) addressed to the same Pharaonic suspicion.

**The Verse's Mass in Four Words**

In Arabic the verse is four words: showed-him / the-sign / the-greatest — a grammar so structured that the whole significance rides on the last adjective: nothing intervened between the display and what the sūrah is about to do with it. Contrast against the sūrah's long openings: five oaths (vv1-5), commission-to-awe tour (vv16-19) — the portrayal of the sign is the shortest stage of the archive: since spectacle occupies one verse, but the duplicate of refusal occupies two, and the pride-scene occupies two, and the seizure condenses into one.

The scene-reading functions as budget-teaching: God assigns the evidence-economy no more verses than it needs and assigns the response-anatomy the bulk — because the lesson was never the display but the divide. Modern audiences who clamor for more sign-naming are therefore gently corrected: the same cursory nomenclature across the Book gives sheer spectacle barely a fingerprint; in contrast the verses distribute enormous space to hearts, intents, etiquettes, and choices (as at vv40-41's closing law): *that* is where the Book's attention wants our attention.

**The Sceptic's Demand Meets the Archive's Answer**

The Meccan street specialised in sign-demands — those very mock-verbal contracts that still recur in every skeptical century: *give us a demonstration and we'll believe*. Here in the oldest hostile archive-pattern the demonstration wasn't just provided: shown, displayed, in the strongest case-history — and the next verse says the reception openly: denied and disobeyed (v21). Demand-fulfilled, belief-withheld; the sequence stamps its permanent argument against the sceptical premise: signs don't convert proudly-willed hearts; only mercy of willings doesn't love signs.

The sūrah is sequenced to answer today's sign-demand with both: (a) evidence-tour (vv27-33) — the cosmos-furniture certified by the very asking; (b) precedent (Egypt): ask for signs all you like; the greatest Pharaonic sign still could not enrol Pharaoh in the paying clients. The verse hereby returns the debate's proper footing: the matter is not evidence-availability, nor evidence-quality, nor evidence-frequency: it is *hawā* — desires — the inward variable that v40 names in the closing law of the sūrah as the only dam or sluice of the soul toward paradise.

**Deny First, Then Disobey — The Response Verse Arrives**

From the display's superlative, the archive drops into its report of reply in the next verse with the cruelty of two five-letter verdicts: *fa-kadhdhaba wa-ʿaṣā* — but he denied and disobeyed. The famed sign-elect disappears into two negatives: his outward reception — the street-denial — and his interior orientation — the disobedience-of-the-fortress — summarizing as two-part verbs what all denial always does: refuse with the mouth, cement with the heart.

The bridge into verse 21 is the turn of the episode into its moral second act: from here forward the sūrah narrates only what Pharaoh's own machinery added to himself after the sign: turning, striving, summoning, crying *I am your lord most high*. The display-verse thus stands at the hinge of the whole episode: before it, God's participatory movement toward mercy (call, invitation, guide, sign); after it, Pharaoh's machine, unchecked, by its own filename (79:22's striving). The briefest sign-verse leads thus into the wildest passage of self-monarchy in sacred history — the summit-claim — and the most documented seizure, both of which belong to the sūrah's closing anatomy of transgression's biographies.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:21

> but he denied and disobeyed ˹Allah˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-Kadhdhaba wa-ʿAṣā: Two Short Verdict-Verbs**

The response is reciped in two verbs, each five letters: *fa-kadhdhaba wa-ʿaṣā* — and he denied, and disobeyed. The and-coupling has its grammar of process: *kadhdhaba* is takdhīb — outward-cognitive action: he assessed the displayed evidence and ruled it false, the rejection pronounced in court before the elite who had asked for evidence earlier in the same record; *ʿaṣā* is disobedience — inward-conative action: at the level where compliance was demanded (purify, incline, be guided, fear), his will simply said no. Coupled together, the two verbs summarize the disaster of unbelief: no assent; no compliance.

Two observations in the short recording belong to the sūrah's own rhetorical practice: first, the economy: the whole psychology of ṭughyān's reaction to demonstrable truth is read out of five-letter words, with no adverb, no stage-direction about gestures, no theatrics: the Book's documentation style externalizes its psychology through terse verbs, letting the verbs' sequence do the depiction. Second, the order: saying *no* first, acting *no* second; exterior first, interior second — the history of the mouth-brain and heart-brain set out as a chain of sin: after evidence's encounter, repudiating speech, then noncompliance in the self's assignments.

**Denial Pronounced Against One's Own Sight**

The more flagrant of the two is the first: *kadhdhaba* with the sign in front of his eyes. Plainly, takdhīb at that juncture is functioning not as insufficient-information but as wilful repudiation — a verdict against one's own observed data. The Book names the same leprosy at every appearance — as in its street-scenes too — where the tongue *declares false* what the observing member has just verified; the mouth here issues a false judgment as against the evidence presented to the eye, and the fraternity of the impulse reaches back to the commentary tradition's technical term (*al-juḥūd*: denial in the teeth of recognition).

Why *judged* false? The archive supplies its own machinery in the next verse: *he turned his back, striving* (79:22): striving (*yasʿā*) by effort against what entered him: deliberate labour of the will on the consequence of the sign, his effort *against* the evidence's evident. The tradition here notes the exact psychology of the throne: obedience would have cost him supremacy, guests at the palace, the temple's apparatus, and Nile-god's salutations — and his file's outsized investment in *what* would be lost governed how he ruled *what* would be believed. Each judgment-account of the sceptical is at the bottom a cost-account of position.

**Disobedience as the Second Verb of the Ṭāghī**

The second verb carries the docket of the disposition: *ʿaṣā* — disobeyed — with its object lying in the premises: he was commanded (though in tones of would-you) to incline toward purification (the real command behind 79:18's courtesy-form), to learn awe (79:19), and at the evidence to affirm. His disobedience comprises the refusal of all four elements: hal laka-no, guidance-no, awe-no, sign-no. Disobedience here is not boundary-line rebelliousness in general but the record — accepted docket, accepted commission, refused fulfillment.

The scholars of the verse note the two verbs' division closely matching two stages of Muḥammed post-commission response: rejection by speech-street (the courtyard) and rejection by conduct-street (the inner-hours of commissioning). The speech-stage takes its documents from his actual reported response-episode (as the full archives in the parallel passages detail: sorcery-accusation, magicians called, contest-engineered); the submitting-stage is most visible at his committee-arch: his striving handlers — and these become the sūrah's next two verses, 22-23.

**The Two-Verb Formula Extends to the Sūrah's Audience**

For the sūrah's primary Meccan audience, the two-verb Pharaoh-verdict reads as the docket-file headers of their own current treatment of the Sign: they too were witnessing a great-sign (the Book and its reported miracles and its speaker's known truthfulness); they too had greeted it with the paired mechanics: *kadhdhaba* — release the street from believing it (and dub it poetry, fables, sorcery) — and *ʿaṣā* — then decline its habitable requirements. The two-verb antique record becomes, upon its own recitation in Mecca's ears, real-time diagnosis: this is what ṭughyān always does in front of Āyāt kubrā-status evidence, and it is what you are, right now, doing.

The archive's protective function in the street-condition is expressed at the sūrah's own nearer indictment-verses (vv37-41): *those who transgressed* (ṭaghā-form, the ṭāghī-verb-pattern the same as ṭaghā in this very report) and *preferred the fleeting life* — verses that read as the verse's own tent into present: the vintage tyrant sits as the ceiling of a column down which all two-verb operators fall; he has the same m.o.; and the Day's housing of the class reads literally the same.

**The Irreducibility of the Pair in the Docket of the Condemned**

The two verbs binding in the sūrah's whole scheme is also the rule of the charge-formalized elsewhere: rejection of signs (78:28) is exactly kadhdhaba; expectation-of-no-reckoning (78:27) is exactly ʿaṣā. The elementary analysis of rebellion everywhere reduces to these two: holding-as-false, being-uncooperative — against the two poles of God's claim — deliverance veracity and lordship. Everything else in a transgressor's file is elaboration (striving in v22), or tone (sneer in v12), or apparatus (summons in v23) of these two verbs.

The treatment meanwhile prescribes in the two complimentary verbs: their antidotes are *taṣdīq* — affirmative coordination with truth — and *ṭāʿa* — compliance coordinated with authority — faith + works: the dual of kadhdhaba and ʿaṣā. At the very end the sūrah's own positive estate closes upon one corresponding reply-to-reply: those who *feared* (awe answering marvel) and *restrained* (compliance answering desire) obtain the Garden (79:40-41) — the two disciplines repairing exactly where ṭughyān had undone: *turīdūn* replaced by fear's check, and ʿaṣā replaced by the checked soul.

**The Turn That Comes Next Is His Machine in Motion**

The next verse shows the two verbs rendered into motion: *thumma adbara yasʿā* — then he turned his back and moved-effortfully: after the two internal completions, the external organization of the same file —how the court organizes after the confirmation of his disbelief: recalibration toward propaganda, consultation, the crowds called, the supreme-slogan delivered. Verse 22's footage begins exactly where the two-verb interior leaves off.

For the tradition's own use of the sentence, every generation must mark how dramatic the switch from āyah kubrā to two verbs is: sight of the greatest sign of its age, replied by five-letters. It is warning-light to every high-evidence spectator: *you always answer something after evidence*; there is no neutral-time after generous evidence; either taṣdīq-believing + complying is attributed on the record, or the two verbs: *kadhdhaba wa-ʿaṣā*. The next verse gives his answer's activities their full visual.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:22

> then turned his back, striving ˹against the truth˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Thumma Adbara: The Physical Geometry of a No**

After the interior pair (denied, disobeyed), the exterior: *thumma adbara yasʿā* — then he turned his back, striving. *Adbara* is the body-verb of epistemology: he turned away — presented the back to the evidence — pivot in place, the king on his dais physically rearranging himself against what had just been shown. The recitation gives movement-language to the refusal for good cause: the Face that had to look at the staff convulsing on his own floor, at the shining hand, now chose its own orientation away — the body's swivel engineering what the heart had already decided.

*Thumma* (then) places the turn as second-order action: after the sentence-of-mind was sealed, the body followed suit; not indecisive wavering but *post-decision choreography*: the reflex of kings equipping their verdict with reflexive motion. Turning-the-back also carries the kingly insult as physical grammar: an Arab, an Egyptian audience of rank-worlds, knows that presentation of the back to a speaker is the court's most unanswerable contempt — and Pharaoh's full divan is the audience of his performance.

**Yasʿā: It Was Labour, Not Leisure**

And what comes next is more terrible than denial itself: he *strove* — yasʿā from *saʿy*: purposeful running, striving-forward. Not passive withdrawal of credence; busy, organized, labour-grade assault on the consequences of the sight. The tradition reads the verb as the mad-dream of the court: the consultation-with-chiefs, the accusation-engine (he is a sorcerer), the summons of every proficient magician in the Nile-valley, the contest-appointments, the arrangements — a full departmental effort to falsify the Sign by outproducing it.

The striving-verb specifies for us the *industry of rejection*: ṭughyān responds to evidence not with greater ease but with *effort*: a new mythology is produced; the media-division is mobilized; every resource of the regime's court is justly named saʿy — running-effort, labor — the tyrant sprints harder at disproving God's light than he ever hearkened to incline toward purity. The paradox of sin at scale: the man who would not turn one inch to purification (79:18) runs miles to destroy the evidence offered *to* him.

**The Parallel of the Efforting-Against-Truth Class**

The striving-lone word deserves its own systemic note in the Book's anthropology: elsewhere when mankind *strives*, the saʿy-word carries positive names (the labour toward God, pilgrimage-ḥajj-saʿy). The same verb on Ṭā for its target is the tragedy: *effort exists* — human possibility is not the issue — effort permanently solvable; sin's matter is never capacity but *aim*: all capacity found expedited down the wrong channel: saʿy against the truth. The Book elsewhere memorializes such striving against the signs of the Messenger with the same register: those who strive against our signs, racing ahead (34:38's expression), is a fixed head-count of the Book's outlaw-file.

Pharaoh's striving also matches, in the Book's later entries on his file, the exact activity: chiefs inclusive, crier sent through cities to the utmost of Egypt, magicians assembled, contest-day mobilized — the state's entire apparatus thrown against one barefoot prophet with a staff; and the same striving deployed later to its military closure — pursuit by host with pride across the split sea. The Nile-state's administrative genius weds itself to the same saʿy, every machine of empire reserved for the service of its lie.

**Why Turned Away Instead of Attacked Directly**

A question its readers pose: why does God picture denial first as *turning the back* rather than direct attack? The composition explains the psychology of evidence: at *the being* of the great sign, the denier's first combat is with his own seeing — visual turn first, corporate steps after. Attack-organization only begins *once the back is firmly presented*, because pivots are how kings leave room to deny having really seen. The idbar-clause carries what psychoanalysis would later call the will-to-unsee: a bodily division of attention — you will-dispose of evidence by disposing of its angle — including his whole solipsistic elite.

And the Book's own physiology of such scenes is consistent elsewhere: the deniers in the same act-of-turning the back at signs dispatched to them — hearing inattention (18:57 reports heedlessness-turned-away; 46:3's turning away); reaching the same structure: the crowd that cannot look long at what condemns them averts itself, turns the physical back as the last defensive wall against vision that is still operative. The back-turned silhouette of a throne was measured once by the drops of the Nile-vision: the king has already left the verdict to the paying-tellers under the tent of the magicians' arrival (79:23 next).

**His Own Apparatus Gathers for the Grand Fabrication**

With the turn-and-striving, the two next verses (23-24) tell what the striving bought: the assembly of his kingdom and the state's own loud summon — *I am your lord, the most high*. The striving-verse thus stages the crescendo. In three verses Pharaoh moves from (a) governing denial at five letters (v21), to (b) kinetic evasive newsroom (v22), to (c) full sovereign self-invention at the microphone (v24) — the exact power-crescendo of a despot closing the playhouse on his own wholesale lie: the next verse has him gather his people to hear it.

For the Meccan counterpart the cautionary template is direct: striving-class exists as deniers' know-how: councils, poets hired, counter-speeches scheduled against the recitation. *Yasʿā* is alive in each age's apparatus-of-messaging: watch a state marshal its ambitious word-manufacture against the plain word that came with a barefoot petitioner; across the sūrah's own geography at its delivery-night, the Meccan counterparts were taking consultation even as these verses issued. The sequence proceeds: after striving, state pageant — verse 23's detail — and then the jungle-loud broadcast of godship — verse 24. And the whole itinerary, from turned back to mustered crowd to shouted claim, will be read back to its runner on the day when striving of every kind is recalled and priced by its direction.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:23

> Then he summoned ˹his people˺ and called out

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-Ḥashara: The Tyrant's Assembly Order**

The striving's first official act: *fa-ḥashara fa-nādā* — he gathered [his people], then he proclaimed. *Ḥashara* is muster-terminology: to round up, to marshal an assembly — to collect bodies into formation under a summons. The verb shows the state in motion at full government-power: heralds through the provinces, criers in the markets — the machinery of the regime kicking in to convene a crafted gathering. Not a spontaneous crowd but a *ḥashr*: a summoned muster of the kingdom.

The use of the muster-verb carries a second literary shudder for the reader attentive: *ḥashr* is the very noun the Book uses for the great Muster of the Day of Resurrection — the Day of *al-ḥashr* — and in the sūrah itself the vocabulary of mass-gathering is already active from the blast-scene (vv6-9, 13-14: all summoned to the sāhirah). The text has the king order his assembly in the very terminology of the Divine Assembly — so the reader learns that Ḥashr-events exist at two levels: tyrants can order musters too, seats-of-the-crowd spectacles built to buttress lie-towers; and the Mustards of the latter day will answer every such ḥashr, in which the ones who mustered by power for self-worship will be mustered by God for audit.

**Fa-Nādā: The Loud Proclamation as State Signal**

Then *fa-nādā* — he cried out, proclaimed loudly. The cry-verb is exactly the same verb-family that governs God's own call: He *called* Moses in the valley; the sūrah-system of nādā ends both voices' contrast: *this* calling is at stadium-volume for a false declaration, the valley's calling was intimate for a true one (79:16). Loudness of proclamation does not trade for truth; rather — and this is a permanent Qur'anic lesson about propaganda — crowds and loud cries are the native costume of self-authored claims. The word is call; and *Ḥashr plus nādā* is the formula for the fabricated messiah-event: an audience assembled not by truth but by herald-power.

Parallel passages of the Book fill out what the levi-summons contained: with his chiefs he meditated; they said he is only a learned sorcerer; then the assembly-festival of sorcery was launched in the very language of statecraft: heralds through the cities demanding every proficient magician. The state's information-apparatus runs in rehearsal both against the news-psalter and against the prophet; priests and media joined in one people's muster. The events issue directly into the proclamation itself, which is the next verse.

**The Dynasty's Divine Advertisement Model**

Why the gathering? The patient tradition of this verse's readers shows: a lie needs *staging*. His dignity-and-supremacy architecture, to remain believable at the level of his own subjects, admitted no negation from a barefoot petitioner; a counter-event was required, with the throne at the center, the masses attentive, the loudspeaker functioning — so that the sheer institutional graphics of the event would drown the *seen* sign of a week prior. Nādā atop ḥashara is the staging-brief: production values used to outbid revelation.

A principle for every observant generation: watch for the musters of self-crowning. The Kingdom is never staged — since God needs no assembly of processional production to be God (He is invoked with or without any stage) — whereas self-ensconced power under challenge always produces ceremonies, loudspeakers, rallies, and crowded optics precisely when its legitimacy is being dented; and the Book's audience in Mecca, being small and un-backed, saw daily that the truth of a thing is not paged through the most microphones. Constantly a full mouth proclaims a full fantasy: see the next verse for its emblematic version.

**Rivalry Mocked by the Very Vocabulary**

The deliberate play of the lexical family also reads polemically: all the world of prophets knew God's own ḥashr-announcements — the Muster summoned at the hour — and the opening of the legend for the tyrant's show is cut in the same terminological cloth. The reader hears it unmistakably: Pharaoh's *ḥashr* is a borrowed counterfeit of God's; *nādā* is imitation of God's call; and in assembly-formation and outgoing proclaim, Egypt is performing the silhouettes of the Kingdom in the world. At the height of the chasing game, man-made kitsch performing the liturgy of the absolute King — one of the Book's most masked but sharpest burlesques of idolized power.

The irony ripens in the last comparison the sūrah will allow: the Day of decisive assembly (79:6-14, 34-36) is headed by *The* King with the valid Muster and the valid Caller; afterward, no bragging assembly of any transgressor is available for leverage; and the chiefs and subject-crowds stand at the plain with no heralds between — where Pharaoh's drama shall content itself with a footnote in the annals read by judges who outrank him (78:38 operational in the family too: ranks and licensed speech).

**The Crowd-Conditioned Audience as Witness Against Itself**

The ḥashr-material also equips the account with witnesses: assemblies gather proof. Had Pharaoh's no stayed a private-vice, few in Egypt would have known how the sign was answered; the Leader open-mic cry delivered in a packed assembly filled contemporaneous Egypt with the evidence of its claim — so that when the takedown came (vv25-26), there was no village later to plead insufficient advance knowledge of what had been claimed. The summoned crowd — will or nil — was made co-attending at the unTruth of its King.

The observers of religious sociology have long noticed in the archives how public blasphemy grows; the sūrah helps its audiences therefore to declaim the innocent refrain *I never said it myself*: when crown-shows arrange themselves, the attending of the crowd is itself part of the production; and the Book's own warnings at such gatherings — wandering-stock at the lote-plains, or the massed ears at courts — translate a standing caution for all who can read them: your seat at a muster makes you a *witness to its claim* — choose before the proclamation opens. Verse 24 has the microphone switch on.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:24

> saying, “I am your lord, the most high!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Top Word of Ṭughyān Spoken Aloud**

The mustered audience gets its proclamation: *fa-qāla ana rabbukumu l-aʿlā* — he said: I am your lord, the most high. This is the summit-phrase of the archive: not merely sultan-claims, not merely divine patronage, but *rubūbiyya* itself asserted in the first person, with the elative of loftiness affixed: *al-aʿlā* — the Most High — the exact name-territory of God's own title. One sentence, containing all the transgression the sūrah documented as ṭaghā: the overflow has crested above every bank on record.

The choice still stuns across the centuries: *ana rabbukum* — I am your lord. Read against the offer he had just been served in the preceding acts — *let me guide you to your Lord (rabbika)* (79:19) — the counter-claim is the answer made loud: No — the lordship you offered is dethroned; *I* am that lord. The story's axis-claim the preparations toward all along is here announced at the microphone; in it the question of all the sūrah's earlier shared term — *rabb* — is put to the test, Egypt hearing both pronunciations together.

**The word al-Aʿlā and the Sūrah's Own Polemics**

The superlative-adjective is precisely theologically placed in the sūrah's own vocabulary of God: *He built the sky, raising it high* (79:27-28), *He is most high* in the Book's attributes; the Book's custody of *al-aʿlā* belongs to the Throned one, upraised, He alone (cf. the neighbouring sūrah of al-Aʿlā itself, the 87th, sealed by the direct cognate pair at v16-17 about preferring the world). The claim by the Nile-king to be *al-aʿlā* is thus a usurpation not just of obedience-territory but of *attribute-territory*: he is borrowing throne-adjectives of heaven's king for fast-food crowns.

Future translations of the scene reproduce the Greek hubris-mythic formula (*overreach of the self-alture*) but the Arabic's structure signifies more nakedly: he openly wore God's own attributes in his self-proclamation. And this is ṭughyān's full definition on the smallest scale: to claim for creature what belongs to Creator. The pride's punishment, evidently built into the metre of the story, is the seizure of the next verse — *for the first and the last* — which the tradition always reads as God's own counter-attribution: He is the First and the Last, and Pharaoh's aʿlā-ness vaporizes between them.

**The Pathology as Worked Anatomy**

Held up as a case-study, the proclamation is the final shape of the overflow-lineage: desire for standing (79:22's striving) becomes *claim* for godship: what the human begins as self-concern ends as self-theology. There may not be one reader earned without noticing how every stage of his story was one metre to this: every rise of class, wealth, or applause a further production-index for the same sound-byte. The adornment of lifestyles and titles in every age is the carbon monologue of the same pathology — to upgrade rank-credit until one day the man speaks *I* where *He* should hold the sentence.

Al-Rāzī's era-time readers wrote the humanistic moral: Egypt did not produce this madness as single-cause; ḥashr and nādā and rank and years of un-restrained applause built the tunnel by which an ordinary man came to say *I am your lord, the most high* without flinching. The sūrah pre-also-recites the same lesson in pronoun-psychology: *you* (vv 10-11) is a pronoun unkempt by any *rabb*, and before long ego builds to a self-crowned form; or it can be held by awe — and self stays in its creature-size, the mould of peace (79:40).

**Every Pharaonic Forum Rehearsed Against Every Recitation**

For the Meccan street, this claim arrives embedded in its own present-tense: senior chiefs had been giving their speeches too, named-standing heights, sworn-in words; God-claims mitigated with easy polytheist conference-speak. Quraysh heard in *ana rabbukumu l-aʿlā* the formal maximal of every supremacy act on their own network: the polemical function of the archive goes right past its story-context, since the sūrah's closing preserves *his name, not their names*, but *classifies* them with ṭaghā (79:37: *who transgressed* — using the same ṭughyān as the charge against him at 79:17).

The polemic also stands, inexorably, for all future audiences: rulers, media-tsars, celebrity-self gods, idea-Nile apologists — *ana rabbukum* re-appears in every epochal form — and the sūrah answers them all with the same seizure-verse (79:25) that is coming now: God's docketing of the claimants by the two-world counter. Still: benchmark of the Book's law: any claim that takes for man what belongs to God inherits the judgment against the author of the claim in the primary record; and the file-end of that pathology is written within the next breath (v25).

**The Most High Title Occupied, and Then—**

Hold the claim with quiet amplitude for a second: *I am your lord, the most high* — said as a proclamation by a man of flesh who would within our reader sūrahs be pictured *dead by drowning, confessing at the water's edge* (the Book's own later update to Pharaoh's file: *now, you believe?* — the sarcastic reply of the angels at his late repentance). This, then, is the exact scale on which ṭughyān's currency devalues over days: from broadcasting most-high at the assembly to confessing in surf. The Book never flatters the scale of claims with the timing of response: the two endings (world + after) are in the verse that closes the archive-affair.

And the sūrah's audience, listening as the same claim-era is playing in Meccan hierarchies, is taught in advance where cry-clips end: with God's two-period seizure — *nakāl al-ākhirati wal-ūlā* — which is the next verse, and with the morals-license at 79:26 read off to every single listener still in a position of choice. The microphone at court goes forever silent in the next verse; and the crackle of the sea, which rose no doubt to the same height of air, does not bother to listen to him anymore.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:25

> So Allah overtook him, making him an example in this life and the next

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fa-Akhadhahu Llāh: The Answer Arrives**

One verb performs the whole flood: *fa-akhadhahu llāhu nakāla l-ākhirati wal-ūlā* — so Allah seized him — the seizing of the next life and the first. *Akhadh* is grasp/capture: the vocabulary of laying hold, sudden and inescapable; the *fa-* connector closes the causality: after the most-high proclamation, *so* Allah seized him. The seizure carries no naming of logistics here; the Book's seizing-files keep that register — the grip is named; the events follow in other passages; here it is stated, not narrated, because the audience knows the story: at the water's edge, the crown went under, confessing too late.

The Book elsewhere documents the ending with its fullest precision: the host pursued, the sea-parted, the waves folded over them; *and We drowned Pharaoh and his people; while the children of Israel We delivered*. The compact grammar here — seized him — gets its mass from its contrasting scale with the preceding acts of summoning-pageant: the sound-byte of God's answer to *ana rabbukumu l-aʿlā*: no podium, no assembly, no contest of heralds. Just *fa-akhadhā*. The most high's file is closed in three Arabic syllables.

**Nakāl: A Exemplar-Making Word**

The noun of punishment itself is chosen for its teaching-function: *nakāl* is not merely punishment but exemplary deterrence — chastisement intended to warn others, a trophy held up as a lesson against imitation. God didn't merely destroy Pharaoh; He installed the destruction as signage. The reinforcement of nakāl's didactic import is direct: punishment-as-example means the outcome's eyes are outward — every generation following him enters as its audience — and the whole annals of the Egypt-night exist so that the following eras would read the same water-logged end in their papers and the same tower-silenced in their fields.

The tradition hears the verse also closing the ages-long loop of the Book's deliverance-rehearsals: a giant tyrant flashes maximum sublime claims — and the seizure names him an example to be read by the long caravan of power-addicts to come: *lessons* are created by God's seize-styles in history so that they can stand as traffic-lights on their own. Merely political reading of history — changes of king and regime — is answered here by the *nakāl* structure: exemplary ends deliberately exacted, intentionally legible, publicly nailed.

**The Two Seizures: In This Life and the Next**

Remember the claim-terms: *I am your lord, the most high* — a double-territory claim over the obedience (world) and over the highest authoritativeness (cosmos/after). God's seizure is docketed as the same duality: *nakāla l-ākhirati wal-ūlā* — exemplary punishment of the next-ākhira and the first-ūlā. Two world-seizures: in this life (ūlā) the drowning in the sea that the Nile-delta had spent millennia worshipping; in the next (ākhira) the exemplary torment that the authentic register of the Book repeatedly assigns his house: daily presentation to the fire, and at the Day's morn: enter the severest punishment (the Book's account of the household of Pharaoh presenting them to the fire morning and evening).

The two-period registry serves the mission-theology throughout the Book: not only afterlife audit's day but world-history's day of seizure too; a man of God's choice in this sūrah does not have to wait until the Judgment to see the answer to *ana rabbukum*: the Nile provided its answers while children watched from its banks — and the comfort of the patient is this: the ūlā-seizure is teased along the file before (and as guarantee of) the ākhira one. One seize-preview shows what another seize has already concluded: God's court is already in partial session within history.

**The Prophet's Hope Read From This Line**

The verse's first-person benefits belong to the carrier of this sūrah too: the oppressed warner in Mecca, watching his audience's old Pharaoh-poses from chiefs who knocked down his congregation's members now and then, could learn both from the two-seize line: God does seize — and He chooses when — history's own court already displays the method textually. The tradition of the believers recited precisely such seizure-verses in the seasons of persecution as (a) hope in the ūlā-release they awaited (deliverance, migration, return with keys) and (b) promise in the ākhira-settlement where all claims fall straight without any coercion from the audience.

For every generation of oppressed reciters after, the verse equally functions as the standing paragraph of the nation's response to overwhelming-power: the Ṭāghūt of the day may seem unseizable, but God's seize has already bent comparable crowns underwater — *nakāl al-ākhira wal-ūlā* — every line of the Book's lesson appropriate to the oppressed becomes actual once more at this verse-equation. To the criminal-watchers in any era, the tradition thus owns a formula to repeat against despair of the tyrannized: the same seize belongs to our ūlā and to their ākhira — God has no missed members of the claim-file, world after-world.

**Matching the Claim: Aʿlā Against Akhira and Ūlā**

Finally the verse's *nakāl* construction bears a lexical counter-fitting of exactness that no alert reader misses: the claimed superlative *al-aʿlā* — Most High — is faced by the double *al-ākhirati wal-ūlā* — the Next and the First — the two titles that God appropriates for Himself among His revealed names: *He is the First and the Last*. Equivalence-flavored revenge is God's documentary habit (the fitting-wage of ch78); here the claim most-HIGH is toppled by a seizure signed by the One who signs His own bio as first-last — the heights of Pharaoh's self-projections towered and were flattened inside the two frames that summarize real highness.

And the smaller note: in verse 27-ff the same adjective *aʿlā* re-enters in God's frame about *His* raising of the sky (رفاء heights), the denied Pharaoh's own self-elective adjective placed quietly beside the One who raised the vault he sneered under and built the storeys above the Nile-palace's roof. Verse 26 next closes with the lesson-verse the Book has made famous in its Egyptian archive: a lesson for the awe-owner. The most-high's story ends — not at the sea but at its summary — with the sūrah's own signpost for readers of each future era: whoever stands in awe of Allah takes it.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:26

> Surely in this is a lesson for whoever stands in awe of ˹Allah˺

**Expanded Commentary**

The story that began with a question addressed to the Prophet now receives its official seal. Ten verses of riverbank, palace, staff, boast, and drowning are folded into a single demonstrative and a single condition, and the sūrah tells its audience precisely what kind of object they have just been handed: not a tale of a faraway king, but an instrument built for one specific class of heart.

**Inna Fī Dhālika La-ʿIbratan: A Ford in Word Form**

The word ʿibra, lesson, grows from the root ʿ-b-r, to cross over. Lexicographers of the language note the family likeness: crossing from one side of a river to the other, a tear (ʿabra) crossing down the cheek, and knowledge that ferries a person from one state into another. A lesson, in this naming, is not a summary or a moral tacked onto an ending; it is equipment for a crossing — a plank thrown from the story's shore to the listener's shore. Pharaoh's file is thereby catalogued as a ford: something engineered so that whoever wades into it can come out on the far bank of their own situation, changed and warned. This is why the revelation refuses to call its narratives entertainment, memory, or antiquarian record. It calls them crossings, and where the crossing succeeds the heart arrives somewhere it could not have reached by argument alone. The drowned king's story was never about the drowned king; the river of it runs through every listener who hears it, and the verse is the signpost planted at midstream.

**Dhālika: The Demonstrative That Carries an Archive**

Notice what the pointer-word *dhālika* (in that) is made to hold. A demonstrative pronoun, one of the lightest particles in the language's toolkit, is here loaded with the entire four-act drama: the gentle call at the sacred valley, the offer of purification and guidance, the great sign displayed, the sneer of supremacy, the gathering, the claim, the seizure. An entire archive compressed into a pointing finger. This loading of the small with the immense is characteristic of the sūrah's economy: five-word verses that carry the resurrection ("a single Shout" in v13), a three-word answer that carries creation's whole workshop ("He built it," in v27). The concentration is itself instructive. Revelation does not need bulk to carry weight; it needs exact placement. When the pointer lands — *in that* — everything narrated since v15 stands behind it in a single rank, marshalled into one exhibit. The listener who has been following the drama at walking pace suddenly finds the whole march reviewed in one glance, and the glance is the point: the story seen whole, as a shape, is the lesson the story in pieces could not quite deliver.

**Liman Yakhshā: The One Heart the Lesson Work In**

The restriction carried by *liman yakhshā* — for whoever stands in awe — is not a courtesy limitation but the verse's sharpest teaching. The ʿibra is available to every ear; it is operative only in a heart whose awe-organ still functions. A deterrent persuades only where danger can still be registered. Pharaoh's court watched the identical staff, stood beneath the identical signs, and felt precisely nothing, because the receiving instrument had been cauterized by supremacy. The same archive is therefore a mirror for one audience and wallpaper for another. Across the river of the story, at one bank, stand hearts awed by what the account certifies about God's reach; at the opposite bank stand hearts which process the report as diverting court gossip from a defeated dynasty. Nothing in the story changes between the two; everything in the hearer does. The verse, with dry precision, files the lesson under the name of the only class able to collect it. Terror of the consequences, it turns out, is a faculty — present in some, extinguished in others — and narratives like this one exist to find and confirm whoever still owns it.

**The Algorithm Inside the Story**

What, enumerated, does the crossing teach? Its algorithm has four moves. First: a call is issued with courtesy, not with artillery — the Prophet is told to go to the tyrant and ask after his willingness to be purified. Second: evidence is supplied on request — the sign is produced promptly when the claim of divine sending is challenged. Third: the claim of self-sufficiency rises to its summit — the boast that there is no lord above the claimant. Fourth: the seizure arrives, doubled, first-world and last-world, exemplary and remembered. Anyone may locate himself on this map. The Meccan chieftains hearing the recitation are being told their coordinate: they have received the gentler call, they have watched the evidence, and the third station lies directly ahead of them if supremacy remains their creed. The lesson is not decorative sympathy with Moses; it is cartography of danger. Every throne that repeats the pattern inherits the pattern's ending, and the map is published precisely so that later travellers can no longer plead that the coastline was uncharted.

**The Seal Placed on Stories Throughout the Book**

This closing technique — narrate, then declare the narrative's function aloud — runs through the revelation like a manufacturer's stamp. In one sweeping statement: "And We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ the stories of the messengers to reassure your heart. And there has come to you in this ˹sûrah˺ the truth, a warning ˹to the disbelievers˺, and a reminder to the believers." (11:120) Steadying, warning, reminding — the three offices of narrated precedent — are all discharged by these eleven verses of the sūrah. The Prophet's heart is steadied: the enemy he faces belongs to a species whose most powerful specimen is already waterlogged. The deniers are warned: the machinery of seizure is older and stronger than their confederacy. The believers are reminded: endurance has precedent, and precedent has a drowning-pool. Announcing all this openly, rather than leaving the moral to inference, respects the listener's agency: the interpretation is given so the responsibility for reacting to it cannot be dodged.

**Now, the Court Above the Court**

Having grounded its argument in the soils of Egyptian precedent, the sūrah lifts its audience's chin: "Which is harder to create: you or the sky?" (79:27). The pivot is tactical. Against the sneerer's favourite objection — that re-creation after decay exceeds any power — the recitation points past every roof of court and hovel to the immense erection above them, asks for a direct comparison of difficulty, and answers on the audience's behalf. The next movement will walk through that answer piece by piece: the raised vault, the dimmed night, the spread earth, the summoned water, the anchored mountains — the built universe audited as the credentials of the One who rules both Nile and sky, and therefore also the dust of the dead.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:27

> Which is harder to create: you or the sky? He built it

**Expanded Commentary**

With the Pharaoh-file sealed, the sūrah changes altitude. History gave way to the moral; now the moral gives way to cosmology. The argument moves from the drowned tyrant to the vault over every tyrant's head — and it moves by question, dragging the audience into the witness-box of their own eyesight.

**Which Is Harder to Create: The Interrogation of Scale**

The opening construction — *are you more formidable as a creation, or the sky?* — is a forced comparison elegantly rigged. The adjective *ashaddu* (harder, more vehement, more intensely wrought) is a human-feeling word, borrowed from the audience's own instinct about what counts as a difficult object; the question thereby enlists their native judgment as the measuring instrument. No abstract theology is requested yet — only a comparison of workmanship, the kind of judgment a craftsman delivers about two artefacts. Yet the comparison is loaded the moment it is asked. On one side stands the listener: a body, a span of years, a grave's worth of matter. On the other side: the entire visible architecture of existence, the roof-line beneath which every civilization has ever pitched its tents. The interrogative design is a trap of the kindest sort — whichever side the addressee answers, the verdict that follows belongs to his own mouth, not to the preacher's. Rhetoric has placed the scales in his hands and walked away.

**Banāhā: Three Letters That Finish the Debate**

Then the answer drops, and its brevity is the punch: "He built it." Whatever verdict the audience reached about the sky's difficulty, the building-permit for the sky already carries His name. The verb *banā* belongs to the vocabulary of constructed edifices — foundations, spans, ordered courses — not the vocabulary of haze or accident. A built thing has been measured, raised, stabilized; the dome overhead is described as engineered structure, not ambient blur. The sūrah's sister-passage makes the same attribution with architectural specificity about layered firmament, "and built above you seven mighty ˹heavens˺," (78:12), giving the construction its storeys. Here the sūrah does not even need the storeys; it needs only the verb. One word assigns the sky its Builder, and with it the entire debate about the plausibility of re-creation collapses into scale-arithmetic. If the ceiling counts among His ordinary products, the workshop's lesser orders — bodies, bones, reassemblages — are beneath its standard billing. The question was asked in one breath and answered in three letters because the ratio between the works is not close.

**Arguing From the Greater Down to the Lesser**

This is the classic logic of the greater implying the lesser, turned on the resurrection-doubter with unusual bluntness. The sneer recorded earlier — "Will we really be restored to our former state" (79:10) — rested on an intuition about impossibility: decay looks like a one-way process, and restoration looks like a demand exceeding any plausible budget of power. The verse confiscates that intuition by re-pricing the budget. The relevant comparison is not between a living body and a rotten one; it is between a body and the sky. Whoever concedes that the vault overhead is fabricated — and the verse permits no third option, asking *which* of the two is harder, not *whether* either is fabricated — has already conceded a workshop whose capacity dwarfs the complaint. Restoration shrinks, on this scale, from miraculous exception to routine maintenance. The argument's cunning is that it requires no special pleading, no appeal to mystery: only honest sight upward plus arithmetic. Denial of the lesser by someone who accepts the greater is not skepticism; it is refusal to run the numbers.

**A Sermon Whose Exhibit Cannot Be Confiscated**

Recall the courtroom of the earlier movement: within Pharaoh's palace, evidence could be disqualified on arrival — labelled sorcery, countered by staged sorcery, shouted down by the apparatus. The sūrah now deliberately relocates the exhibition to a venue no apparatus controls. The sky is an exhibit that cannot be impounded, adjourned, or counter-programmed; the court of creation sits in permanent session over every court of men. This changes the politics of the argument. At Mecca, chiefs could regulate what entered the public ear; they could not regulate the skyline their own skylit streets slept beneath. Every evening, the distractors withdraw beneath the very artefact their mockery denies being made; every dawn, the exhibit re-opens without their permission. Persuasion that depends on such evidence quietly outlasts every news-cycle the sneerers manufacture. The revelation's calm in pointing upward is the calm of an advocate who knows the decisive exhibit cannot be removed from the hall.

**A Question Addressed to the Sneerer Personally**

The most disarming feature of the comparison is its first clause: *you*. The questioner is made to measure himself against the dome — his weight, his reach, his durability against that span — and the measuring-strip snaps in his hands. Humility is installed not by scolding but by geometry; the verse administers smallness as a dose of perspective rather than as an insult. There is further grace folded into the format: a question, unlike an accusation, preserves the addressee's agency to the last moment. He may answer wrongly; he may answer honestly; the rope is in his hands. The Book repeatedly chooses this interrogative courtesy with its opponents, granting them the dignity of a verdict-form to fill in rather than a sentence already served. What makes the strategy devastating is that any honest filling achieves the argument's purpose — the sneerer, answering about himself against the sky, has done the preacher's work while believing he was only estimating architecture.

**Raised, and Finished Well**

The line's own continuation begins the specification: "raising it high and forming it flawlessly." (79:28). Two workmanship-verbs will now carry the detail — elevation as deliberate act, completion without warp — and behind them the challenge the Book issues elsewhere, inviting every generation to audit the dome for defects and report back. The sky, in other words, is about to be walked through as a building inspection. The builder of a body hidden in a mother's dark is also the roofer of the visible world; the sneerer's doubt about the dark-work falls, plank by plank, as the roof-work is described above his head.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:28

> raising it high and forming it flawlessly

**Expanded Commentary**

Two verbs carry this entire line: one for altitude, one for finish. The sky, having been assigned a Builder, is now described under construction — lifted by decision, then finished without a flaw. Seven words that function as a building-inspection report on the largest structure in experience.

**Rafaʿa Samkahā: The Vault Lifted by Decision**

The noun *samk* names the upper reach of a thing — its roof, its height-curve — and the verb *rafaʿa* declares that this reach was hoisted, not drifted into. The language insists on deliberateness: a lifter raised it, the way a builder raises a ridge-beam, the way a tent-master hauls a canopy onto its poles, though with no poles at all — a suspension none of the audience's builders could imitate on any budget. Height here is not descriptive background but product: distance between floor and ceiling was itself manufactured. This matters for the argument underway. A sky that simply happened to be far could be dismissed as luck of arrangement; a sky *raised* belongs to a campaign of construction, with intent readable in the gap between earth and vault. The elevation is also measurable mercy — clearance for light to travel, for weather to move, for wings to use — but the verse lets the sheer act of lifting carry its own testimony first: before anyone calculates what the height is good for, the raise itself signs the work.

**Fa-Sawwāhā: The Workshop's Finishing Verb**

The second verb, *sawwā*, belongs to the vocabulary of leveled, proportioned, completed work — the even hand-finishing by which a made thing exits the shop un-warped, un-cracked, true to form. Remarkably, the same root serves elsewhere in the Book for the fashioning of the human frame, formed upright and proportioned in the womb's workshop. Cosmology and anthropology share a verb here by design: the verse's original question — you or the sky, which is harder? — is quietly answered by the shared signature. One Workman finishes both the vault above the visitor and the visitor himself, using the same quality-mark on both scales of product. That finishing quality is the observable wonder: spans without sag, orbits without wobble, a canopy whose geometry does not drift. Where human construction requires scaffolding, patching, and eventual repair, this erection holds its lines across every inspection age. The Builder does not revisit the sky with maintenance crews; the finish specified at erection still holds.

**Flawlessness Under Cross-Examination**

The Book does not leave the claim of perfection as decorative praise; it converts it into an auditable challenge. "You will never see any imperfection in the creation of the Most Compassionate. So look again: do you see any flaws? Then look again and again— your sight will return frustrated and weary." (67:3-4) There is the test protocol in full: look, look again, look until the eyes tire, and record what the inspection finds. The verdict announced in advance — no flaw encountered, sight returning humbled — is the kind of claim only made where the manufacturer knows the stock. Note the psychological finish-line the passage sets: not merely failure to find defects, but the *weariness* of the searching eye, the inspection outlasting the inspector. Every age's instruments have enlarged the resolution of the looking, and the line's invitation has absorbed each enlargement; the finer the lens, the deeper the order reported back. This verse's *flawlessly* is the compressed certificate of the same examination: two syllables carrying a dare.

**Height as a Preacher of Majesty**

Beyond the engineering, the lifted roof trains the creature who lives beneath it. Human posture toward the sky is older than any creed's manual: faces turn upward in appeal, hands open toward the high-place, the heart aims above the ceiling of its circumstances when it hopes for rescue from beyond its own resources. The architecture wrote the instinct. A roof pressed low upon the world would have bred a different species of soul — hunched, un-aspiring, convinced that nothing exceeds the immediate. Instead the vault was hauled to its present altitude, and with it came the grammar of upwardness: majesty read off altitude, petition directed past the rafters, kings humbled when they remember that their palace roof is not the top of the building they live in. The verse's first verb thereby preaches without words. Every glance upward rehearses the lesson that the station above is occupied — raised deliberately — and that the direction of the lifted roof is the direction of the lifted Hand.

**The Counter-Signature Against the Aʿlā-Claim**

The sequence inside this sūrah is not accidental. Two verses ago, a claimant announced himself as the high-one among lords; the recitation's next movement answers with a sky *raised high* — the adjective of the boast literally erected overhead at cosmic scale, signed by a different Raiser. No rebuttal-speech is needed; the juxtaposition performs it. A man declares altitude; the very next lines measure actual altitude, in units no Nile-engineer's rod reaches, and credit the lift elsewhere. This is the sūrah's quiet method against supremacy-claims generally: it does not argue with the boast directly; it describes the world until the boast becomes inaudible. Every later throne that repeats the claim inherits the same embarrassment — an assertion spoken indoors, indoors beneath a roof it did not raise, a roof whose erection notice belongs to the passage now being recited against it. The counter-signature technique is patience itself: let the claimant finish his sentence; then describe the ceiling.

**And the Light Is Managed Next**

The construction report continues into the management of what plays beneath the vault: "He dimmed its night, and brought forth its daylight." (79:29). The same hand that raised the canopy also runs the lighting beneath it — dimming and summoning, scheduling the darkness and the blaze. The tour of credentials moves from structure to operation, from the statics of the raised roof to the daily switching of the world's lamps, and the sneerer will find the same signature on the lighting schedule as on the beams above it.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:29

> He dimmed its night, and brought forth its daylight

**Expanded Commentary**

Beneath the raised vault sits the world's lighting system, and this verse hands the switches to the same Builder. Night is dimmed in; the blaze of forenoon is drawn out — two operations, both verbs of deliberate management, wrapping the audience's entire lived rhythm inside a maintenance schedule they never noticed being signed.

**Aghṭasha Laylahā: Darkness as Something Administered**

The verb *aghṭasha* means to darken, to dim, to draw the deep shade over; its startling implication is that night is a product, not a residue. The common instinct treats darkness as light's mere absence — what remains when the lamp fails, owing nothing to anyone. The verse reassigns ownership: the dark is *dimmed in*, administered, drawn like a curtain by hand at the proper hour. And the Book elsewhere certifies the motive of the administration as mercy: "It is out of His mercy that He has made for you the day and night so that you may rest ˹in the latter˺ and seek His bounty ˹in the former˺, and perhaps you will be grateful." (28:73). Rest engineered into the schedule; recovery built into the planet's rotation; the body's nightly repair-shifts staffed by the dimming itself. A world of unbroken glare would exhaust its creatures within days — poets in every latitude have intuited the gift of the dark without knowing its invoice. This verse delivers the invoice: the mercy that dims the lamps each night, on time, for uncounted sleepers, is the same mercy whose household the sneerers deny exists.

**Wa-Akhraja Ḍuḥāhā: Morning as an Extraction**

Against the dimming stands the out-bringing: *akhraja* — He drew forth — applied to *ḍuḥā*, not the grey first crack of dawn but the high, full forenoon blaze. Daylight is described the way water will be described two verses later: as something drawn out of concealment, hauled up from its place of storage and released across the world. The wording converts sunrise from passive fading-of-dark into an act of extraction — the brilliance is *produced*, fetched, delivered. Storehouse imagery haunts the line: light kept in reserve, dispensed each morning in a quantity sufficient to run the commerce of the whole hemisphere. The choice of ḍuḥā rather than a fainter word for morning intensifies the claim — not the timid edge of day but its full-noon confidence is what gets brought out. Every forenoon, at the hour when markets fill and fields receive their workers, the world's biggest lamp arrives on schedule, by extraction, by decision. The audience's own working hours are thus framed as guests at a dispensing they neither stock nor schedule.

**The Alternation as a Twice-Daily Rehearsal**

What makes the cycle evidentiary is precisely its repeatability. Elsewhere the Book stamps the same daily swap with its standard verdict: "He covers the day with night. Surely in this are signs for those who reflect." (13:3). Signs — plural, recurring, located in the regularity itself. A one-time marvel could be archived as anomaly; a marvel on a twenty-four-hour loop becomes curriculum. The alternation is the classroom where reflection keeps being re-enrolled: each dusk re-poses the question of who dims, each dawn re-poses the question of who draws forth, and the accumulated repetitions build the case with the slow force of a sediment. Notably, the verse assigns no mechanism-language, no physics; it assigns agents and verbs. Whatever intermediate mechanisms an age maps — rotation, orbit, atmosphere — the scheduling remains authored, because mechanisms are precisely what authored orders run on. The Book's confidence here is serene: the deeper the machinery described, the more the twice-daily punctuality itself demands a Keeper of the timetable.

**Night and Day in This Sūrah's Sister-Inventory**

The alternation appears again within the parallel chapter the audience has just been reciting, priced in the currency of use: the dark drawn down "and made the night as a cover," (78:10) — the cover-metaphor matching this verse's curtain-drawing — and the bright half set to work "and made the day for livelihood," (78:11). The two passages cross-reference each other like twin ledgers kept in one hand: the same Auditor listing the same fixtures, from slightly different angles, a chapter apart. Such internal rhyme is not redundancy; it is the Book's signature method of rendering its claims checkable. A reader who heard the earlier chapter finds its fixtures re-certified here under construction-vocabulary; a reader who starts here meets mercy-economics in the other. The alternation also quietly indicts: the sneerers schedule their plowing, their caravans, their evening councils by this exact dimming and out-bringing — their entire calendar is subcontracted from the schedule they mock. Tenants run their livelihoods on the landlord's lighting rota while disputing the landlord's existence.

**Refuting the Steady-State by the Clock**

The pagan instinct toward a self-running cosmos — a world that simply *is*, requiring no one behind its cycles — founders on this verse's smallest fact: the swap happens, on command, every rotation. A static world permits no toggling; an alternation implies an alternator. Whatever name an age gives the cosmic mechanism, the twice-daily flip between administered dark and extracted blaze is a *decision executed at scale*, and decisions executed at scale, relentlessly, forever, are not the signature of drift. The sneerers' world-model can absorb the sky's stillness — perhaps it has always stood — but the cycling defeats it: yesterday's dimming was not once-for-all; tonight's requires the schedule to still be running. Someone must still be at the switches. The verse thereby converts the most familiar rhythm in experience into the standing refutation of every creed that has no Operator, and it does so with two verbs and no raised voice.

**After the Roof, the Deck**

The tour of construction descends a floor: "As for the earth, He spread it out as well," (79:30). The same passage that lifted the vault now unrolls the ground beneath it — the deck spread for occupancy after the roof was raised. Roof, then floor; then the floor's utilities: water, grazing, anchors. The credentials audit proceeds from the overhead to the underfoot, and at each station the sneerer's own life — his rest, his morning labor, his footing — turns out to be running on the Builder's account.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:30

> As for the earth, He spread it out as well

**Expanded Commentary**

The vault is up; now the floor goes down. One compact verb unrolls the ground of the world, and a small phrase of sequence — *after that* — opens one of the commentary tradition's delicate conversations about the order of making. The deck is spread for occupancy: that is the line's whole thunder.

**Baʿda Dhālika: A Delicate Question of Sequence**

The words *after that* prompted the commentators to careful work, since the Book elsewhere narrates the earth's making before the heavens' forming in seven stages — "So He formed the heaven into seven heavens in two Days, assigning to each its mandate." (41:12) — following there upon the earth's own accounting. The reconciling instinct of the tradition took *after* here as an order of display and rank rather than a strict clock: the earth, existing from the first campaign, receives its final spreading-out and furnishing subsequent to the heaven's erection — the deck completed, so to speak, once the roof stood over it. Whatever the harmonization adopted, the verse's own emphasis is unmistakable: the spreading is a later *finish*, a commissioning, distinct from raw existence. The ground was there; then it was *made ready* — rolled flat for feet, opened for springs, prepared for seed. Read this way, the small phrase deserts chronology-quarrels and joins the verse's real theme: the earth is not merely present; it is *prepared* — and preparation is the signature of intention. A campsite found is luck; a campsite laid is hospitality.

**Daḥāhā: The Spreading Verb With a Desert Pedigree**

The verb *daḥā* belongs to the vocabulary of spreading wide, flattening out, extending level — and the Arabs heard in it images from their own landscape, among them the ostrich smoothing and widening its nest-bed in the sand: a broad, worked patch of ground, deliberately prepared for what will be laid and hatched upon it. The pedigree of the image is worth holding. A bird's nest-bed is not spectacular; it is *serviceable* — and that is exactly the register in which the verse wants the earth's expanses heard. The continents are a nest-bed: crushed, rolled, worked out flat so that something can live on them. The metaphor quietly reverses the instinct that only the spectacular certifies design. Nothing looks less miraculous to the habitual eye than level ground; the verse replies that level ground is precisely the miracle that had to be made. An un-spread earth — jagged, vertical, impassable — is the default no one thinks to imagine, because no one has ever stood on it.

**Spread for Occupancy, Not for Scenery**

The functionality deepens under a second look. Spreading is the precondition of everything the coming verses will list: water needs runnels, pasture needs plains, plowing needs furrows, herds need range, caravans need passes. The deck precedes the furniture — and the furniture, as the following lines will show, was planned with the deck. Note also what the spreading does to the human scale: it renders the world *walkable*, sized to the stride of its tenants. The creature's foot was not adapted to the ground; on this verse's telling, the ground was unrolled to receive the foot. That inversion — provision running ahead of need, environment fitted to inhabitant rather than inhabitant lucky in environment — is the verse's quiet argument, and it never even has to raise its voice. Wherever the audience stands as the recitation reaches them, they stand on the exhibit: the spread is the sermon, delivered underfoot, re-delivered with every step they take away from the gathering.

**The Look-At-It Imperative of the Parallel Chapter**

The Book elsewhere turns this same fact into a direct command to stare: "and the earth—how it was levelled out?" (88:20). The interrogative *how* is doing the work of a whole treatise — by what flattening, what rolling, what grading was the crust of a fire-balled planet rendered meadowable and roadable? The twin passages share the Book's characteristic move: take the most over-familiar datum in experience and prosecute it with a question mark until it confesses. Levelled out *how* — and for whom? The rocks of the high country certify what un-levelled ground looks like; between them, the valleys lie open like unrolled scrolls, and the scrolls are agricultural. The spreading-verb thus keeps company with the splitting-edge marvels of the narrative chapters, claiming equal rank: the sea divided for one nation once; the earth unrolled for every nation daily. Familiarity has demoted the greater wonder and promoted the lesser, and the recitation's whole method across this sūrah is the undoing of that demotion.

**Vertical, Then Horizontal: The House in Two Strokes**

Join this line to the one before it and the description of the world reduces to two construction strokes of astonishing economy: the vault hauled up (the vertical), the deck rolled out (the horizontal). House-building in two motions — ceiling and floor — on the scale of existence. The audience's own houses are nothing but miniature quotations of these two strokes: they know exactly what it costs to lift a roof and level a floor, and the recitation deliberately employs their trade-vocabulary so the attribution will bite. Who raised your ridge-beam? You did, with ropes and strain. Who raised the one over the valley? Who spread the one under it? The proportion of forces is not close, and the verse lets the carpentry-grammar carry the whole comparison without a simile or a shout. A house has been provided at a scale no tenancy-deposit could purchase, and the lease-question — on what terms do we occupy it? — is exactly where the sūrah is marching its audience, toward the Day the tenancy audit arrives.

**Then the Utilities: Water and Grass**

A spread deck is still only potential until its services run, and the next line switches them on: "bringing forth its water and pastures" (79:31). The out-bringing verb returns — the same extraction-verb that drew forth the morning blaze now draws the earth's hidden stores: water summoned to the surface, grazing summoned out of dust. The house was roofed; the house was floored; now the house is stocked. The credentials tour is completing itself station by station, and each station lands closer to the audience's daily mouthful than the last — as if the Builder intends His audit to end, undeniably, at their own tables.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:31

> bringing forth its water and pastures

**Expanded Commentary**

The deck now receives its utilities. Two commodities only are named — water and grazing — yet between them they constitute the entire survival-ledger of the verse's first audience. The verb of extraction, used for the morning blaze, now works underground and underfoot: hidden stores, summoned out on schedule.

**Akhraja Minhā Māʾahā: Water Summoned From the Ground**

The phrase's architecture rewards attention: He brought forth *from it* — from the just-spread earth — its water. The vital fluid's location is the marvel: stored *in* the ground, in darkness, beyond the reach of any bucket technology until released — and then released *from it*, as if the deck were a cistern with a Keeper's tap. The Book's parallel inventories return to this extraction repeatedly: gardens and "caused springs to gush forth in it," (36:34) — springs, the very image of water *brought out*, pressurized up from concealment into daylight. The audience knew wells: pits sunk on hope, brackish yields, the water-table as rumor. Against that anxious economics of access, the verse declares the whole planetary waterworks an administered dispensing — rain received, stored, filtered through rock, and re-issued at spring-mouths and river-heads on a schedule no tribe manages. The extraction-verb is exact: not created-on-the-spot each time, but *brought forth*, released from appointed holding. Hydrology here is payroll: reserves under management, issuance on time, for populations that never see the accounts office.

**Wa-Marʿāhā: The Pasture Before the Loaf**

The second extraction is *marʿā* — grazing-ground, the green the flocks live on. Note what is named and what is not: no grain, no bread, no vine — the verse names the food *of the food*. Grass feeds the herd; the herd feeds the human; the provisioning-chain the verse cites begins one link earlier than the audience's own breakfast. There is precision in this priority. Grain agriculture is seasonal, fragile, civilized; grazing is the peninsula's deep economy, the substrate that survives drought-years and migration-years. By naming pasture, the verse anchors providence in the audience's most resilient life-support: even when harvests fail, the marʿā carries the herds that carry the people. And the pasture's own pedigree is paradoxical wealth — it grows from waste ground, thorn-country, the neglected margins, converting the unusable into milk and meat. An economist of the desert would recognize the claim immediately: the earth's least promising surfaces are made to yield, on extraction again, the green of survival. The grazing lands were never the tribe's achievement; they arrive, after rain, like a posted credit.

**The Same Verb, Three Products: A Workman's Habit**

Track the verb across the construction-passage: daylight was *brought forth* in v29; water here is *brought forth*; pasture is *brought forth*. One extraction-verb serves light, water, and grass — three utterly different commodities delivered under a single workman's habitual motion. The repetition is the fingerprint. In a craftsman's shop, the recurring gesture betrays the single hand; in this passage, the recurring gesture is out-bringing, and the single hand is the argument. The world's goods are not autonomous emanations of soil and sky; they are *deliveries*, each hauled out of its appointed concealment by the same agent: light from behind the night's curtain, water from beneath the crust, green from beneath the dust. Concealment-plus-summons is the shared structure — and it reaches, within the sūrah's own architecture, toward the grave: the dead too are stored in ground and will be brought forth. The audience has been watching the resurrection's verb rehearsed on their morning, their wells, and their meadows all along.

**The Book's Command to Examine One's Own Mouthful**

The parallel chapter that opens with the human food-supply issues a blunt imperative: "Let people then consider their food:" (80:24) — and then tracks the sequence: rain split open, earth furrowed, grain and grape, fodder and fruit, descending the chain item by item to the mouth. This verse is the compressed form of that entire audit: water and pasture, the chain's two foundational links, named in five words as extractions to be examined. The imperative's significance deserves emphasis: the Book does not merely *state* providence; it instructs its hearers to perform the audit themselves, to conduct the inspection at their own tables and trace the mouthful backward through pasture and water to the extraction-verb. Such an audit is falsifiable-in-principle, inspectable, walkable: the wells are there, the grazing arrives, the chains run. Faith here is not assent against evidence but the conclusion of evidence honestly retraced — which is why the verse can afford to be this brief. Five words, and the burden of inspection is transferred to the listener's own next meal.

**A Cycle That Preaches Return From Death**

The pasture has one more office in the Book's curriculum: it dies and returns. The golden, then the dust-dry, then — after rain — the green again: the dead-earth revival is the standing visual aid for the sūrah's disputed claim, and the Book says so outright: "There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat." (36:33). The sneerers walk every spring through the theatre of the very operation they deny: ground gone bare and hard, then re-clothed. The verse's pasture-word thereby smuggles the resurrection-argument into the provisioning-argument — the extraction of green from dead dust is the annual rehearsal of extraction of the buried from their clay. Whoever manages the second never lacked capacity for the first. The audience's economy, twice over, is run on the disputed power: their survival depends daily on the issuing-office whose master-decree — the re-issuance of the dead — they mock. The pasture is the evidence that grazes at their feet.

**Then the Anchors of the Deck**

One utility remains before the purpose-clause lands: "and setting the mountains firmly ˹upon it˺—" (79:32). The spread deck, like any broad fabric, must be held down against its own restlessness, and the fastening-instruments are the most violent-looking objects on the landscape — the mountains, here named as the settled anchors of the rolling earth. The construction tour moves from utilities to fasteners, and then, with the whole edifice standing, the verse after will name what all of it was *for*.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:32

> and setting the mountains firmly ˹upon it˺—

**Expanded Commentary**

The construction tour ends its list with the heaviest objects in sight. Mountains — the wilderness's most untamable masses — are introduced here not as terrain that happens to rise, but as fastenings driven: set, anchored, seated firmly into the spread deck like rivets through fabric. The verb does everything.

**Wal-Jibāla Arsāhā: The Anchoring Verb**

The verb *arsā* carries the sense of setting firm, of anchoring into stability — its root (r-s-y) runs through the language wherever restlessness is brought to rest, where a vessel at anchor stops dragging, where a structure's sway is killed by its seating. Applying such a verb to mountains performs a deliberate shock of reclassification: the objects an audience experiences as the ultimate un-moved movers of the landscape are described as *themselves moved into place* — seated, settled, installed. Once that reclassification lands, the mountains' look changes: their massiveness stops reading as the earth's own brute necessity and starts reading as *work*, the deliberate emplacement of exactly the right counterweights for exactly this deck. The image underneath is of a spread expanse that must be held: fabric pinned at intervals so wind and ripple cannot take it. The most violent-looking formation in nature, on this telling, is the landscape's mooring system. Nothing in observation contradicts the description; every later science that has mapped the crust's restlessness has only deepened what the verb already said.

**Firmness Against the Trembling of the Floor**

The Book states the mountains' stabilizing office in so many words, more than once. "He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you, as well as rivers, and pathways so you may find your way." (16:15). And again, in the prophets' chapter: "And We have placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with them, and made in it broad pathways so they may find your way." (21:31). The stated purpose — *so it does not shake with you* — deserves to be lingered over. The beneficiaries are named in the stabilizer's job-description: the deck is steadied *for its tenants*, the trembling suppressed *with you aboard*. The audience knows trembling: the earth's shudder is the one event that dissolves all human footing at once, the horror of the floor's betrayal. Against that horror stands an installed countermeasure, emplaced before the tenants arrived, sized like nothing they could have engineered. The verses also bundle the mountains with rivers and pathways — the anchors come with the plumbing and the roads — completing the image of a fitted-out dwelling rather than a wild heap.

**The Pegs of the Sister-Chapter Reappear**

This sūrah's immediate predecessor had already filed the mountains under their homeliest metaphor: "and ˹made˺ the mountains as ˹its˺ pegs," (78:7) — tent-pegs, the Bedouin's fastening of choice, the driven hold-fast on which a camp's whole shelter depends. Peg in one chapter, anchored-seating in the next: the imagery varies, the attribution and function stay identical, and the identity is itself an argument. Two audiences, two passage-architectures, one Inventory — the fixtures match across the chapters the way twin manifest-copies match in one clerk's hand. For the listening Meccans, the peg-image would have landed first: every one of them had struck a tent-peg and trusted a shelter to it. Now the recitation offers the engineering register above the domestic one: not just pegs, but *settled anchors*, seated with finality. The mountain you watch on the horizon is a fastening the Builder drove, and — the metaphor's quiet extension — if the world's very deck is pegged down by His hand, the notion of slipping His grasp on any Day is carpentry-naïve. What He stakes, stays.

**The Ladder of the Inventory Arrives at Its Top**

Stand back and count the steps the construction-passage has climbed: vault raised, alternation switched, deck spread, water extracted, pasture brought forth — and now the fasteners, the list's final and most immovable item. The sequence is not a heap; it is a ladder. Each rung has tightened the scale of the attribution: from the overhead vastness no one touches, down through the lighting-schedule everyone keeps, down through the ground everyone walks, down through the wells and grasses everyone consumes, ending here on the masses that hold the whole assembly still. The choice to *close* on mountains is rhetorically exact: the inventory terminates at the one object in the audience's landscape that nobody can budge, muscle, bribe, or move. Argument from the fixity of the visible floor — if the unmovable is His installation, then every motion underneath, including the motion of empires and the motion of graves, is under the same administration. The ladder's top rung is a rivet; the sneerer's case has been riveted shut beneath it.

**An Audit Any Aʿlā-Claimant May Attempt**

Read against the chapter's earlier movement, the line carries its dryest irony. The claimant of lordship-on-high is invited, by implication, to the public-works test: let the high-one shift a ridge one cubit, or re-seat a single anchor once seated. The audit exposes the boast's currency as counterfeit — command-pronouns over obedient peasants and armed retinues, nothing over stone. Even that claimant's most ambitious masonry — and his civilization's monuments were the ancient world's largest artworks of piled rock — sits within a single anchored massif the way a toy sits within a fortress. The Book's method, again, is not to debate the claim but to describe the standings: *here* is height actually raised, spread actually unrolled, anchors actually driven. Supremacy is measured in works; the works are on display; the claimant's portion of the display is zero. The listener performing this comparison learns the technique permanently — every future supremacy-claim, from any pulpit, can be audited against the inventory in one glance.

**Now the Purpose of the Whole Edifice**

Vault, alternation, deck, water, pasture, anchors — the assemblage stands complete, and the next line names its destination: "all as ˹a means of˺ sustenance for you and your animals." (79:33). The entire majestic inventory folds into one word of provision and one pair of beneficiaries. The Builder's tour, it turns out, was a steward's inventory all along — taken for the tenants, signed over to their use, awaiting only their signature at the bottom. The sūrah will then pivot once more: from the house built for the tenants to the Day the tenancy is audited.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:33

> all as ˹a means of˺ sustenance for you and your animals

**Expanded Commentary**

Every majestic line of the construction tour — the raised vault, the switched alternation, the unrolled deck, the extracted water, the summoned pasture, the seated anchors — is gathered into a single purpose-word and handed across. The universe just described turns out to be a provision-contract, with the audience and their flocks named as beneficiaries.

**Matāʿan: A Word of Provision With an Expiry Inside It**

The word *matāʿ* names goods-for-use: provision, convenience, the serviceable stuff of life — yet the Book's wider usage has dyed the term with temporariness: matāʿ is what you hold for a term, enjoyment-ware with its limit built in. Both tones sound here. As provision, the word dignifies the whole inventory: sky and earth and their works exist *as* service, delivered; the cosmos is not indifferent scenery but stocked equipment. As temporary-use, it quietly posts the expiry: this splendid assemblage is *interim*, a running facility, and facilities close. The Book elsewhere uses exactly this vocabulary to draw exactly this conclusion: "These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination." (3:14). The pleasures are real — matāʿ is not illusion — and precisely because real, their service-status matters: sustenance is a means, and means exist toward ends beyond themselves. To confuse the facility with the destination is the exact error the sūrah will shortly attach to its doomed class. The stock is lavish; the stock is interim; both halves of the definition must be carried together.

**Lakum — Then, Remarkably, Your Animals**

The beneficiaries come in two named classes: *for you* — and then *for your livestock*, the ʿanʿām, the grazing herds. The second naming is a marvel of consideration buried in a routine suffix. The flocks did not need to be mentioned; the humans were already covered, and the animals would presumably follow. Instead the herds receive explicit equity in the divine provision-plan: God's architectural campaign is formally certified as being for *them* too. Men grow from this a serious trusteeship: the livestock under their rods are fellow-beneficiaries of the same grant — not property-ephemera but named recipients whose pasture, water, and deck-stable earth were designed into the contract on their behalf. The human relation to the herd is thereby reframed as stewardship between two client-classes of one Provider: the shepherd is the grant's administrator, not its owner-of-record. In a desert economy where flock and family stand or fall together, the verse's pairing also simply tells the truth of lived experience — and grounding the pairing in heaven's own provision-language sanctifies the whole economy of the tents.

**The Identical Line, Twice-Certified in the Book**

Here is a fact of the text too striking to pass over: this closing line appears again, word for word, elsewhere — "all as ˹a means of˺ sustenance for you and your animals." (80:32) — sealing the parallel chapter's own cosmic-food audit. Identical purpose-clauses cap two separate provisioning inventories, like a clerk's standard endorsement stamped on two files in succession. The repetition is a pedagogy of emphasis: the Book's refrain-engineering ensures that no attentive reciter can march through either passage without arriving at, and retaining, the identical destination. There is also an interlock-argument available to any reader who reflects: two chapter-contexts, differently built, converging on the same sentence — testimony to the single authorship supervising both files, drawing both inventories into one doctrine of provision. For memorization cultures, such twin-stamping does quiet permanent work: learn the one, and half of the other is already lodged. The verse is, in the most literal sense, unforgettable by design — a seal pressed twice so the wax of two separate hearts sets with the same device.

**The Whole Majestic Tour Refiled Under a Single Purpose**

Consider the reclassification this verse performs. The sequence seemed to be engineering: roofs, schedules, foundations, stocks, fasteners. The closing line declares the true folder: all of it — every vertical and horizontal, every extraction and anchoring — is *sustenance*. Cosmology is a lunch-ledger. The scope of the claim should stagger: the largest objects in experience are re-filed under the category of the audience's daily need, which means the audience's daily need is paperwork signed at the scale of creation. Nothing is too small for His provisioning and nothing too large for His provisioning-instrument — the sky exists, in part, so that the meal exists. Against every instinct that divides the majestic from the mundane — one class of things for worship-wonder, another for eating — the verse welds the classes: wonder is the meal's overhead roof, and the meal is the wonder's beneficiary. The sneerers, on this accounting, occupy the position of lodgers who grandly dispute the landlord while standing inside a building, on a floor, beneath a roof, beside a well and a grazing-range, all of it certified *for them* in the Builder's own filing.

**Provision-Irony: The sūrah's Standing Theme Restated**

This tilts the verse toward irony, and the sūrah has been cultivating the irony from its opening. The mocking audience — the men who demand whether decayed bones will really be reassembled — run their entire material existence on the exact provisioning-flow the verses certify: their morning is His extraction, their well-water His extraction, their herds' green His extraction, their floor His anchoring. Denial, in such a posture, is not a conclusion from evidence; it is a consumption of evidence while refusing the source-label. The sister-chapter's version of the same audit was even more explicit about the supply-lines of the denial: rain sent down, grain and gardens issued — "and sent down from rainclouds pouring water," (78:14) — while the Day that will audit the ledgers is dismissed as fantasy. A creature can be thoroughly, minutely, hourly sustained, and still translate no sustenance into acknowledgment; that is the human marvel the sūrah exhibits, and this verse is its quiet summation: everything for you — and from you, what?

**Then the Cover-All Arrives**

The provision-contract has an audit-date, and the recitation turns to it without transition-mercy: "But, when the Supreme Disaster comes to pass—" (79:34). The same creation that stands stacked here as service will stand re-categorized at that arrival; the matāʿ-hour expires; the ledger of provision becomes available as the ledger of evidence. The tenants who filled the purpose-clause will meet the Purpose-Giver's settlement-day, prepared or unprepared, stocked only with what they sent ahead. The sūrah has brought its audience from oath to cosmology to lunch-table; it will now bring them, in three strikes, to the bifurcation of every striving underneath that roof.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:34

> But, when the Supreme Disaster comes to pass—

**Expanded Commentary**

The provision-tour ends, and the alarm sounds. Without a single transitional mercy, the recitation turns from the stocked house to the day the house-audit arrives — and it names that day with one of the most fearsome titles in the whole vocabulary of the Last Things: the Overwhelmer, the Supreme One.

**Aṭ-Ṭāmmatu l-Kubrā: Named by What It Covers**

The noun *ṭāmmah* grows from a root of covering-over, submerging, overwhelming utterly — the catastrophe defined not by its mechanism but by its *effect*: everything else goes under it. Financial collapse, military disaster, bereavement — every lesser calamity men know has a waterline, some precinct it does not reach, some remnant of ordinary life that continues beside it. The Ṭāmmah is the event with no dry precinct: the occurrence under which all other occurrences are submerged, the news beside which there is no other news. The appended *al-kubrā* — the Supreme, the Greater — then completes the rating. Other calamities are submersions of a field, a city, a generation; this is the submersion of the categories themselves. The Book's naming-strategy here is worth stating plainly: it does not describe the Day by what it *is* inside — no man's categories would hold — but by what it *does* to everything outside it. Disaster-theology in two words: an event whose scale can only be expressed as the covering of every scale you possess.

**The Gallery of Names the Sūrah Has Built for This Day**

Within these forty verses the Day has now collected a procession of titles, each isolating a different instrument of its terror. It opened as the quaking — the Rājifah of v6, named by the tremor in the crust. It waited within "a single Shout" (79:13) — named by the summons that raises. Here it arrives as the Cover-All — named by the overwhelming spread. Quaking, shout, submerging: ground beneath, command above, waterline over all. The multiplication of names is deliberate eschatological pedagogy. A reality too large for one name is declension'd through many, and each name re-arms the warning against the specific deafness that survived the last. The heart untouched by *quaking* may register *summons*; the heart that waves off a shout may grasp *submersion*. And there is a second service in the gallery: the names certify one another, accumulating like attestations. A fiction is typically one-dimensional; the Day arrives in the text with a whole registry of aspects, the way real things arrive — which is why the Book keeps generating new ports of entry for the same appointment rather than repeating one label until it dulls.

**Fa-Idhā Jāʾat: The Grammar of Scheduled Certainty**

The opening construction is worth grammatical attention: *idhā* — *when* — governing the verb of arrival. In the eschatological register this particle never behaves like the speculative *if*; it functions as the scheduler's *when*, the certainty-marker of an appointment already on the calendar. The Book does not hypothesize the Ṭāmmah; it timetables it. Against this stands the deniers' grammar from earlier in the sūrah — their mocking "Will we really be restored...?" casting the resurrection as a conjectural absurdity, an *if* padded with scorn. The two grammars confront each other across the chapter: sneering conditionality versus announced scheduling. And the Book's *when* has a track record the reader is invited to weigh — the same *when* structures its flood-warnings and its verdicts on earlier nations, and those appointments all kept their dates. Language performs, here, the faith it asks for: certainty so complete that the future event is discussed in the grammar of the train-schedule rather than the grammar of the wager. The audience is left with a stark either-or in which hedging is no longer one of the options.

**The Dash: Suspense as a Warning-Technique**

The verse ends mid-air — *comes to pass—* — and both translation and Arabic leave the clause hanging before the consequences arrive in the next breath. The suspension is itself the device. Recitation places the audience exactly where the event will place them: at the threshold, leaning forward, weight on the front foot, with no settled ground yet described beyond the arrival. A threat fully diagrammed can be methodically negotiated; a threat suspended at the dash forces the heart to supply its own completions — and the heart, left to complete it, always under-imagines, which keeps the dread live rather than diagrammed-away. The technique belongs to the Book's deeper rhetoric of mercy: the pause is a doorway deliberately held open. As long as the sentence has not yet listed the consequences, the listener stands in the antechamber where turning back is still possible. The breath before the next verse is, in miniature, the whole space between the warning and the event — and the recitation makes the audience wait inside it, exactly once, to feel its dimensions.

**What Goes Under: The Calculations of Escape**

The Ṭāmmah's waterline reaches, specifically, the human systems for dodging the audit: schedules that assume continuance, fortifications that assume shelter, wealth that assumes bail-out options, connections that assume advocacy. About the lesser prototype of the same truth the Book had already joked grimly at the escape-planners of every fortress: "Wherever you may be, death will overcome you—even if you were in fortified towers." (4:78). Towers are the honest symbol of the instinct — height, stone, stocked blankets, certified walls — and they fail at the waterline of a single heartbeat's decree exactly as a tent would. Now scale the image to the Day that covers *all days*: there is no fortress-logistics for the Ṭāmmah, no altitude above its reach, no appointment-book that runs past it. The naming as *submersion-event* thereby does juridical work: it forecloses in advance the entire genre of evasive planning by which the sneering mind copes with unwelcome appointments — postponement, mitigation, exemption. The one appointment with no exemption-clause is being named, and the audience is being given this verse to settle the matter while settling remains possible.

**Then the Memory Module Is Switched On**

The hanging clause resolves, and the first consequence named is interior — the archive opens by itself: "the Day every person will remember all ˹their˺ striving," (79:35). Before any scale is set up, before any book is flown into any hand, the Day's first instrument is the human's own memory, switched into compelled recall. The striving-verb is coming — the same striving that filed the tyrant's record will be the unit in which every record is read back. The Overwhelmer covers the outer world; the forced remembering unseals the inner one — and the next verse steps from the waterline directly into the courtroom.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:35

> the Day every person will remember all ˹their˺ striving

**Expanded Commentary**

The Ṭāmmah's first listed instrument is not fire, not scales, not even a summons. It is memory. On that Day the human archive opens itself, and every person reads back — with total recall and total clarity — the whole of what he spent his striving on. The courtroom's first piece of evidence is the defendant's own recollection.

**Yawma Yatadhakkaru: The Grammar of Compelled Recall**

The verb *yatadhakkaru* — he will remember, he will recollect — carries in its form the sense of strenuous, reflective re-collection: not a passing thought but the mind *assembling* what was filed, re-collecting the scattered into one view. In this world, memory is the mercy of drift: deeds fade, embarrassments blur, the long-ago becomes anecdote. The Day verb announces the end of drift. Recall becomes *compelled* — total, orderly, and involuntary, like an archive whose locks all release at once under the Keeper's order. The significance for the proceedings is deep: the judgment requires no external projector to establish the facts. Each witness carries the footage, and the Day's first ruling is that the footage plays. This also reframes earthly forgetfulness: what the sneerers comfort themselves by forgetting is not erased, only *deferred*, filed in the very vault of the self that will, on command, open. Man is his own evidence-bag, and the seal is dated. To hear this verse aright is to understand that nothing once done ever truly recedes — it only waits for its Day's verb.

**Mā Saʿā: The Striving-Word Comes Home**

What gets remembered is specified by the same verb the sūrah used for the tyrant's record: *saʿā* — "then turned his back, striving ˹against the truth˺." (79:22). The striving-word is deliberate universalization. Every human life is, mechanically, a deposit of saʿy: effort expended, motion spent, days poured into channels. Palaces differ from hovels only in the scale of the channels. At recall-time the audit does not weigh the striving's *volume* — all men strive full-tank and exhaust — it weighs the striving's *aim*: toward what the effort was vectored. That single refinement converts the verse into a live instrument for the present moment: every current exertion is a future recollection-in-waiting, and the exhausted sleeper at day's end has, without exception, banked one more entry to be read back at the assembly. The tyrant's saʿy was vectored against the truth and its reading will be torment; the verse extends identical mechanics to every striver, neutral and absolute. Striving itself is not the variable — direction is. And the choice of *this* verb, at *this* station of the Day, turns the audience's very industriousness into the audit-trail by which they will be known.

**The Twin Verse Where Remembering Breaks Into Regret**

The Book owns an exact twin for this verse, in which the recall and its consequence unfold in one breath: "and Hell is brought forth on that Day—this is when every ˹disbelieving˺ person will remember ˹their own sins˺. But what is the use of remembering then? They will cry, 'I wish I had sent forth ˹something good˺ for my ˹true˺ life.'" (89:23-24). The twin adds the two beats this verse leaves implicit: the uselessness of recall that arrives after the lock-out, and the cry it rips out — *what is the use of remembering then?* Remembering is salvation's organ while it can still edit behavior; post-lockout, the same organ is punishment's whip, because it only replays. Note the wish's precise grammar: *sent forth* — the regretter does not wish he had *been* different in some vaporous sense but that he had *capital forwarded*, good dispatched ahead of him like provision posted to the future address he now occupies. Regret, at that Day, speaks in the idiom of remittance, of deposits not made. This sūrah's compressed line holds the same cry folded inside it — every recalled striving that was mis-vectored arrives with its own inaudible *too late* attached.

**Insān: The Singular That Dissolves the Crowd**

The verse remembers *al-insān* — man, each human, in the singular-form collective. The muster-scenes of the earlier verses gathered crowds: the earth splitting with emerging masses, humanity swarming to the caller's voice. Here the crowd-grammar dissolves into units. No tribe remembers; no council remembers; the singular man remembers, one archive per soul, and the plural comforts — numbers, company, shared excuses, the reassuring anonymity of belonging to a large group — fall away at the recall-verb. This re-sorting into singularity is one of the Day's quietest terrors, and the poetry of its placement should be marked: *assembly* was the great gathering; *remembering* is the great isolation. In the interior theater of the recall there are no co-defendants, no gallery, no advocate yet — only the one self and its total footage. The Book returns to this solitude under several images across the sūrahs, but its simplest form is here: the plural species reduced, by a verb in the singular, to separate files, each reading itself in the locked room of its own memory.

**Memory as the Court's Own Technology**

Step back and the architecture of the judgment clarifies: evidence-production on that Day does not depend on summoning witnesses who might be bribed, intimidated, lost over time, or confused — court-failure modes familiar to every human legal history. The perpetrator himself is the archive, the archive is perfect, and the archivist's release-command opens it. No retriever can lose years, no record can be misfiled between departments, no appeal can challenge the footage's authenticity, because the keeper of the footage and the accused are the same person. Justice administered through the witness every man carries in his skull is justice with its procedural gaps at zero — and knowing this changes the present: the vault being filled today is one's own skull, the filing is continuous, and no deed needs to be *sent anywhere*; it is already filed at origin. The merciful corollary follows for the well-vectored: the same total recall will also play back the hidden good, the unwitnessed kindness, the striving no ledger on earth recorded. The archive is as complete on the credit side as on the debit — the terror and the comfort of it are one mechanism.

**Then the Exhibit Is Unscreened Before Every Eye**

Recall is private; what follows is public: "and the Hellfire will be displayed for all to see—" (79:36). The interior archive having been read, the exterior destination is unveiled — exposed in open view, for every eye in the muster. The verse-to-come turns memory's warrant into sight's verdict, and with the display of the Fire the bifurcation machinery of the sūrah's final movement begins: the two files, the two strivings, the two homes.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:36

> and the Hellfire will be displayed for all to see—

**Expanded Commentary**

Recall has done its interior work; now the exterior exhibit is unveiled. The Fire — until this moment a matter of warning, metaphor, and mockery — is brought out into unscreened view, and every eye assembled at the muster is made its witness. The threats end here and the tour of consequences begins.

**Burrizat: The Verb of Total Exposure**

The verb *burrizat* — from a root meaning to come out into the open ground, to be fully disclosed in the clear space — carries a theatrical exactness: something long kept behind partitions is now staged with none. The Fire had been the most scoffed-at item in the preaching precisely because it was unseeable; deniers trade on the invisibility of the warned-of, and every sneer in the sūrah's earlier movement drew credit from the fact that no blaze could be subpoenaed into a Meccan assembly. This verb forecloses that trade. The concealed is unscreened; the disputed object becomes the environment of the assembly itself. Note the divine passive: no agent need be pictured hauling it forth — the grammatical form is the pure event, the exposure *happening*, as if curtains simply cease to exist. And the placement matters: the display follows immediately upon the forced remembering, so that every soul meets the exhibit *with its own record already running in recall*. Evidence inside, destination outside — the two disclosures are synchronized.

**Liman Yarā: For Whoever Sees**

The clause hangs two depths inside three words. On the surface: the display is staged *for every seer* — no screened boxes, no obstructed views, no delegation sent to look and report back; the muster itself is the audience-hall, and attendance is universal. Beneath that runs a second reading the commentators marked: *for whoever sees* hints that sight itself, on that Day, is granted — the dead-eyed, the blind, all made seers of this; perception is requisitioned for the occasion whether welcomed or not. The Book elsewhere plays on this enforced clarity with terrible courtesy: hearing sharp, sight sharp, on the Day they come — the sensory faculties, so manageable in the world's fog, returned to full specification precisely when their sharpness becomes testimony against their owner. One may also hear the clause as a grim equalizer: kings and peasants share one eyesight-row before this exhibit. There are no boxes at the display of the Fire, and no one is excused the view for rank, fragility, or disbelief.

**The Twin Verse: Brought Forth on That Day**

The Book's parallel scene uses a companion verb from the same family of forced emergence — "and Hell is brought forth on that Day" (89:23) — and there the emergence is explicitly sequential: first the Lord comes for judgment, then the ranks are formed, then the Fire is hauled into view, then the remembering and the regret-break. Such twin attestations do the Book's usual double duty. For the listener, repetition across chapters converts scene into doctrine: this is not one passage's lurid imagination but a fixed station in the Book's eschatology, attested from two chapter-positions with the same choreography. For the denier, the twins close the escape-suggestion that the image is decorative: reiterated, structured, placed in sequence with muster and judgment, the display is procedural — a step in a procession of steps, each with the sober grammar of arrangement rather than the wild grammar of fantasy. The recitation treats the Fire's unveiling with the calm of a procession-marshal naming the next station, and the calm is more unnerving than any shriek would be.

**The Pass-Over Decree That Includes Everyone**

How total is this exposure? The Book's most sweeping statement makes the sighting — even the crossing — a universal decree: "There is none of you who will not pass over it. ˹This is˺ a decree your Lord must fulfil. Then We will deliver those who were devout, leaving the wrongdoers there on their knees." (19:71-72). None exempt; decree framed as *must-fulfil*; and the differentiation reserved for the passage's second half: deliverance for the devout, kneeling for the wrongdoers. The display of this verse is therefore not a spectacle merely for the damned — it functions differently for the two great classes who witness it. To its destined inmates: unmasking, the loss of the last comfort of abstraction. To the delivered: the sight of what their awe had always taken seriously and their Lord had always carried them past — their rescue underlined by the viewing of the danger. Same exhibit, two educations. The Book's justice-theatre is complete only because both classes see: vindication requires that mercy's people witness the fire-line they were carried over.

**The Display as the Warnings' Vindication**

Within this very sūrah, the spectacle closes a long evidentiary file. The Day had been named by its trembling — hearts in horror at v8; by its summons — the one shout of v13; by its cover-all — the Ṭāmmah of v34. Mockery had treated each name as empty thunder. The display verb converts every earlier predicate into verified description: the trembling was warranted, the shout was scheduled, the covering was literal. Nothing in the warnings was exaggeration; the under-statement, if anything, was on the side of the words. This is a general law of the Book's eschatological rhetoric: its terror-language, attacked as fanaticism in the safety of the world, is framed in advance as *reportage* — the wording insists it will one day be recognized as sober inventory. Every preacher who warned on this Book's authority deposits this verse as his receipt: what he described verbally, the Day will demonstrate optically, and the gap between the two will itself be an accusation against every auditor who called the report inflated.

**Then the Two Files Are Called by Name**

With the exhibit standing in open view, the sorting proceeds by the grammar of *as-for*: "then as for those who transgressed" (79:37). The crowd of the muster — every soul now seer of the Fire — is re-filed by two markers only: transgression joined to world-preference on one side, majesty-awe joined to desire-restraint on the other. The next five verses perform the entire bifurcation of the species in two mirrored sentences, each ending with its own *certainly* and its own *home*. The display was the common evidence before both benches; what differs is what each file did with its striving — and the verse-by-verse exercise that follows shows how little, in the end, separates the two records: one preference, one restraint, and the distance between two homes.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:37

> then as for those who transgressed

**Expanded Commentary**

Five words, and the sentence refuses to finish itself. The first file of the bifurcation is opened and named — the transgressors — but the verdict is held in suspension across three more verses, so that the diagnosis can be fully spelled out before the sentence lands. The grammar imitates the court: charge first, specification second, verdict last.

**Fa-Ammā: The Sorter's Particle**

The construction *fa-ammā...* — *then as for...* — is the language's machinery for partitioning a whole into addressed cases: a holder of many files opens one and says, *now, this one*. Paired shortly with its sibling (*and as for...*), it will exhaust the assembly into two and only two files — a fact of theological arithmetic worth pausing on. At the Day's sorting there is no third ammā, no miscellaneous drawer for the undecided, no provisional shelf. The Book's bifurcations are always total, and the particle here is their little gate: through it passes the whole human crowd, case by case, into complete classification. The *fa-* prefix stitches the sorting to what preceded, tying consequence-order to sight-order — the display stood revealed, *then* the sorting begins; first the common evidence, then the differentiated sentences. Procedure again: the Book's Day runs on sequence, not on explosion, and every particle of the sequence is deliberate.

**Alladhīna Ṭaghaw: The File Named by Its Verb**

The class is not called unbelievers, pagans, sinners-at-large; it is named by one verb — *ṭaghaw*: they exceeded, overshot, transgressed the bound. The root (ṭ-gh-y) runs through images of water overflowing its banks, of a measure spilling past its mark: transgression pictured as substance crossing the line its nature was given. The naming is diagnostic news about the human problem itself. Man's rebellion is not pictured here as deficient substance but as *unboundaried* substance — appetite, pride, energy overflowing the bank its Lord marked for it. The law-and-limit frame this imposes on the whole moral life is permanent: every soul is a measured channel, and catastrophe begins at the overflow-point. Note also the grammar of plurality: the file is a class, *those who*, defined purely by the shared verb — membership is behavioral, not tribal, racial, or confessional. Anyone who runs this verb is in this file; no one outside the verb is. The classification is at once terrifying and perfectly open — it convicts without respect of persons and cannot be bribed by identity.

**The Root the Tyrant Already Gave the Sūrah**

The sūrah has met this root before, in its central courtroom. Of Pharaoh, heaven's own indictment was filed in the same letters: his Lord described him as the one who had transgressed — the tyrant's whole biography compressed into the ṭ-gh-y verb before a gauntlet of signs and seizures. Now the singular becomes plural, the case becomes the category. The movement is a master-stroke of legal-pedagogical design: the audience watched one man carry the root through his whole arc — transgression, indulgence, supremacy-claim, double seizure — and then finds the *same root* naming an entire contemporary class, open for enrollment, defined by nothing Egyptian at all. Any Meccan chief who followed the middle story with satisfaction is here handed the classification-form: the verb generalizes; the prototype was merely first in line. And because the Pharaoh-file ended in the water, the class-file is shadowed in advance by that ending — to carry the verb is to carry the prototype's itinerary, and the itinerary's fixed stops are public record.

**Why the Charge Stands Before the Sentence**

The structure of these five words and their two successor verses — class named, then conduct specified, then verdict delivered — mirrors the due sequence of a judgment that means to be seen as just. No sentence falls on an unnamed party; no party is sentenced on an unspecified charge. The Book could have compressed the whole bifurcation into a single parallel sentence; instead it stretches the first file across three verse-units, letting the specification (their preference of the near life) be stated and weighed before the home is declared. The stretch is a courtesy to comprehension and a deepened dread at once. For the reflecting listener: the charges are checkable — examine the specification against your own record before the verdict of the passage arrives. For the implicated: the suspense is itself a mercy-period, a held gavel, the last interval in which the specification might still be edited. Judgment in this Book is never an ambush; it is a procession, and processions grant the watching crowd time to understand each station as it passes.

**The Specification Chosen: One Preference Only**

When the charge is spelled, it is a single act of ranking: "and preferred the ˹fleeting˺ life of this world," (79:38). Not a tally of atrocities, not a curriculum of vices — one preference, named once, carries the file. The economy is stunning and needs to be received slowly: the whole difference between the two final homes is traced, in this sūrah's summation, to a choice of what to put first. A soul need not have built temples of cruelty; the preference alone, held and acted from, generates the file. This strips the deniers' favorite comfort — *we are no tyrants, no criminals* — of all function: the specification is set deliberately low and central, at the priority-level where every ordinary person lives. At the same time it locates the evil where it genuinely roots: crimes are the preference's foliage, and the Book, pruning to the root with one verb, obliges the audience to inspect their own ordering of loves rather than their police records.

**The Suspended Predicate and What It Holds**

Grammatically, this verse's ammā-clause waits three units for its completion — the home sentence that finally lands — and the suspension is a discipline for the reader as much as for the assembly. Holding the verdict back trains the heart to experience the charge's weight un-numbed by a fast resolution; the Book refuses to let the sentence become a formula skimmed past. Meanwhile the second file waits its turn behind the first: "And as for those who were in awe..." — the counter-construction already in view. The sūrah is walking its audience over a bridge planked with two *as-for*s, and the middle planks are the preference and the restraint, the two pivots on which every human record turns. The transgressor's file now stands open, specified, and awaiting only its address — the address the Fire's display has just been staged to make concrete.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:38

> and preferred the ˹fleeting˺ life of this world

**Expanded Commentary**

The charge against the first file is now spelled out, and it is exactly one act: a preference. They did not merely err, drift, or get trapped — they *preferred*. The whole difference between the two eternal addresses is located, in this line, at the level of what a soul puts first.

**Wa-Ātharū: Preference as a Deed With Consequences**

The verb *āthara* means to prefer, to give precedence, to choose one thing over another — and its forensic power lies in treating preference as an *action*, something done, with the full moral weight of a deed. Men instinctively classify their cravings as weather: things that happen to them, conditions they live under. The verb re-files craving as conduct: at every fork of consequence, the self held two objects and *performed* a ranking, and rankings — repeated, embodied, funded — are choices as real as signatures. This transfers the locus of judgment inward, past behavior into ordering: the court does not merely ask what the file did; it asks what the file *put first*, reading conduct as the printout of priority. There is a precision here modern ears should catch: no one prefers in a void. Preference is comparison executed — nearer weighed against greater, visible against promised — so the verb implies an audit, albeit a corrupt one. The transgressor was not unconsidering; he considered, and mis-ranked. Culpability at the level of the scales themselves, not only at the level of what they dispensed.

**Al-Ḥayāta d-Dunyā: The Name That Contains the Demotion**

What was preferred carries its own verdict inside its name: *ad-dunyā*, from a root of nearness and lowness — the *nearer, lower* life. The Book did not wait for philosophers to devalue the present world; it named the world, from the first, by its rank: the near one, the lower one — near in access, lower in worth. Everything in the world's favor is packed into *near*: immediate, sensory, graspable, certain-in-hand. Everything against it sits in *lower*: a brief tenancy inside matāʿ, the provision-ware with the expiry built in. Even the translators' added gloss — *fleeting* — merely surfaces the Book's standing doctrine about this object: "These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination." (3:14). Two addresses offered across every day of a man's life, and the name of the cheaper one tells him, in advance, exactly what it is. To prefer the near-lower over the far-higher is thus not to be deceived about the goods; it is to buy, knowingly, at the bad ratio — the label was never hidden.

**The Arithmetic of the Broken Scale**

How does a rational creature mis-rank so catastrophically? The Book's diagnostic tradition answers with the.clock of desire: the near object enjoys a privilege in every comparison because desire counts immediacy as weight. The promised, being distant, enters the scale aged and discounted; the present, being touchable, enters with interest pre-paid. Unless an external weight — awe, in this sūrah's own prescription — is placed beside the promised, the broken scale tips near every time. And the Book marks where desire left as sole guide terminates: "And who could be more astray than those who follow their desires with no guidance from Allah?" (28:50). *Misguidance*, in this analysis of the preference-pathology, is not chiefly an information failure — it is a weighting failure, guidance absent from the scale-pan. Hence the sūrah's architecture: before narrating this preference, it spent nine verses exhibiting the awe-producing evidences — the roof, the cycles, the deck, the Ṭāmmah — supplying the missing counterweights, so that no listener could mis-rank afterward and call the scales honest.

**The Whole Inventory They Preferred It Against**

The bitterness of the preference sharpens when set beside what was passed over. They preferred the near-lower *against*: the raised vault and its flawless finish; the administered night and the extracted blaze; the spread deck, the summoned water, the pasture, the anchors — and the Purpose-Giver of the entire audited assemblage, who had re-filed creation itself as sustenance *for you*. The provision-contract had stated its beneficiaries with embarrassing clarity; the preference still chose the provisions over the Provider, the matāʿ over its own destination. Nor is this exotic: the refusal-pattern described is the species' daily metabolism — creation's furniture accepted, creation's inference declined. The verse thereby measures the transgressor's file with terrifying fairness: not men who saw nothing, but men who saw everything and ranked it last. The evidence for the far-higher had been stacked to the vault; *preference* looked at the stack, then at the near-lower, and put the near-lower first. Judgment, when it comes, does not arrive upon the ignorant; it arrives upon the choosers.

**Preference Read as Verdict**

The deeper reason one preference can carry a whole file is that the Book treats life's final sentence as latent in life's ordering of loves. Priorities are the true creed: a man's real theology is whatever he chooses first under pressure, whatever he funds, defends, and organizes his mornings around — not what he recites when asked. The Day's sifting, on this view, is less a fresh verdict than a reading-off: the preference held through life, now surfaced as the file's operative line. This is why the sūrah can afford its brevity. No catalogue of transgressions needs enumerating; interpret the priority and the catalogue is implied, since every practice downstream of a mis-ranked preference inherits the mis-ranking. For the listener, the verse installs a single diagnostic question with Day-force: *what, today, is chosen first?* — because the answer given by conduct now is the verdict read aloud later. The gap between professed and performed preference is the exact gap the Day closes, and this one verb, *they preferred*, is the hinge of its closing.

**The Address Their Preference Purchased**

And now the held gavel falls, for the first file: preference becomes address. The sentence is the shortest of the chapter's heavy lines and the most unsoftenable in its certainty — the Fire itself as *home*, in the next breath of the recitation. The near thing that was preferred first is succeeded by the near thing that cannot be refused: a permanence to match the preference it was built on. The verse's structure leaves the injustice impossible: a man is not sentenced for another's ranking, nor surprised by a standard never announced; he receives, stably and forever, the house of the god he prioritized — and the sūrah has spent its whole length announcing both the house and the alternative.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:39

> the Hellfire will certainly be ˹their˺ home

**Expanded Commentary**

The suspension breaks. Across three verses the file was opened, named, and specified; now the verdict lands with doubled emphasis and one terrible word of address: the Fire, *home*. Six words that complete the first half of the bifurcation and turn the whole preference-analysis into a sentence of permanent residence.

**Fa-Inna: The Gavel of Doubled Certainty**

The particles *fa-inna* — *so then, truly, certainly* — close the conditional arc that began with *as for those who...*, and their combined force is emphatic in the way of final orders: no hedging, no appeal-window built into the sentence's own grammar. Arabic emphasis here is not rhetorical surplus. Earlier in the sūrah the deniers performed certainty-mockery — *will we really be restored?* — sneering at the very notion that aftermaths could be assured. The verdict answers in their own register of assurance: *really* — with the doubled particle doing what their sarcastic *really* could not do, namely certify. Observe, too, that the certainty attaches to the most doubted object in their entire creed: not the Hour generally, not the judgment abstractly, but this Fire, this residence, this outcome for this file. The Book deliberately spends its strongest grammatical guarantees on the items its audience most strongly doubted, meeting incredulity at the level of assertion-force and out-certain-ing it. Emphasis-particles as counter-sneer: the sentence's form enacts its content.

**Hiya: The Fire Itself — Nothing Standing Between**

Between the certainty and the home stands the pronoun *hiya* — *it, itself* — grammatically separable, semantically iron: the Hellfire, *it and no substitute*, is the shelter. The pronoun rules out every softening proxy: not a symbol of it, not a consequence merely *like* it, not a state of mind poetically its equal — *itself*. The Book's eschatology has always resisted being evaporated into metaphor by the squeamish of later ages, and this little pronoun does much of the resisting at the sentence-level: identity is asserted, not resemblance. Whatever analogies the fire of this world offers — and every burn-victim and furnace-tender knows their rudiments — the verse claims the original for the Day, the thing of which all worldly burning is only the distant sample. A severe mercy hides inside the insistence: vagueness about *what exactly* the consequence is would be the final cruelty toward those still weighing the preference. The verdict's candor is part of the warning's completeness — the sentence names its referent so that no one may later say he mis-ranked in the dark.

**Al-Maʾwā: Home as the Bitterest Word in the Verdict**

Then comes the word that gives the sentence its unique chill: *maʾwā* — shelter, dwelling, refuge, *home*. In the language's ordinary use the word is warm: the sought place, the roof after the road, the end of exposure. The verdict deliberately keeps this warm word and changes its house. Why not simply *destination* or *punishment*? Because *home* asserts what the punishment-vocabulary cannot: permanence, belonging, the closing of all further moving-day. A sentence can be served; a home is returned to — the term disallows the imagination of release-in-time the way *destination* might permit it. There is, moreover, a juridical subtlety in lodging the transgressor in a *home*: the outcome is presented not as invasion from outside but as the preference's own consummation — men receive, stably, the address corresponding to what they chose first; the court's work is the consignment of each file to its natural terminus. The Book will shortly give the identical word to the Garden — one noun, two residences — and the twinning makes the word's full range audible: *home* awaits every file; only the neighborhood is in dispute, and the neighborhood was chosen upstream.

**The Mirrored Sentences of the Two Files**

The full architecture of the verdict now stands compare-able, since the second file receives, two verses later, a sentence built from the same materials: "Paradise will certainly be ˹their˺ home." (79:41). Same certainty, same *itself*, same *home* — the parallelism is the justice. No loaded grammar blesses one file and burdens the other; the sentence-form is scrupulously equal, and only the destination differs, because only the upstream lives differed. Symmetry of verdict-construction is the Book's recurring signature at bifurcation-points: the scales of the grammar themselves are level, so that equity is visible in the very shape of the sentences, before a single theology-argument is offered. It also hands every listener a stark comparison-exercise: two identical skeletons, two opposed completions — and the variable is exactly one preference, one restraint. Grammar as the last witness called: the sentences stand side by side in the recitation like the two roads in the old parables, and the audience walks between them hearing both endings read in the same tone of certainty.

**Address as the Preference's Mirror-Image**

Step back along the file and the sentence's fairness shows station by station. Exhibit: the Fire displayed before every eye — no hidden doom. Charge: transgression — a self-defined class any man may step out of by abandoning the verb. Specification: one preference — stated openly, checkable by the listener against his own ordering of loves. Verdict: home — the preference made permanent. Each station offered an exit that the next narrowed; the sequence as a whole is less an ambush than a narrowing corridor of announced turns. Against this corridor the deniers' whole posture — delay, mock, out-mock the warning — appears as the only irrational move available: it treats a documented process as an unsettled rumor. And beneath the severity runs the sūrah's positive aim: this verdict is published *here*, at length, in mercy's time, precisely so that some file-holders will hand their preference back across the counter while the counter is still open. A sentence read aloud in advance is, by the mere fact of being heard, not yet a sentence.

**And the Counter-File Is Called**

The recitation does not leave the assembly standing at the first bench. Across the courtroom now comes the counter-construction, and with it the reversal of every term — the file "who were in awe of standing before their Lord" (79:40), the restraint laid upon the self, and the other *certainly*, the other home. The sūrah's bifurcation is about to double back over identical ground with opposite signs: where the first file leaned forward into the near-lower, the second stands back in awe of the Standing itself; where the first let the self run, the second forbids it. The two sentences are approaching that will measure every human record by the same pair of pivots — and the clause that opens the counter-file contains, in the phrase *standing before their Lord*, the whole secret the first file never kept in view.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:40

> And as for those who were in awe of standing before their Lord and restrained themselves from ˹evil˺ desires

**Expanded Commentary**

The counter-file opens, and with it a complete doctrine of salvation in two conditions: awe of the Standing, and restraint of the self. Everything the first file failed to keep in view is here named as the second file's entire practice — and the verdict waiting at the end of this verse is the mirror of the one just delivered.

**Wa-Ammā Man Khāfa: The Counter-Sorter's Particle**

The sorting-machine's second arm — *and as for whoever...* — now takes up the crowd from the other side. Note the slight grammar-shift worth lingering on: the first file was *those who*, a class; this one reads *whoever* — the door-word of open enrollment. The choice is the Book's kindness built into a pronoun: the doomed class was stamped closed by its verb, but the saved class is phrased as an invitation — *whoever* — any soul, from any file of the past tense, may enter under this description by taking up its two practices now. The verb itself, *khāfa*, feared, was-awed, is in the Book's special register the fear that governs rather than the fear that paralyzes — khashya's twin: the reverence that organizes a life around its object. Fear here is not deficiency but principle of order, listed as the counter-file's founding credential exactly where the first file's transgression was listed as its founder. The two files are, at root, two relations to the same coming event: one mocked it out of mind, the other feared it into view.

**Maqāma Rabbihi: Fear of the Standing-Place**

The awe's object is given with unusual concreteness: not God's wrath abstractly, not death generically, but *the standing before his Lord* — *maqām*, the stance-site, the place of being stood for review. To fear the Standing is to keep the specific event present: the summoned halt, the uncovered record, the master before whom posture is made perfect and nothing lean-on-able remains. Fear so specified is a remarkable psychological instrument — it is prospective, detailed, and personal (*his* Lord: the relationship named, not a terror of the void), and it re-values every present moment as pre-hearing material. Merchants fear audits only when audits are on the calendar; this awe pins the audit to every day, since the day's date is hidden — the concealment of the Hour, seen from this verse's side, is what makes the awe continuous rather than seasonal. The sūrah has been building this posture from its opening: "[The deniers'] hearts on that Day will be trembling ˹in horror˺," (79:8) — the trembling that arrives involuntarily at the event is, for the counter-file, voluntarily rehearsed before it: they trembled early, on purpose, in time.

**The Book's Own Double-Attestation of This Awe**

So load-bearing is this exact phrase — awe of the Standing — that the Book stakes a whole promise-scheme on it elsewhere in nearly the same words: "And whoever is in awe of standing before their Lord will have two Gardens." (55:46). Two attestation-points, one posture, paired reward-systems — and the doubling is itself a doctrine: the awe is not one sūrah's local theme but a fixed peg in the bargain-structure of salvation, repeated so that no attentive reader can misfile it as rhetoric. The *whoever* of that parallel verse matches the *whoever* here, re-opening the same open door; and the *two Gardens* there balances tidily against the home this sūrah is about to name, expansion upon expansion. Repetition across chapters, once again, is the Book's self-certification: a reader meeting the formula the second time is meant to feel the click of a lock — the same key turning in a different door — and to conclude, correctly, that the architecture behind both doors is one. Revelation repeats precisely those lines on which destinies depend; the echo here is the sound of destiny-dependence.

**Wa-Nahā: The Self Forbidden by the Self**

The second condition descends from the sublime to the daily: he *restrained the self from desire* — *nahā*, forbade, prohibited, the same verb-family as formal prohibition, now aimed inward. The portrait is of a man policing his own nafs: not passionless, but *lawed* — carrying an internal magistrate empowered to refuse his appetites at the counter before the transaction. Note the exquisite reflexivity: the self that desires is the self that is forbidden, by the self that fears the Standing — one soul, three stations of an interior court. The awe supplies the law's authority; the restraint is the law's execution. Neither survives alone, and the verse lists them in their causal order: vision of the Standing first, policing of desire second — the near is only resistible in the light of the far. This ordering quietly indicts every ethic of mere willpower: desire out-muscles resolve across a lifetime, as the first file's preference demonstrates; only an object large enough to dwarf the desire — the Standing itself — gives the magistrate his warrant. Awe is therefore not ornamental piety; it is the restraining-order's only source of jurisdiction.

**The Desire-God the Other File Serves**

What exactly is restrained, when desire is restrained, the Book diagnoses elsewhere at full depth, in its most psychiatric passage: "Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god? ˹And so˺ Allah left them to stray knowingly, sealed their hearing and hearts, and placed a cover on their sight." (45:23). Desire enthroned is divinity misplaced — the analysis to end all analyses of the first file: their preference of the near-lower was, at bottom, worship; the hawā had the throne, the self had the liturgy, and the sealing of the organs was the enthronement's sacrament run to completion. Against that enthronement this verse's restraint is not prudery but counter-worship: each refusal of the desire is a dethronement-act, a daily denial of the rival god's claim, performed at the exact altar where the rival demands sacrifice. The two files, at their inmost depth, are thus two congregations — and the Day merely homes each congregation with its deity: the desire-servers to the home their service purchased, the Standing-fearers to the Lord their awe never let them forget.

**The Other Certainly, the Other Home**

The counter-file's suspension breaks with a verdict in parallel columns: "Paradise will certainly be ˹their˺ home." (79:41). Same grammar of certainty, same *itself*, same *home* — and at last the warm meaning of *maʾwā* returns to its native element: shelter after the road, refuge after the trial-ground, the Standing's exhaustion ended in residence. What the awe rehearsed as dread arrives, for them, transformed: they feared the Standing honestly, and the Standing's Lord seats them past it. The mirrored sentences now stand complete — the two files, the two practices, the two certainlys — and the sūrah wastes not a word marking the achievement; it simply lets the two homes face each other across the verses while the assembly — and every audience after it — chooses which sentence to finish in.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:41

> Paradise will certainly be ˹their˺ home

**Expanded Commentary**

The Garden sentence arrives in the exact grammatical mold of the Fire sentence — same certainty, same *itself*, same *home* — and in that identical mold lies the whole doctrine of measured justice: the two files are finished with the same gavel, and only the addresses differ. The bifurcation of humanity is now complete, word for mirrored word.

**The Mirror-Sentence and Its Equal Grammar**

Set the two verdicts side by side and the matching is total: certainty-particle, subject, *hiya*, *home*. Nothing in the sentence-form flatters the saved or sneers at the lost; the Book's justice declines even grammatical partiality. This equilibrium is a theological statement before it is a stylistic one. If mercy had wanted to trumpet, it could have appended to this verse the celebrations lavished on Paradise elsewhere — rivers, shades, throngs of attendants. Instead the sentence stays courtroom-bare, symmetrical with its twin, as though the court itself wanted the record to show that both verdicts were delivered with the same even hand. The reciter passing from the Fire's *certainly* to the Garden's *certainly* in consecutive breaths experiences the Book's scales bodily: two weights, one beam, no thumb. For a Messenger-and-audience accused by opponents of inventing consolations, the symmetry is also self-evidence: wish-fulfillment literature does not balance its sentences this way; it slants them. Level grammar is the signature of a Speaker for whom both outcomes are equally real.

**Maʾwā Restored to Its Native Warmth**

When the first file received *home*, the word's ordinary warmth turned to frost: shelter with no release date, belonging as doom. Here the same noun returns home to itself. *Maʾwā* — refuge, the place sought through the whole journey, where the sought is finally housed — now carries its full native freight: the end of exposure, the road exhausted, the door that stays open to you forever. The re-warming of the identical noun across five verses is one of the sūrah's quietest feats: the audience has just heard *home* used as the horror-word and now watches the same letters glow again, and in the watching learns the entire doctrine — the difference between the files was never in the *kind* of completeness each received, only in which completeness. There is no version of the Day in which a soul ends homeless: every file goes home. Salvation and damnation are, structurally, the same event with different records — the consignment of each striving to its corresponding residence — which is why the awe that practices for the Standing is described in the Book as the only rational posture: everything of the Day is fixed except which home a man is still packing for.

**The Difference the Sentences Carry: Direction, Not Degree**

Because the verdict-sentences match so exactly, the eye is pushed to the single point where the files diverged — and the sūrah has kept that divergence deliberately small: *preference* versus *restraint*, both pivoted on the self's relation to the near world. No chasm of ritual achievement, no gulf of spiritual athleticism separates the condemned from the saved in this closing summary; a preference held long enough, a restraint maintained daily enough — these decide. The smallness of the pivot is mercy disguised as severity. Small pivots mean movable records: the distance between the files is precisely the distance a man can still travel before the trumpet, since the preference can be handed back and the restraint taken up any day before the single Shout. The early ascetics of the community, in sayings attributed with varying chains to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, compressed the diagnosis into the maxim that love of this world is the head of every error — *attributed, not securely documented*, yet faithful to this sūrah's arithmetic, in which one preference generates the whole doomed file. Reverse the maxim and the counter-file's whole creed results: awe of the Standing is the head of every survival.

**The Bifurcation Completed: The Sūrah's Movement Logic**

Look now at what these five verses (37–41) have constructed from the display of v36: a total classification of the species with two markers only. Transgression plus world-preference: file one. Standing-awe plus desire-restraint: file two. The architecture answers the chapter's own opening unrest with engineered completeness — the trembling hearts of the preamble have been given their full etiology and their full outcome. And the movement beyond the verdicts has already been plotted: having sorted humanity by the two practices, the recitation will next record humanity's actual live response to the whole scheme — not a repentant silence but a schedule-question, asked of the Prophet in the tone of cross-examination. The bifurcation is doctrine; the question that follows it is biography: how real audiences, freshly shown the two homes, behave. The sūrah thereby closes the gap between its eschatology and its sociology without a seam — verdicts delivered, and immediately the market re-opens with its old noise, as if to demonstrate on the spot how desperately the warning-ministry of the coming verses is needed.

**Two Conditions as the Portable Creed of the Sūrah**

Before following the recitation into that closing exchange, it is worth fixing the counter-file's two practices as the chapter's lasting deposit — a whole piety portable into any century. First practice: keep the Standing in fear's focus — the audit-date concealed, its certainty total, therefore rehearse it daily until the rehearsal is reflex. Second practice: forbid the self its hawā at the counter — prior to the act, inside the intention, where desire is cheapest to refuse. Neither practice requires era, language, temple, or treasure; both run on the interior equipment every serious person already owns, and both are defined against the precise failure-modes the first file exhibited. The sūrah's eschatology, followed closely, has turned out to be a practical discipline all along: the cosmology armed the awe, the narratives illustrated the preference, the verdicts fixed the stakes, and the creed that survives the whole descent of the chapter fits in one hand — fear the Standing; deny the want. What remains is the Hour itself, and about the Hour the Prophet is about to be cross-examined.

**But When? The Question Fired Back**

So the scene re-opens on the live audience, doing what warned audiences do when a warning lands too close: they ask for the date — "They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the Hour, 'When will it be?'" (79:42). The ask is the world's oldest deflection: convert the warning's *whether-you-are-ready* into the calendar's *when*, and argue with the calendar instead of with the soul. The recitation's final movement is heaven's management of exactly this maneuver: the knowledge redirected upward, the ministry redirected to awe, and the world's duration, at last sighting, deflated to an evening. The two homes have been named; it only remains for the Book, closing, to show what a whole lifespan spent disputing them looks like at the far end — brief as half a day.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:42

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the Hour, “When will it be?”

**Expanded Commentary**

The verdicts have barely been read when the live audience does what warned audiences always do: they reach for the calendar. The question of the Hour's date is fired at the Prophet — and with it the sūrah's closing movement begins, in which heaven answers not the question but the evasion underneath it.

**Yasʾalūnaka: The Question as a Tactic of Delay**

The present-tense *they ask you* carries the ongoing-ness of a habit: this is a recurring market-routine, not a once-asked curiosity. And its placement — immediately after the two homes have been sentenced — exposes its tactical shape. The kind of question a warning provokes diagnose its hearer: the convicted heart asks *what must I do*; the cornered debater asks *when*, because *when* safely relocates the whole matter from his conscience to the Speaker's information. As long as the discussion remains calendrical, nothing is required of the asker yet; the date, being unknown, makes the subject feel postponable, and a postponable ultimatum is, for practical purposes, a dead one. The recitation understands this perfectly and treats the question accordingly — not as theology to be answered but as behavior to be redirected. In its full context this verse is therefore less an inquiry than a dodging-technique entered into the record at the exact point where the bifurcation's demand pressed hardest: ask about the deadline, and you never have to answer the audit.

**Ayyāna Mursāhā: When Does the Ship Drop Anchor?**

The question's own wording is freighted with the answer it refuses to hear: *mursāhā* — its anchorage, its mooring, its settling-in — comes from a root used for ships coming to anchor, and *ayyāna* is simply *when*. The Hour is imagined, within the very vocabulary of the sneering question, as a vessel under sail toward a fixed berth: the askers themselves picture it as *arrival*, scheduled, inevitable, needing only the port-time. The image refutes the askers' motive by its logic. One does not ask a ship's mooring-time to dispute whether ships exist; the *when*-only posture is a concession that the arrival is certain — the whole controversy has silently shrunk to scheduling. Pagan cross-examination, at its cleverest stake-out, thus already stands inside the preaching's frame: the Hour as an inbound vessel whose timetable is withheld. What remains genuinely in dispute is only who holds the shipping ledger, and on that the recitation will rule within two verses. The deniers' own tongue has testified to the transit; the rest of the movement merely settles who keeps the schedule.

**The Identical Question, Answered Elsewhere at Full Length**

The Book records the same interrogation — in one place, with its complete official answer appended, word-parallel from the outset: "They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the Hour, 'When will it be?' Say, 'That knowledge is only with my Lord. He alone will reveal it when the time comes. It is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth and will only take you by surprise.'" (7:187). The coordination between that answer and this sūrah's closing verses is exact to the point of manifest authorship: knowledge located with the Lord exclusively, disclosure timed by Him exclusively, arrival guaranteed as ambush. Yet another passage adds, in the same register: "People ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the Hour. Say, 'That knowledge is only with Allah. You never know, perhaps the Hour is near.'" (33:63) — and there the deflection is answered with urgency: the concealed date reframed as *perhaps near*, turning ignorance of the timetable from an excuse into the very engine of watchfulness. Three passage-stations, one doctrine, complete consistency: the schedule is Above; the nearness is possible always; the asker should rearrange his life around both facts.

**They Ask as Though the Messenger Kept Its Timesheet**

The full-length parallel exposes the question's hidden insult: they ask *as if* the Prophet were privy to the calendar — as if prophethood included eschatological shipping-rights, and could therefore be embarrassed by a refused answer into confessing the whole preaching fraudulent. The trap is as old as revealed religion: demand of the spokesman a knowledge class never assigned to him, then read his declining as disproof of his actual message. The Book's counter is to publish the limits of the prophetic portfolio as openly as its powers: he is a warner, not a scheduler — and the refusal to answer *when* is itself evidence of the message's divine origin, since an impostor-inventor would surely invent the date too, the more dramatic the better. History has verified the point with monotony: every fraudulent apocalypse sells a date; the revealed Hour withholds one. The verse's news that even the Messenger was not given the Hour's timing is thus not a crack in the message to be exploited but a structural feature — the signature of a communication whose sender is not guessing.

**The Well-Worn Question-Habit of This Sūrah's Mockers**

Within this very chapter the asking-voice has been heard before, in the same deflecting key: "Will we really be restored to our former state," (79:10) — the mockery-question about bones and reassembly. Put the two questions side by side and their family resemblance is total: both aim interrogatives at the preaching's pressure-points, both substitute disputation for decision, both treat the unknowns of the afterlife as debating-matter to keep life's ledger unopened. The sūrah's closing movement answers the second question exactly as its opening movement answered the first: not by feeding the interrogative what it demands (no date for the Hour, just as no diagram for the reassembly) but by re-aiming the audience at the certainties already given — the built creation overhead, the two homes ahead, the striving already on file. Interrogation answered with orientation: that is the pattern of the Book toward hostile questioning generally, and the final verses of this sūrah are its compact masterclass — question deflected to awe, awe redirected to warning, warning vindicated by the arrival itself at the last verse.

**The Prophet's Share of the Answer**

The first word of heaven's management of the question is a boundary, courteously but totally drawn around the Messenger himself: "But it is not for you to tell its time." (79:43). Before any speech to the askers, the Prophet's own relation to their demand is settled: the file's announcing is not in his brief. The next verse will locate where the file does terminate — and discover, at the chain's end, that its terminal office belongs to the Custodian of the schedule, the Lord of the Warner and the askers alike. The calendar will stay unopened; the ministry will go on without it; and the audience will be left holding what the sūrah has placed in their hands from its first oath: everything needed to act, and nothing whatsoever to excuse delay.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:43

> But it is not for you to tell its time

**Expanded Commentary**

Heaven's first response to the schedule-question is not addressed to the askers at all — it is addressed to the Prophet, and it draws his portfolio's boundary in public. The announcing of the Hour's time is not in the Messenger's brief. The boundary, spoken over his ministry, becomes one of the most instructive verses on what a prophet is and is not.

**Fīma Anta Min Dhikrāhā: The Question That Is Also a Ruling**

The Arabic shape of the verse is itself a mild interrogative — *in what [position] are you regarding its reminder?* — a question-form deployed as a verdict: what share have you in its announcing? The rhetorical courtesy of the construction is worth feeling: the Prophet is not rebuked, since he never claimed the knowledge; the put-down is aimed past him at the demanders, yet routed *through* him so that the boundary is fixed at the level of sender, not at the level of spokesman's reluctance. By framing the limit as a question about the Prophet's standing, the verse also universalizes it: if he who is the recipient of revelation holds no warrant here, what warrant could any other mortal hold? Every later claimant to Hour-knowledge — soothsayer, calculator, date-seller, system-builder — stands pre-refuted by a question addressed, in loving firmness, to the best of men. The ruling's gentleness and the ruling's completeness are the same sentence; the Book often achieves this double effect when it instructs its Messenger while aiming at the crowds around him.

**A Boundary Published Before All Parties**

Why publish the prophetic limit so openly, rather than handling it privately? Because the limit is load-bearing for the audience's own spiritual safety. The schedule-demanders hoped to make the Messenger's silence their license: *he cannot say when — so the matter lapses*. The verse forecloses the inference at its root by relocating the silence: it is not missing information at his desk; it is a sealed drawer at the level of the whole creation. Once the filing-system is understood in this way, the refusal to disclose ceases to be arguable-with — there is no address at which the question may be lodged within the creaturely order. The same publication also immunizes the ministry: prophets warn, and warnings are not weakened by their issuer's lack of a timetable — a watchman is not dismissed because he cannot say which night the thief comes; the thief-policy obtains on every night. Demands for dates function, in every age, as tests of whether a preaching is commerce or commission; the verse fixes the commission's character permanently by showing the Prophet himself administratively unable to sell what was never consigned to him.

**The Jibrīl Precedent: The Ruling Demonstrated Live**

The Prophet enacted this verse's rule in the most famous examination-scene of the tradition. A man of striking appearance came to the sitting assembly, questioned the Prophet about Islam, faith, and excellence, and then asked about the Hour. The answer was the living form of this verse: *the one asked knows no more about it than the one asking* — and when the stranger departed, the Prophet identified him: that was Jibrīl, come to teach you your religion. The report stands in the most rigorously authenticated collections (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim), and its lesson-architecture is exact: the highest questioning-attendant heaven could send, dressed in human form, puts the schedule-question to the highest messenger earth had, and even in that summit-exchange the knowledge is declined — from both directions of the conversation at once. If angel-student and master-prophet share one ignorance on this item, the item's filing-location is established beyond appeal. The tradition thereby leaves the community a permanent inoculation: its founding generation watched the limit honored at the very peak of revelatory access.

**The Mercy Built Into the Non-Disclosure**

The sealing of the date is not mere reserve; it is engineering for the soul's best functioning. A published deadline produces deadline-humans: long complacency, last-hour panic-repentance, devotion with the sincerity of tax-filing. A concealed appointment produces, in the serious, something far better than either: *standing* readiness, the life kept in order because the audit hovers over every day alike. The unknown date is thus a distributional mercy — it spreads watchfulness evenly across a lifetime instead of concentrating it in a final weekend, and it keeps the market for religious theater small, since performance must be continuous to be timed right. The parallel-answer elsewhere in the Book turns the concealment into outright urgency: "You never know, perhaps the Hour is near." (33:63). Nearness-as-possibility replaces the calendar; the disciple is to live *as if* imminent what he cannot prove soon. Under this rule the only rational delay is none — the deepest critique the verse makes of the askers' waiting-game is that, on the information actually published, waiting never had a safe edge to cling to.

**Reminder as the Prophet's Actual Estate**

Note the last trace the verse leaves of what the Prophet *does* hold: the root of *dhikrāhā* — its reminder, its mention — places him inside the Hour's file in the capacity of remembrancer. The portfolio's positive side will be named outright two verses later with the office of warner, but already here the hint is planted: he carries the *mention* of the Hour, not its date — the message about it, not its timetable. The distinction is the whole difference between news and scheduling, between the ministry of warning and the science of prediction, and the reliabilities of the two are inversely related across history: date-prediction has an unbroken record of failure, while the warning-ministry has an unbroken record of producing the communities that outlasted the mockers. Man's Messenger, sent to creatures who would be steady only under continuous recall, is given exactly the instrument continuous recall requires; the instrument works precisely because it is *un*-schedulable. What he brings is not when, but *that* — and the next verse drives the remaining question to its final office.

**Where the File Terminates**

If neither asker nor asked holds the knowledge, one address remains at which the inquiry terminates — and the recitation now names it, folding possession, custody, and final disclosure into a single phrase: "That knowledge rests with your Lord ˹alone˺." (79:44). The chain that began in the market's cross-examination ends in the divine cabinet, with the possessive *your Lord* holding the seal: the Messenger's own relationship is the letterhead on which the final classification of the Hour's file is stamped. The question that was meant to embarrass the ministry ends by documenting its humility — and sets up the ministry's true office, which the verse after next will pronounce in three words: only a warner.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:44

> That knowledge rests with your Lord ˹alone˺

**Expanded Commentary**

The schedule-question is carried to its last address and filed. *With your Lord is its end* — the knowledge-chain terminates in the divine cabinet, and every other desk in the chain, prophetic and angelic included, is certified empty on this item. Five words that close a market, define a custody, and console a Messenger.

**Ilā Rabbika Muntahāhā: The File's Final Page**

The noun *muntahā* is the terminating-point, the furthest reach of a thing — and here it marks where the Hour's knowledge-chain runs out: *unto your Lord is its end*. The image is administrative and total. Knowledge, in this depiction, is not a vapor available anywhere but a file with a chain-of-custody; trace the custody upward through every creaturely office and the chain hands you, at the summit, to a closed door whose keeper is named. The question thus receives its most complete possible answer without receiving a date: not *nobody knows*, but *Somebody knows, and the knowledge stops there*. That distinction is the verse's center of gravity — agnosticism about the deity replaced by localization of the knowledge in Him. The Hour is not an orphan event drifting undated through a mindless future; it is a scheduled appointment whose schedule sits on the desk of the Lord who made both time and the appointment. Uncertainty about the *when* belongs to the creation; the event itself is as fixed as anything in the fixed decree — fixed by being *known*, at the only level where knowing settles anything.

**Rabbika: The Intimate Possessive on the Cabinet Door**

The seal carries one grace-filled detail: *your* Lord — the second-person singular, addressed to the Prophet, attaching the most inaccessible file in creation to the relationship the Messenger knows best. The possessive performs a subtle diplomacy. It consoles: the drawer is shut even to you, but the hand on the drawer is the one you already call upon, the Custodian whose name has headed every revelation you ever received. And it instructs the audience overhearing: the chain's last office is not alien bureaucracy but *his* Lord — the same Lord whom the whole sūrah has been exhibiting in the raised sky, the dimmed night, the spread earth, the anchored mountains. The One demonstrably able to raise the vault is the One who keeps the vault's audit-date: possession of the timetable follows naturally from authorship of the cosmos. There is even an equity in the filed location: since the Hour concerns every creature that ever drew breath within His lordship, the *when* of it properly belongs to that lordship alone — the defendant does not set the court-date, and the litigant is not copied on the judge's calendar.

**A Constitution for Creaturely Knowledge**

The verse legislates, beyond this case, a standing constitution: some files are creature-shareable and some are cabinet-only, and the Hour is the type-specimen of the second class. The parallel answer elsewhere in the Book states the constitution's disclosure-clause explicitly: He alone "will reveal it when the time comes." (7:187). Disclosure is itself scheduled — the appointment will be revealed *at* its appointment, so the teaching of the timing is timed together with the event, leaving no interval in which negotiations could run. Theology calls this the boundary of the unseen, and it functions as a permanent audit of religious claims: movements that traffic in the sealed class expose themselves by the very act of trafficking. Nor is the class arbitrary. The sealed files are consistently those whose disclosure would corrupt their function — the date that would manufacture insincerity, the timing that would abolish watchfulness. The seal's wisdom was sketched in the previous verse; this verse fastens it institutionally, so that no later century can treat the concealment as an oversight awaiting the right scholar.

**The Market for Rumors Foreclosed at Its Source**

Every calendar-calculation of the world's end, from that day to this, stands as a footnote to this verse — and the footnotes read identically: confidence, arithmetic, date-stamp, sunrise afterward, silence, eventually a revised arithmetic. The verse forecloses the trade in advance by stating the custody once: since the file ends Above and is never consigned Below, all Below-level *knowledge* of it is counterfeit at mint. The policy protects the community's credibility as much as its creed. A religion based on falsifiable dates has a falsification scheduled; the revealed Hour carries no scheduled falsification because it carries no date — its falsification-condition is placed instead at its arrival, "too tremendous for the heavens and the earth." Thus insulated, the preaching stays perpetually referendum-proof: it cannot be embarrassed by a sunrise, while its demand — live ready — continues to bind in every sunrise that arrives. Rationally considered, the custody-doctrine converts the audience's own skepticism into the demand's strongest form: the less you know of *when*, the more continuously *that* binds you. Ignorance, correctly filed, is the sternest governess in the chapter.

**What Is Consigned, Then, to the Messenger**

The chain-of-custody's negative finding — no date — is about to be balanced by its positive consignment, and the balance is the ministry itself. If the Messenger holds no timetable, what he does hold becomes the whole of his relief-duty toward the askers: the warning, addressed with surgical precision to exactly the hearts where warning works. The next verse reduces his entire office into a compressed commission — *only a warner* — and specifies its operative audience in the sūrah's by-now-familiar filter: those in awe of the event. Prophet and people, in other words, exit the custody-verse in their correct positions: he to his warning-rounds, they to their awe — the timetable left, where it always was, at the chain's end in the cabinet of the Lord of the worlds. And when the warning has done its work, one shared appointment remains for warner and warned alike — the arrival whose *when* this verse seals and whose *that* no one will miss.

**The Duty Delivered in a Single Line**

So the commission comes down: "Your duty is only to warn whoever is in awe of it." (79:45). The exchange that opened as cross-examination closes as job-description. The askers wanted a schedule to litigate; the Prophet is handed a constituency to awaken — the awe-primed, the trembling-hearted, the files that can still be edited. The sūrah's whole doctrine of the khashya-filter, planted in its opening oaths and stated at its midpoint lesson, now becomes official ministry-demographics: aim the alarm where the alarm has purchase. One verse then remains, and it ends the chapter where every concealment ends: at the sight of the thing itself.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:45

> Your duty is only to warn whoever is in awe of it

**Expanded Commentary**

The schedule refused, the custody sealed, the Messenger is now handed his entire office in six words: only a warner, and to the awe-primed. The verse compresses the prophetic ministry to its irreducible core and names — with the sūrah's own by-now-familiar filter — the only audience the alarm ever actually reaches.

**Innamā Anta Mundhir: The Ministry Compressed to One Office**

The particle *innamā* — *only, solely* — is the Book's celebrated instrument of relief, deployed at the Prophet's heaviest pressures: *your duty is only X*, where X is one clean deliverable instead of the unbounded obligations the opposition and the moment would pile on him. Here X is *mundhir*, warner: the office of issuing the alarm in advance of the danger. The compression cuts in several merciful directions at once. Against the askers: the warner-office explicitly excludes the timetable-desk they besieged, so their pressure-evidence is returned marked *not his department*. Against the sneerers' deeper demand — produce belief in us, manufacture our assent — it excludes that too: warning is deliverable; assent is the audience's own file. And against the Prophet's own burdened heart, the *only* is sheer relief: the commander of the message is not commanded the outcome. The same absoluteness that elsewhere makes the warning terrifying makes the office bearable: Day-outcomes are sealed matters in the cabinet of custody; the warner's shift ends each day with his warning delivered, his ledger closed.

**Man Yakhshāhā: The Audience Diagnosed by Its Tremor**

To whom is the alarm addressed? *Whoever is in awe of it* — of the Hour itself. The specification quietly astonishes: the ministry's effective field is not the curious, not the respectful, not even the assembled, but the already-awe-touched. The alarm works only where the dread-organ functions — which means the warner's real successes will be measured in hearts where some trembling pre-existed his voice, and the professional mockers, for all the volume they occupy in the public square, are not the ministry's operative constituency at all. This is a profound economics of preaching: efforts sort themselves by the state of the receiving soil, and the Book directs its Messenger to the receptive class rather than to the theatrical class, sparing his person the war of attrition against professional deafness. Note the tenderness folded into the arrangement for the awe-touched themselves: the tremblers are precisely those already half-crushed by what they sense coming, and to them the warner is sent — the alarm, to the already-alarmed, carries its other face: guidance on where to run before the fire-line arrives.

**The Sūrah's Own Filter, Now Made Official**

Long-time listeners of this sūrah will recognize the demographic. The chapter planted it at its midpoint, over the Pharaoh-file, closing the narrative with its lesson-filed verdict for the awe-holder alone. The same filter now becomes the ministry's official targeting: narrative-lessons for the awe-primed, final-warnings for the awe-primed — a single consistent economy of receptivity running from the stories of the drowned tyrant to the commission of the living Messenger. The consistency is itself a finding about revelation's method: God does not blast all hearts with equal force and sort the casualties; He sends the rain where the soil can grow, and the soil's readiness is awe. The preacher reading this sūrah is thus handed both the message and the mailing-list, while the skeptic reading it is handed a mirror: if the alarm seems not to be addressed to him, that fact is itself the diagnosis of his organ's state, and the only way he becomes addressable is that the organ wake. The chapter's pedagogy thus doubles back on its ministry-instruction: hearers cultivate awe to become students; warners find the awed to become fruitful.

**Warning as Discharged Duty, Not Failed Persuasion**

The relief-structure of the *only* deserves its full weight because the Prophet's context was attritional: years of warning delivered to a town where the respectable exacted mockery as their price of admission. Under siege like that, the natural metric of a ministry shifts from *warning delivered* to *converts produced* — and by the shifted metric, whole years looked like failure. This verse restores the commanded metric and, with it, the truthful accounting: the duty is discharge, not harvest; the warner who warned has succeeded at the entire assignment regardless of the sneer-count beside him. The Book states the same consolation at its sternest in the patience-chapter: "So endure patiently, as did the Messengers of Firm Resolve. And do not ˹seek to˺ hasten ˹the torment˺ for the deniers." (46:35). Patient endurance joined to non-hastening — the warner is even forbidden from asking heavento accelerate the verdict that would vindicate him, since vindication-timing also sits in the sealed cabinet. Ministry, as the verse frames it, is fidelity inside a closed loop: alarm in, harvest Above. Askers, sneerers, even the slow-coming coverts — all belong to files outside the deliverable.

**The Hour at Work Before It Arrives**

A last wonder hides in the pronoun: in awe *of it* — of the Hour, the sealed-date event, functioning already as an active fear-object inside living hearts. The concealed appointment is thereby revealed as anything but inert: unknown and unarrived, it exerts present force, producing the trembling that qualifies the warner's audience, slicing the world at every moment into those the fact governs and those it does not yet touch. Eschatology, on this verse's accounting, is a present ministry before it is a future event — the Hour works every day it is awaited, which is to say, in every awe-awakened heart it has in some sense already arrived. This also explains the sūrah's own long fuse: forty-five verses spent building trembling — oaths, quakings, shout, signs, narratives, cosmology, display, bifurcation — are the Hour's advance-work, manufacturing in the audience the very awe this verse names as the warner's field. The chapter does not merely inform about the appointment; it recruits for it. And once the awe is awake, the appointment's concealment converts from excuse to engine — every dawn a possible docket-day, every evening an audit survived or squandered.

**And Then They See It**

One scene remains after the commission, and it belongs to the far side of the Hour's arrival: "On the Day they see it..." — where the calculation of a whole life's duration collapses to its retrospective measure, "no more than one evening or its morning" (79:46). The askers who demanded the schedule meet the schedule's arrival; and at the sight, every bargain about postponement closes out with the same figure on the ledger: the long delay they mocked in was, at audit, half a day. The sūrah's last verse is a single stroke of perspective so total that it re-prices every delay ever given to anyone — and it leaves the warning standing at the chapter's threshold, issued, complete, and terribly vindicated in advance.

## Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt 79:46

> On the Day they see it, it will be as if they had stayed ˹in the world˺ no more than one evening or its morning

**Expanded Commentary**

The sūrah ends where concealment ends: at sight. No description of the Hour itself is offered — the chapter closes instead with the perception of the arrivals, and with the single most devastating figure in all of eschatological arithmetic: a whole worldly existence, at the moment of seeing, re-priced at half a day.

**Yawma Yarawnahā: The Sight That Ends Every Argument**

The verse's first move is optics: *the Day they see it*. The Hour, the disputed object of forty-five verses — mocked at the bones-question, besieged at the schedule-question, deflected through ritual cross-examination — here becomes simply *seen*, and seeing is the Book's terminal category. Every controversy of the chapter presupposed the event's invisibility; the last verse removes the presupposition that carried them all. Nothing more is litigated: the seeing-frame makes the whole preceding debate-readable in a single glance — the deniers' questions fossilize, in retrospect, into exhibits of the fog-era, when dispute was possible because sight was absent. And the pronoun *it* carries the authentic muffled quality of the inexplicable: the arrival needs no adjectives here, since vision itself is now transmitting. One verb of perception ends the longest argument of the chapter, which is also the longest argument in every skeptic's classroom: what was *whether* and *when* becomes *there* — and the whole scaffolding of postponement, built entirely on un-seen-ness, comes down with the scaffolding's only pillar.

**Kaʾannahum Lam Yalbathū: The Great Retroactive Shrinkage**

Then comes the arithmetic: *as if they had not stayed* — the retrospective verdict on the world's whole duration. The simile is not about subjective quickness in the agreeable sense; it is a foreclosure of value: the total estate of time in which every plan, empire, quarrel, and postponement was housed turns out, at the far end, to have been a sum so small it embarrasses the ledgers kept in it. Stay — *labatha* — is sojourn-language, dwelling for a term; the discovery at the sighting is that the term was never the length the tenants budgeted on. The psychology is exact and universally attested at every deathbed: years telescope; the long game looks, from its end, like the short one. What the verse performs is the installation of that end-of-life perspective at the hearing of the chapter — by revelation, in advance, for everyone free of charge. The retroactive compression, imported into the present, re-prices everything the present holds: if the total will look like an evening, then today's postponement of the right and postponement of the truth are purchases made at the robbery-rate — a morning paid for an evening.

**Illā ʿAshiyyatan Aw Ḍuḥāhā: Half a Day at Full Price**

The figure's own terms are chosen with a poet's economy and a theologian's aim: *ʿashiyya*, evening, or *ḍuḥā*, forenoon. Not even *a day*: either the day decaying, or the day only just cresting — a lifetime estimated at the daylight's ragged edge, in one case, or its short climb, in the other. The word ḍuḥā has walked through this very sūrah before: it was the forenoon-blaze the Builder drew forth in v29, the extracted brightness the sneerers scheduled their entire commerce by. Now that same unit — their workday's pride, their timetable's unit of conquest — is handed back to them as the total estimate of their existence: your entire stay, at audit, values at one shift. And notice the *or*: the verse grants the uncertainty of its own simile — evening or morning; audit arrives at different hours for different files — but holds the estimate fixed: whichever half-day it was, it was half a day. The dread and the mercy in the figure are twins. Dread: the mockers' margin evaporates utterly. Mercy: anyone hearing the verse still occupies some fragment of his own half-day in which the past is editable — the book of life, at midnight, still being unclosed ink.

**The Book's Triple Attestation of the Collapsed Sum**

This estimation is repeated across the Book's separates scenes, in grades and witnesses. Emphatic elsewhere too: "˹as though˺ they had not stayed ˹in the world˺ except for an hour of a day" (10:45) — an hour, the shorter grade. And in the patience-chapter's parallel, the estimate stands at the same *hour of a day*, with the verdict attached: "Then, will anyone be destroyed except the rebellious people?" (46:35). The wicked, in the Byzantium-chapter's scene, go further — they *swear* to the figure: "the wicked will swear that they did not stay ˹in this world˺ more than an hour. In this way they were always deluded ˹in the world˺." (30:55). Three scenes, one figure-class — hour, evening, forenoon — and the oath in the third is the grimdest testimony of all: men will put it on oath, at the end, that the whole thing was that short, and the Book reads back, over the oath, the diagnosis that they were *always* being eased onward — delusion-by-drift right up to its final day. Attestation so multiplied is doctrine: the compression is not one audience's impression but the species' shared audit-figure, sworn at last by those who fought it hardest.

**Why Half a Day Is the Whole Argument's Verdict**

The figure retroactively decides every dispute the chapter hosted. The bones-question mocked reassembly as absurd effort; at the audit-figure, the mocker faces the One for whom an evening's stay was erected, lit, provisioned, and audited — the effort-argument dies of scale. The schedule-question demanded the date as license for postponement; at the audit-figure, every postponement purchased at evening-rate is shown to have been nearly the entire budget — the date, sought as an alibi, would have shown nothing but *now*, repeated. The preference of the near-lower life, specified as the first file's entire crime, receives at this figure its fitting price-display: the near-lower life, preferred, bought its preferers a forenoon. And the awe-conditioned counter-file — the tremblers, the self-restrainers, the warner's true constituency — stands vindicated by the same arithmetic in the opposite direction: they alone spent the half-day on what outlasts it. Half a day is the sūrah's last and most portable measuring-device; carried into a market, an office, an argument, a postponement, it converts this world's price-stickers one after another back to cost-price.

**The Chapter Closes at the Threshold of Sight**

The sūrah could have continued — the Hour seen, the files marched, the homes received; the Book tells all of that at other stations. Here it declines, ending instead at the *instant of seeing*, with the wreckage of world-time still drifting through the arrivals' minds. The choice of ending is the final act of the chapter's mercy toward its readers: the freeze-frame holds open, forever, the interval in which its own audience still stands — the interval in which the figurehead *as if* is still a lesson rather than a summing-up. For the arrivals, the stay has already compressed; for the hearers of the recitation, it has not yet, which is the entire difference the whole ninety-two verses were recited to preserve. The chapter began with the pluckers of souls gently and hard at work, and it ends with the stay itself evaporating to an evening: between the two stands the warning, issued in full, waiting exactly where its warner was told to leave it — with whoever is in awe of it, and with the ink of their half-day still wet.
