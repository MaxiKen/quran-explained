# Sūrah al-Fatḥ (Chapter 48) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Manifest Triumph and the Sanctuary of Tranquility**

Al-Fatḥ, "The Triumph" or "The Victory," is the forty-eighth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 29 verses of late Medinan revelation. It takes its name from its electrifying opening proclamation: *“Indeed, We have granted you a clear triumph (*fatḥan mubīnā*)”* (48:1). In prophetic tradition, Sūrah al-Fatḥ holds an exalted, emotionally overwhelming station: ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (4833) that while riding with the Prophet ﷺ on the return journey from Ḥudaybiyah, the Messenger of Allah turned to him and declared: *“Tonight, a chapter has been revealed to me that is dearer to me than the entire world and everything within it!”*—and then recited the opening of Sūrah al-Fatḥ. Uniquely, the "clear triumph" celebrated in this chapter was not a military slaughter or the physical conquest of a citadel; it was the historic Treaty of Ḥudaybiyah in 6 AH—a peace pact that appeared to human eyes as a bitter diplomatic compromise, yet was recognized by Divine Wisdom as the greatest strategic and spiritual victory in early Islamic history.

**The Structural Modulation and the Garden of the Companions**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Fatḥ moves from divine forgiveness to psychological tranquility, and from battlefield covenants to the glorious biblical portrait of the Companions. The opening movement (1–10) announces total divine forgiveness for the Prophet's past and future liabilities, the completion of divine favor, and the sending down of *al-Sakīnah* (Divine Tranquility) into the hearts of the believers to increase them in faith upon faith. It affirms that the angelic and cosmic hosts of the heavens and earth belong exclusively to Allah, condemns the hypocrites and polytheists who harbored evil suspicions of God, and defines the cosmic weight of the Pledge of the Tree in verse 10: *“Indeed, those who pledge allegiance to you, [O Muḥammad]—they are actually pledging allegiance to Allah. The Hand of Allah is over their hands.”* The second movement (11–17) exposes the cowardice of the bedouin tribes who stayed behind from the expedition, concocting transparent excuses about their wealth and families, and clarifies the legitimate exemptions for the blind, the lame, and the ill. The third movement (18–26) records God's eternal pleasure with the 1,400 believers who took the oath: *“Certainly was Allah pleased with the believers when they pledged allegiance to you under the tree, and He knew what was in their hearts, so He sent down tranquility upon them and rewarded them with an imminent conquest”* (48:18). It reveals why God restrained the Muslims' hands from fighting inside Makkah: to protect vulnerable, hidden believing men and women living unknown among the pagans, whom the Muslim army might have trampled unknowingly. The climactic movement (27–29) validates the Prophet's original vision of entering the Sacred Mosque with shaved heads and shortened hair in total security, declaring that Islam has been sent with guidance and the religion of truth to prevail over all religion. The chapter concludes with the magnificent, immortal portrait of the Companions in verse 29: *“Muḥammad is the Messenger of Allah; and those with him are forceful against the disbelievers, merciful among themselves. You see them bowing and prostrating, seeking bounty from Allah and [His] pleasure. Their mark is on their faces from the trace of prostration.”* It cites their description in the Torah and their parable in the Gospel: like a seed that puts forth its sprout, strengthens it, thickens, and stands firm upon its stem, delighting the sowers, sealed with the divine promise of forgiveness and an immense reward for those who believe and do good.

## Verse 48:1

> Indeed, We have granted you a clear triumph ˹O Prophet˺

**The Companion Who Could Not Accept It**

The verse's claim was not received as news of victory. The most forceful objector at Ḥudaibiyah was ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, and he did not merely object; he interrogated. In the account preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (3182), he asked, "O Allah's Messenger! Aren't we in the right and our opponents in the wrong?" He was told, "Yes." He pressed on: were the slain of both sides where he assumed them to be? Yes. "Then why should we accept hard terms in matters about our religion? Shall we return before Allah judges between us and them?" The answer given him was not a proof but a status: "I am the Messenger of Allah, and Allah will never degrade me." Ibn Masʿūd is reported to have summarized the community's later view with equal directness, saying that the Quraysh considered the conquest of Makkah to be the victory, while to the companions the victory was the treaty conducted at Ḥudaibiyah. The objection survives in the same traditions as the treaty itself, which tells the reader that the verdict of this verse was not unanimous at the moment it fell.

**A Name for What Was to Come**

The commentators divided on what the word names. Many understand it as a reference to the conquest of Makkah and, by extension, to the Muslim victories that followed, on the ground that a triumph spoken of in the past tense at Ḥudaibiyah cannot be the event at which it was spoken, and must be the event that proves it. Others, and among them Ibn Masʿūd and Anas ibn Mālik, understand it as the treaty itself, because the treaty did what a conquest would have done more slowly: it let the two peoples mix freely, and the mixing produced the spread. The later verse about the fulfilled vision (48:27) holds the two together, since the entry it predicts is the same entry the opening was worked for.

**The Pledge That Named the Journey**

The journey of which this verse is the announcement carries a second name from the community's own memory: the pledge of Ḥudaibiyah, remembered as the pledge of satisfaction. The occasion for it was the delay of ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān. The Prophet had sent him into Mecca with the statement of the Muslim purpose, and the Quraysh held him back; the news that he had been killed reached the camp, and the Prophet called upon the faithful to pledge that they would not leave him, would not turn back, and would fight rather than abandon their envoy. They passed one by one beneath the tree and put their hands in his. ʿUthmān, absent, was not forgotten: in the account of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (3698), the Prophet held out his own hand and said, this is the hand of ʿUthmān, and pledged on his behalf with the other. The sūrah will name the result of the delay as a clear triumph, and later verses will explain what the pledge was actually to. The tree under which it was made stands in the sūrah's structure as the point where a journey of pilgrimage became a covenant of a community.

**A Sentence Left Unfinished**

The grammar of the verse is the smallest piece of evidence for its meaning. It states the triumph and then stops; the purpose of the triumph does not arrive until the next verse, and it arrives as a chain: forgiveness (48:2), tremendous help (48:3), serenity (48:4), admission to the Gardens (48:5), the punishment of the hostile (48:6). The first verse is therefore a subject with a postponed predicate, and the sūrah is the sentence it begins. The device explains a feature that would otherwise be puzzling, namely why the triumph is announced before any of its contents. The reader who knows the end of the story reads the announcement as a receipt: the account of what the opening produced is to follow item by item, and every item that follows can be checked against the claim made here. The sūrah's structure is its first argument, and the argument is that a victory is measured by what it is for, not by the noise it makes.

## Verse 48:2

> so that Allah may forgive you for your past and future shortcomings, perfect His favour upon you, guide you along the Straight Path

**The Feet That Swelled Anyway**

The Prophet's own answer to the verse survives in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (1130, and again in the section on the Prophetic commentary of the Qurʾān, 4836). He stood in night prayer until his feet swelled, and someone told him that Allah had forgiven him his past and future sins — the words of this verse, offered back to him as an answer. His reply was a question: "Should I not be a grateful servant?" The tradition does two things that the exegesis alone could not. It shows the verse functioning in the community's memory as a permission that was declined, and it fixes the Prophet's relation to the verse as gratitude rather than relief. The verse does not say *you may now rest*; it says *your fault is remitted*, and the remission, in the life it is about, produced not an easing of worship but its intensification. Forgiveness here is not the end of the accounting with God; it is what makes the accounting possible without despair, and the swollen feet are the physical proof that the accounting continued.

**Completing a Favour That Had Already Begun**

The middle clause names the second purpose: to perfect His favour. The commentators distribute the completion over several objects. It is the blessing of prophethood itself, brought to the point where nothing is added to it any more. It is wisdom, the second gift in the same sentence. It is the conquests of Khaybar, Makkah, and Ṭāʿif, the military completion of what Ḥudaibiyah had opened. And it is Paradise, the completion in the afterlife of what the treaty had only begun. The range of the readings is itself informative. A favour that can be completed in politics, in revelation, and in the hereafter is not a gift that has been given once and received once. The verb for completing assumes a process that is under way, and the sūrah's own history is the proof: the religion was not delivered complete on the first day, and the favour of this verse is the promise that the process has a terminus. The completion is the point at which the favour stops being a stream and becomes an estate.

**The Straight Path After a Diversion**

The final clause guides the Prophet along the Straight Path, and the preposition does the work. It is not *to* the path; it is *along* it. The one addressed had already received the path; what the verse promises is maintenance, continuation, an unbroken line. The timing makes the choice of preposition visible. The most recent event in the community's memory was a diversion: a journey of pilgrimage that ended in a truce, a dream of entry that was postponed, a treaty whose terms some of its own signatories resented. To be guided *along* the path after such an event is to be told that the path did not bend at Ḥudaibiyah. The route the triumph takes — delay, return, treaty, spread — is not a detour from the line but the line itself, and the verse says so with a single small word. The Straight Path in the sūrah's context is the course of the community's history, and the promise is that the course holds, whatever the detour looks like from inside it.

## Verse 48:3

> and so that Allah will help you tremendously

**The Help That Arrived as Absence**

The help the verse announces arrived in a form the pilgrims did not expect, and the form is the point. At Ḥudaibiyah the help took the shape of what did not happen. The Quraysh, who had barred the camp from the Sacred Mosque and held the envoy, did not attack. The two armies stood facing one another in the valley outside Mecca, and the fighting that the logic of the situation made probable never came. The sūrah will later describe the event in military language: Allah held back their hands from you and your hands from them (48:24). That holding back is the tremendous help in its material form. A help that is delivered as the absence of catastrophe is hard to recognize at the moment it arrives, because it has no visible agent and no dramatic event, and the community had to have the sūrah to learn what had been done to it. The lesson is structural: the help of God is not always an added force; sometimes it is the removal of the force that was closing in, and the removal is the harder of the two to witness.

**The Help That Was Conversion**

The second shape of the help appeared in the years that followed the truce, and it can be counted. The treaty released the message to move where armies could not: in the peace that followed Ḥudaibiyah, thousands from different tribes came to Islam, and the communities of the frontier tribes that had watched the Muslim camp from a distance sent their delegations to Medina. The help measured in conversions is the same help as the help measured in withheld hands, because the treaty was what made the conversion possible. A community that is at war with Mecca cannot receive Meccan emissaries; a community that has a ten-year covenant with Mecca receives the whole of Arabia at its door.

**A Later Sūrah Names the Same Help**

The Quran returns to this help in its final and shortest form. The sūrah that names the help of Ḥudaibiyah in the sixth year after the Hijrah is completed by the sūrah an-Nasr, which gives the help its arrival-time: "When Allah's ˹ultimate˺ help comes and the victory ˹over Mecca is achieved˺, and you ˹O Prophet˺ see the people embracing Allah's Way in crowds" (110:1–2). The two sūrahs bracket the same process from its beginning and its end. The help announced at Ḥudaibiyah is the help that arrives at the conquest, and the conquest is the help's visible terminus. The shorter sūrah says nothing else; it states the help, states its visible sign, and commands glorification. Read across the twenty-one years between the two verses, the tremendous help of this verse is the longest single promise in the Quran to be kept on schedule, and the schedule is the one the sūrah of the opening established.

**Forces the Pilgrims Did Not Have**

The sūrah states the help in the next verse in forces, and the contrast is arithmetic. The pilgrims at Ḥudaibiyah numbered in the thousands, carried no siege apparatus, and had come with weapons sheathed to visit, not war; the forces of the heavens and the earth belong, by the sūrah's own statement, to Allah alone (48:4). The tremendous help is, in the sūrah's logic, the help of a force that is not the pilgrims' force, which can act for them without their seeing it act. The Quran gives the precedent: when the Prophet and his companion were in the cave, after the emigration, Allah "supported him with forces you ˹believers˺ did not see" (9:40). The help of this verse belongs to the same class. It is real, it is tremendous, and it is delivered by agents the campaign's participants never counted among their own. The community's later memory of Ḥudaibiyah — as triumph, as the pledge under the tree, as the opening that Mecca could not close — is the record of a help that had arrived while no one was looking for it.

## Verse 48:4

> He is the One Who sent down serenity upon the hearts of the believers so that they may increase even more in their faith. To Allah ˹alone˺ belong the forces of the heavens and the earth. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise

**What the Sakinah Is**

The verse introduces a word that becomes a technical term in the Quran's own usage: *sakīna*, serenity, the descent of a calm that is not produced by the person who receives it. The sūrah attributes it to Allah's action, "He sent down," and the direction of the verb matters, because the serenity is not something the hearts generate in their own steadiness; it is something that arrives from above, sent, like rain or like the angels. The exegetes describe it in several registers, and the registers fit together. It is Allah's mercy made operative in a community under strain. It is an angel, or a presence of angels, settling in the hearts of the believers and steadying them. It is the intellect at its point of balance, the faculty that calms the pull of desire. And it is the state of a person who has been calmed *about* something, the root meaning to settle, to bring to rest, to take the motion out of a thing. What the readings share is the doctrine behind the word: that calm in the face of loss is not a psychological achievement but a gift, and that the gift is addressable, that it can be sent where it is needed.

**The Forces the Pilgrims Did Not Have**

The verse's second sentence is a correction of the arithmetic. To Allah alone belong the forces of the heavens and the earth. The sentence follows immediately on the promise of increase, and the sequence is the argument: the increase is possible because the forces that make it possible are not the pilgrims' forces. The pilgrims at Ḥudaibiyah had no cavalry, no siege, and no leverage beyond their own willingness to wait; the Quraysh had the city, the house, and the sanctuary. The sūrah refuses to let the reader compare those two inventories, because the comparison is the wrong one. Every army, every tribe, every power that the visible world contains belongs to the One who sends the serenity, and the verse's claim is that the campaign was never being conducted by the fourteen hundred who had come to make the pilgrimage. The sentence is stated as a fact about ownership, not as a threat, and its function in the sūrah is to remove the ground on which the disappointment had been standing.

**Serenity at the Point of Strain**

The sūrah sends the serenity three times in its own text, and the three descents mark the three points of maximum strain. The first is here, at the turning back from the Sacred Mosque. The second is at the pledge under the tree, when the envoy was believed killed and the community was asked to bind itself to a fight it had not come to make (48:18). The third is at the moment of the pride, when the hearts of the disbelievers had filled with the pride of ignorance and the believers were being tested by the sight of it (48:26). The Quran gives the precedent of the class: in the cave, at the moment of the most intimate danger of the emigration, Allah "sent down His serenity upon the Prophet, supported him with forces you ˹believers˺ did not see" (9:40). The pattern the sūrah is building is that the serenity arrives at the point of strain, not after it, and that its arrival is the mark of the point. A community's hardest moments are, in this account, the moments at which it is being held, and the sūrah names the holding.

## Verse 48:5

> So He may admit believing men and women into Gardens under which rivers flow—to stay there forever—and absolve them of their sins. And that is a supreme achievement in the sight of Allah

**The Garden Called the Supreme Achievement**

The verse calls the entry into the Gardens a supreme achievement in the sight of Allah, and the phrase is a standing expression in the Quran, used in over a dozen places to mark the highest good that God's decree produces. The usage tells the reader how to read the sūrah's first section as a whole. The triumph announced in the first verse was called *clear*; the Gardens are called a *supreme achievement*, and the two adjectives place the same event at two altitudes. The treaty at Ḥudaibiyah is a clear opening, visible in history; the Gardens are the achievement that the opening was made for, and the sūrah refuses to let the reader stop at the visible altitude. Some of the commentators go further and take the supreme achievement as a stage of nearness to God that is beyond the Gardens themselves, so that the phrase names not the place the believers enter but the relation they acquire. On either reading, the verse's function is the same: to state that the end of the sūrah's chain of purposes is not a political settlement but the highest thing God's decree can produce, and that the treaty is the first move of the game that ends there.

**Absolution Before Admission**

The order of the two verbs in the verse is part of its meaning. The believers are absolved of their sins, and they are admitted into the Gardens; the absolution comes first, and the admission follows on it. The Quran states the same order elsewhere, most fully in the verse in which God answers the supplicants who asked for forgiveness and for good in both lives: "I will certainly forgive their sins and admit them into Gardens under which rivers flow, as a reward from Allah" (3:195). The sequence is the doctrine: entry is not a reward for a life completed without fault, but a state that is made possible by the removal of the fault. The Gardens are not an estate in which the believer's sins are forgiven later; the forgiveness is the condition of the estate, and the sūrah's verse is a compact statement of the whole process. The believers who made the journey to Ḥudaibiyah are the first named community to be covered by it, and the sūrah's later history of the truce — the conversions, the delegations, the spread — is the history of the community growing toward the condition the verse describes.

## Verse 48:6

> Also ˹so that˺ He may punish hypocrite men and women and polytheistic men and women, who harbour evil thoughts of Allah. May ill-fate befall them! Allah is displeased with them. He has condemned them and prepared for them Hell. What an evil destination

**The Thought, Not Only the Deed**

The verse's rarity in the Quran is its object. The Book condemns acts throughout, and the acts of the hostile are elsewhere named in full: the barring of the house, the delay of the envoy, the plotting against the messenger. Here the condemnation is aimed at the heart's calculation, and the reason the thought is named is that it is the only thing if could not be seen. The Quraysh's refusal was visible; the delay of the envoy was visible; the thought that God had been outmanoeuvred was not visible, and it was the one that would produce the next act. The sūrah's doctrine, stated here, is that the relation of a person to God's character is the ground on which the person's acts stand, and a person who has formed an evil thought of God — who has supposed that God's wisdom can be defeated by a delay — has already chosen the act that the thought requires. The verse therefore does not add a new prohibition; it names the point at which the existing prohibitions are broken, and it places that point under the same decree as the visible acts, so that the inner and the outer stand on the same footing before the judgment.

**Why the Verse Closes the First Section**

The verse is the last of the sūrah's first section, and its position is the reason for its completeness. By the sixth verse, the sūrah has named the triumph, stated its purposes, described the serenity, given the believers their destination, and given the hostile theirs. The population of the event is fully divided, and the section can close. The next verse repeats the sentence about the forces of the heavens and the earth (48:7), and the repetition is the section's seal: the same ownership that was stated in the fourth verse is stated again, so that the reader leaves the first section with the ground of the whole account fixed. The sūrah's first section is, in practice, a statement of the event's meaning, and this verse is the half of the statement that deals with the group that does not accept the meaning. The section ends with the hostile placed beyond the mercy, and the rest of the sūrah can then turn to the groups inside the camp — the believers who pledged, the believers who stayed behind, and the Prophet himself — without having to return to the question of what the triumph did to those who stood against it.

## Verse 48:7

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belong the forces of the heavens and the earth. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise

**The Host in Its Kinds**

The sūrah's word for forces is the word for a host, an army, and al-Tustarī is reported to have answered the question of who the host actually is, with an answer the tafsīr tradition preserves. The host, he said, is of several kinds, and not all of it is in the sky. In the heavens the host is the prophets, and among the people of the earth it is the saints; in the heavens it is the hearts, and on the earth it is the souls. Whatever God sets in motion over a person belongs to the host, and that includes what is inside the person. A soul that God sets against its owner will bring the owner down; the bodily members, set in motion against him, do the same; a soul that takes the heart by force drags the person after mere appetite. The heart, when it takes the upper hand over the soul and the members, is the host in its best form: it binds the members to their due, drives them to the worship that is their task, and clothes them in the sincerity of the service. The report is a piece of interior exegesis, and it does what the sūrah's context invites: it takes the military image of the first section and turns it inward, so that the host over which the sūrah declares ownership is not only the armies and the angels of the visible world but the faculties of the person who stands in the camp. The verse's claim, read with the report, is that the person's own composition is a theatre of forces, and the ownership stated in the verse extends into it.

## Verse 48:8

> Indeed, ˹O Prophet,˺ We have sent you as a witness, a deliverer of good news, and a warner

**The Three Offices of the Mission**

The verse states the Prophet's mission in three nouns, and the three nouns are the offices he was sent to hold: witness, deliverer of good news, warner. The order is fixed, and the three offices divide the work of the message. The witness stands before; the deliverer of good news opens the door; the warner marks the threshold. None of the three is the mission itself, and all three together are the mission, for the message that does not witness is a report, the message that does not give glad tidings is a demand, and the message that does not warn is a rumor. The sūrah places this statement at the beginning of its second movement, immediately after the section that announced the triumph and sealed it with the ownership of the forces. The sequence is the sūrah's claim about cause and effect: the triumph is the result of the offices, and the offices are now stated so that the result can be read as what it is.

**A Witness to What and to Whom**

The first office is the one the sūrah gives first, and the exegesis divides on what it witnesses to. One line of the commentary takes the witness as bearing witness to divine oneness, the testimony that the whole message is built on, so that the Prophet stands as the standing testimony of the community to the one God. Another line takes the witness as the one who will bear witness for or against people in the hereafter, the testimony that stands over the outcomes, so that the office is Last-Day as well as present. The two lines are not opposed, because the testimony to oneness in this world is what makes the testimony over the people possible in the next. The Quran gives the same triple description of the mission elsewhere, in the sūrah al-Aḥzāb, where it says, "We have sent you as a witness, and a deliverer of good news, and a warner" (33:45), and the parallel fixes the formula as a standing description of the office rather than a comment on a single event. The witness is the one who does not look away, and the sūrah's placement of the office first is its way of saying that the message's first act is to stand before.

## Verse 48:9

> so that you ˹believers˺ may have faith in Allah and His Messenger, support and honour him, and glorify Allah morning and evening

**The Purpose of the Sending**

The verse completes the sentence of the eighth, and the completion is the purpose. The sending was not an end in itself; it was sent so that a response would occur, and the response is stated in four actions: to have faith in Allah and His Messenger, to support him, to honour him, and to glorify Allah morning and evening. The first of the four is the ground, and the other three are its fruits. Faith is the relation; support, honour, and glorification are what the relation does in the life of the person who holds it. The sūrah states the purpose in the plural of the community, the believers, and the placement of the verse at the head of the second movement fixes the purpose as the standard by which the community that follows is measured. The sūrah's later verses will divide that community into those who pledged, those who stayed behind, and those who harboured the evil thought, and the division will be the application of the purpose stated here. The verse is the criterion before the census.

**The Rhythm of the Day**

The final action of the verse is the one that fixes the others in time: to glorify Allah morning and evening. The sūrah gives the command in the frame of the day, and the frame is the doctrine. The faith, the support, and the honour are not events that happen once and are then complete; they are a rhythm, and the rhythm is the morning and the evening, the two turns of the day at which the community turns back to the name. The Quran places the same frame in several of its earlier addresses, in the command to remember the name morning and evening and to prostrate before it during part of the night, "Remember the Name of your Lord morning and evening, and prostrate before Him during part of the night" (76:25–26), and the range of the parallels fixes the rhythm as a standing form of the message rather than a comment on a single occasion. The sūrah's verse ends where the day begins and where the day ends, and the ending is the sūrah's way of saying that the purpose of the sending is a life, not a moment.

## Verse 48:10

> Surely those who pledge allegiance to you ˹O Prophet˺ are actually pledging allegiance to Allah. Allah’s Hand is over theirs. Whoever breaks their pledge, it will only be to their own loss. And whoever fulfils their pledge to Allah, He will grant them a great reward

**The Pledge That Is a Pledge to God**

The verse opens with the transfer, and the transfer is its doctrine. The hand given to the one sent under the tree is, in the sūrah's statement, a hand given to the sender, and the fixing is not a figure of speech but a standing relation. The Quran states the same relation elsewhere in its plainest form, when it says that whoever obeys the Messenger has truly obeyed Allah (4:80), and the logic of the two statements is identical: the one sent stands in the place of the sender, so that the act done to the one sent is done to the sending. The pledge under the tree was not a political contract between the community and its leader; it was a covenant of the community with God, held in the hand of the one God sent to hold it. The sūrah's statement of the transfer fixes the status of the event for the whole of the community's later memory, and the name the memory gave the event, the pledge of good pleasure, is the name of the transfer made into a title.

**The Hand Over the Hand**

The second sentence of the verse is the one that has occupied the commentators longest: the Hand of Allah is over their hands. The exegesis preserves three positions, and each position has its reason. The first takes the complete meaning of such a phrase as beyond the reach of human understanding, and reserves it to God alone, on the ground that the human mind cannot hold the divine attribute without reducing it. The second holds that the wording has its literal force, yet reads it as a pointer to one of the divine attributes, the attribute of strength that the image of the hand carries, so that the image is an attribute and not a limb. The third keeps the literal sense as the sense that is true, yet holds that the actuality of the sense is unknown to the creature, because what appears of the attributes in the human order is not of the same order as the attributes themselves, and nothing is like Him (42:11). The sūrah's own norm for the question is the verse it points to, the statement that there is nothing like Him, and the norm settles the matter: the hand is affirmed, and the likeness is denied, and the meaning that stands is the power that secures the pledge. Some of the commentators go further and take the verse as the highest praise of the Prophet in the whole of the Book, because the sentence places the hand of the one sent under the Hand of the sender, and the placing is the praise.

**The Covenant Remembered**

The final phrase of the verse, the pledge made to God, carries an allusion the exegesis traces to the first covenant, the one recorded in the sūrah al-Aʿrāf, when the Lord brought forth from the loins of the children of Adam their descendants and had them testify about themselves: "Am I not your Lord?" They replied, "Yes, You are! We testify" (7:172). The pledge under the tree, read with the allusion, is not a new act but a reaffirmation, the community's testimony given again in the body, in the hand, in the valley outside Mecca. The sūrah links the event of Ḥudaibiyah to the first act of the human kind, and the link is the sūrah's claim about the nature of the pledge: it was always owed, and the tree was the place where it was paid. The covenant remembered is the ground on which the transfer of the first sentence stands, for a pledge to the one sent is a pledge to the sender, and the sender is the one to whom the first testimony was given before the loins.

## Verse 48:11

> The nomadic Arabs, who stayed behind, will say to you ˹O Prophet˺, “We were preoccupied with our wealth and families, so ask for forgiveness for us.” They say with their tongues what is not in their hearts. Say, “Who then can stand between you and Allah in any way, if He intends harm or benefit for you? In fact, Allah is All-Aware of what you do

**The Question That Replaces the Answer**

The verse does not command the Prophet to argue with the excuse; it commands him to answer it with a question. Say: who then can stand between you and Allah in any way, if He intends harm or benefit for you? The question moves the ground of the debate. The excuse stands on the ground of the worldly: the wealth, the families, the fear of the Quraysh, the logistics of the stay. The question removes the ground, because the question is not about the logistics but about the ordainer, and on the ground of the ordainer the logistics are irrelevant. The exegesis gives the sense of the question: no one can avert what God desires or has ordained, and the Quran states the same sense in its plainest form elsewhere, when it says that if Allah helps, none can defeat, but if He denies help, then who else can help (3:160). The question is the sūrah's answer, and the answer is that the excuse is addressed to the wrong court. The wealth and the families cannot stand between a person and the intention of God, and the sūrah's later verse will show what the intention of God was with the group.

**Forgiveness and Its Limits**

The group's request is the one the verse does not answer: ask for forgiveness for us. The sūrah withholds the answer here, and the answer is given elsewhere, in the sūrah that bears the name of the believers, where the limit of the Prophet's intercession is stated in the plainest terms: it is the same whether you pray for their forgiveness or not, Allah will not forgive them (63:6). The two verses together state the sūrah's doctrine of the excuse. The request for forgiveness is the form the group gives its staying behind, and the form is the confession, for a person who is genuinely preoccupied asks forgiveness of the preoccupation, while a person whose preoccupation is a pretext asks forgiveness of the pretext, and the asking is the admission that the pretext was known to be a pretext. The sūrah's reference to the limit of forgiveness fixes the group's state for the reader: the state in which the forgiveness asked is the forgiveness that will not be given, because the state is not the state from which forgiveness is asked but the state that has made the asking a performance.

## Verse 48:13

> And whoever does not believe in Allah and His Messenger, then We surely have prepared for the disbelievers a blazing Fire

**The Verdict Moves From Group to Class**

The two verses that exposed the laggards closed on the state assigned to the group, and this verse moves the judgment out of the group and into the class. The sūrah has been dealing with a specific population, the nomadic Arabs who stayed behind, and the verse widens the frame to the disbelievers as such. The movement is the sūrah's structure in action: the census of the camp is complete, and the sūrah now states the ground that stands under the camp and under every camp. The thought of the laggards, the thought that the Messenger and the believers would not return, is the seed of the thought of the disbelievers, and the first section of the sūrah had already named that thought in the hostile. The verse unifies the two groups under the one verdict, and the unification is the sūrah's claim about the relation between the camp's interior and the camp's exterior. The division is a fact of the camp, and the joining is the deeper fact of the thought. The sūrah's own sequence shows the move: the group is exposed, the group is offered, the group is divided, and now the class is judged, and the class is the group's genus. The judgment that was made for the group is stated here for the class, and the statement for the class is what makes the judgment for the group a judgment and not a comment.

**The Blazing Fire in Its Various Names**

The word the verse gives is the bare word for the blazing fire, and the Quran carries the prepared fire under several names throughout its text. It is asserted as a humiliating punishment in the earlier sūrahs, as a painful punishment in others, and it is named together with its instruments, the chains and the shackles, in the sūrah that opens with the praise: "Indeed, We have prepared for the disbelievers chains, shackles, and a blazing Fire" (76:4). The range of the names is the point of the sūrah's choice. By taking the bare word, without the chains and without the shackles and without the adjectives, the sūrah states the fire in its naked form, and the naked form is the verdict. The fire does not need its instruments to be named; the instruments belong to the description, and the description is not the sūrah's business here. The sūrah's business is the fact of the preparation, and the fact is stated in the one word that is the fire itself.

## Verse 48:14

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Sovereignty, Not Mere Force**

The sūrah's first section spoke in the vocabulary of forces, the hosts of the heavens and the earth, and this verse speaks in the vocabulary of the kingdom, and the shift of vocabulary is the shift of subject. The hosts are what the owner has at his command, the armies and the angels and the faculties of the person, and the kingdom is what the owner owns, the heavens and the earth and everything between them. The sūrah moves from the event to the ground, and the ground is stated in the form of ownership rather than in the form of power, because the event it is grounding is not a battle but a decision, the decision to forgive or to punish. A battle is won with forces, and a decision is made by an owner, and the sūrah's second section is the section of decisions, the excuses and the refusals and the opportunities, and the decisions stand on the ownership. The verse is the foundation stone of the section, and the stone is laid before the first decision of the section is made.

**The Two Freedoms of the Owner**

The middle sentence of the verse states the two freedoms of the owner, and the two freedoms are the verse's doctrine. The first freedom is the ability: the owner can forgive all sins, and the ability is the ground of the hope that the sūrah's later verses will direct to the lagging group. The second freedom is the determination: no one may assign the categories of the forgiven and the punished, for the assignment belongs to the owner alone, and the one who assigns the categories for himself places himself beyond the owner's hand. The sūrah gives the formula its standing context elsewhere, in the address to the communities that spoke of themselves in the language of the chosen, where the Jews and the Christians each said, "We are the children of Allah and His most beloved," and the answer was the same formula, "He forgives whoever He wills and punishes whoever He wills. To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth" (5:18). The formula is the sūrah's answer to every presumption of the chosen, and the presumption is the one that the lagging group is about to make, when it asks to be let into a promise that was made for others.

**The Formula's Standing Form**

The formula of the verse is a standing form in the Quran, and the sūrah's use of it here fixes the form as the ground of the second section. The formula appears in the sūrah of the cow, where the ownership of everything in the heavens and on the earth is stated together with the two freedoms, "To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth... He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills. And Allah is Most Capable of everything" (2:284), and it appears in the sūrah of the family and in the sūrah of the covenant and in the sūrah of the stars, and the range of the appearances fixes the formula as the standing ground of the Quran's own address. The sūrah's second section, from the excuses of the laggards to the approval of the pledge, stands on the formula, and each verse of the section is a decision made on the ground the verse lays. The formula is the sūrah's foundation, and the foundation is stated once, at the head of the section, so that the section's decisions are read as standing on it.

## Verse 48:16

> Say to nomadic Arabs, who stayed behind, “You will be called ˹to fight˺ against a people of great might, who you will fight unless they submit. If you then obey, Allah will grant you a fine reward. But if you turn away as you did before, He will inflict upon you a painful punishment.”

**The People of Great Might**

The exegesis divides on the identity of the people of great might, and the three lines of the division are worth weighing. One line takes them as the tribe of the Ḥanīfa, the community that the armies of the first caliph, Abū Bakr, defeated in the years after the Prophet's death. Another takes the verse as pointing ahead to the future wars against the two great empires, Byzantium and Persia, the powers that would come against the Muslim state. A third takes them as the tribes of the Hawāzin and the Thaqīf, the confederacy that met the Muslim army in the field after the conquest of Makkah. The third line is the one the later history first confirms, and the confirmation is the battle of Ḥunayn, the first great fight the community engaged in after the treaty, fought by the very community that had pledged at Ḥudaibiyah against a people of great might, the Hawāzin with their allies. The sūrah's description, the people of great might, is the description of that fight, and the fight is the calling's first arrival. The verse's future was the sūrah's way of saying that the community that had been turned back from the Sacred Mosque would go on to meet the might of Arabia in the field.

**The Two Options Stated as One**

The verse states the options of the calling in a single clause, and the single clause is the sūrah's point. You will fight them, or they will submit, and the sense, the exegesis gives, is the two options the idolatrous tribes of Arabia were given when the Muslim army confronted them. The clause joins the two options with the or, and the joining is the removal of the third option, the option of staying behind and waiting. The group's pattern, the pattern of staying behind for the pilgrimage and returning for the spoils, is removed from the menu by the clause, and the removal is the sūrah's claim. The calling will not wait for the group, and the group's waiting is no longer a position the sūrah recognizes. The two options stand, and the standing is the sūrah's way of saying that the future of the group is the future of the fight, and the fight is what the calling is for.

## Verse 48:17

**The Parallel Verse and Its Frame**

The Quran gives the exemption in its full form elsewhere, in the sūrah that closes the revelation, where the parallel is stated with its condition and its fourth category: "There is no blame on the weak, the sick, or those lacking the means ˹if they stay behind˺, as long as they are true to Allah and His Messenger. There is no blame on the good-doers. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" (9:91). The parallel adds to the sūrah's list the fourth category, those lacking the means, the poverty that is the ground of the stay as the blindness is the ground of it, and it adds the condition, the truth to Allah and His Messenger, which fixes the exemption on the heart rather than on the body alone. The two verses together state the doctrine of the exemption in its full form. The bare form is the sūrah's way of stating the principle, and the full form is the Quran's way of stating the application, and the application is the principle in its details.

**The Obedience That Admits**

The verse's second sentence states the pair in its plainest form, and the plainness is the sūrah's move. Whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger, the one who has the ground of the going and takes it, is admitted by Him into the Gardens under which rivers flow, and the admission is the Gardens. The sūrah states the obedience as the condition of the admission, and the condition is the double obedience, the obedience to Allah and the obedience to His Messenger, the same double that the belief of the thirteenth verse stated in its positive form. The Quran states the stark contrast of the obedience and the turning away throughout its text, and the sūrah of the light gives it in the form the sūrah's verse takes: the obedience to God and to the Messenger is the obedience that carries the person to the right guidance, and the messenger is accountable only for the burden with which he was charged, while each of the people is accountable for the burden with which they were charged (24:54). The sūrah's verse takes the contrast and states it without the intermediate, the obedience admits, and the turning away punishes, and the without-the-intermediate is the sūrah's way of stating the freedom of the person who has the ground.

**The Turning-Away That Punishes**

The verse's third sentence completes the pair, and the completion is the statement of the freedom. Whoever turns away, the one who has the ground of the going and refuses it, is subjected by Him to a painful punishment, and the punishment is the pair's other half. The sūrah's form is the one subject carrying the two alternatives, the same whoever in the two sentences, and the one subject is the sūrah's way of stating that the two alternatives are the two positions of the one person. The person who can go and does not go is in the position of the turning away, and the person who cannot go is in the position of the exemption, and the two positions are the sūrah's division of the stayers. The painful punishment is the word the sūrah has used for the punishment of the group that turned away in the sixteenth verse, and the reuse of the word fixes the relation: the turning away of the individual is the turning away of the group, and the group's turning away is the individual's. The sūrah's treatment of the stayers is complete when the three forms are stated, the exemption, the opportunity, and the exposure, and the verse is the one that states the first of the three in its plainest form.

## Verse 48:26

> ˹Remember˺ when the disbelievers had filled their hearts with pride—the pride of ˹pre-Islamic˺ ignorance—then Allah sent down His serenity upon His Messenger and the believers, inspiring them to uphold the declaration of faith, for they were better entitled and more worthy of it. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things

**The Third Descent of the Serenity**

The verse is the sūrah's third sending of the serenity, and the sending is upon the Messenger and upon the believers, and the double object is the sūrah's way of stating that the serenity reached the leader and the community together. The context of the third descent is the whole week accumulated: the turning back from the house, the delay of the envoy, the terms of the treaty that some resented, the command that met the sitting, the refusal of the people of Makkah to allow the names at the head of the document. The serenity, in the sūrah's account, is the descent that meets the accumulation, and the meeting is the sūrah's way of stating that the state of the believers at the close of the week was the state of the held. The three descents of the sūrah, at the turning back, at the tree, and at the close of the week, are the sūrah's way of fixing the serenity at the three points of the strain, and the third point is the point at which the strain had run its full course.

## Verse 48:27

> Indeed, Allah will fulfil His Messenger’s vision in all truth: Allah willing, you will surely enter the Sacred Mosque, in security—˹some with˺ heads shaved and ˹others with˺ hair shortened—without fear. He knew what you did not know, so He first granted you the triumph at hand

**The Vision That Made the Journey**

The verse states the fulfillment in its first clause, and the first clause is the sūrah's way of closing the account of the journey. God has fulfilled for His Messenger the vision in truth, the verse says, and the fulfilling is the sūrah's way of stating that the journey had its ground in the vision. The exegesis gives the vision in its form: in the dream the Prophet saw himself and his people making their way into the city and going round the house, and he spoke of the dream to the community in the houses of Medina, before a single pilgrim had mounted, and the speaking is the ground on which the journey was made. When the camp set out in the year of the valley, the companions walked under the assumption that the year itself would bring the entry, and the assumption is the sūrah's way of stating the state of the community at the start of the journey. The sūrah's claim, by the first clause, is that the vision was the ground of the journey, and that the journey was the vision in its movement, and that the movement ended in the fulfillment the verse states.

**The Question of the Year**

The exegesis preserves the exchange that the week produced, and the exchange is the sūrah's way of stating the distinction that the verse protects. The treaty was signed, and the camp turned homeward with the rite unperformed, and the turning produced the sadness in a number of the companions, and the sadness is the state the verse addresses. Out of the sadness ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb formed his question, and the question was the one the dejection had shaped: the Prophet had spoken of the entry and the going round the house, and the companions had taken the words for the coming year. The answer carried the distinction in a single clause: the entry had indeed been promised, but the promise had not fixed the year, and the not-fixed is what the answer states. The exchange is the sūrah's way of stating the distinction between the promise and the timing, and the distinction is the sūrah's way of preserving the truth of the vision against the disappointment of the year. The vision was not false because the year was not the year, and the not-false is what the answer states, and the stating is what the verse's first clause confirms.

## Verse 48:28

> He is the One Who has sent His Messenger with ˹right˺ guidance and the religion of truth, making it prevail over all others. And sufficient is Allah as a Witness

**The Sending Stated in Its Final Form**

The sūrah now states the sending in its final form, and the form is the ground of everything the sūrah has stated. He sent His Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth, the verse says, and the sending is the sūrah's way of placing the event on its first ground. The movement of the sūrah has carried the reader from the announcement of the triumph to the account of the pledge and the plea and the serenity and the spoils and the vision, and the verse now states the ground under the movement: the event was the event of the sent one, and the sent one was sent with the guidance and the religion. The sūrah's placement of the verse at the close of its account is its way of saying that the account is the account of the sending, and that the sending is what the account was made to show. The guidance and the religion are the two objects of the sending, and the two objects are the sūrah's way of stating that the message was the guidance for the way and the religion for the life.

## Verse 48:29

> Muḥammad is the Messenger of Allah. And those with him are firm with the disbelievers and compassionate with one another. You see them bowing and prostrating ˹in prayer˺, seeking Allah’s bounty and pleasure. The sign ˹of brightness can be seen˺ on their faces from the trace of prostrating ˹in prayer˺. This is their description in the Torah. And their parable in the Gospel is that of a seed that sprouts its ˹tiny˺ branches, making it strong. Then it becomes thick, standing firmly on its stem, to the delight of the planters—in this way Allah makes the believers a source of dismay for the disbelievers. To those of them who believe and do good, Allah has promised forgiveness and a great reward

**The Two Faces of Those With Him**

The verse gives the community its two faces, and the two faces are the sūrah's way of stating the whole of the character. Those who are with him are severe against the disbelievers and merciful among themselves, the verse says, and the two faces are the sūrah's way of holding the character in its two directions. The tradition states the sense of the severity: the community never softens its standing against the disbelievers, and it meets them in the field when the field is the duty, and the not-softening is the sūrah's way of stating the face that is turned outward. The balance of the two faces is stated in an analogy the exegesis preserves: the light of the sun falls hardest on the black surface and gentler on the white, and the believers, on this account, display the truth to the disbelievers with the greater intensity of the light, while among themselves the truth appears as the gentle warmth. The sūrah's claim, by the two faces, is that the character of the community is the character of the light, and that the light is what the verse describes in its two directions. The saying of the Prophet, preserved in the tradition, states the mercy in its ground: the Lord does not extend His mercy to the one who withholds his, and the one who shows mercy to the dwellers of the earth is shown mercy by the Lord of the heaven. The other sūrah of the Quran states the same two faces in its own words, when it describes the community that God replaces in place of the one that abandons the faith: they will be humble with the believers but firm towards the disbelievers (5:54).
