# Sūrah al-Aḥzāb (Chapter 33) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Siege of the Trench and the Exalted Paradigm**

Al-Aḥzāb, "The Confederates," is the thirty-third chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 73 verses of late Medinan revelation. It takes its name from verses 9–27, which chronicle the epic geopolitical crisis of the Battle of the Trench (*Khandaq*) or the Battle of the Confederates (*al-Aḥzāb*) in Shawwāl of 5 AH (627 CE), when a massive coalition of ten thousand pagan and tribal warriors besieged the Muslim commonwealth in Madinah. Sūrah al-Aḥzāb is celebrated for containing some of the most foundational constitutional, prophetic, and domestic verses in the sacred canon: the establishment of the Prophet's spiritual fatherhood over the believers (*“The Prophet is closer to the believers than their own selves, and his wives are their mothers,”* 33:6); the immortal declaration of prophetic exemplarity (*“There has certainly been for you in the Messenger of Allah an excellent pattern (*uswah ḥasanah*),”* 33:21); the eternal seal of prophethood (*Khātam al-Nabiyyīn*, 33:40); the cosmic trust (*al-Amānah*, 33:72); and the divine command for all creation to shower blessings upon the Prophet (*“Indeed, Allah and His angels confer blessings upon the Prophet. O you who have believed, ask [Allah to confer] blessings upon him and ask [Allah to grant him] peace,”* 33:56).

**The Ten Thousand Warriors and the Terror of the Trench**

The historical furnace out of which Sūrah al-Aḥzāb emerged was the greatest existential threat faced by early Islam. Incited by the exiled Jewish leadership of Banū al-Naḍīr, the Quraysh united with the fierce desert tribes of Ghaṭafān and others, assembling an unprecedented coalition army of ten thousand men to wipe out the Muslim community. Defending an oasis of only three thousand fighters, the Prophet ﷺ, upon the brilliant advice of Salmān al-Fārisī, ordered the digging of a massive trench across the open northern perimeter of Madinah. As freezing winds, near-starvation, and sleepless exhaustion gripped the defenders, the Jewish tribe of Banū Qurayẓah inside Madinah violated their mutual defense pact, conspiring to open the city's gates to the enemy from behind. Verse 10 captures this psychological terror: *“When they came at you from above you and from below you, and when eyes shifted [in terror], and the hearts reached the throats, and you assumed about Allah [various] assumptions.”* The sūrah depicts how the hypocrites panicked, claiming the Prophet's promises were delusions, while the true believers said: *“This is what Allah and His Messenger promised us... and it only increased them in faith and submission”* (33:22). After a month-long siege, God sent a freezing, howling hurricane that tore down the Confederates' tents, overturned their cooking pots, and drove them into frantic retreat, breaking the siege without military engagement.

**The Structural Unfolding of the Community Charter**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Aḥzāb moves from civil-legal reforms to battlefield psychology, and from the domestic life of the Prophet's household to the cosmic drama of human existence. The opening movement (1–8) dismantles pagan social fictions: declaring that God has not made for any man two hearts inside his breast, abolishing the pagan practice of *ẓihār* (a form of abusive divorce), and outlawing legal adoption that erases biological lineage, commanding that adopted children be called by their real fathers' names to safeguard inheritance and identity. The second movement (9–27) conducts the exhaustive military autopsy of the Trench and the subsequent reckoning of the treasonous Banū Qurayẓah, celebrating the steadfastness of the men *“who were true to what they promised Allah; of them is he who has fulfilled his vow [by martyrdom], and of them is he who awaits, and they have not altered in the least”* (33:23). The third movement (28–59) establishes the sacred etiquette of the Prophet's household and the community: offering the Mothers of the Believers (*Ummahāt al-Mu'minīn*) the choice between worldly luxury or devotion to Allah and His Messenger; affirming their unique spiritual station; purifying the Prophet's household (*Ahl al-Bayt*, 33:33); celebrating the absolute spiritual equality of men and women across ten virtues in verse 35; settling the sensitive marriage of the Prophet to Zaynab bint Jaḥsh to definitively shatter the taboo of marrying the former wife of an adopted son; proclaiming Muḥammad ﷺ as the Seal of the Prophets (*Khātam al-Nabiyyīn*, 33:40); and legislating the outdoor cloak (*jilbāb*, 33:59) to protect believing women from harassment. The climax (60–73) rebukes the hypocrites who spread malicious rumors, reminds humanity of the tragedy of those who followed corrupt leaders in history, and concludes with the breathtaking metaphysical mystery of the Cosmic Trust (*al-Amānah*, 33:72): offered to the heavens, the earth, and the towering mountains, which all recoiled from its terrifying weight in awe, yet borne by humanity, who proved unjust and ignorant, yet capable of ultimate redemption.

**The Exalted Paradigm and the Sacred Trust**

The defining theological insight of Sūrah al-Aḥzāb is that the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ is the living center of the Muslim community's identity. He is the *Uswah Ḥasanah* (the Excellent Pattern)—the leader who swung the pickaxe in the freezing ditch with hunger-stones strapped to his stomach, the statesman who upheld absolute justice, the husband who treated his wives with boundless compassion, and the prophet upon whom God and His angels send perpetual blessings. Sūrah al-Aḥzāb teaches that when external enemies lay siege and internal hypocrites plot betrayal, victory is secured not by panic, but by gathering around the moral leadership of the Prophet and carrying the sacred Trust of moral responsibility with unyielding fidelity.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:1

> O Prophet! ˹Always˺ be mindful of Allah, and do not yield to the disbelievers and the hypocrites. Indeed, Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Name That Makes the Command a Duty of Office**

The sūrah opens with a vocative used nowhere else with this density: *yā ayyuhā al-nabī*, "O Prophet." It returns at 33:28, 33:45, 33:50 and 33:59, and it carries the whole of Sūrah al-Ṭalāq (65:1). The title is doing work. Muḥammad is not addressed as a private man whose comfort is being protected, nor as a clan leader defending his standing. He is addressed by office, and the command that follows therefore belongs to the job he was given rather than to his personal dignity. That frame governs everything after it. The rulings on marriage, the siege narrative, the etiquette of visiting the Prophet's house — each is presented as a requirement of the messenger, which is exactly why none of them can be traded away in a negotiation with his critics. A man is free to yield his own preferences; a messenger cannot yield what he was sent with, because he does not own it. The sūrah will enforce that distinction in law after law.

**Two Kinds of Enemy, One Demand**

The verse pairs "the disbelievers and the hypocrites," and the pairing is the argument. They were not the same threat. The Quraysh could be met in the open; the hypocrites sat inside Medina, prayed in the same rows and spoke the same creed. The command covering both is not "do not fight them" or "do not argue with them" but "do not obey them" — the word is *ṭāʿa*, obedience, which presumes that someone has claimed the right to be followed. Strip away the circumstances and both groups wanted one thing: a say over revelation. The Quraysh asked for certain words never to be spoken; the hypocrites asked for rulings quietly softened, or for the community to be steered toward safer friends. Both requests reduce to a demand that the messenger take instruction from men. The verse refuses in a single clause and then gives the reason in the next one: the One who knows cannot be corrected by those who do not.

**The Delegation Behind the Verse**

Reports preserved in the exegetical literature connect the verse to a specific meeting. After the defeat at Uḥud, leaders of the Quraysh came to Medina under the Prophet's assurance of protection. They asked him to stop rejecting their idols and to acknowledge that those idols could intercede for their worshippers. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb asked for permission to kill them; the Prophet refused, because safe conduct had been granted, and had them escorted out of the city instead. Whether or not every detail of the report is sound, it captures the situation the verse addresses: a request dressed as diplomacy, advanced by men who had come to bargain over the content of the message. The Prophet's answer distinguishes two things that are easily confused — what may not be conceded, and how opponents must be treated. He did not concede a word of doctrine, and he did not harm a single delegate.

**A Refusal That Kept the Rules of Hospitality**

The refusal in this verse is doctrinal, not personal, and the Book states the difference elsewhere in plain terms: "And if anyone from the polytheists asks for your protection ˹O Prophet˺, grant it to them so they may hear the Word of Allah, then escort them to a place of safety, for they are a people who have no knowledge." (9:6). Protection granted to an enemy remains binding; the argument happens under the shelter of it. The same sūrah tradition remembers the Prophet as lenient to those who wronged him, and 3:159 grounds that leniency in mercy rather than weakness: "It is out of Allah's mercy that you ˹O Prophet˺ have been lenient with them. Had you been cruel or hard-hearted, they would have certainly abandoned you." (3:159). Read together, the three texts define the posture: the message does not bend, and the messenger does not become brutal in defending it. Refusal of an idea is one act; humiliation of a person is another.

**Mindfulness First: Why the Command Starts with Taqwā**

The order inside the verse is deliberate: be mindful of God *before* you refuse. A refusal can decay into an appetite of its own — the pleasure of being right, the satisfaction of watching an opponent lose. Taqwā places the whole encounter under a third party's gaze, so that the stand taken is measured by God's law rather than by the temperature of the room. The Book states the standard without softening it: "O believers! Be mindful of Allah in the way He deserves, and do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission ˹to Him˺." (3:102). A person conscious of God cannot use His cause for his own temper, and cannot let a legitimate grievance excuse an illegitimate method. In a chapter filled with pressure from outside and betrayal from inside, the opening command fixes the inner discipline that makes the outer firmness survivable.

**All-Knowing, All-Wise: The Reasons the Verse Gives**

Note what the verse does not say. It does not say, "do not obey them, for they are wrong," although they were. It says God is All-Knowing, All-Wise — *ʿalīm*, *ḥakīm* — and leaves the reader to draw the conclusion. Knowledge covers the hidden: the delegation's private intentions, the counsels the hypocrites held behind closed doors, the difference between the question a man asks and the answer he wants. Wisdom covers the arrangement of events: the timing of a refusal, the protection of a young community, the long consequences of a concession never made. Together the two names tell the Prophet that he does not need to justify the message to its critics or to prove that the truth is his, because the One who sent it already knows everything the critics conceal. Delivery is his task; the outcome belongs to God.

**What the Prohibition Leaves Standing**

"Do not obey them" denies a claim to command; it does not cancel the ordinary duties that life in the same city requires. The believers still traded with these people, treated their sick, honoured their guests and kept faith with their envoys, and nothing in this verse licenses contempt for persons as distinct from rejection of a demand. The chapter will later require the community to hold its own members to a standard of speech and conduct, which is a further sign that firmness is not hostility. What is forbidden is precisely what was asked for: that the messenger take instruction from the disbelievers of Mecca or from the malcontents of Medina. Everything else — fairness, hospitality, honouring a safe conduct — remains in force, and the verse's insistence on God's knowledge warns that these duties are seen as clearly as the secret meetings that made the command necessary.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:2

> Follow what is revealed to you from your Lord. Surely Allah is All-Aware of what you ˹all˺ do

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Completes the Prohibition**

Verse 1 removed a loyalty; verse 2 supplies the replacement, and the two sentences are one instruction cut in half. "Follow what is revealed to you from your Lord." There is no third option in the Qur'an's account of a human life, no neutral position of following nobody: everyone is walking behind something, whether a revelation, a party, a market or an appetite. The command is therefore not an addition to the previous verse but its positive face. Refusing the Quraysh and refusing the hypocrites would mean nothing if the messenger were left with no directive at all. What fills the space is *al-waḥy*, the revealed text, which in this sūrah will be asked to settle questions of inheritance, adoption, marriage, modesty and war. Nothing human is held to be outside its reach, and no custom is protected from it merely because it is old.

**Following Is a Road, Not a Gesture**

The verb *ittabaʿa* means to follow closely, to walk behind someone along a path, to track a trail. Its sense is movement rather than agreement, which is why the Qur'an uses it for the step-by-step conduct of a life and not for a person's opinion about the truth. To follow the revelation is to walk where it points in each decision that arrives, and the Book supplies the model of what that looks like when the road is unpopular: "Now We have set you ˹O Prophet˺ on the ˹clear˺ Way of faith. So follow it, and do not follow the desires of those who do not know ˹the truth˺." (45:18). Following, in this usage, is what a man does with his feet and his money and his hours once he has understood the command. A community can recite a revealed book for generations and still be following something else entirely; the verb forecloses that possibility from the outset.

**From Your Lord: The Answer to the Charge of Invention**

The clause "from your Lord" is not decoration. Between the accusations of Mecca and the pressure of Medina stood a single charge that had to be answered everywhere the message went: that the Prophet had written the Qur'an himself or gathered it from helpers. The Book rejects the claim in exactly these terms: "The disbelievers say, “This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but a fabrication which he made up with the help of others.” (25:4) Their claim is totally unjustified and untrue!" (25:4), and it states the alternative in Sūrah Yūnus: "It is not ˹possible˺ for this Quran to have been produced by anyone other than Allah." (10:37). Placing "from your Lord" inside a command to obey is a quiet argument. If the text came from God, then following it is not submission to Muḥammad's preference, and the men who negotiate with him are not negotiating with a man's opinion.

**All-Aware of What You Do: Why the Address Widens**

The verse addresses the Prophet alone, then closes with a plural verb: *mā taʿmalūn*, "what you ˹all˺ do." The shift is not loose grammar. It draws every hearer into the sentence — the believers who were holding the line, the waverers, and above all the hypocrites whose real conduct differed from their speech. God's awareness is set against the management of appearances that defines a divided community. Men can arrange what a town sees; they cannot arrange what God sees. In a chapter where secret counsel, whispered discouragement and quiet withdrawal from danger will soon be described, this closing phrase is also a warning, and it gives the command its teeth: the following of revelation is measured by what a person actually does when it costs him something.

**A Reading That Turns the Verse Toward the Hypocrites**

The exegetical literature records that some early readers recited the final verb as "what they do" instead of "what you do." The variant is not part of the standard text, and it does not alter the argument, since God's awareness covers both addresses. What it shows is how naturally verse 2 was heard as aimed at the people whose conduct verse 1 had just exposed. On that hearing, the sentence about revelation is a dividing line: the command to follow it is spoken to the Prophet, and the report that God is aware of deeds is spoken over everyone who heard the command, including those who had no intention of keeping it. The reading is a reminder that the Qur'an anticipates its own audience — it knows that a public claim of loyalty is cheaper than a private one.

**A Fixed Point in a City Under Pressure**

Medina was a place where everything had recently been rearranged: who inherits, who counts as a son, who may marry whom, how a guest behaves in a Prophet's house. When customs move, people reach for something that does not move, and the verse identifies it. The revealed text, and nothing else, is the reference point. That is why the command arrives immediately after the refusal of two powerful interest groups: the answer to pressure is not a compromise with the pressure but a return to the source. The chapter will go on to show what human beings do when that fixed point collides with their fears, and the contrast it draws will be as sharp as this verse's opening imperative. There is a second purpose in the command, which is to make the messenger's own position clear from the start. He is the first person addressed by it, and he is bound by the same text he delivers. The verses that follow will require him to accept rulings that cut across his own domestic arrangements and his own preferences, and this opening line establishes in advance that the revelation is not his to adjust. A community cannot be asked to measure itself against a standard its own messenger treats as optional, and the sūrah removes that possibility in its second sentence.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:3

> And put your trust in Allah, for Allah is sufficient as a Trustee of Affairs

**Expanded Commentary**

**Trust Comes Third, and the Order Is the Point**

Three commands stand at the head of the sūrah, and they are stacked. Be mindful of God; follow what was revealed to you; then put your trust in God. The third is not an escape from the first two. A community that leans on trust while neglecting its duty has confused confidence with carelessness; a community that trusts only its own preparations has not trusted at all. The sequence gives trust its proper place as the last act in a chain of obedience, the moment when a person has done what was commanded and hands the result to God. That order will be tested in everything this chapter describes. Men who had just been told to hold a line against a coalition far larger than themselves are told, first, where the line came from and, only then, who holds the outcome.

**Wakīl: The One Who Handles What You Cannot Carry**

The name at the end of the verse, *wakīl*, belongs to the language of agency and delegated affairs. A *wakīl* is the person appointed to see a matter through on someone else's behalf when the principal cannot manage it himself. That is its precise sense here, and the precision matters. Trust is not the transfer to God of work a person is capable of doing; a client does not appoint an agent to do what he will do himself. Trust concerns the outcome — the arrival of rain, the break of a siege, the change of a heart, the ultimate reckoning — those parts of an affair that lie beyond any creature's reach. The Book uses the same title for God across a range of chapters, including 4:81, 4:132, 4:171 and 17:65, always with the same force: matters are returned to the One competent to handle them.

**Alliances and the Temptation of a Shortcut**

In the Arabia of this period, safety was purchased through pacts. A tribe survived by attaching itself to stronger neighbours, and a leader secured his position by balancing obligations to several allies at once. The temptation for the Prophet, and for the Muslim community, was to buy protection of that kind by paying in principle — to keep a powerful tribe content by softening a ruling, or by keeping a discreet silence about its idols. This verse forecloses the trade. God suffices as a guardian, which means the community does not need any alliance whose price is a part of the revelation. The instruction has a cost, and the cost is not hidden: refusing such bargains leaves the community visibly dependent on God alone, which is precisely the condition the very next verses of the chapter will describe in practice.

**Sufficiency Is a Claim About What God Is, Not About What Happens**

*Kafā bi-Llāhi wakīlā* — "Allah is sufficient as a Trustee" — asserts adequacy, not comfort. The construction with the prefixed *bi-* strengthens the verb: not merely that God suffices, but that He suffices entirely, in a way that leaves no deficit another ally could cover. What follows from that is a particular kind of courage. The person who trusts does not expect that no harm will come, and the verse does not promise immunity; it promises that the matter has been placed with One whose management of it is not subject to error or neglect. Events can still be bitter. The siege still came, and hunger still came with it. The claim being made is that nothing done for God is ever left unattended, and nothing entrusted to Him is ever lost.

**Trust Did Not Cancel the Spade**

The most famous illustration of this verse in the Prophet's lifetime involved a great deal of digging. When Medina learned that a confederate army was marching against it in 5/627, the defence of the city was decided in consultation, and Salmān al-Fārisī, a Persian convert familiar with siege warfare, proposed a trench along the exposed northern side where cavalry could otherwise ride straight in. The Muslims dug it themselves, and the Prophet carried earth with them. The confederate force of roughly ten thousand faced a defending community of about three thousand, and it failed to cross. Nothing in that preparation contradicts the command to trust God. The trench was the work men owed the situation; the outcome — a coalition that broke apart without a decisive battle — was beyond their control, and the Book attributes the end of the siege to a wind and to forces the defenders could not see.

**Two Things Trust Is Not**

Trust is not the belief that events will go the way a person wants. The defenders of Medina did not want a siege, and they got one; the same community would later bury its dead. Trust is not the suspension of ordinary means either, since the same Prophet who commanded reliance on God ordered the trench, and the same Book that names God a Trustee names Him a Reckoner of deeds. What the verse does assert is narrower and harder to counterfeit: the affair is with One who cannot fail to manage it. That is why the command sits after two others rather than before them. A person who has neglected what he was commanded has nothing to hand over, and a person who has done it grudgingly is not handing over an affair but abandoning one.

**The Same Words at the Middle of the Sūrah**

Nearly the whole cluster of commands from 33:1–3 reappears at 33:48 in almost the same order: "Do not yield to the disbelievers and the hypocrites. Overlook their annoyances, and put your trust in Allah. For Allah is sufficient as a Trustee of Affairs." (33:48). The repetition frames the chapter. What begins as an instruction to the messenger returns later with one addition — a command to overlook the annoyance of opponents — which shows how the earlier commands were meant to be lived out over time rather than as a single decision. Note also what stays identical: the refusal of obedience to two groups, the entrusting of the affair to God, and the closing name *wakīl*. A sūrah of law and war is careful about its bookends; it tells the reader twice, in the same words, on what the community is supposed to lean.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:4

> Allah does not place two hearts in any person’s chest. Nor does He regard your wives as ˹unlawful for you like˺ your real mothers, ˹even˺ if you say they are. Nor does He regard your adopted children as your real children. These are only your baseless assertions. But Allah declares the truth, and He ˹alone˺ guides to the ˹Right˺ Way

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Chest Does Not Hold Two Loyalties**

The verse begins with anatomy in order to make a moral point. A person has one chest and therefore one heart, and the heart is the organ of loyalty in the Qur'an's language — the place where intention, love and decision are seated. From that single fact the verse draws a conclusion about the divided life: a man cannot hold the revelation in one hand and the standards of its opponents in the other. The image is not about emotions running in two directions at once; everyone knows what it is to be pulled both ways. It is about a claim of final authority. Two masters can be served only by cheating one of them. The Book states the standard plainly: a community is told to be mindful of God as He deserves to be feared and not to die except in submission to Him (3:102). Divided reverence is not a compromise between two faiths; it is the subordination of one to the other while keeping the vocabulary of both.

**Ẓihār: When a Wife Became Neither Wife Nor Free**

The second clause deals with a practice that had been doing quiet damage in Arabian households. *Ẓihār* was a form of repudiation in which a husband said to his wife, "You are to me like my mother's back." The words ended the marriage in practice, because the husband would no longer touch her, yet they did not free her: she remained bound to him and could not marry another man. A woman could spend years in that condition, neither married nor released. This is why the Qur'an, in Sūrah al-Mujādilah, records the plea of a woman who brought her case to the Prophet and then to God, and why it answers with a flat denial of the formula's pretence: "Those of you who ˹sinfully˺ divorce their wives by comparing them to their mothers ˹should know that˺ their wives are in no way their mothers. None can be their mothers except those who gave birth to them. What they say is certainly detestable and false." (58:2). Verse 33:4 belongs to the same correction, and its argument is the simplest possible one: a word cannot create a mother.

**A Claimed Son Is Not a Son**

The third clause addresses the other relationship that Arabian custom had built out of speech rather than blood. Zayd ibn Ḥārithah had been brought into the Prophet's household as a young man; when his father came to take him home, Zayd chose to stay, and the Prophet declared him his son, so that he was known as Zayd ibn Muḥammad and, by the custom of the time, would have inherited from him. That declaration is what this verse rejects as a legal fact. The point is not that affection was unreal or that the Prophet's care for Zayd was a mistake; it is that a bond created by announcement cannot carry the consequences of a bond created by birth. The Qur'an's argument is symmetrical with the case of the wife: just as saying "you are my mother" does not make a mother, saying "you are my son" does not make a son. Custom had confused naming with being, and the verse separates them.

**Words From Mouths, Truth From God**

"These are only your baseless assertions," the verse says, and the phrase marks the whole class of arrangements it has just dismantled. The Arabic describes speech that has no reality behind it — words thrown out by the mouth with nothing to stand on. Against that, God "declares the truth," and the contrast is complete: human speech creates nothing by being spoken, while God's speech is the very mechanism of existence. The Book says of Him that when He wills a thing He says to it "Be," and it is (36:82), and that He created the heavens and the earth in truth, or through truth (39:5). The legal rulings in this passage, then, are not the imposition of an arbitrary rule on private life. They are the removal of fictions that people had mistaken for facts, and the restoration of the actual structure of kinship as God made it.

**What the Correction Protects**

It is worth stating plainly what this verse is not. It is not a denial of adoption as an act of mercy. The Book never discourages taking in an orphan, raising a child, or loving someone else's son as one's own; the Prophet himself cared for Zayd for the rest of his life, sent him on campaigns, and grieved when he was killed. What the verse denies is the transfer of lineage through a declaration. A child taken into a home does not thereby acquire that home's ancestors, and it is the child who is protected by the refusal. Every institution of inheritance, guardianship and marital eligibility in a society runs on the question of who is related to whom; a fiction at that point does not merely confuse the record, it exposes the child to a lifetime of disputed claims. The correction fixes what can be fixed and leaves affection free. A man may be a father to a child in every way that matters and still be accurate about the child's origin, and the Qur'an treats those two facts as entirely compatible.


**Guidance in the Middle of a Legal Revolution**

The verse does not end with the denials. It ends with a claim about direction: "Allah declares the truth, and He ˹alone˺ guides to the ˹Right˺ Way." That closing is what turns a list of prohibitions into a programme. Each ruling that follows in this sūrah — names, inheritance, marriage, the status of the Prophet's household — will be presented as a step on a road, not as a fence around a community. The people being asked to accept these changes were being asked to give up arrangements that had organised their families for generations, and the verse supplies the reason they could: the same God who corrects the fiction is the one guiding those who accept the correction. Guidance here is not a private feeling of certainty but a described path, and the chapter will now walk along it ruling by ruling.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:5

> Let your adopted children keep their family names. That is more just in the sight of Allah. But if you do not know their fathers, then they are ˹simply˺ your fellow believers and close associates. There is no blame on you for what you do by mistake, but ˹only˺ for what you do intentionally. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Child Keeps His Father's Name**

The correction announced in the previous verse is given a practical shape here. If a claimed son is not a real son, then the first thing to be restored is the name by which he is known, because a name is how a society records a fact. The command is to call adopted children after their fathers. The Arabic verb is *udʿūhum*, from the same root as the word for calling and claiming, and it governs the whole of a person's public identity: what is written on him, what he inherits, whom he may marry. The verse does not treat this as a matter of taste. It gives a reason: this is more just in God's sight. Justice is assessed at the point of accuracy, not at the point of sentiment. A family that takes in a child and gives him a name he does not have is, in the long run, handing him a legal identity that will be contradicted by every document and every inheritance claim for the rest of his life. Restoring the name protects the child as much as it protects the truth.

**The Report of ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar**

The change is visible in the historical record. It is reported from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar that the community used to call Zayd ibn Ḥārithah nothing other than Zayd ibn Muḥammad, until this verse came down, after which he was Zayd ibn Ḥārithah. The report is short and its significance is large. It shows the practice was not a technicality confined to one household; an adopted son of the Prophet carried the Prophet's name before the whole city, and the correction was public. It also shows how quickly a community can be persuaded to change an inherited practice when the alternative is a clear command. A name change is, in ordinary life, an inconvenience; the community accepted it because the ruling came with its reason attached. ʿUmar's own son is the one reporting the detail, which places the correction firmly inside the first generation rather than in later legal discussion.

**Mawālī: The Ties That Replace an Invented Lineage**

The verse does not leave the adopted child with nothing. Where the father is unknown, the child is "your fellow believers and close associates" — brothers in religion and *mawālī*. The term *mawlā* covers a range of close relationships at once: a client, a freedman, an ally, a protected associate, and, in other contexts, an heir. The Qur'an uses its root for God as Master and for the believer's protecting friend, and its plural here expands the child's circle rather than shrinking it. The point is that removing a false lineage does not remove a real relationship. The community that takes in a child owes him protection, belonging and support; what it does not owe him is a fabricated ancestry. The Book makes the same move in relation to inheritance: "As for those you have made a pledge to, give them their share. Surely Allah is a Witness over all things." (4:33). Loyalty can be honoured without a lie about blood.

**Mistake and Intention**

The verse then adds a limit that protects everyone who lived through the change: "There is no blame on you for what you do by mistake, but ˹only˺ for what you do intentionally." People who had called a child after the wrong father out of ignorance, or who had used a formula they had inherited without knowing it was wrong, were not to be punished for it. The Prophet stated the principle in a report preserved from Abū Dharr al-Ghifārī and others, which al-Arnāʾūṭ graded sound: "Allah has forgiven for my nation their mistakes and forgetfulness, and what they are forced to do." (Ibn Mājah 2043). What the verse does hold a person responsible for is the deliberate act — the choice to keep a fiction after it has been exposed, or to attach a name to a child knowing the name is not his. This distinction runs through the whole of Islamic law and it is announced here first about something as intimate as a family name.

**How the Rule Protects the Child**

The verse covers the ordinary case and the harder one. Where the father is known, the child keeps his name and his place in that family's line, whatever the circumstances of his upbringing. Where the father is unknown, he is not left nameless in the community's eyes: he becomes a brother in faith and a client of those who raised him, with protection, belonging and standing secured by that relationship. The alternatives that people might have preferred — a borrowed name and a fabricated ancestry — are exactly what the verse rules out. There is a hard edge in this, since a child raised in a household will ask about a father whose name he now carries. But the community is given something to offer him in the same sentence that refuses him a false name, and the offer is not empty. In the Qur'an, brotherhood in faith is a real tie with real obligations, and the term used for it here is never applied to a stranger who happens to live nearby.


**Forgiving and Merciful in the Middle of Family Law**

Closing with the names All-Forgiving and Most Merciful, the verse sets the tone for the legislation that follows. A law that rewrites names, adoption, inheritance and marriage is a law people will stumble over; some will fail through habit, some through ignorance, some because a practice was woven into their honour and their family. The closing names tell the community how to carry the change: correct the record, protect the child, honour the ties that remain, and do not turn the correction of a mistake into an instrument of shame. Legal firmness and mercy are not alternatives in this sūrah; they are the two halves of the same instruction, and this verse is where both are named together for the first time.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:6

> The Prophet has a stronger affinity to the believers than they do themselves. And his wives are their mothers. As ordained by Allah, blood relatives are more entitled ˹to inheritance˺ than ˹other˺ believers and immigrants, unless you ˹want to˺ show kindness to your ˹close˺ associates ˹through bequest˺. This is decreed in the Record

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Claim on the Believer That Outranks the Self**

The verse places the Prophet in a relationship that no other human being occupies: he is closer to the believers than their own selves. The Arabic *awlā* is a comparative of closeness and authority at once — nearer, and therefore entitled to a prior claim. This is not a statement about affection, which would be easy to say of any beloved leader. It is a statement about the ordering of a life. The Book spells out the same priority in Sūrah al-Tawbah when it tells the believers what has no right to compete with the messenger: "Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'If your parents and children and siblings and spouses and extended family and the wealth you have acquired and the trade you fear will decline and the homes you cherish—˹if all these˺ are more beloved to you than Allah and His Messenger and struggling in His Way, then wait until Allah brings about His Will.'" (9:24). The claim of the verse is heavier than any family tie, and it is precisely because the family ties of the previous verses had just been reorganised that it needed to be said.

**Mothers of the Believers**

The second sentence gives the Prophet's wives a title that has lasted fourteen centuries: they are the mothers of the believers. The phrase did two things at once in the society that received it. It gave the wives of the Prophet honour and a claim of respect from every Muslim household, and it placed them outside the ordinary marriage market: a mother is not someone a man may marry. The sūrah later gives that implication explicit legal force, and the effect was to protect the household from becoming a subject of political bargaining after the Prophet's death. The title is also a warning about the kind of attention the wives could attract, and the chapters that follow will describe the standard of conduct their position required. What the verse establishes here is simple: the household of the Prophet is not a private household into which the community may intrude at will; it belongs to the community as a whole in the way that a mother does.

**Inheritance Before and After the Hijra**

The third sentence deals with a legal arrangement the community had been living with since the migration. In the early Madinan period, inheritance flowed along the lines of belief and migration as much as along the lines of blood: a man who had no close relatives among the emigrants could inherit from, and be inherited by, his fellow believers, and the Book states the rule in Sūrah al-Anfāl: "But only blood relatives are now entitled to inherit from one another, as ordained by Allah." (8:75). This verse confirms that the movement of the line back toward kinship is settled law, not policy. What it does not cancel is the older obligation of loyalty: the "close associates" whose relationship had been created by migration are still to be honoured, though by a different mechanism. The change took a bond that had been compulsory and made it voluntary — which is exactly the shift the next clause describes.

**The Bequest That Keeps Kindness Possible**

The exception is the verse's warmest element: unless you want to show kindness to your close associates through bequest. Instead of inheriting from one another automatically, the believer now honours his old allies by a gift he chooses to make, within the limits that the law of wills sets. The structure is important. The obligation is removed; the opportunity to be generous is left open. A community whose members had risked everything for one another across the migration was not going to be told to forget each other. They were told that the bond of blood must not be defeated by the bond of friendship in the division of an estate, and that friendship could be honoured out of a person's own share. The verse therefore protects both the family and the friend, and it leaves the moral effort where it belongs — in a deliberate gift rather than a legal entitlement.

**What the Believers Receive in Return**

The verse is not only a claim on the believers; it is a gift to them. Two things are handed over in it. The first is a protector whose concern for them is placed above their own immediate interests — a leader defined by his claim on them rather than by what he takes from them. The second is a household they are permitted to honour as their own, with the wives of the Prophet holding the standing of mothers to every Muslim man and woman rather than the ordinary position of a family the community observes from outside. Both elements matter to the community the sūrah is addressing, which had just been told that its own ties of blood and migration were being rearranged. A believer whose inheritance went to his brother instead of his ally, and whose adopted child now carried another man's name, was given, in the same passage, a relationship that no ruling could dissolve. The Prophet stands closer to him than he stands to himself, and that is where the ties of the new community were anchored.


**Decreed in the Record**

The verse closes by locating the ruling outside of human negotiation: this is written in the Record. The commentators took the phrase in two ways. Some read it as the Preserved Tablet, the register in which God's decrees are inscribed before they occur in time; others read it as the Book itself, meaning that the ruling is fixed by revelation and not subject to the community's preference. The first reading is the stronger of the two, since the Qur'an uses the same formula for events long before they happen, but the two agree on the practical consequence: the change in inheritance is not a settlement to be revisited when it becomes inconvenient. A community that had just been told that its dearest relationships were being reordered is being told, at the same time, that the reordering is not arbitrary. It was decreed in the Record before it was announced in Medina.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:7

> And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the prophets, as well as from you ˹O Prophet˺, and from Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, son of Mary. We did take a solemn covenant from ˹all of˺ them

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Covenant Taken From Every Prophet**

The verse opens a window on something that happened outside of human history altogether: God took a binding agreement from the prophets. The Book describes its content elsewhere. In Sūrah Āl ʿImrān, the covenant obliges every prophet to believe in and support any messenger who comes after him confirming what he already has: "˹Remember˺ when Allah made a covenant with the prophets, ˹saying,˺ 'Now that I have given you the Book and wisdom, if there comes to you a messenger confirming what you have, you must believe in him and support him.'" (3:81). The implication for the community addressed by this sūrah is exact. If every prophet of God was bound in advance to acknowledge the messenger who would come with the same message, then the arrival of Muḥammad is not a departure from the religion of Noah or Moses. It is the fulfilment of the pledge those prophets had already given.

**The Names and Their Order**

Four prophets are named, and the order carries meaning: Noah, Abraham, Moses and Jesus son of Mary. These are the figures the Qur'an elsewhere gathers as the founders of the single religion its messengers all taught. In Sūrah al-Shūrā the same four are named together as the content of a shared law: "He has ordained for you ˹believers˺ the Way which He decreed for Noah, and what We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ and what We decreed for Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, ˹commanding:˺ 'Uphold the faith, and make no divisions in it.'" (42:13). The sequence runs in historical order, from the earliest messenger recorded after Adam in the Qur'an's narrative to the last messenger of the previous communities, and it lands exactly at the point where the new message enters. Jesus is named with his mother, as he is throughout the Qur'an, and the naming keeps him inside the line of prophets rather than above it.

**"As Well As From You": The Messenger Inside the Covenant**

The verse's most striking element is not its list but its inclusion. Before naming the four great figures, it says a covenant was taken "as well as from you," addressing the Prophet himself. He is not the beneficiary of the covenant of the prophets; he is one of its parties. The Book makes the same point at the end of Sūrah al-Aḥzāb, when it says that Muḥammad is the Messenger of God and the seal of the prophets (33:40), and the two statements work together. He stands in the line of the men whose pledge he now recalls, shares their history and their obligation, and completes their number. This is also why the covenant is meaningful for his audience: the messenger who binds them to himself is himself bound to the same Lord, and his mission is the object of a promise older than his own lifetime.

**Ghaliẓ: The Weight of the Pledge**

The closing words of the verse describe the covenant twice, and the second description adds a texture: it was solemn, *ghalīẓ*. The word means thick, coarse, heavy — the opposite of what is thin or flimsy. In this context it is applied to the agreement, and the physical sense of the word is doing the work: this pledge was not a light undertaking that could be brushed aside with the passage of time. The Qur'an uses the same adjective for the covenant taken from the Children of Israel and for the covenant of marriage, which tells us what kind of thing it has in mind. A covenant in this vocabulary is contract, witness, obligation, and its weight is measured in what it costs to keep. The prophets who gave it gave it with witnesses — the verse ends with the emphatic construction, "We did take a solemn covenant from ˹all of˺ them," leaving no exception and no prophet outside its scope.

**Covenants in the Qur'an's Vocabulary**

The word *mīthāq* is not used sparingly in the Book. It is the term for the pledge taken from the Children of Israel at the mountain, for the agreement God made with those who were given the Scripture, and for the covenant He made with the prophets in this verse. It is also the term the Qur'an uses for the strongest bond between human beings, including the marriage contract, which the Book calls a solemn pledge. Reading the verse against that background sharpens it. This is not a private arrangement between God and a few favoured men; it is the same instrument of obligation that God uses to bind communities and couples, applied to the transmission of revelation itself. It also explains why the pledge has to be recalled in a sūrah of conflict. When a community is under siege and tempted to reinterpret what it has received, the verse reminds it that the men who carried the message before them had already answered the same question, and had answered it before the pressure arrived.


**Why a Pledge, and Not Only a Message**

The verse is not an aside in a sūrah about war and household law. It answers the central accusation of the community's opponents: that the new message was a novelty invented in Medina and Mecca. By grounding the mission in a pledge taken from every prophet who preceded it, the verse shows that the religion being defended was never a local incident. It also shows how revelation was transmitted — not as private insight granted to isolated men, but as a public obligation each messenger accepted before his own people heard a word of it. The community is therefore not being asked to make an unprecedented leap. It is being asked to accept what Noah, Abraham, Moses and Jesus had already accepted, and to stand, at last, where all of them stood.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:8

> so that He may question these men of truth about their ˹delivery of the˺ truth. And He has prepared a painful punishment for the disbelievers

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Purpose the Covenant Served**

The verse states why the pledge was taken. God will question the truthful men about their truth — *li-yasʾala al-ṣādiqīna ʿan ṣidqihim* — which is a remarkable way to describe an accountability. The people being called to account here are not the deniers but the ones who kept faith with the message. This is the logic of a trust: a messenger who accepted responsibility for a delivery is asked what he did with it, and the question is put to the honest precisely because the honest are the ones who can answer it. In Sūrah al-Aʿrāf the Book says the same of both parties to the exchange: "We will surely question those who received messengers and We will question the messengers ˹themselves˺." (7:6). The covenant at the head of this sūrah is therefore not a ceremony; it is the opening of a ledger that will be read out on a day the Book describes again and again.

**Ṣidq: Truthfulness as a Way of Standing**

The word used for the men being questioned is *ṣādiqīn*, the truthful — not "the messengers" as a job title but truthfulness as a character. Arabic uses the same root for a statement that matches reality and for a person whose life matches his claim, and the Qur'an exploits both senses. A prophet is a *ṣādiq* in that he reports the revelation honestly; he is also a *ṣādiq* in that his conduct does not contradict what he reports. The verse asks about *ṣidq* in this double sense, which is why it can be applied to the community that inherited the message and not only to the four names of the previous verse. Claiming to accept a revelation is easy. The question at the end of the covenant is whether the claim was true — a question that no vocabulary and no ceremony can answer in advance.

**A Question That Does Not Seek Information**

No accounting in the Qur'an exists to inform God. The Book states that He knows the hidden and the evident, that not a leaf falls without His knowledge, and that He will gather all the messengers and ask them what response they received, to which they will answer that they have no knowledge compared to Him and that He alone knows the unseen (5:109). Questioning in this register is public, not investigative. It fixes the record before the parties concerned, in the way a court reads out the facts it already possesses. For the messengers, the question is the moment their delivery is acknowledged; for the community that received the message, the same proceeding exposes what was done with what was heard. The verse attaches that proceeding to the covenant in order to make the point that a delivered message leaves nobody in a neutral position.

**Punishment Prepared, Not Improvised**

The second half of the verse turns to those on the other side of the delivery: a painful punishment has been prepared for the disbelievers. The verb is in the form that indicates readiness — prepared beforehand, kept in place — and the same form runs through the sūrah's descriptions of reward and punishment, so that mercy and severity are both presented as things already arranged rather than reactions that appear at the moment of provocation. This matters for the verse's argument: the denial of a message is not an offence against a messenger who must now be avenged, but the rejection of a covenant that God Himself had established with every prophet. The Book treats the fate of the deniers as a settled consequence rather than an outburst, and it names the punishment without dwelling on it, because the purpose of the verse is to draw attention to the question, not to the sentence.

**The Question the Community Will Also Face**

The verse mentions the truthful men, but its warning is addressed to the people reading it. A message that has been delivered is a trust in the hands of those who heard it, and the sūrah will spend the rest of its length showing what that meant for the community of Medina: how it behaved during the siege, how it treated the Prophet's household, how carefully it spoke about believing men and women. Nothing in the verse absolves the hearer of the responsibility the messenger discharged. The prophets answered for the delivery; the audience answers for the response. That is the shape of the whole chapter's moral argument, and it explains why the covenant is recalled here, immediately before a narrative of siege and betrayal. The men who heard the message first were about to be asked, in the plainest possible circumstances, whether their claim to have accepted it was true. The question is not reserved for prophets or for the day of judgment; it is put to a community every time it is told something and decides what to do with it.


**The Messengers' Honour and the Audience's Account**

Read together, the two halves of the verse hold the whole history of revelation in one frame. There are men who carried a trust and answered for it, and there are people who heard the carried trust and rejected it. Between those two groups stands the community reading the verse, and the sequence of this sūrah places it there deliberately: before the siege narrative, before the rulings about modesty and inheritance, the chapter establishes that the message has a history and its hearers have a responsibility. The covenant of the prophets was taken so that honesty would be asked about its object — a truthfulness wholly measured by what it did with what it was given.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:9

> O believers! Remember Allah’s favour upon you when ˹enemy˺ forces came to ˹besiege˺ you ˹in Medina˺, so We sent against them a ˹bitter˺ wind and forces you could not see. And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Favour Remembered From Inside the Danger**

The command is to remember a favour, but the moment being described is the worst the community had yet lived through. The Arabic places the two together deliberately: remember God's favour *when* the forces came. A blessing is normally recognised after the fact, when the outcome is visible and the fear has passed. Here the community is told to look back at the day of terror and see generosity in it, because the deliverance was already arranged while the danger was at its height. This is the sūrah's way of teaching gratitude: not a mood produced by comfort, but a judgement passed on a past crisis with the benefit of hindsight. The same method runs through the Book's dealings with its readers — a defeated people is asked to reread the account of its worst week and count what came out of it.

**Al-Aḥzāb: The Coalition That Came to End Medina**

The forces in question were the largest army the Muslims had faced. Roughly ten thousand fighters from the Quraysh, the Ghatafan, Banū Sulaym and other allied tribes marched on Medina in 5 AH, corresponding to 627 CE, under Abū Sufyān's leadership. The Muslim defenders numbered about three thousand. In consultation, Salmān al-Fārisī, a Persian convert who knew how sieges were fought in his homeland, proposed digging a trench along the northern approach where cavalry could otherwise ride straight into the city; the Muslims dug it themselves, and the Prophet carried earth alongside them. The confederates, unable to cross, settled into a siege that lasted some weeks. At its end they withdrew without a decisive battle, having suffered from cold, hunger, quarrels among the allies, and the collapse of their camps. The verse compresses all of that into a phrase: the forces came, and God sent against them what they could not withstand.

**A Bitter Wind and a Broken Alliance**

The wind that ended the siege was, according to reports, a cold easterly, the *ṣabā*. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī preserves the Prophet's words on the subject from Ibn ʿAbbās: "I have been granted victory with the ṣabā, and ʿĀd was destroyed with the dabūr." (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4105). The Qur'an names the same instrument of judgment on an earlier nation, when the devastating wind was sent against ʿĀd (51:41), and the memory is not accidental: the sūrah's audience knew what a wind from God could do. But the wind was not the whole story. The confederate coalition was held together by interest and resentment rather than by common loyalty, and it began to come apart in the camp, between the Quraysh and their Bedouin allies, and in the negotiations with the Jewish tribe that had been expected to open the city from within. A wind alone would have dispersed tents; a divided alliance was already dispersing itself, and the verse claims both as the work of God.

**Forces You Could Not See**

The verse does not say that God sent a wind only. It says a wind and *junūd* — forces the defenders could not see. The Qur'an uses this language of angelic reinforcement in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān, where the Prophet is told to encourage the believers with the promise of three thousand angels sent down in support (3:124), and Sūrah al-Tawbah records the same pairing in the aftermath of Ḥunayn: God sent down forces you could not see and punished those who disbelieved (9:26). For a besieged community counting its own numbers, this is the answer to despair: the arithmetic of a battle does not include every participant. It is also the reason the next verses can describe panic among the believers without treating the panic as the whole truth about the situation. The force that decided the siege could not be counted by any scout on either side.

**What the Enemy Expected, and What It Got**

The confederates did not come to raid; they came to remove the community. Their preparation, their numbers and the length of the march all point to a plan for a decisive end to the Muslim presence in Medina, and they had reason to think it would work. They had an understanding with a tribe inside the city, and they had the advantage of choosing the moment. None of it came to anything. The coalition that gathered in the first month of 5 AH was a coalition bought with promises and grievances, and it fractured the moment the trench removed the possibility of a quick victory. The Prophet's use of the interval is instructive: while negotiations went on, he worked at the seams of the alliance and considered a payment to the Ghatafan to detach them, and he was dissuaded by advisers who thought the price too high. The lesson the verse draws is not that the Muslims outmanoeuvred their enemies, but that the outcome lay with God even where human effort was at its most careful.


**Seen by the One Who Counts Conduct**

The verse closes with a formula that has already appeared in this sūrah: "And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do." Earlier it warned that no hidden intention escapes notice (33:2). Here it converts the deliverance into an assignment. A community that has been saved from an army now lives in the light of the fact that God watched how it behaved while it was afraid — who stood, who slipped away, who spread panic and who steadied others. Deliverance is not the end of the story; it opens a new account. The rest of the siege narrative, which this verse begins, is the record of exactly that conduct, and the sūrah will report it without flattery.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:10

> ˹Remember˺ when they came at you from east and west, when your eyes grew wild ˹in horror˺ and your hearts jumped into your throats, and you entertained ˹conflicting˺ thoughts about Allah

**Expanded Commentary**

**Attacked From Every Direction**

The Arabic of this verse is spatial rather than geographic: they came at you from above you and from below you, an idiom of complete encirclement in which the enemy occupies both ends of the valley and every approach in between. The rendering "from east and west" carries the same meaning in English — nowhere was left unwatched. What the setting gives the verse is a physical fact the defenders could see for themselves: no flank open, no route out, no manageable quarter to defend while the rest was abandoned. This is the situation the sūrah wants held in view before it describes what people began to think. Panic in the Qur'an is never treated as an abstract failure of faith; it is attached to circumstances that explain it, and the circumstances here were as bad as they could be for a small force with families inside the walls.

**Eyes Rolling, Hearts in Throats**

The description that follows is unusually physical. Eyes grow wild, wandering in every direction as the mind refuses to settle; hearts rise into the throats, so that a person can feel his own pulse where his breath should be. The Book reports terror without moralising about the sensation, because the sensation is not the fault being described. Feeling fear is not the same as abandoning duty, and the chapter's judgement will fall on what men said and did, not on what their bodies did when the army appeared. This is worth marking in a sūrah that will soon report cowardice and treason. The Qur'an is exact about the difference between a frightened believer who holds his post and a frightened man who finds a reason to leave.

**Conflicting Thoughts About God**

The verse reaches its subject in the last clause: you entertained conflicting thoughts about Allah. The Arabic is emphatic, using the plural form, *al-ẓunūn* — conjectures, opinions, guesses in several directions at once. Different people were entertaining different suspicions about the same promise. Some doubted whether help would come; some doubted whether it had ever been promised; some doubted the wisdom of having believed at all. Unstated in this verse but visible throughout the passage is the opposite kind of thinking, the confidence that kept others in the trench. The Book treats both as *ẓann*, as thinking shaped by the moment rather than by knowledge, and in doing so it refuses to make the promise of God dependent on how a frightened person feels at a given hour.

**Fear Recorded From Inside**

The chapter's narrative method deserves attention. Rather than reporting that the Muslims were frightened, the verse places the reader inside the experience with a chain of second-person clauses: the enemy came at you, your eyes rolled, your hearts rose, you thought. This is memory spoken to eyewitnesses, and it does what memory does best — it makes an event impossible to dismiss as a report about other people. The community reading this text in later generations is not allowed to imagine that the anxiety was merely a detail of someone else's story. The chapter's purpose in retelling the siege is moral instruction, and instruction of this kind works only if the hearer recognises his own heart in the description.

**Fear Is Not the Failure; Despair Is**

The verse reports terror and does not rebuke the sensation. What it does record is a turn in the mind: the thoughts about God began to pull in opposite directions. The Book treats that turn as the real danger, and it warns against it in stronger language than it uses for any bodily weakness. God forbids despair of His mercy outright, and the words the sons of Jacob used to steady each other, that no one should despair of God's mercy except the disbelieving, are quoted with approval (12:87). The same Book tells a community whose members had exceeded every limit not to lose hope in God's mercy (39:53). The distinction runs straight through this verse: a heart can pound, an eye can wander, and faith can still be intact; what faith cannot survive is the conclusion that God has already abandoned the field.


**Judging the Frightened Without Cruelty**

One guard must be kept while reading this verse. The Book says "you" — the whole community — and does not name the men whose eyes rolled or whose hearts rose into their throats. The individuals whose wavering is condemned in this sūrah are those whose speech and conduct the chapter goes on to quote: the ones who told the city to give up, the ones who invented excuses, the ones who talked of God's promise as a delusion. Panic itself is reported as a shared condition rather than charged to particular people, and nothing in the passage invites later generations to hunt for names to despise. That restraint is part of the instruction. A community under pressure needs its frightened people steadied, not identified; and the man who trembles but holds his position is not the man this chapter is describing when it turns, a few verses later, to those who wish they were somewhere else.


**Why the Crisis Is Remembered Rather Than Buried**

A community could easily prefer to forget the week it came closest to ruin, and to speak only of the deliverance. The Book does the opposite: it recounts the fear, the whispers and the wavering in the same breath as the wind that ended the siege, and it addresses all of it to believers as a favour. The reason is that hardship survived is evidence, and evidence is only useful if it is preserved. The men who stood through that week would need the memory later, when the community grew confident and its fears became smaller and easier to disguise. What the verse establishes is that the truth about a crisis can be told in full without weakening the claim of faith — and that a person's worst hour, honestly remembered, is often the strongest thing he has to hand down.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:11

> Then and there the believers were put to the test, and were violently shaken

**Expanded Commentary**

**Then and There: The Verse Fixes the Moment**

The verse opens with a word of place and time, *hunālika* — at that point, on that spot — which ties what it says to the scene just described. It is not a general statement about believers and trials; it is a verdict passed on one week in Medina, when the coalition had surrounded the city and the thoughts of its defenders had scattered in every direction. The Book's habit of anchoring moral judgement to a specific date is worth noticing. Abstract warnings are easy to acknowledge and easy to forget; a warning attached to a day that people remember cannot be argued with, because the hearers were there. The community addressed in the verse knew who had left the trench, who had stayed, and what each of them had said while the wind was still blowing the wrong way.

**What "Put to the Test" Means Here**

The test in question is not a riddle set to confuse people, nor an examination whose answers were hidden from them. The community was tested with a situation in which the true and the false could no longer be kept in the same posture. Hypocrisy is a sustainable arrangement in ordinary times: a man can attend, pay, praise and keep his real opinion to himself for years. It stops being sustainable when taking a side has a cost. This is why the Book describes hardship as a sorting rather than a punishment, and why it states the principle as a rule of history rather than an exceptional event: "Do people think once they say, 'We believe,' that they will be left without being put to the test?" (29:2). The siege is an illustration of a law that applies to every generation of believers.

**Violently Shaken: The Image of the Earthquake**

The second half of the verse uses a word of physical convulsion, *zulzilū*, from the root that gives the Arabic for an earthquake. The choice of metaphor is exact. What happened to the community was not a gentle examination but a tremor that moves the ground under people's feet, so that the assumptions they had been standing on shift and crack. The Qur'an uses the same image in describing previous communities whose faith was tested to breaking point: those afflicted with suffering and adversity were so violently shaken that even the Messenger and the believers with him cried out, asking when God's help would come (2:214). The answer given there closes with a statement the sūrah of the Confederates illustrates in narrative: God's help is always near.

**Why the Verse Names the Believers, Not the Hypocrites**

The sentence says the believers were tested and shaken. That is a deliberate restriction, and it does a great deal of work. It means the trial fell on the people who had something to lose by failing, and it means the community's worst week was experienced by its most sincere members in a way the others could not share. It also means the shaking did not disqualify anyone: the verse reports the fear of believers and still calls them believers. The Qur'an treats a shaken faith as faith that is being tested, not as faith that has already failed, and this distinction is the verse's gentleness. It allows no comfort to those who abandon the field, and it offers no easy verdict against those who stay in it trembling.

**The Test Was Also a Separation**

Another purpose is stated in the Book for trials of this severity. God does not leave a community in the condition it was in, mixed and unexamined, until He has distinguished the good from the evil among it (3:179). The siege did that work in a few weeks. Men who had worshipped in the same mosque, agreed in the same meetings and praised the same leadership were suddenly visible for what they were, because a trench on a winter night does not care about anyone's reputation. This is the other half of the verse's meaning. The shaking was not only a burden placed on the believers; it was also a sorting that cleared the community's own ranks, and the Book consistently presents that clarity as worth what it cost. A community that knows who its waverers are can protect itself; one that does not must wait for the next crisis to find out.


**A Trial Measured to What People Can Carry**

The Book does not present testing as an unlimited weight dropped on whoever happens to be standing in the way. It states a rule about the measurement itself: “Let the man of wealth provide according to his means. As for the one with limited resources, let him provide according to whatever Allah has given him. Allah does not require of any soul beyond what He has given it. After hardship, Allah will bring about ease.” (65:7). The siege of Medina holds to that pattern. The trench was dug, the nights were cold, the food ran short, and the community came through — not untouched, but intact. What made the shaking endurable was partly its limit, and the limit was set by the same knowledge that set the test. The Qur'an describes God as not burdening a soul beyond what He has given it, and in the same sūrah that says the soul will answer only for what is within its reach (2:286). The community that lost its nerve in the dark tents is judged by that measure, and so are the generations who read the story centuries later and find their own smaller crises in it.


**A Verse That Offers No Relief, and Does Not Need To**

Nowhere in this verse does God tell the believers that things will be fine. The verse reports the test and the shaking and stops. Relief does not arrive from a promise inserted into the narrative; it arrives later, in the conduct of those who held to the promise and in the unexpected end of the siege. That order is deliberate. A revelation that promised comfort before describing the ordeal would produce people whose faith depended on the comfort. What the Book gives instead is the ordeal itself, told truthfully, together with the memory of who came through it and what they were leaning on when they did.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:12

> And ˹remember˺ when the hypocrites and those with sickness in their hearts said, “Allah and His Messenger have promised us nothing but delusion!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence Said Out Loud**

The verse preserves a sentence spoken during the siege: "Allah and His Messenger have promised us nothing but delusion!" The Book does not describe these people as silent doubters. They said it, and they said it while others were listening — while the trench was being held by men whose families were inside the walls. Wartime speech is a form of action, and the Qur'an treats it as one. A rumour that the promise is empty travels through a besieged city faster than news of any wall, and it reaches the people whose courage is already strained. This is why the chapter records the words themselves and reports them as a betrayal rather than as an opinion. What made the sentence dangerous was not that it was thought, but that it was broadcast at the moment when it could do the most damage.

**The Hypocrites and the Sick in Heart**

Two groups are named, and the pairing recurs throughout the Qur'an. The hypocrites are those whose outward profession of faith does not match their inner conviction; they have something to gain from being counted inside the community and something to protect outside it. Those with sickness in their hearts are a broader category, people whose attachment to the faith is infected by doubt, appetite or resentment. The Book uses the second phrase in several chapters for a range of people, from the hesitant converts of Medina to those who preferred the world to the Hereafter (22:11), and its grammar is consistent: it describes a condition, not an identity. That matters for anyone reading this verse later. The sentence is reported as the utterance of people in a particular state, and the state is one the Book warns against entering, not one reserved for a fixed set of names.

**An Entire Chapter of the Same Diagnosis**

Within this sūrah the diagnosis recurs at three points, and the repetition is a sign of how the chapter is built. The same people appear later when the Prophet is told to speak to his wives, when the rumourmongers of Medina are threatened, and when the sūrah closes by distinguishing between hypocrisy and belief. In each case the condition is described by its effects: doubt that spreads, fear that looks for an excuse, speech that damages the community while claiming loyalty to it. The sentence in this verse is the first and the bluntest of those effects. It names God and His Messenger together as the target, and it says that what they promised was a deception — a charge that does not accuse them of weakness but of deliberate falsehood.

**Ghurūr: The Accusation Hidden in the Word**

The word rendered here as delusion is *ghurūr*, the noun for a thing that deceives by appearing trustworthy. Its use in this sentence turns the accusation back upon its speakers. To call the promise *ghurūr* is to say that the community was baited: that it was fed hope and led to disaster, and that the source of the hope knew it. Nothing in the siege narrative supports the charge, and the chapter will shortly show why. The confederates were actually dispersed; the promise was actually kept. The men who spoke those words therefore stand convicted by the events they lived through, not only by the argument of the verse. Their judgement was wrong about the facts as well as about God, and the Book lets the facts do the convicting.

**What the Promise Actually Was**

The sentence is a lie about the past, and the Book supplies the record on which it can be tested. The promise was that God would aid His messenger and His community; the Book restates it and the same sūrah will show the confederates dispersing without a victory. Elsewhere the language is put into the mouths of the believers themselves when help is delayed: the Messenger and those with him cried out, asking when God's help would come, and the answer is that God's help is always near (2:214). Two responses to one delay are recorded, and the difference between them is not the amount of fear each group felt. It is what each group decided about the character of the One who had promised. The hypocrites concluded that the promise was bait. The answer the Book gives them is a wind, an alliance dissolving overnight and a city still standing.


**The Cost of Saying It Out Loud**

The verse's last word about these men is not that they doubted but that they spoke, and the distinction has consequences for how a community handles such speech. Passing doubt is not an offence anyone can be tried for; a sentence announced in the hearing of frightened people is another matter, because it does its damage in the minds of those who hear it. The Qur'an elsewhere treats the deliberate broadcasting of discouragement among believers as a serious wrong, and it does so without ambiguity: the blame falls on those who seek exemption while they have the means to fight, and who prefer to sit with those who stay behind (9:93). The principle is not a licence to police thoughts. It is a recognition that words carry in wartime, that morale is a shared asset, and that a man who spends it in a public sentence has spent something that was not his alone.


**Why the Words Are Preserved**

There would be no loss to the story if the verse simply said that some people despaired. Instead the Qur'an quotes the sentence and reports that it was said — and quotes it, notably, in the voice of the speakers, with no softening clause attached. The reason is that this sentence is the clearest possible statement of what the trial was testing. Everything in the siege story comes down to the question of whether the promise of God and His Messenger can be trusted when the evidence is against it. The hypocrites answered no, and they answered in public. By preserving the words, the chapter gives later generations the exact shape of the failure they are warned against, spoken in the first person so that it cannot be mistaken for something remote.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:13

> And ˹remember˺ when a group of them said, “O people of Yathrib! There is no point in you staying ˹here˺, so retreat!” Another group of them asked the Prophet’s permission ˹to leave˺, saying, “Our homes are vulnerable,” while ˹in fact˺ they were not vulnerable. They only wished to flee

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yathrib: The Old Name Used as an Argument**

The shout recorded in this verse addresses the defenders by the city's pre-Islamic name, Yathrib. That choice was not neutral. Medina had become Madīnat al-Nabī, the city of the Prophet, the place where tribal loyalties had been formally subordinated to a single community and its charter. Calling the people of Yathrib summoned them to an older identity: the town of the Aws and the Khazraj, of old quarrels, of districts and family interests. The men speaking were themselves from those tribes, and the phrase works on the audience it was aimed at, the local Muslims whose homes, families and land were all inside the walls. The rhetoric of the sentence is therefore as important as its content. It was an attempt to persuade the city to think of itself as a place rather than a community, because a place can be evacuated while a covenant cannot.

**"There Is No Standing Place for You"**

The first group's argument is the argument of inevitability: there is no point in you staying here, so retreat. It is the most demoralising claim that can be made inside a besieged city, and it is made in the form of common sense rather than as a proposal. Nothing in it is offered as an opinion that might be tested. The trench still held. The alliance outside was already fraying. The claim was nonetheless spread as a settled fact, and the men spreading it knew what a settled fact of that kind does to people who have been awake for nights. In modern terms the sentence is an exercise in the manufacture of despair, and the Book records it because the technique has never gone out of use.

**The Second Group and the Excuse of the Exposed House**

The other group chose a different method. They came to the Prophet and asked permission to leave, with a reason attached: our homes are vulnerable. The complaint was plausible, since some of the houses stood on the side facing the enemy, and a man's first duty is to his household. The verse denies the premise — the homes were not exposed — and then names the motive: they only wished to flee. The distinction between a real danger and a usable excuse is not visible from the outside, which is why the verse supplies the interior judgement rather than leaving it to the reader. A besieged city cannot function if every household can decide on its own that its walls are the weakest point in the defence.

**A Pretext, Not an Exemption**

The Qur'an is careful to distinguish between people who cannot serve and people who will not. Exemption exists for those who are genuinely unable, and the Book states the terms: the weak, the sick, those without the means to equip themselves, and those whose service would harm the community are not to be blamed when they stay behind. Blame falls where the capacity exists and the excuse is manufactured: "Blame is only on those who seek exemption from you although they have the means. They preferred to stay behind with the helpless, and Allah has sealed their hearts so they do not realize ˹the consequences˺." (9:93). That is the category into which this verse places the men with the vulnerable houses. What they sought was not relief from a duty but release from a risk, and the door was opened for them by a claim that the verse says was not true.

**Two Methods, One Campaign**

Read together, the two groups describe a coordinated erosion of the defence. One faction argued in public that resistance was pointless; the other worked privately, one household at a time, with requests phrased as needs. Neither group announced that the city should surrender to the confederates, and neither had to. The effect of a hundred individual departures is the same as the effect of a single order to retreat, and it arrives without anyone having to take responsibility for it. The sūrah's exposure of the two methods is therefore more than a record of cowardice. It is an anatomy of how a community under pressure is dismantled from within — by argument that removes hope in front, and by exceptions that remove bodies behind.

**A Warning Handed Down Through a City's Own Memory**

The passage has a second audience beyond the men in the tents. Every generation that reads it is given the same crisis in miniature, and the two tactics described here are the two it will face whenever a community is under pressure. In front, someone will announce that the position is hopeless and that honour requires withdrawal. Behind, someone will ask quietly for an exemption, with a reason that sounds like a personal need. The Book preserves both because it wants the pattern recognised rather than relearned in every generation, and it attaches the recognition to a specific city and a specific winter so that the pattern has a verdict already written against it. What happened in Medina's fourth year of the Hijra is the case study; the reasoning applies wherever the same sentences are spoken.


**What the Chapter Does With the Information**

The verse states the facts and stops. No punishment is announced here, no list of names is given, and the men who made the requests were not expelled from a community they had tried to leave. The sūrah's purpose in this passage is exposure, and exposure was itself the remedy: the Prophet's knowledge of their real motive, which the verse discloses, meant that the excuse could not be repeated. Later Madinan rulings will continue to distinguish between the failure of the weak and the manoeuvre of the calculating, and the community that reads this account is being taught to make the same distinction rather than to take the loudest profession of loyalty at face value.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:14

> Had their city been sacked from all sides and they had been asked to abandon faith, they would have done so with little hesitation

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Trial That Never Came, and a Verdict That Did**

The city was never breached. The confederates never entered Medina from any side, and the question this verse considers was never put to anyone. The Book still answers it, and the answer is what makes the sentence remarkable: had the walls fallen and the defenders been told to give up their faith as the price of their lives, they would have paid it almost at once. The judgement is not speculation about an event. It is a disclosure of what was already inside the men who had been talking about retreat while their homes stood safe behind the trench. Their words during the siege were the evidence, and the test that never happened would only have made the evidence public. This is one of the ways the Qur'an exercises its authority over the inner life that no human court can reach.

**What "Abandoning Faith" Names in the Arabic**

The phrase behind the translation is *al-fitna*, and the word carries more than the English suggests. Its root has to do with turning and with the process of separating something valuable from what surrounds it, which is why the Qur'an uses it for trial, for persecution, for civil strife, and for the ruin that follows a community turning on itself. In this sentence it means the surrender of faith itself, the state the speakers would accept in exchange for their lives. Translating it as abandoning faith is accurate to the meaning here, and the wider sense of the word is instructive: what the verse says they would have accepted was not merely a change of opinion but a turning back to the state they had come from, with all the division that turned them against.

**The Grammar of a Sentence About Intentions**

The verse uses a conditional form: had the city been overrun, and had they been asked to give up their faith, they would have done so. Arabic builds such sentences to describe what did not occur, which means the Book is speaking about a possibility rather than an event. What the construction accomplishes is a statement about character. A person's reaction to a situation that never arose is not observable, and no witness could testify to it. Revelation states it anyway, because the disposition had already produced conduct that anyone could see. Cowardice in a small matter and apostasy in a great one come from the same place, and the verse connects them so that no one can claim that the failure was merely a matter of circumstance.

**Little Hesitation: The Speed of the Trade**

The clause carries a note about how quickly the deal would have been made. There would have been little hesitation, no scraping between the two options, no long argument about whether survival was worth the price. The verse does not describe men who would have agonised and then fallen; it describes men who were already leaning. The speed is the measure of what they actually valued. Everything they had professed about God, the messenger and the Hereafter is placed on one side of a scale, and their lives on the other, and the sentence tells the reader which side goes up without a struggle. It is a deliberately uncomfortable detail, and it explains why the chapter spends this passage on the inside of its audience rather than on the enemy outside.

**The Book's Right to Report What Was Hidden**

No human being could have known the answer to the conditional. This is the point of including it. The Qur'an repeatedly reports intentions, secret counsels and motives that no observer could have seen, and it does so to establish the reach of its knowledge: the same God who sent the wind against the confederates was reading the hearts inside the city. For the community, that knowledge was protection as well as rebuke. A leader who knows which of his men will stand can plan a defence; a congregation that knows that God hears its excuses cannot keep a public position and a private intention in separate compartments. The verse removes that option for everyone who heard it, then and later.

**Why the Book Tells What No One Could Observe**

The disclosure in this verse is not there to satisfy curiosity about the past. Its function is to establish that the community's knowledge of its own members does not depend on spies or confessions. The Qur'an reports intentions that no witness could testify to, and it does so repeatedly, which is a claim about the reach of revelation: the message knows the inside of a person as well as the outside. That knowledge changes the way the community handles itself. A man cannot sustain a public posture and a private reservation, because the one thing he counted on — that no one can see the difference — is exactly what the verse removes. It is also a mercy of a hard kind: the warning arrives before the test does, so that a person reading it can correct what no courtroom could ever examine.


**What a Person Trades for Safety**

The sentence is a compact statement about exchange: faith placed against survival, and both weighed. The Book insists elsewhere on the value of the thing being handed over, and it states the valuation in a line that leaves nothing to be argued: "Whoever is spared from the Fire and is admitted into Paradise will ˹indeed˺ triumph, whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment." (3:185). Read against that line, the men of this verse were not choosing life; they were choosing a shorter and lesser thing. The remainder of the sūrah will show the other possibility, the men in the same trench who held their positions through the same night and asked for nothing in return.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:15

> They had already pledged to Allah earlier never to turn their backs ˹in retreat˺. And a pledge to Allah must be answered for

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Earlier Pledge**

The verse appeals to a promise made before the siege, and the commentators identified it in more than one way. The most natural reading places it at al-ʿAqabah, where the men of Medina pledged themselves to the Prophet before the migration: an undertaking to protect him as they protected their own families, which is exactly the promise broken by leaving a battlefield where he stood. Others took the reference to be the general covenant of faith each believer makes, or the words spoken when the Anṣār accepted the community's charter in Medina. The first reading is the stronger, because the verse says "earlier" and because the obligation not to turn one's back was connected in those pledges to the defence of the city and of the messenger himself. The men being addressed had given a specific word on a specific occasion, and the chapter will not let it be forgotten.

**What "Never to Turn Their Backs" Requires**

The pledge is described in the language of the battlefield. Turning one's back means flight from a position while the enemy is still in front, and the Book treats that act with particular severity because of its consequences: it exposes the men beside you. The wording of the pledge reported here is not that they would win or that they would be brave, but that they would not turn. That is a promise about position rather than outcome, and it matches this sūrah's understanding of what faith demands under pressure. The believer is not asked to guarantee the result; he is asked to remain where the obligation placed him. It is worth adding that the obligation has limits and that the Book legislates for genuine inability elsewhere, so the pledge is not a claim that no circumstances can release a man from a front.

**Oaths as Speech That Cannot Be Withdrawn**

The Qur'an treats a promise made by a human being as a real transfer of obligation, and it legislates for it in detail. The Book commands the believers to honour the covenant of God when they make a pledge and not to break oaths after confirming them, having made God their guarantor (16:91). It places the same duty in the list of fundamental obligations that a person will be questioned about: "And honour ˹your˺ pledges, for you will surely be accountable for them." (17:34). There is also a penalty structure for careless oaths and a heavier one for deliberate oaths, which shows that the seriousness is legal as well as moral (5:89). A community in which a man's word carries that weight cannot treat the abandonment of a sworn position as an ordinary prudential decision.

**Why the Pledge Is Raised Now**

The order of the argument in these verses is deliberate. First the words spoken during the siege, then the conditional verdict on the intentions behind them, and only now the pledge that turns the whole episode into a breach of a promise rather than a failure of nerve. A moment of weakness is one thing; a broken word is another, and the chapter is careful to establish which of the two it is describing. What the men of verse 13 were doing, on this account, was not simply retreating in fear. They were negotiating their way out of an obligation they had accepted publicly and for which they had called God to witness. That is why the verse can say their earlier pledge had been to Allah, and not merely to the leader who received it.

**A Promise With No Private Exit**

The verse leaves no route out of the obligation it describes, and that is a deliberate feature of how the Book handles pledges. There is legislation for the person who cannot serve, for the weak, for the sick, and for those without the means to equip themselves, and the Book states plainly that there is no blame on such people so long as they remain true to God and His Messenger (9:91). What it does not provide is a clause that lets the person concerned declare himself exempt. Release comes from the stated causes, and even then the condition attached is loyalty rather than withdrawal. The men of the siege had not gone to the Prophet for a ruling on inability; one group argued that resistance was pointless and the other asked permission on a false ground. The sentence about the pledge is aimed exactly at that gap between a genuine excuse and a self-issued one.


**Every Pledge Has an Owner**

The verse closes by saying that a pledge to God must be answered for, and the phrase lifts the whole question out of the local politics of a siege. A promise made to a man can be renegotiated, excused, forgotten, or settled privately; a promise made with God as witness stands in its own account. Later in this sūrah the Book will remind the Prophet, in the middle of a chapter about his own household, that God holds the covenant of the prophets (33:7), and the same vocabulary runs through the passage: covenants are the connective tissue of revelation and of community life. No one who has given his word to God can quietly walk out of a position, because the party to the contract is not the officer standing next to him. This is the sūrah's answer to the vulnerable houses and the useless position, and it is the reason the men who stayed in the trench needed no argument beyond the one they had already given. A sworn word had fixed their place for them, and the noise of an army could not unfix it.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:16

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Fleeing will not benefit you if you ˹try to˺ escape a natural or violent death. ˹If it is not your time,˺ you will only be allowed enjoyment for a little while.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prophet Is Told to Answer, Not to Punish**

The verse opens with a command to speak: say to them. That instruction is itself a decision about how the community handles men whose conduct has just been exposed. They are not expelled, disarmed, or publicly named in a penalty; they are answered, in an argument addressed to their own reasoning. This is consistent with the passage's method, which has so far reported their words, judged their intention, and recalled their pledge, and now hands the Prophet a sentence with which to confront them. The Book treats many of its hardest warnings as propositions to be considered rather than as sentences to be executed. The men being addressed remain inside the community that is correcting them, and the correction is designed to reach them as listeners rather than as defendants.

**Two Ways of Dying, and Neither Can Be Outrun**

The sentence names the two deaths a person might hope to escape: ordinary death, and death by violence. In the Arabic the pair is *al-mawt* and *al-qatl*, dying and being killed, and the choice is exact. Men who flee a battlefield are not trying to become immortal; they are trying to die later, in a bed, at home, among their family. The verse answers that hope at its root by placing both endings under the same appointment: "No soul can ever die without Allah's Will at the destined time." (3:145). The argument is not that courage is better than caution, though the Book believes it is. The argument is that the outcome the coward is purchasing with his honour is not for sale. The time of a death is written, and no amount of distance travelled alters the entry.

**Fortified Towers: The Same Reasoning Elsewhere**

The Book states the same case to a different audience and makes the physical image explicit: "Wherever you may be, death will overcome you—even if you were in fortified towers." (4:78). A tower is the most that human planning can build against mortality, and it is not enough. The men of the siege were proposing a much weaker measure: a departure to a safer district, with the enemy still outside and the obligation still unpaid. The verse does not insult their intelligence or question their courage; it dismantles their calculation. In a sūrah that will describe a trench, a wind and an alliance falling apart, the point is worth stating in military terms as well as moral ones: the defenders' safety lay in holding a position against a coalition that could not sustain a siege, not in redistributing the same small force across a larger area.

**"A Little While": The Ceiling on Enjoyment**

The second half of the verse grants the escape a term. Even on the assumption that flight worked — that a man reached some quiet distance and lived — what he would receive is enjoyment for a little while. The Arabic word for that enjoyment is the ordinary one for provision and use, the same word used for the goods of this life generally, and the adjective attached to it sets the ceiling. The Book states the valuation elsewhere without hesitation: "Whoever is spared from the Fire and is admitted into Paradise will ˹indeed˺ triumph, whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment." (3:185). The verse therefore completes its own argument. Flight does not avert death, and what it wins by delaying it is not worth the price it charges.

**What the Argument Reveals About the Sūrah's Audience**

These men are addressed as people who believe in God's decree in principle but are not living as if it were true. That is a more useful diagnosis than simple disbelief. Someone who denied the appointed term entirely would not be argued with in this register; he would be told about the resurrection first. The men being answered here have the vocabulary of faith and are using it to justify an evacuation. The verse works inside their own assumptions and follows them to a conclusion they had not wanted to reach, which is why it is phrased as a question about benefit rather than as a threat. The same technique is used by the Book where a community's theory and practice have come apart; the correction is not a new revelation but the old one taken seriously.

**What the Men Who Stayed Understood**

The verse's argument has an unspoken counterpart, and the chapter supplies it a few verses further on. There were believers in the same city, under the same hunger and the same wind, who did not count their chances in the way the waverers did, and the difference was not that they had more courage as a personal quality. They had settled the prior question: whose permission mattered. Once a person has decided that his life is on loan with a fixed return date, the arithmetic of a siege changes. He can still be frightened — the narrative elsewhere says plainly that hearts rose into throats — but fear no longer has the authority to move him, because the calculation it might have corrupted has already been made and handed over. Everything the verse denies to the men who wanted to leave, the men who stayed possessed: a reason to stand that no argument from numbers could touch, and a certainty that the outcome was already managed by the One who had appointed their term.


**The Doctrine That Makes a Small Force Stand**

There is a practical consequence to this doctrine, and the sūrah's history shows it. A defending force of roughly three thousand held its ground against a coalition of about ten thousand, did not break, and did not need to fight a decisive battle. A community that believes death arrives on schedule can hold a line; a community that believes it can be negotiated with will look for somewhere safer the first night it is frightened. The verse's instruction is addressed to individuals, but its effect is collective. The men who stood in the trench were not men who did not mind dying; they were men who had accepted that the timing of their deaths was not in the hands of Abū Sufyān, and the refusal to run followed from that acceptance.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:17

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who can put you out of Allah’s reach if He intends to harm you or show you mercy?” They can never find any protector or helper besides Allah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Question, Aimed at the First Answer**

Where the previous verse attacked the plan to escape, this one attacks the assumption behind it: that any power could stand between a person and God. The Prophet is told to ask the question directly, and the phrasing puts the waverers on the spot without accusing them of anything they had not already said. It is the second move in a short sequence of arguments, and it is built to be unanswerable. No one in Medina could name a protector who could shield a man from the decree of God. The men who wanted to leave had counted the confederate cavalry and their own weak houses; the verse widens the count to include the one variable they had left out.

**The Image of Shielding**

The Arabic verb behind the question belongs to a root meaning to protect, guard or hold fast, and its physical sense is that of a hand closing over something so that nothing can reach it. The question asks who among the creation is able to shelter a human being from God. It is the same image that appears in the fortresses of 4:78, and the same one the earlier verse used when it spoke of fleeing. The Book's argument here is spatial and simple, and this is why it cannot be argued away with words: there is no position outside the reach of the One whose decree covers everything. The frightened man is not asking to be safe; he is asking to stand somewhere God is not, and no such place exists.

**Harm and Mercy From One Hand**

The question is symmetrical, and the symmetry is the verse's sharpest point. It asks who could prevent God from intending harm, or from intending mercy. Both possibilities lie in the same will. There is no second agency that could veto a decree or supply what God has withheld, and this is the doctrine that dismantles every alliance built on the hope of protection from a source other than God. The Book elsewhere describes God as the one who commands harm and benefit, and the Qur'an's consistent position is that creatures serve as means but never as causes with independent power. An ally who protects is an instrument of mercy; the same ally, relied upon instead of God, is a false shelter. The question does not deny that armies exist. It denies that they own the outcome.

**Walī and Naṣīr: Two Names for Two Needs**

The verse closes by stating that these people will never find a protector or a helper besides Allah, and the two titles are not synonyms. A *walī* is one who takes charge of another, stands close to him and manages his affair; a *naṣīr* is one who gives victory and support in a contest. The pair therefore covers both the quiet need of a person who has no one to look after his interests and the loud need of one who is being attacked. By denying the availability of both outside God, the verse removes every remaining exit from the argument: there is no patron to arrange safety, and no champion to fight for it. The same pair closes the sūrah's description of the Fire, where its inhabitants are told they will find no protector or helper (33:65), so the formula is used by the Book for absolute abandonment.

**Why the Question Is Put to Believers**

The question is addressed to men who would have answered it correctly if it had been asked in a lesson. They knew that God alone protects; the failure described in these verses is not a failure of information but of application. Pressure had made a familiar truth abstract again. That is the condition this passage keeps diagnosing — an intact vocabulary of faith attached to conduct that assumes the opposite. The verse's remedy is to make the doctrine concrete: not "God is able", but who else could shield you, right now, from the army outside the wall. The Book's habit of asking such questions rather than asserting them leaves the reader with the work of answering, which is precisely why the answers stay with him.

**The Question That Also Covers the Camp Outside**

The men in the trenches of the confederate camp were asking a version of the same question with their own plans. Their coalition had been assembled on the assumption that numbers decide outcomes, and they had assembled the largest number Arabia could produce. The verse's question is addressed to the defenders, but it disposes of the besiegers just as completely: no army, however gathered, sits outside the reach of God. The wind that tore through the tents at the end of the siege was the practical form of the answer. What the Qur'an denies is not the reality of force but the belief that force is an independent power. On that understanding, a commander who trusts his numbers is making the same mistake as a man who trusts his distance from the battlefield, and the sūrah reports both failures in a single passage.


**What This Does Not Say About the Enemy**

One conclusion must not be drawn from the verse. It does not say that the enemy has no power, or that the confederates were not dangerous; the trench, the cold nights and the killed defenders are proof enough that the danger was real. What it says is that no power, including the enemy's, is outside God's will, and that no one can therefore be protected by a shield held in the enemy's direction. This distinction governs the entire siege narrative. The community is asked to be militarily serious and religiously certain at the same time, and the sūrah's later verses about preparation, deception and diplomacy show what that combination looked like in practice.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:18

> Allah knows well those among you who discourage ˹others from fighting˺, saying ˹secretly˺ to their brothers, “Stay with us,” and who themselves hardly take part in fighting

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Work of Holding Others Back**

The verse names a category of person by their activity rather than by their state of belief: those who discourage others from fighting. The offence is not refusal in their own case, serious as that is. It is interference in someone else's decision, the deliberate reduction of the number of men willing to stand. A community under siege can absorb a certain amount of private hesitation; it cannot absorb a campaign to spread the hesitation, because the campaign multiplies what it touches. The Book describes the act plainly and lets its consequences speak. Every person persuaded is a person who will not be in the line, and the man doing the persuading has arranged this without ever raising a weapon against his own side.

**"Stay With Us": Recruiting Company for Failure**

The quoted sentence is one of the most recognisable lines in the whole passage: they say secretly to their brothers, stay with us. Its purpose is not to explain a theological position or to argue the merits of the defence. It is to secure companionship in withdrawal. A man who leaves a position alone carries the shame of it by himself; a man who leaves in company can call it a decision rather than a desertion. This is why the verse reports the persuasion as a distinct offence and places it beside the non-participation that follows. The tactic has an old logic that has never changed: failure becomes far easier to bear when it has been made normal, and making it normal requires a few energetic people who are willing to do the recruiting.

**Said Secretly, Denied Publicly**

The adverb matters. These conversations happened quietly, between people who trusted each other, and they were never offered for public examination. A proposal to abandon the defence, argued openly in the mosque in front of the community and the Prophet, would have been answered and defeated. Argued in private between brothers, it leaves no record and no one to answer it. This is the standard shape of subversion in a besieged place: the argument moves from the assembly to the household, where rank and kinship give it weight that it could never carry on its own. The Book records this detail because the danger it describes is not a crowd or a speech. It is a series of small private conversations that will subtract men from the wall while everyone continues to believe the community is united.

**"And Who Hardly Take Part"**

The second clause names the other half of the conduct: they themselves hardly take part in the fighting. Not that they refuse outright, and not that they fight badly. They take part a little, enough to be counted present, not enough to change anything. The Qur'an is precise about degrees of participation, and it treats this middle position as worse than an open refusal in one respect: an open refusal can be planned for, while a nominal presence disguised as loyalty cannot. The character it describes is familiar in every generation of every community's hard moments — the man who appears at the post, agrees with the plan, leaves before the dangerous part, and is thanked afterwards for having come at all.

**Knowledge Announced Before Any Verdict**

The verse begins with a statement of knowledge: Allah knows well those among you. No punishment is announced, no name is given, and no procedure is described. The disclosure itself is the response, and it follows the pattern the sūrah has used since verse 2, where the believers were told that God is aware of what they do. The effect is to remove the one advantage the tactic depended on: the secrecy of the private conversation. A man who believed that what he said to his brother in a tent would never be known now learns that the account is complete. The Book's warnings are frequently like this — an announcement of knowledge, and time to act on it before anything else happens.

**Why a Community Is Warned About Speech Rather Than Only Deeds**

The chapter's emphasis here says something about how communal disaster actually arrives. Armies are visible and can be answered; the erosion that the verse describes happens in conversation, and it is not forbidden by any law of war that existed or exists. A man may sit in a tent, tell his brother that the position is hopeless, and commit no punishable act. The Book nonetheless records the conduct as one of the failures the siege exposed, because the community's strength at such a moment is composed of exactly the decisions these conversations were designed to change. What is being protected here is not military information but the shared resolve to stand, which no wall and no trench can supply, and which can be talked to death by people who have never held a weapon.


**The Language of Brotherhood Turned Inward**

The people being described spoke to their brothers, and the word is the one the community used for itself. That is the sentence's real offence. Brotherhood in this sūrah has just been defined as a bond that survives the rearrangement of names and inheritances, a relationship created by faith and honoured through loyalty. The men of this verse used the same word to mean something entirely different: a bond to be exploited so that one man's failure would feel normal to another. Vocabulary does not survive that kind of service intact in any community. When the strongest word a society has for mutual obligation is spent on persuading people to abandon one another, the chapter is right to record it as an event worth remembering.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:19

> ˹They are˺ totally unwilling to assist you. When danger comes, you see them staring at you with their eyes rolling like someone in the throes of death. But once the danger is over, they slash you with razor-sharp tongues, ravenous for ˹worldly˺ gains. Such people have not ˹truly˺ believed, so Allah has rendered their deeds void. And that is easy for Allah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Refusal That Runs From Start to Finish**

The verse opens with the phrase rendered here as totally unwilling to assist you, and the Arabic carries a sense of grudging closeness to go with the refusal. These men were not distant from the community; they were inside it, and their help had to be extracted like a debt rather than offered like a gift. The Book is describing a disposition, not a single decision: a person who has to be coaxed into every contribution, whose presence among the believers is a permanent cost to everyone around him. It is the opposite of the relationship described in the sūrah's account of the Prophet, who is closer to the believers than they are to themselves. There, the claim runs toward self-sacrifice; here, everything is measured in what can be withheld.

**Fear That Has No Room For Anyone Else**

Once the fighting begins, the verse describes what the believers see, and the description is entirely physical. These men stare at you, eyes turning in their heads like someone in the throes of death. It is the same terror the chapter reported earlier in the community at large, but with a difference that matters: this terror has nowhere to go except inward. A frightened believer who holds his post is still part of the line, and his fear is distributed through the collective because others are standing with him. The man described here is consumed by his own survival, and that consumption is visible on his face to the men beside him. He has become a spectator of the battle in the middle of it.

**Razor Tongues Once the Danger Passes**

The verse then executes a turn that exposes the whole character. When the danger is over, the same men slash at the community with sharp tongues, eager for whatever profit is going. The fear that had silenced them and paralysed their faces a day earlier disappears the moment there is something to be gained. The Book's word for their greed ties the two halves together: they were not brave in the fight and they are not generous in the peace, and the common factor is that the community is a resource rather than an obligation. Anyone who has watched a crisis end will recognise the type — the person who is missing while the work is dangerous and present while the rewards are being distributed, with information, opinions and complaints in abundance.

**What "Not Truly Believed" Means in This Sentence**

The verse's verdict is blunt: such people have not truly believed, and so God has rendered their deeds void. The sentence distinguishes between believing as a claim and believing as a state, which the book does throughout its treatment of hypocrisy, and it attaches a consequence to the difference. The deeds are not judged worthless because they were small; they are rendered void because they were never done for the sake of the One who sent the messenger they claimed to follow. The closing remark, that this is easy for Allah, keeps the matter in proportion. No investigation is required to sort such conduct out, no tribunal is needed to weigh mixed intentions. The ruling follows from what the deeds were.

**The Test of a Person's Leadership, and of His Community**

The verses provide a usable measure, and it cuts both ways. A community's health can be read from the conduct of its members during the difficult half of a shared task: who arrives early, who stays late, who does the part that will not be noticed, and who speaks best when the danger has passed. The same measure applies upward. A leader who permits the sharp tongues and rewards the late arrivals has taught his people what he actually values, whatever he has said in public. In this passage the point is made about a specific group at a specific time, but it is stated with the precision of a rule, and the sūrah's earlier insistence that the Prophet's claim on the believers exceeded their own self-interest supplies the standard against which this conduct is being weighed.


**What the Community Owes the Men Beside It**

One more implication follows from the description. A line of defence is held by people who are visible to one another, and the conduct of any one man is a fact about the safety of the rest. The Book's concern with these particular men is not an interest in their private character; it is the effect their presence had on the people who were counting on them. That is why the punishment described is the voiding of their deeds rather than any earthly penalty. The community is not instructed to hunt them down but to understand what such conduct costs, and the understanding is placed in the middle of a war narrative where the cost can be measured in the men who had to stand a longer front because others had gone home.


**The Chapter's Account of a Type, Not Just of Men**

Nothing in these verses provides a legal procedure against the people described, and nothing invites the community to hunt for them. The sūrah is doing something else: it is describing a character with enough precision that later readers can recognise it in themselves. The features are assembled across several verses — secret discouragement, half participation, private withdrawal, profit after danger — and the description is complete without a single name. This is how the Qur'an writes moral instruction that outlives its occasion. The siege of Medina ended and its participants died; the conduct described here has kept turning up in every community that has ever been asked to bear a burden together, and the sūrah's diagnosis is as usable now as it was when the wind was still blowing. The men in the tents were not the last to prefer safety to a share in a burden, and the sentence that voided their deeds was not written for their century alone. What the passage denies is a way of belonging that pays nothing, and the denial is as firm here as anywhere in the Book.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:20

> They ˹still˺ think that the enemy alliance has not ˹yet˺ withdrawn. And if the allies were to come ˹again˺, the hypocrites would wish to be away in the desert among nomadic Arabs, ˹only˺ asking for news about you ˹believers˺. And if the hypocrites were in your midst, they would hardly take part in the fight

**Expanded Commentary**

**Fear That Outlasts the Danger**

The allies had gone. The siege was over, the tents were down, the road home was open, and the verse reports that these men still think the alliance will come again. A mind formed by months of terror does not reset when the threat lifts; it keeps the threat alive because keeping it alive is what it knows how to do. The Book states the fact without comment, and the fact is a diagnosis: this is not a sober assessment of a strategic situation that has changed but a settled way of looking at the world in which the danger is permanent and one's own obligations are always about to be revised downward. Fear of its character is a condition of the heart rather than an argument about the facts, which is exactly why it keeps operating when a check of those facts shows that the danger has passed.

**If the Allies Came Again**

The verse then follows the thought to its conclusion by imagining the alliance's return. On that assumption, the hypocrites would wish to be out among the Bedouin, far from the city, waiting for news about the believers. Every element of that wish is worth noting, because together they describe a complete withdrawal from the community's fate. The enemy must be avoided; the distance must be provided by others; the information must flow in; and the fighting must be left to those who never leave the wall. The conditional form does not describe what happened. It exposes what was already there and would have become visible had there been a second siege, and the chapter's placement of the sentence shows how little difference there was between their present conduct and their imagined future behaviour.

**The Desert and the Nomads: Where Safety Was Imagined**

The place the wish names is Arabia's interior, among the nomadic tribes who lived beyond the settled towns and took no part in the community's defence. For a man who wanted to survive without belonging to anyone's fate, that was the logical destination: no walls to hold, no oath to a town, no neighbours to answer to. The Arabic term for these tribes is the same one the Book uses for the people whose unfriendly neutrality during the siege was a recurring worry for the Prophet. It is notable that the wish is not to join them as one of them but to sit among them as a spectator, which keeps a foot inside the community for reporting purposes while locating the body safely outside the danger.

**"Asking for News About You"**

The phrase completes the picture, and it is the most damning part of it. They would be asked for news — anxious, interested, curious, watching how the believers fare from a distance. It is the position of the reader of a war report rather than that of a participant in it. Modern life has made this posture easy and familiar: a person can follow a crisis in great detail, hold strong opinions about who is right, grieve the losses of his own side and never once be touched by a cost. The Qur'an's judgement here is not that interest is a substitute for help but that interest, when the body is somewhere else by choice, is its own kind of betrayal.

**Present in the Midst, Absent in the Ranks**

The verse closes with the sharpest formulation in the passage. Even if the hypocrites were in your midst, they would hardly take part in the fight. Physical presence will not save them from the charge. The community may be entirely composed of people who show up; some of the people who show up are not in it. This is what the sūrah has been building toward since it began: a definition of belonging that runs deeper than attendance, geography and public profession. The men in the mosque, in the market and in the street could be as absent as the man among the nomads, and the difference between them would only be visible on the day the burden was distributed.

**The Spectator's Position, and Why the Book Refuses It**

The Qur'an returns to this posture more than once, and its verdict is consistent. Believers are told to strive with their wealth and their lives, and the Book does not recognise as a substitute the careful observation of other people's striving. What the wish in this verse represents is the private arrangement of a person's comfort: let the danger be someone else's address, let the news arrive, let the opinion be formed. The community that tolerated this arrangement would carry two populations — one that holds the wall and one that comments on it — and the second would grow, because the arrangement is always easier than the alternative. This is why the sūrah spends so much of its length on a single week of one siege: it wants the difference between these two postures stated so clearly that it cannot be confused with a difference of temperament.


**Loyalty as a Share, Not a Location**

Put together, the verse teaches that membership in this community is measured by the share a person takes in its burden. The Prophet's claim was that he was closer to the believers than their own selves; the household rulings made mutual obligations real; the covenant bound men to a post. Against all of that, the chapter now sets a character whose entire plan is to have the community without its cost. The sūrah does not treat this as a small difference of temperament. It describes the position clearly, gives it a name, and leaves it in the record without any softening clause, so that every reader afterwards understands what is being offered to him when he is invited to stand in a line.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:21

> Indeed, in the Messenger of Allah you have an excellent example for whoever has hope in Allah and the Last Day, and remembers Allah often

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse the Chapter Turns On**

After a passage that has described fear, excuses, private persuasion and profit-seeking, the sūrah changes its register. It lifts its eyes from the failures and points to a man who was in the same city, under the same siege, and behaved differently. The placement is the argument. The reader has been shown what the pressure produced in some people; now he is shown what it produced in the Prophet, and the contrast is presented not as a rebuke of the community but as an open invitation. The verse does not say that the believers failed and the Prophet did not. It says that in the Messenger of God there is something to copy, and it leaves the reader to decide how much of his own conduct falls short of it.

**Uswa: A Pattern That Is Followed, Not Admired**

The word translated as example is *uswa*, and it means a model to be imitated rather than a figure to be applauded. The Qur'an uses it for one other subject: Abraham and those with him, whose break with their people and trust in God are offered to the believers as a pattern (60:4). The choice of the same term for the Prophet places him in that line of model figures, and it settles a question the community might otherwise have asked. The Prophet is not presented as a man whose conduct belonged to his own time and temperament. He is the pattern the Book expects to be reproduced, and the requirement is stated without qualification in the preceding sentence about God's awareness of what people do.

**For Whom the Example Exists**

The verse adds two conditions, and both are about the interior of the person looking at the model: whoever has hope in Allah and the Last Day, and remembers Allah often. These are not restrictions on who may study the Prophet's life; they describe the disposition that makes imitation possible. A man who has no expectation of a reward beyond this world has no reason to copy conduct that cost the Prophet comfort, safety and rest. A man who does not keep God in his mind has no mechanism for recalling the pattern at the moment when it matters. The verse therefore ties the example to hope and remembrance, the two forces that move a person from admiration to action, and it implies that a reader without them will find the model impressive and useless.

**Hope as the Engine of Imitation**

*Rajāʾ*, the hope named here, is not optimism about outcomes. It is the expectation of a reward from God, held with enough confidence to shape what a person does when no one is watching and nothing can be gained. The Prophet's conduct during the siege was not a performance for the community; the reports of the period describe him working, praying, encouraging and taking decisions under pressure, in the same danger as everyone else. What sustained that conduct was precisely what the verse attaches the example to: the expectation of an outcome that lay beyond the walls. A community that shares that expectation will find the model reproducible. One that has misplaced it will produce the kind of conduct described in the preceding verses, whatever it says about the Prophet in the abstract.

**Remembrance as the Practice That Keeps the Pattern Present**

The second condition is *dhikr* — remembering God often, not occasionally. The Book treats remembrance as an activity with a location: it happens in the market, on the road, at night, in the middle of a task. Its function in this verse is to keep the example available. A model of conduct can be known and still be absent from a person's decisions if nothing brings it to mind at the moment of choice, and a besieged city offers a great many such moments. The command to remember God frequently returns later in the sūrah as an instruction to the believers, which is where this verse is heading: an example, hope to make it desirable, and remembrance to keep it in view. Together they describe a discipline rather than a sentiment.

**What the Example Looks Like in Ordinary Matters**

The Prophet's example is not confined to the trench. The Book describes his character in a single line that has no military context at all: "And you are truly ˹a man˺ of outstanding character." (68:4), and it describes him as concerned by the suffering of his community, anxious for their well-being and merciful to the believers (9:128). These descriptions cover the ordinary hours of a life: how a man speaks to his household, how he handles the complaints of his companions, how he treats the people who oppose him. The verse's location in a chapter full of household rulings is not accidental. The pattern to be copied is meant to govern the same territory the rest of the sūrah legislates for, and the siege was only its most visible test.

**The Mercy and the Model Together**

The Book pairs the example with the purpose of the mission: "We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a mercy for the whole world." (21:107). The two statements answer each other. The reason a human being can be asked to imitate him is that everything he did was intended for the benefit of those who saw it, and the reason the imitation is possible at all is that his conduct was shown to real people in real circumstances rather than described in general terms. When this sūrah tells its readers that they have a fine example in the Messenger of God, it is not offering them a picture. It is offering them a week of one man's life under pressure, recorded in the same chapter as the failures of others, and inviting them to reproduce it.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:22

> When the believers saw the enemy alliance, they said, “This is what Allah and His Messenger had promised us. The promise of Allah and His Messenger has come true.” And this only increased them in faith and submission

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Sight, a Different Answer**

Two groups looked at the same army, and the Book records two different things about the experience. The men described in verses 12 to 20 saw the confederates and concluded that the promise had been a deception. The believers in this verse saw the confederates and said that this was exactly what God and His Messenger had promised, and that the promise had come true. Nothing in the enemy's numbers had changed between the two responses, and nothing in the walls of the city had changed either. What separated the two groups was the content they assigned to the word promise. One had been expecting an easier outcome, and the sight of the army falsified it; the other had been told that the road would pass through exactly this, and recognised the moment when it arrived.

**What Had Been Promised**

The promise the believers were recalling was not that the community would never be besieged. The Book states the opposite in several places: a community that claims faith will be tested, as those before it were tested (29:2), and the testing of earlier believers went as far as suffering, adversity, and being violently shaken until even the messengers called out asking when God's help would come (2:214). The Prophet had foretold pressure and the eventual aid of God, and the believers understood the siege as the arrival of the first part of that forecast. This is why their sentence is a declaration that the promise has proven true rather than a wish that it had been different. They were not looking past the danger; they were reading it as evidence.

**Said in the Middle of the Danger**

The timing in the verse is precise: they said it when they saw the alliance, with the alliance still outside the walls. A sentence of this kind costs nothing after a victory. Said while the fires of the enemy camp are visible from the roofs, with supplies uncertain and no news of relief, it is a statement of a different order. The chapter's earlier descriptions of terror do not apply to these people as an accusation, because fear and certainty were evidently present together. What the verse reports is a community that could see its own danger accurately and still read God's promise accurately in the same glance, which is the precise opposite of the denial that had spread through the other group a few days earlier.

**Increased in Faith and Submission**

The verse names two effects of the sighting: faith increased and submission increased. The Arabic pairs belief with *taslīm*, a word for the unreserved handing over of a matter, and the pairing is exact for the situation. Their certainty about God grew, and along with it their readiness to accept whatever the outcome would be. These are the two things an army at the gate is designed to destroy, and what the verse describes is the opposite result. This is why the Book repeatedly describes trials as productive for the sincere rather than merely destructive: the same event that strips away a superficial loyalty also concentrates the real one, and the community that went through that week came out of it with a certainty that could not have been argued into it in peacetime.

**Compound Fear and Compound Faith**

The contrast between the groups is worth stating in the language of arithmetic. One group's fear added itself to their doubt until the total became despair, and their thoughts about God multiplied in every direction. The other group's fear submitted to a settled conviction, and the net result was a stronger faith than they had brought into the siege. Neither response was a matter of temperament, and neither was produced by the size of the enemy. The difference lay in what each group had already decided about the reliability of the One who had spoken. That decision precedes the crisis and is tested by it, and the sūrah's placement of these verses makes the comparison unavoidable for anyone reading the chapter afterwards.

**The Sentence That Later Generations Ate From**

This short declaration became, in the community's memory, a model for reading hardship correctly. The believers did not deny the danger, minimise the enemy or claim that the promise had guaranteed them a comfortable outcome. They accepted the difficulty as part of what had been described to them in advance, which left them able to say that the word of God had been vindicated at the very moment it looked most doubtful. Later generations would face sieges, defeats and dispossession, and this verse provided them with the pattern: read the situation honestly, place it inside the promise, and refuse the conclusion that God has failed. What the Book preserves in verse 22 is therefore not only an event but a habit of thinking that the community could reuse.


**Where the Difference Is Located**

The Book locates the difference in a sentence rather than a feeling. The believers said, in effect: this is what we were told, and it is a confirmation rather than a betrayal of the promise. That is a judgement about the character of God's word, made in public, at the point of maximum pressure and with no information about how the siege would end. Such a judgement is not available to someone who is waiting to see whether events turn out comfortably, because the testing is precisely what a promise of aid in the Book includes. What the verse presents as exemplary is not their confidence that things would go well, but their confidence that God's word means what it says, whatever the sequence of events turned out to be.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:23

> Among the believers are men who have proven true to what they pledged to Allah. Some of them have fulfilled their pledge ˹with their lives˺, others are waiting ˹their turn˺. They have never changed ˹their commitment˺ in the least

**Expanded Commentary**

**Men Who Proved True to What They Pledged**

The verse answers the account of the broken pledges earlier in the chapter. There, men were recalled to a promise they had given and were found negotiating their way out of it. Here the Book names a different category: believers who were true to what they pledged to God. The verb for proving true is the same root as the word for truthfulness used of the prophets in verse 8, and the connection is deliberate. What the covenant of the prophets required of them — honesty in delivery — is what these believers delivered in their own smaller covenant, and the Book shows the two kinds of fidelity standing in the same line. The community under siege was not a single body with a single verdict; the chapter has been sorting it since the first verse, and here it names those who came through.

**Those Who Fulfilled Their Pledge With Their Lives**

The first group described are those who completed what they promised by dying. The siege produced them. The reports record that Saʿd ibn Muʿādh, the chief of the Aws and one of the community's most respected men, was struck by an arrow at the trench and died of the wound later in the same year. Others fell in the fighting at the trench itself, and the numbers on the Muslim side were small enough that each death was known to the whole city. The verse treats their deaths as the completion of a promise rather than as a loss to be explained away, and this is a hard doctrine: the value of the pledge is not measured by how long the person who made it survived, but by whether he was standing where he had said he would stand when his time came.

**Those Still Waiting Their Turn**

The second group are those still alive, waiting. The word describes a state of expectation, and in the Book it is used of people who are anticipating something they know will come. The men in this category had no idea how long their wait would last, and no guarantee that their end would be an easy one; what the verse affirms is that their resolution was the same as the resolution of the dead. This is the sentence in which the community's living members were given their own position in the account. The verse does not reserve its praise for the martyrs and leave the survivors with the ordinary business of living. It places them in the same list, on the same terms, waiting for the appointment that the sūrah has already described as fixed beyond negotiation.

**Never Changed Their Commitment in the Least**

The verse closes with a construction that intensifies the claim: they did not alter their word at all. The Arabic repeats the root of the verb to give the sentence the force of an absolute negation, and against the background of the chapter it reads as a direct contrast to the equivocations described earlier. Changing a commitment does not usually look like a betrayal; it looks like a series of adjustments, each one justified by circumstances, until the original word is unrecognisable. The verse says of these men that the process never happened. Between the day of the pledge and the day of the siege, through hunger, fear, casualty lists and rumours about the enemy's return, the word they had given remained the word they were living by.

**An Open Roster**

The verse begins with the phrase "among the believers," and the formula keeps the list open. It does not say that the community of Medina contained these men and that the story is closed. It describes a category inside a body, and the Book's habit of describing categories rather than complete sets is what allows every later reader to ask whether he belongs to it. The example of the Prophet in the previous verse was given on the same terms, attached to whoever has hope in God and the Last Day. Nothing in the chapter restricts either the model or the standard to the men who lived through that winter. The chapter is written for a community that will face its own pressure with the memory of this one in its hands.

**What the Two Categories Have in Common**

The men who died and the men who waited were not different kinds of believer; they were at different points on the same road. The verse's structure makes this plain by giving both groups a single description: they never changed their commitment. A community that reads the passage carefully gains two things from it. It learns that the honour belongs to the pledge rather than to the ending, which frees the survivor from the anxiety of wondering whether he has been passed over. It also learns that the covenant is lived out in time — through years of ordinary work after the crisis has passed — and that the waiting itself is part of the fulfilment rather than a postponement of it.


**The Pledge That Survives a Siege**

What these men show, and what the rest of the sūrah is arranged to teach, is that a pledge survives a siege when the person who made it has decided in advance what he values. The men described in verse 14 would have surrendered their faith for safety with little hesitation, which means their pledge had never reached the centre of their lives; the men of this verse kept theirs through the same circumstances because they had already settled where their loyalty was anchored. The chapter records both, in the same chapter, in the same siege, and it leaves the two accounts side by side so that the reader can see what the difference looks like when it is finally tested in public.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:24

> ˹It all happened˺ so Allah may reward the faithful for their faithfulness, and punish the hypocrites if He wills or turn to them ˹in mercy˺. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Expanded Commentary**

**Why the Chapter Was Allowed to Happen**

The verse supplies the purpose behind everything the sūrah has described: the siege, the shaking, the exposed intentions and the two different responses to the same army. It happened so that God may reward the faithful for their faithfulness and deal with the hypocrites according to His will. History in this account is not a sequence of accidents with moral lessons attached afterwards. The trial was arranged, and it was arranged because it produces a verdict. The Book states the same principle elsewhere in a single line, that a community claiming faith will not be left untested (29:2), and this sūrah shows what such a test looks like when it is imposed on a whole city at once.

**Rewarded for Faithfulness Itself**

The reward is attached to the quality rather than to the quantity of what a person did. The Arabic pairs the noun faithfulness with the same root as the verb for proving true, so that the community which remained honest in its pledge is recompensed for that honesty as such. There is no inventory of achievements in the sentence: no count of trench-duty nights, no calculation of wounds or provisions. What the Book records as the thing worth rewarding is the truthfulness of the person, and this is consistent with how the whole passage has judged the two groups. The hypocrites were not condemned for a shortfall in performance; they were condemned because their word and their conduct were two different things. The reward follows the same measure, applied in the other direction.

**Punish or Turn in Mercy**

The sentence leaves the fate of the hypocrites genuinely open, and the openness is startling so soon after the severity of the preceding verses. They may be punished, and they may be forgiven if God wills it. The Book does not treat the exposure of hypocrisy as a final sentence. It records the offence, describes its consequences, and then holds the door of return ajar, because the same knowledge that exposed the conduct is also the only thing that can judge whether the person has turned. This balance is characteristic of the sūrah: it will end with the same distribution, punishing hypocrite men and women and turning in mercy to believing men and women (33:73), and its closing words are the names by which the possibility of that return is named.

**A Door That Stays Open in a Passage About Judgment**

A community reading these verses could easily conclude that a man exposed as a hypocrite has no way back, and the Book blocks that conclusion twice in one sentence. The condition attached to punishment is God's will, not the community's memory of what the man did during the siege. Anyone who wants to return is not held to the terms he broke; he is dealt with by the One who forgives, and the verse's final names are chosen to say so. This is not a softening of the earlier judgement. The judgement stands, and the deeds remain void as long as the person remains where he was. What the sentence refuses to do is treat a person's past as a permanent verdict on his future.

**The Names That Close a Verse About Punishment**

The verse ends with the declaration that God is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. Placing those two names at the end of a sentence that has just mentioned punishment is a pattern that recurs throughout the Book, and it does a specific job here. It tells the community what kind of severity it is dealing with — not the severity of an offended party seeking compensation, but the discipline of One whose mercy is a permanent attribute rather than a mood. The men who had talked about delusion and slipped away from the trench were still within reach of it while they lived. So was every reader who recognised his own conduct in the description.

**What the Community Owed the Men Who Came Through**

The verse's purpose clause also settles a question of gratitude inside the community. If the trial existed so that faithfulness could be rewarded and hypocrisy dealt with, then the men who held the line during that week were not merely doing their duty; they were earning something that the community would do well to acknowledge. The chapter has already recorded their deaths and their waiting without comment. This sentence supplies the accounting that the narrative deliberately left out, and it locates the accounting where it belongs. The community can honour its dead and its veterans, but the reward named here is prepared by God, and the men who stood in the trench had not been promised a share of the spoils as their motive.


**How a Community Should Read Its Own Crises**

The verse gives the community a way of reading hardship that does not require it to know in advance how the story ends. A trial is not evidence that God has withdrawn; it is the mechanism by which loyalties become visible and rewards become distributable. That reading has practical consequences. It makes a crisis a place to work rather than a place to despair, and it prevents the community from concluding that the measure of God's favour is the absence of difficulty. The siege of Medina supplied the demonstration, and this verse supplies the explanation, placed immediately after the account of the men who died, the men who waited, and the men who spent the week looking for a way out.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:25

> And Allah drove back the disbelievers in their rage, totally empty-handed. And Allah spared the believers from fighting. For Allah is All-Powerful, Almighty

**Expanded Commentary**

**Driven Back in Their Rage**

The verse reports the end of the most dangerous campaign the community had faced, and it does so in a single action: God drove the disbelievers back. The word chosen for their condition as they left is rage. After weeks of preparation, a march of hundreds of kilometres, an alliance assembled from several tribes and an understanding with a party inside the city, they withdrew with nothing. The rage is the rage of a force that has spent everything and gained nothing, and the Book presents that as the outcome of the enterprise rather than as an accident of weather. A campaign that had been intended to end the Muslim presence in Medina ended in a bad-tempered dispersal of the coalition that had financed it.

**Empty-Handed: What the Campaign Failed to Take**

The phrase rendered here as totally emptyhanded describes the absence of every kind of gain a campaign of this type was supposed to produce. No victory to build a reputation on, no booty to distribute, no captured leaders to bargain with, no favourable treaty forcing the Muslims to terms, and no weakening of the community's position for the next confrontation. The Book elsewhere describes the same expedition as a defeat in the only currency that mattered to its organisers: they had come to remove a community and returned having strengthened it. The confederate leaders understood this within weeks, and the Quraysh's strategic position after the siege was never the same. The verse compresses that reversal into two words.

**Spared From Fighting**

The Book then states something remarkable about the defenders: God spared the believers from fighting. This was not a battle they had won. The trench had prevented a battle from beginning; the weather, the failure of the alliance and the collapse of its supplies finished the siege. The verse attributes the result to God without pretending that the Muslims had achieved a military victory, and this realism is important for how the community read the event. They had been saved by means they could not have arranged. A force of about three thousand had stood opposite about ten thousand without a decisive engagement, which is not the shape of an ordinary triumph, and the verse names the difference plainly.

**Effort and Outcome, Kept Apart**

Nothing in this statement excuses the community from preparation. The trench was dug by the Muslims, on the advice of Salmān al-Fārisī, with the Prophet carrying earth alongside them; guards were posted; negotiations were conducted with tribes in the coalition to detach them from it. That work was not treated as a substitute for God's help, and God's help was not treated as a substitute for the work. The verse assigns the outcome to God because the outcome was not something the work could produce; the army did not leave because the trench was deep. The distinction between what a community does and what God decides is a recurring theme of the sūrah, and it is stated here at the turning point of the whole narrative.

**Power and Might Without Qualifiers**

The verse closes with the attributes of God that explain the account: He is All-Powerful and Almighty. Two names for strength stand together at the end of a story in which nobody was overpowered. That is the point. The power of God operates through arrangements that do not look like a contest at all — a wind, a broken alliance, a failed supply line, a tribe that stayed neutral — and the Book still calls it power. The confederates had measured their chances in the only way they knew, by counting men, and the verse invites the reader to measure strength by a different standard. What happened outside Medina had an Author, and the two names tell the reader where the Author's capacity lies.

**What the Defenders Learned About Their Own Weakness**

There is a further lesson in the verse that the community could not have learned without living through the siege: their safety had not depended on their own strength at any point. A trench delays an enemy, but a force of three thousand cannot feed itself indefinitely, and the reports of the period show how close the community came to hunger and to the collapse of the alliance of tribes inside the city. The Book states the outcome as God's act because the community had watched its own resources run down while the crisis lasted. A people who understand that will prepare seriously for the next emergency without confusing preparation with the source of their security.


**A Victory That Did Not Look Like One**

The community gained no territory from the siege and took no spoils; it buried its dead and repaired its walls. By the ordinary standards of the period it had survived rather than won, and the Book refuses to call the event by that smaller name. The sūrah later describes the disbelievers going back with their rage and the believers spared from battle, and the reward that follows the chapter is measured in a different coin altogether. What the verse teaches is that a people can be given a decisive result without a victory parade, and that reading the outcome correctly requires knowing who arranged it. The confederates did not know, and their rage was the sound of that ignorance. What the verse asks of the community is not excitement about having been delivered but accurate memory: the siege was broken by its Author, and the report is short because the fact is enormous. The verse's brevity is part of its method, and the community that read it aloud in later generations heard the whole campaign compressed into a single sentence in which nobody is credited except God.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:26

> And He brought down those from the People of the Book who supported the enemy alliance from their own strongholds, and cast horror into their hearts. You ˹believers˺ killed some, and took others captive

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fortress That Was Meant to Hold**

The verse turns from the departing army to the party inside the city that had been expected to open it. Those addressed are from the People of the Book, and they came down from their strongholds — fortified quarters of date groves and walled compounds on the edge of Medina. The campaign against the Muslims depended partly on them: they were to join the attack from within when the trench line was breached, and their fortified position was the guarantee of their safety whatever happened. The verse records that the fortification gave them nothing. A stronghold is only as good as the calculation behind it, and the calculation had assumed an alliance that dissolved outside the walls before it could be used.

**Horror Cast Into Their Hearts**

The verse gives the reason for the collapse: God cast terror into their hearts. It is the same instrument the chapter described earlier in the opposite direction, when it said that God sent against the confederates forces that could not be seen. Here the operation is internal, and it decides the outcome more completely than any weapon could. A fortified position held by men who believe they will lose cannot be held for long, and the Book treats the fear as something imposed rather than as a natural consequence of the circumstances. This language appears at other decisive moments in the Book, where God casts terror into the hearts of a hostile force and the result is achieved without a battle, and it belongs to the same understanding of events as the wind that ended the siege.

**The Breach of a Covenant**

The charge against them is stated in the language of alliance: they supported the enemy against the community. The background is a treaty. The tribes of Medina, Muslim and non-Muslim, had been joined by a written agreement that bound each party to defend the city and forbade any of them from aiding an outside force against the others. Certain of them had been bound by that document, and their support of the coalition was a breach of it, not a change of opinion. The Qur'an does not treat the breach as an error of judgement to be overlooked in a difficult week. It treats it as the offence that made them a party to the campaign, and it notes elsewhere that those who break their agreements without fear of the consequences share a common character (8:56).

**Killed and Taken Captive**

The verse reports the result in one sentence: some were killed, and others were taken captive. The Book does not describe the proceedings that followed the surrender of the fortified quarters, and it does not list numbers. The historical accounts describe the judgment carried out after the siege, including the ruling of Saʿd ibn Muʿādh, which the Prophet endorsed in the words recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3804. The sūrah's own account is deliberately spare: the fate of a people who joined an army against their own city is stated as a fact and then left. What the Book chose to emphasise is not the machinery of the outcome but the cause of it, and the cause is a covenant that was thrown away in the belief that the winning side had already won.

**Why the Account Is Written This Way**

The brevity of the sentence is a decision about emphasis. The chapter has spent its length on the interior of a community under pressure: on fear, on talking, on excuses, on the difference between those who held and those who hedged. Against that backdrop the fate of the tribe appears in two clauses, and the restraint is a form of instruction. The point being made is not that the tribe's end was remarkable; it is that the same failure — calculating safety by siding with the strongest army — appeared in different forms in different parts of the city, and it produced different verdicts. The chapter's own lesson is that the calculation was wrong everywhere it was made.

**A City Divided Against Itself**

The siege revealed that Medina's danger had never come only from outside. It had a fortified quarter inside its own boundary, bound by treaty, in contact with the besieging army, and waiting for the moment to act. The sūrah has already described another internal party, the waverers and the sick in heart, who were not parties to a treaty but were members of the believing community by profession. Two kinds of internal failure are therefore set side by side in this chapter: the betrayal of a written covenant by one group, and the erosion of morale by another. Both weakened the defence; both were answered in the same weeks; and both are recorded in this sūrah as things that happened to a real city, not as dangers it imagined.


**The Warning That Remains in the Account**

For the community that received this sūrah, the verse's lesson is not about the identity of the tribe but about the nature of a covenant. A treaty in the Book is a trust, and its breach is not excused by the strength of the other side or by expectations about the outcome. The men of the fortified quarters had made a calculation of odds and found the confederacy more convincing; the alliance they backed went home, and they were left with the calculation. This is the same error the sūrah diagnosed in the waverers who wanted to leave the trench and in the men who preferred the camp of the nomads, and it is the error the Book spends this chapter correcting: safety is not produced by betting on the strongest party in the field.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:27

> He has also caused you to take over their lands, homes, and wealth, as well as lands you have not yet set foot on. And Allah is Most Capable of everything

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Immediate Inheritance**

The verse names three things the community took over: their lands, their homes and their wealth. These are concrete gains after a period of fear, hunger and casualty lists, and the Book lists them without apology. A fortified quarter that had joined an attacking coalition forfeited what it had been defending with, and the community that had endured the siege acquired fields, buildings and property on the edge of the city. The verse presents this as an act of God rather than as the outcome of an operation: He caused you to take over. That attribution does not deny that the properties changed hands through the events of the siege; it places the disposal of them in the hands that had already arranged the wind and the fear.

**Lands You Have Not Yet Set Foot On**

The verse then reaches beyond the immediate settlement. Lands you have not yet set foot on: territory that the community of that moment had never seen and had no plan to occupy. The commentators took this as a promise of later expansion, and it was fulfilled in stages within the lifetimes of the people who heard it — the oases of Khaybar a few years later, and then the openings of the wider region as the community grew. The verse is not a map; it makes no list of places. It tells a besieged people who had just been counting their losses that the reach of their community would eventually extend to ground they had never walked on, and it attaches that promise to the same power that had ended the coalition.

**Wealth After Fear: The Test Changes Shape**

There is a transition hidden in this verse, and the sūrah is aware of it. The community that came through the siege intact was about to become a community with property to distribute and income to manage, and the Book's concern about wealth is everywhere in evidence: it warns against the love of it, against hoarding, against the confidence that possessions provide security. The same body that had held a trench through a winter would now have to handle abundance, which is a different kind of test. Nothing in the verse celebrates the acquisition as an end in itself. It records a gift, and the description of God as Most Capable of everything is followed, later in the sūrah, by instructions that govern how the community is to live with what it has been given.

**What Was Not Earned by Numbers**

The community that received these lands had been outnumbered by roughly three to one during the siege and had won no battle. The farms and homes it acquired belonged to a party that had backed the stronger army and lost. The Book's account therefore does not read as the record of a military conquest but as the redistribution that followed a failed attack on Medina. This is worth stating plainly because the chapter's whole argument about power is at stake. What delivered the community was not its own strength, and what enriched it was not its own campaign. Both were the work of the One whose capacity the verse names at the end.

**The Promise That Had to Be Believed Before It Was Seen**

For the men reading this verse in the weeks after the siege, the second half of the sentence required faith of a high order. The first half described something they could see from their own rooftops. The second described territory on the far side of the peninsula that most of them had never encountered. The Book places the two in the same sentence precisely because they belong to the same act, and the community that took heart from the promise would find its own horizons changing within a handful of years. This is the pattern the sūrah has been teaching in every verse about safety: what lies beyond the present crisis belongs to the same hand that carried the community through the crisis itself.

**Nothing Said About How the Gains Were Distributed**

The verse does not describe the distribution of the property, the shares owed to the fighters and the families, or the rulings that would govern it. That legislation appears elsewhere in the Book, and its absence here keeps the verse focused on the sequence of events rather than on the law of spoils. What the passage is doing is closing a narrative: an army came to end the community, the community held, the army went home, and those inside the city who had joined the attack lost what they had. There will be many later verses about wealth, about how to divide it and how to give it away, and they make more sense when read against this account of where the wealth first came from.

**The Attribute That Explains the Whole Passage**

The verse closes with a declaration of God's capability over all things, and the placement turns it into a summary of everything that has happened since verse 9. A coalition of ten thousand dispersed by wind; a fortress quarter surrendered to fear; a psalm-singing community of three thousand given lands it had not marched for; a horizon named that none of them could see. The attribute answers the question the reader is left with, which is how any of this was possible. It was possible because the One who arranged the siege also arranges its aftermath, and the Book's narration of that week ends with the statement that nothing in it exceeded His capacity.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:28

> O Prophet! Say to your wives, “If you desire the life of this world and its luxury, then come, I will give you a ˹suitable˺ compensation ˹for divorce˺ and let you go graciously

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Revelation Turns to the Household**

After a narrative of siege and a passage of legislation about names, inheritance and adoption, the sūrah addresses the Prophet's own household. The command is to say something to his wives, and the fact that God Himself supplies the words is significant: this is not a private domestic dispute being settled by a sage, it is a ruling recorded in the Book for the whole community to read. The wives of the Prophet hold the position described earlier in the chapter, mothers of the believers, which means their affairs are not private business in the way an ordinary family's affairs are. What is decided in that household is decided in front of the community, and the community is meant to learn from it.

**An Offer, Not a Threat**

Read carefully, the sentence offers a genuine choice with a generous exit attached. If you desire the life of this world and its luxury, come: I will give you a suitable compensation for divorce and let you go graciously. There is no accusation in the wording, no penalty attached to the choice, and no suggestion that a woman who takes it has sinned. The Prophet is instructed to present the option of leaving the household with a gift and with her honour intact. This is the Book's manner of dealing with a genuine disagreement inside a home: it clarifies the two roads and requires the man to state the terms of the departing road honestly.

**Why the Choice Became Necessary**

The setting explains the bluntness. These were years of scarcity, and the household of the Prophet lived in the same poverty the community lived in, sometimes without fire in the stove for weeks. The Book elsewhere describes the Prophet's own situation as one in which he had no need of tribute and sought none, and the reports of the period show the household going without. Against that background, some of the wives wanted more household provision than the moment allowed. The verse does not describe the discussion; it presents the resolution. The Prophet is not told to promise what he cannot provide, and he is not told to keep anyone in a marriage by refusing to discuss the terms.

**Compensation and a Gracious Release**

The legal element of the sentence is worth pausing on. A woman released from a marriage by the husband's wish is given a parting provision, and the Book elsewhere makes the manner of that release a duty: Divorce may be retracted twice, then the husband must retain ˹his wife˺ with honour or separate ˹from her˺ with grace. It is not lawful for husbands to take back anything of the dowry given to their wives, unless the couple fears not being able to keep within the limits of Allah. So if you fear they will not be able to keep within the limits of Allah, there is no blame if the wife compensates the husband to obtain divorce. These are the limits set by Allah, so do not transgress them. And whoever transgresses the limits of Allah, they are the ˹true˺ wrongdoers. (2:229). The Arabic words for the gift and for the release both carry the sense of something done well and without resentment, and the verse that closes this passage uses them together. What is being protected here is the woman's dignity after the marriage. A household that must shed members on grounds of material expectation is not licensed to do it grudgingly, and the command to bestow and release graciously prevents the choice from becoming a punishment.

**A Prophet's Domestic Life as Public Instruction**

There is a reason this material sits in Sūrah al-Aḥzāb rather than in a private report. The community watched the Prophet's household closely, and it took its own standards from it. If the wives of the Prophet had been handed a privileged settlement at public expense, the example would have been set for every household in Medina. Instead the Book records a choice offered, a gift promised to anyone who leaves, and a very different reward promised to anyone who stays for other reasons. The household becomes a demonstration of the choice the whole chapter is built on: the world and its luxury on one side, and something else on the other.

**What "the World and Its Luxury" Names**

The phrase pairs the world with its adornment, the ordinary pattern of things and the things people hope to get from them. The Book does not describe the world as evil in this verse, and it does not condemn the wish for a decent life. What it identifies is a desire for luxury that determines a person's choice when the two roads diverge — the point at which the wish to live well becomes the standard by which everything else is judged. The Prophet's wives were offered exactly that line as a decision to make, and the community was allowed to see the terms of the offer, which is why the verse can be read by every household that has to decide what it values when the two roads are placed in front of it.

**The Household Held to a Public Standard**

The chapter has already made the Prophet's family the community's family: his wives are their mothers, and his claim on the believers exceeds their own claims on themselves. The verse that follows from that status is not a privilege but a standard. What the community is told here is that the household closest to the revelation will not be permitted to settle its affairs on the ordinary calculations of comfort. It will be offered a choice with its terms in the open. That is a hard form of honour, and the sūrah presents it as the first ruling in a section that will describe the conduct expected of these women for the rest of their lives.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:29

> But if you desire Allah and His Messenger and the ˹everlasting˺ Home of the Hereafter, then Surely Allah has prepared a great reward for those of you who do good.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Road**

The previous verse set out the terms of leaving; this one sets out the terms of staying, and the contrast between the two is the argument of the whole passage. The one who stays does not stay because no alternative was offered, and does not stay because a husband refused to discuss it. She stays because she desires God, His Messenger and the Home of the Hereafter. The choice is made between realities, not between men. A woman who takes this road is not trading her comfort for a better settlement; she is saying that the object of her desire is God, and the rest of her life in that household will be measured by that statement.

**Desire Directed at Persons**

The wording is remarkable and easy to pass over. The Book does not say, if you desire the reward of God, or if you desire Paradise. It says, if you desire Allah and His Messenger. The longing is directed at God Himself, joined to the messenger who is standing in the room. This is the language of love rather than of calculation, and it is used in a legal context, in a verse that belongs to a set of rulings about marriage and provision. The Book takes for granted that a believer's attachment to God can be the decisive motive in an ordinary domestic choice, and the community is invited to hear that sentence as one that describes its own households as much as the Prophet's.

**A Reward Already Prepared**

The verse attaches a promise to the choice: God has prepared a great reward for those of you who do good. The verb is in the form that indicates prior preparation, the same form used throughout the sūrah for the punishment of the deniers and the reward of the faithful. Nothing about this reward is improvised or adjusted; it stands ready. That is the answer the verse gives to the arithmetic that would otherwise dominate the decision. A woman weighing the comfort she is giving up against the life she will continue to live is being told that the account is not settled in this world, and that the settlement has already been arranged. The Book leaves the size of the reward unnamed, and it uses the same word, great, that it uses for the reward promised to the believers at large.

**"For Those Who Do Good"**

The promise is not attached to the declaration alone. It applies to those of you who do good, and the phrase puts conduct back into the sentence at the moment when it might have been reduced to a sentiment. Choosing God in one decision is the beginning; doing good is the practice that follows the choice across years of ordinary life. The form of the Arabic word describes women who are characterised by doing good rather than women who perform a good act, and the reward is promised to that standing character. This matters because the passage is about a long marriage in a poor household. A single dramatic decision serves nothing if the years after it are spent in resentment, and the Book's wording quietly demands the whole stretch.

**The Standard the Household Was Living Under**

The verse cannot be read without the poverty it presumes. These were the years in which the community's own warriors had been living on short rations, in which the Prophet's household had gone without cooked food for extended periods, and in which the wife who complained of household allowance was complaining about something real. The Book does not respond by promising an improvement, and it does not rebuke the complaint. It offers a choice and a reward, and it leaves the maintenance arrangements where they were. What the community was watching, in that small set of rooms, was whether the closest household in Islam would accept the same material conditions as everyone else, and the answer was left to the women themselves.

**A Pattern for Decisions That Cannot Be Reversed**

The structure of the two verses has proved useful to the community ever since, because it models how the Book handles a choice between the world and God. It sets out the terms of both roads plainly, it refuses to punish anyone for taking the world, and it attaches an unnamed reward to the harder road. No pressure is applied by withholding the terms of departure, and no threat is issued to those who stay in half-heartedness. The decision is placed where decisions belong, in the interior of the person making it, and the Book does what no community can do in such a case: it records the two options in a form that later generations can apply to their own divisions between what is comfortable and what is right.

**Why the Answer Is Left Open**

The verses relate the offer and the promise, and the sūrah does not announce how each woman chose. The reader is told the terms, not the results, which keeps the passage's emphasis where the Book wants it: on the shape of the choice rather than on the identity of the choosers. What follows in the chapter describes the conduct expected of the Prophet's wives in a manner that presumes they stayed, and the community's memory records no departure. But the silence here is instructive. The Book has exposed the hearts of hypocrites and the calculations of waverers earlier in this sūrah; in its own household it states the options, promises the reward, and leaves the decision to the women whose decision it is.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:30

> O wives of the Prophet! If any of you were to commit a blatant misconduct, the punishment would be doubled for her. And that is easy for Allah

**Expanded Commentary**

**Addressed by Their Standing, Warned as Individuals**

The sūrah turns to the wives of the Prophet directly, and the address itself carries the whole tension of the passage. They are named by their relationship to the messenger, which is the highest honour a woman in that society could hold, and the sentence that follows is a warning about what she herself might do. Honour and accountability arrive in the same breath. This pattern governs the section: the same status that gives these women their distinction gives them their exposure. They are not addressed as an undifferentiated part of the household but individually, with each held answerable for her own conduct, which is the first thing the passage establishes about their moral standing.

**Double Punishment and the Reason for It**

The warning is specific: if any of them were to commit a blatant misconduct, the punishment would be doubled. Doubling is not arbitrary severity. In the Book's moral order, responsibility scales with the position a person occupies and with the effect of the act, and the wives of the Prophet occupied a position no other women occupied. Their conduct was the model for every household in the community, and a scandal inside the Prophet's home would have been used against the message itself by every opponent in Arabia. The doubling measures the size of the arena rather than the size of the sin. It says that the people closest to the source of the message bear the heaviest consequence for contradicting it.

**The Meaning of Blatant Misconduct**

The Arabic phrase pairs a word for indecency with a word meaning clear and open. The combination matters. What is in view is not a private lapse that a person regrets and conceals, but conduct that is manifest, known, and therefore instructive to everyone watching. The Book's concern is not the size of the sin but its visibility in a household that the community studies. Where an ordinary woman's failing might remain between her and God, a public failing in this house would become a fact about the Prophet's family, and the verse legislates for that consequence. The community is being told that its teacher's household is held to a standard that accounts for the audience.

**The Cost of Being Watched**

The warning also states something true of any community's leading family: examples are expensive to maintain. The Book elsewhere addresses the wives' situation in terms of what they are to say, how they are to sit, how they are to meet people, and every instruction presupposes that they are visible. The verse is not an expression of distrust. It is an acknowledgment of the position, stated plainly, so that a woman in it understands what she is carrying. This is a form of respect: the Book treats these women as capable of weighing a heavy consequence and choosing anyway, rather than as ornaments whose conduct is not their own business.

**"That Is Easy for Allah"**

The sentence ends with the same reminder the sūrah has used for punishment and reward elsewhere: this is easy for God. The clause answers the unstated objection. A crime committed in a prophet's household might go unnoticed by the city; a private failing might never be known; and human oversight could never reach the interior where intention lives. The Book states that the accounting is not dependent on witnesses. The same phrase has been attached to the reward of the faithful and to the dispersal of the confederate army, and it does the same work here: it locates the enforcement in hands that miss nothing and are not burdened by the task.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

Nothing in this verse accuses anyone. It is framed as a conditional, and the condition is never met in the chapter. The Book states the possibility in order to state the standard, and the community that reads it learns what the position required without being told that any woman failed. This restraint is characteristic of the sūrah's treatment of the household. Its hardest passages about the community's own members name no individuals among the believers; and here, where the warning is at its most severe, no one is named and no failure is reported. The verse is a fence built around a household rather than a sentence passed on it.

**The Position No Woman Chose but Every Woman Understood**

The warning is severe, and the Book states it without softening, but the whole of what this sūrah has said about the household deserves to sit beside it. These women were not asked whether they wanted the position; it came with the marriage. What the verse does is tell them what the position costs, in terms they could weigh, at a time when they were still free to leave on honourable terms. A community that honours its leading household with a title must also tell it the truth about the exposure that comes with the title, and the sūrah states the exposure plainly before it states the reward. That sequence — warning first, then promise — is deliberate, and it is the same sequence the Book uses with communities.


**Honour That Carries Its Own Discipline**

Set against the earlier verse that made these women mothers to the believers, the warning completes a picture. The community has been told to honour them as it honours its own mothers; the verse now tells the women that such a position is not simply a possession but a duty with a higher price attached to failure. Every community has families whose conduct carries more weight than the conduct of others, whether it wants that arrangement or not. The Book takes the fact and uses it, converting the visibility of the Prophet's household from a burden into a responsibility, and leaving the women themselves the moral agents of the passage.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:31

> And whoever of you devoutly obeys Allah and His Messenger and does good, We will grant her double the reward, and We have prepared for her an honourable provision

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Other Half of the Doubling**

The warning of the previous verse was doubled punishment for blatant misconduct. This verse supplies its counterpart: double reward for obedience and good conduct. The Book does not leave the household under a threat without adding the corresponding promise, and the symmetry is exact. The same position that magnifies a failure magnifies a success, and the women are told that their years of ordinary devotion in that household are not counted as the devotion of ordinary people. Two verses, one standard, and it is applied in both directions. A reader who takes the warning seriously must take the promise seriously too, since the reasoning behind them is one and the same.

**"Devoutly Obeying": A Sustained Posture**

The word rendered here as devoutly obeying describes a settled state rather than an act. It belongs to a family of words the Book uses for people whose devotion is a standing feature of their lives, and it appears again in this same sūrah in the long verse about Muslim men and women, devout men and women. What is being described is not intensity of feeling but continuity of practice: a woman who prays when it is inconvenient, keeps the discipline when nobody is watching, and remains constant across the years of a marriage. The Book chooses this word because the promise it is making covers a lifetime rather than a moment, and the conduct it is rewarding is the kind that can be sustained.

**Good Conduct Beyond the Requirements**

The verse adds doing good to obedience. The two are not the same: obedience fulfils an obligation, while doing good goes beyond what can be required and shapes the way a person treats others. In the context of the Prophet's household the addition covers a great deal of daily life — patience with scarcity, generosity with what there is, kindness toward the community that constantly came to the door, and the ordinary decencies that no law can compel. The reward promised is attached to this whole pattern, and the community is being shown that the standard it should admire in that household is not only ritual observance but the quality of its daily conduct.

**An Honourable Provision**

The verse closes by promising that God has prepared an honourable provision for such a woman. The word chosen for the provision conveys generosity and dignity, and commentators differed about where it is received. Some read it as the provision of this world, understood as a life maintained in honour despite the household's poverty; others read it as the reward of the Hereafter. The second is the stronger reading, since the verse begins with the promise of a doubled reward and would otherwise repeat itself. Either way the word matters. These women were being asked to accept material hardship, and the Book answers with a provision described in the vocabulary of nobility rather than sufficiency.

**Why These Women Receive Twice**

The reason for the doubling is not favouritism. A larger reward corresponds to a harder task, and the task set out in this section is substantial: living in a household that is watched by a whole city, in poverty, under instructions about speech and conduct that will be spelled out in the next verses, with a public responsibility for the message's credibility. The reward doubles because the burden doubles, and the Book states that correspondence openly so that the community does not mistake these women for privileged dependants. They are being asked to carry the most demanding domestic role in the community, and they are being paid for it in a currency that is not available to their neighbours.

**Their Moral Agency Stated in Full**

This chapter attributes a great deal to these women's own choice. They are given an offer with terms; they are warned of the consequences of misconduct; they are promised a reward for obedience; and the entire passage presupposes that the decision is theirs. The Qur'an does not describe the Prophet's wives as objects of his household's arrangements but as believers with their own standing before God, whose reward and punishment are calculated on their own conduct. In a chapter that has just legislated about marriage, inheritance and adoption, this treatment of the women's responsibility is consistent with everything around it, and it is stated in the plainest possible terms.

**What the Verse Assumes About a Woman's Standing**

The whole passage takes the moral agency of these women as settled. They are addressed, offered terms, warned, promised and rewarded directly, and the passage treats their choices as consequential in their own right rather than as extensions of their husband's standing. This is worth stating because later custom in some Muslim societies treated women's religious accountability as mediated through the household. The Book, in the verses at the centre of the sūrah about the Prophet's own family, does the opposite. Each woman is answerable for her own conduct, promised for her own devotion, and spoken to as a person whose relationship with God is the decisive fact about her.


**The Household as a Model of the Chapter's Choice**

The two roads offered in the earlier verses — the world with its luxury on one side, and God, His Messenger and the Hereafter on the other — are now given their accounts. Those who take the first road leave with an honourable settlement and their dignity. Those who take the second are promised twice the reward and an honourable provision. The chapter presents the second road as costly and worth it. It does not pretend the cost is small, and it does not measure the reward in the goods of the world the women were giving up. What it does instead is place the women's own conduct, and the accounting that follows from it, at the centre of a sūrah about the survival of a community.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:32

> O wives of the Prophet! You are not like any other women: if you are mindful ˹of Allah˺, then do not be overly effeminate in speech ˹with men˺ or those with sickness in their hearts may be tempted, but speak in a moderate tone

**Expanded Commentary**

**"You Are Not Like Any Other Women"**

The address repeats the title of the previous two verses and then states its consequence: these women are not like other women. The comparison is not flattery. It follows immediately from the declaration that they are the mothers of the believers and the household the community studies, and it means that the ordinary latitude available to other women is not available to them. The Book adds a condition before the instruction — if you are mindful of Allah — which places the difference where it actually lies. What distinguishes them is their standing, and the standing only has meaning if it is carried with God-consciousness. The remainder of the verse tells them what that consciousness requires in a specific and practical matter.

**The Manner of Speech, Not the Right to Speak**

The instruction concerns how they speak, not whether they may speak. The Book does not silence these women and does not confine them to their rooms in this verse. It forbids a particular quality of speech — the soft, inviting, deliberately alluring manner that belongs to courtship rather than to conversation — and then it commands the alternative: a recognised, straightforward tone. This distinction has governed the passage's reading in every generation of Muslim scholarship. The Prophet's wives had to speak to men: they taught, they transmitted, they answered questions, they conducted business within the household. What the verse removes is not their voice but the manner that invites a certain kind of attention.

**"Those With Sickness in Their Hearts"**

The reason given for the instruction is that men with sickness in their hearts might be tempted. The phrase has already appeared in this sūrah, where it described the waverers and defeatists of the siege, and it recurs later when the troublemakers of Medina are threatened. It describes a disorder in a person rather than a criminal category: the covetous, the unstable, those whose attention goes where it should not. Naming that group is part of the verse's fairness. The Book does not blame the woman for a sickness in the man; it removes the opportunity that the sickness would exploit, and it does so while stating that the sickness belongs to the one who has it.

**A Moderate Tone: The Standard Called Recognised Speech**

The phrase used for the required manner is the ordinary word for what is known and acknowledged, the same term the Book uses for honourable dealings in trade, marriage and divorce. It means speech that no one can mistake for a proposition, speech that says what it means without coarseness and without invitation. The standard is not harshness; a woman is not instructed to speak like a soldier. The instruction occupies the middle ground, which is why it can be applied to the whole community as a rule for conduct with the opposite sex. It is the same standard the sūrah hands to the believers later when it tells them to be mindful of God and to say what is right (33:70).

**Why This Household Needed the Rule First**

The wives of the Prophet lived in a house that was also a public institution. People came to ask about religion, to seek rulings, to bring news, to deliver goods; the women of that household were visible to the community in ways that other women were not. Every exchange in that setting carried extra weight, and the sūrah's concern is that the household's accessibility should not create a channel for any other interest. The instruction is therefore protective in both directions: it protects the community from a scandal that would be used against the message, and it protects the women from the approaches of men whose motives were not what they appeared.

**Reputation as a Form of Protection**

The verse's deeper reasoning concerns reputation, which the Book treats as something a community must manage rather than ignore. Rumour and scandal circulate in any society; in a besieged and divided city, with an active party of hypocrites looking for material, they circulated faster. The sūrah's later verses deal directly with rumourmongers and with slander against believing men and women, and this instruction belongs to the same defence. What is being protected is the space around these women so that no one can manufacture a story about them. The rule is not a burden imposed on their nature but a wall built around a trust.

**Rumour, Scandal and the Protection of the Household**

There is a defensive purpose in the instruction that the following verses make explicit, where the sūrah threatens those who spread rumours in Medina and those who offend believing men and women without cause. The household of the Prophet was the community's most exposed target: any story about it travelled faster than news of a military campaign, and a party inside the city was looking for exactly such material. The rule about speech is one of a series of protections built around this household in the chapter, and it belongs with the barrier mentioned later for visitors and with the instruction to the wives about their own conduct. Reputation in a divided community is part of the defence, and the Book treats it as such.


**A Standard for the Whole Community, Stated at Its Centre**

Although addressed to the Prophet's wives, the verse's logic applies to every believer who reads it. Speech between men and women has consequences beyond the speakers, and the community bears the cost of what is said carelessly. The Book states the rule at the point where the stakes are highest, in the household that the whole city watches, and it states it as a manner rather than a prohibition of contact. Later in the sūrah the same community is told to speak what is right and to guard its tongues in the disputes of the city. The standard set here for the Prophet's household is the standard eventually expected of everyone.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:33

> Settle in your homes, and do not display yourselves as women did in the days of ˹pre-Islamic˺ ignorance. Establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and obey Allah and His Messenger. Allah only intends to keep ˹the causes of˺ evil away from you and purify you completely, O members of the ˹Prophet’s˺ family

**Expanded Commentary**

**Settling in the Home**

The first command addresses where the women are to be and what they are to make of their houses. The Arabic verb has the sense of remaining settled and stable, the same root the language uses for something that stays in place, and its command form here has been read by scholars in more than one way. Some have taken it as an obligation that keeps the Prophet's wives at home; others, noting that the same root appears in a famous instruction about prayer and that the Prophet's own wives are reported to have attended the mosque, have read it as an instruction to make the home the centre of their lives rather than a prohibition on leaving it. What both readings agree on is the emphasis: these houses, not the public square, were to be the setting of their daily work.

**The Days Before Guidance**

The verse then forbids a specific practice and names the era it belonged to: do not display yourselves as women did in the days of ignorance. The term for that era, *jāhiliyya*, has become a technical label for pre-Islamic Arabia, but its sense here is moral rather than chronological: the time before the guidance, when people lived without a standard they could appeal to. The practice being forbidden was the deliberate display of a woman's beauty to attract attention in public, and the Book ties it to the period rather than to the persons, which means the community is being asked to leave a habit behind as it left the era behind. The command is part of the transition the whole sūrah keeps describing.

**Duties the Household Could Not Escape**

The verse then lists the obligations that belong to every believer: establish prayer, pay the alms-tax, and obey Allah and His Messenger. The list is deliberately ordinary. It contains nothing specific to the Prophet's family, no elevated devotion and no privilege, and its presence here makes an argument about the household's status. The women closest to the messenger were not exempted from the duties that applied to the community at large, and the community is being told this directly so that the household is neither idolised nor resented. Whatever special responsibility the earlier verses attached to these women, it did not consist of a different religion. They prayed the same prayer and paid the same alms as everyone else.

**"Allah Only Intends to Remove Impurity From You"**

The verse then supplies the purpose behind all the instructions of this section. God intends to remove impurity from the household and to purify them completely. This statement follows a series of commands about how the women of the house are to live, and it explains why those commands are not arbitrary: they are the means by which a household is made clean, in the way the Book uses purity language for the heart, the intention and the community's collective character. The phrase also implies a limit to what the community should read into the rules: the aim is purification, not the reduction of these women's standing, not the extinguishing of their presence in the community's life, and not punishment.

**Who Are the Members of the Family?**

The closing phrase, O members of the Prophet's family, has generated one of the best-known disagreements in the exegesis of this sūrah. One reading, favoured because the whole passage addresses the wives in the feminine plural, takes the family here to mean the wives themselves, with the instruction and the purification applying to them. Another reading, supported by well-known reports in which the Prophet gathered ʿAlī, Fāṭima and their two sons under a cloak and prayed over them, takes the phrase to designate those five. A third combines the two, hearing the wives addressed as part of the household of the Prophet while the phrase retains its wider reference. Each reading has weight: the grammar of the passage favours the first, and the reported occasion favours the second.

**The Reading That Holds Them Together**

The strongest position is that the Book speaks here of the household of the Prophet in a sense broad enough to include everyone the house contained, wives and near kin alike. The passage's commands about conduct are addressed to the wives because they are the ones whose conduct the community would watch, and the purity that follows is promised to the household those commands protect. Nothing is taken from either group by this reading: the wives are given the instruction and the promise directly, and the Prophet's closest family are included in the purification the verse announces. The sūrah's larger design, which keeps describing the Muslim community as one with obligations shared across every household, supports this way of holding the two readings together.

**A Household Set Apart for a Purpose**

What the verse establishes, on any reading, is that the Prophet's household is set apart, not for luxury but for a purpose. Its members are marked out by an intention that the Book states in its own words, and the rules in this section are the practical form that intention takes. This is the same logic the sūrah applied to the Prophet himself: he was given a claim on the believers that no one else holds, and it came with obligations that were correspondingly heavier. The household around him received a similar relationship and a similar charge. The passage ends where such passages usually end in this chapter, with the community instructed to look at the nearest example it has and take its standards from it.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:34

> ˹Always˺ remember what is recited in your homes of Allah’s revelations and ˹prophetic˺ wisdom. Surely Allah is Most Subtle, All-Aware

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Recitation That Filled Those Houses**

The command to remember what is recited in your homes describes an ordinary domestic fact and turns it into a religious duty. The Prophet recited the revelation in his house; the women of the household heard the verses that the community would later read from manuscripts and read aloud in mosques for the rest of its history. The Book calls on them to remember what they heard, which means to keep it present, to consider it, and to let it govern the life of the home. There is no suggestion that the recitation happened somewhere else and was reported to them. The command presumes that the verses were being spoken in the rooms where these women slept, ate and worked.

**Revelation and Wisdom**

The verse names two things that were recited in the house: the revelations of God and wisdom. The pairing is important, because the two are not identical. The revelations are the revealed text; wisdom, in this usage, covers the understanding and the teaching that accompanied it, the way the Prophet explained, applied and lived what he received. Commentators have read the pair as the Book and the practice of the Prophet together, which is why this verse has been cited in discussions of the authority of the sunna. What the household is told to remember is therefore not only the words of revelation but the whole body of guidance that grew from them in that house.

**A Duty of Attention, Not of Memory Alone**

The word for remembering carries a sense of holding something in mind and acting in accordance with it, which is why the command is repeated to communities throughout the Book. Applied to this household, it places a duty on the women that is heavier than listening. They were to keep the recitation of their homes at the centre of their lives, so that the revelation was not merely an event they had witnessed but the structure of their conduct. The instruction also anticipates the role these women would play after the Prophet's death, when the community would come to them for what had been said and done in those rooms. What was remembered in the house would become the community's memory.

**Most Subtle, All-Aware**

The verse closes with two names of God: the One whose knowledge is subtle, penetrating what is hidden and delicate, and the One who is aware of everything. The two names together describe the reach of an attention that misses nothing in a private house: the tone of a conversation, the intention behind a kindness, the difference between a recited verse that was loved and one that was merely heard. This is the Book's way of attaching a reward to invisible conduct. The women of the household were being asked to do things no one could properly measure, and the verse ends by naming the One who measures them.

**The Wives as Teachers of the Community**

The role the verse anticipates was fulfilled. The Prophet's wives narrated a substantial body of what the community knows about his life at home: how he prayed at night, how he treated his family, how he spoke and what he said in private. ʿĀʾisha is reported as the transmitter of roughly two thousand two hundred reports, the largest number attributed to any woman in the tradition and among the largest of any transmitter at all. That material, preserved and studied for centuries, is the direct product of the ordinary recitation this verse commands the household to remember, and it is why the command here is not a small domestic duty but one of the main channels by which the community received its religion.

**A Home That Became a School**

Read in this light, the two verses about the household describe a place of learning. The house where revelation was recited, where the Prophet answered questions and corrected conduct, and where the women remembered what they heard, became the community's living archive. Visitors came to ask; the wives answered; scholars in the next generation travelled and collected what they said. The sūrah's regulations about modesty and speech, rather than closing the house, defined its channels: the women would teach from behind the barrier, through the veil, in their own homes, and the knowledge would move through the community without the household losing its privacy.

**The Difference Between Hearing and Knowing**

A house can be full of recitation and empty of understanding, and the command in this verse is addressed to that gap. The women of the Prophet's household heard the revelation as it arrived, verse by verse, often before anyone else in the community heard it, in the middle of ordinary domestic situations. What the Book asks of them is that this proximity produce knowledge rather than familiarity. The distinction runs through the whole of Islamic teaching about revelation: the people nearest to a source are in the greatest danger of taking it for granted, and they are also in the best position to understand it. The command to remember is therefore a command to convert exposure into comprehension, and the community's later reliance on these women shows how well the conversion was carried out.


**What the Community Owes That Memory**

The chapter's emphasis suggests an obligation on the community itself. A household that remembered the revelation has given the whole community its legacy, and the Book's command to these women is mirrored by the community's duty to keep what they were given. The sūrah's later verses address the believers about their own conduct in the Prophet's house: how they enter, how they sit, how they speak to the wives. Those rules are framed as protection of the Prophet's household, and this verse explains what was being protected. A community that respected the house without receiving its memory would have kept the wall and lost the treasure.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:35

> Surely ˹for˺ Muslim men and women, believing men and women, devout men and women, truthful men and women, patient men and women, humble men and women, charitable men and women, fasting men and women, men and women who guard their chastity, and men and women who remember Allah often—for ˹all of˺ them Allah has prepared forgiveness and a great reward

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Came Before the Verse**

The exegetical literature connects this verse to a question asked by Umm Salamah, one of the Prophet's wives. She asked why women were not mentioned in the Qur'an as men are, and she reported that shortly afterwards she heard the Prophet reciting from the pulpit the verse that names men and women together, pair by pair. The report is recorded in Musnad Aḥmad and cited by Ibn Kathīr in his commentary. Whatever weight a reader gives the occasion, the verse answers the question in its structure: ten qualities, each named twice, once for men and once for women, with one promise at the end covering everyone in the list. Women had already appeared in the Book as subjects of rulings, as figures in narrative, and as believers addressed in the plural. Here they are named as the possessors of the virtues the Book praises, which is the specific gap the report remembers being closed.

**Ten Qualities, One Life**

The ten are named in sequence: submission, faith, devout obedience, truthfulness, patient endurance, humility, charity, fasting, guarding chastity, and remembering God often. The order is not a ranking. It moves from what a person accepts as true to what a person does with his day, and from the inward posture to the visible habit, ending with the practice that sustains everything before it. Nobody is asked to specialise in one item; the reward is promised to those in whom the qualities are found together. Read this way, the list describes a life rather than an examination. Submission establishes the ground, faith settles it, devout obedience and truthfulness describe the person, patience and humility describe how he carries difficulty, and the last three turn the whole thing into conduct that other people can see and benefit from.

**Why Submission and Faith Are Listed Separately**

The two opening items are not synonyms, and the scholars drew a point from their separation. Submission is the entry: the acceptance of a claim and the joining of a community. Faith is the settlement of that acceptance in the heart, with knowledge and trust behind it. The Book treats the difference as a real stage in a person's life when it tells a group of desert Arabs that they have submitted although faith has not yet entered their hearts (49:14). This verse puts both on the list because both are needed, and it does not pretend that the first automatically produces the second. A person can be counted inside the community by his profession and still have work to do on the interior; the list asks for the interior without discarding the outside.

**Women Named as Those Who Do the Virtues**

The grammatical method of the verse is its clearest statement. Each quality is given twice, in the masculine and the feminine plural, and the feminine form is used ten times in a single sentence. In a society where religious language in public was frequently addressed to men and women were discussed as members of households, this repetition is not decoration. It makes women the grammatical subjects of patience, truthfulness, charity, fasting and chastity, and it makes them the subjects of worship in the final item, remembering God often. The Book is not describing women as recipients of someone else's religion. It is describing them as people who perform it, and the promise attached at the end is attached to them in their own right.

**The Virtues Are Ordinary, and That Is the Point**

Nothing on the list requires wealth, status, travel or exceptional strength. Fasting, charity, truthfulness and patience are available in a small house with little food, which is the setting this sūrah has been describing since it began addressing the Prophet's household. The list would have been reachable by the poorest woman in Medina and by the wealthiest man in the community, and the Book's decision to praise these things rather than heroic acts is a deliberate emphasis. A religion whose admired virtues are available to everyone cannot be accused of being built for a class of heroes. What it demands instead is continuity, and continuity in small things is the hardest demand of all.

**Forgiveness First, Then a Great Reward**

The promise at the end comes in two parts, and the order matters. Forgiveness is named before the reward, because the list will never be completed perfectly by anyone. A person who fasts will miss days; a person who guards his chastity will still have thoughts; a person who remembers God often will also forget. The Book names the remedy before the payment, which is a pattern that runs through the whole sūrah: the household rulings were followed by mercy, the account of the hypocrites by the possibility of return. The reward is then left unspecified, described only as great. The Book does not encourage people to calculate, and it does not let the value of the thing promised be reduced to a quantity that a merchant could compare with his losses.

**Where the Verse Sits in the Chapter**

The passage immediately before this one addressed the wives of the Prophet, giving them an offer, a warning and a promise. This verse opens the door to every believer, male and female, and it does not distinguish between the household of the Prophet and the rest of the community when it lists the qualities or distributes the reward. The move is characteristic of the sūrah, which keeps pulling the reader from a particular case to a general law: from the siege to the standing rule about trials, from the Prophet's household to the whole community's conduct, from a household dispute to a ruling on divorce. What the chapter has just said to a handful of women in Medina it now says to everyone listening.

**The Same Principle in a Single Line**

The Book states the same equal standing elsewhere without a list, in a sentence short enough to be remembered: “But those who do good— whether male or female—and have faith will enter Paradise and will never be wronged ˹even as much as˺ the speck on a date stone.” (4:124). The two verses do different work. The one in this sūrah provides the qualities; the one in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ provides the verdict, and the phrase about the date stone's speck rules out the one thing people fear when they think about divine justice, that a difference in reward might come from something other than the deed. Read together, they leave no room for the idea that a believer's gender is a variable in the reckoning.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:36

> It is not for a believing man or woman—when Allah and His Messenger decree a matter—to have any other choice in that matter. Indeed, whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger has clearly gone ˹far˺ astray

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence and the Two People It Binds**

The verse states a rule about believing men and believing women in the same breath, and it states it as a property of belief rather than as a command issued to strangers. The construction is closer to a description than an order: it is not for a believer to keep an option open once God and His Messenger have decided. The wording assumes that a person may have wanted something else, and it does not treat that wish as a crime. What it removes is the standing of that wish as a basis for action after the matter has been settled. The Book frequently works this way, granting the reality of human reluctance and then deciding what the reluctance is allowed to determine.

**Whose Decree the Verse Names**

The authority named in the sentence is precise: God and His Messenger. Commentators drew attention to that precision because it excludes everything else. A tribe does not acquire the right to overrule a revealed command by seniority, a family does not acquire it by custom, and a ruler does not acquire it by holding office. Later jurists used this verse in discussions of marriage to make the same point from the other direction: the decree that binds a Muslim is God's, not a guardian's preference, which is why a father cannot cite this verse to justify overriding his daughter's consent. The verse binds the believer directly to the revelation, and it leaves no room for a chain of command in which a human being stands between a person and the command of God.

**Deliberation Before, Submission After**

The verse does not abolish consultation. The Book requires it: “So pardon them, ask Allah's forgiveness for them, and consult with them in ˹conducting˺ matters. Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah. Surely Allah loves those who trust in Him.” (3:159). The two halves of that sentence mark the boundary this verse is also marking. Before a matter is decided, arguments belong in the room: the community is asked to think, to disagree and to advise. After it is decided, the argument changes character, and continuing to press it becomes a claim against the decision itself. The Prophet is told to consult and then, having decided, to place his trust in God, which means that the end of discussion is itself an act of worship rather than a defeat for the losing side.

**The Older Loyalties That Had to Give Way**

Medina's tribes had their own rules about who decided a marriage. A woman's family, particularly her male relatives, expected to determine the match, and an individual who resisted was resisting a whole structure of standing and obligation. This verse enters that structure and replaces its foundation. What a believer weighs is no longer what the tribe wants or what the elders have arranged but what the revelation has decreed. The chapter has already rearranged the same terrain: it dissolved the pre-Islamic adoption that gave a man an invented lineage, it reordered inheritance, and it made the Prophet's claim on the believers exceed their own claims on themselves. Verse 36 states the principle behind all of it, and it states it for men and women alike, since both are now answerable to the same authority.

**The Distance the Word Astray Measures**

The verse's closing phrase is not a mild one. The Arabic verb belongs to the image of a road, and the form used here means to go far away from it, to wander off beyond sight of the track. The Book uses the image for the person who has left the path and can no longer be called back by landmarks. What makes the sentence forceful is that it is attached to a refusal that would have looked small from outside: an unwillingness to accept one ruling, in one household, in one matter. The Book does not measure the distance travelled by the size of the decision. It measures it by what the decision reveals about who is being followed.

**What the Verse Assumes About Revelation**

One assumption sits under the whole sentence, and it is easy to pass over. A believer is told to submit to a decree, which means the decree must be knowable — not a private instinct, not the preference of a community's elders, but a command that can be identified and pointed to. The verse therefore belongs to the Book's larger claim that guidance is delivered and available rather than discovered by intuition. A person is not asked to guess what God wants from the shape of his circumstances. He is asked to accept what God and His Messenger have decided, which presumes a text, a teaching, and a messenger through whom the decision arrives. That is also why the sentence can be stated as a description of believing men and women at all: without a knowable decree, submission would be a mood rather than an obligation.

**When the Decision Is Hard to Recognise**

The community would later need one more thing this verse implies. Decrees arrive through a messenger who lives among people, and the community must be able to distinguish his rulings from his personal preferences, his teaching from his ordinary opinions. The Book protects that distinction in the same sūrah by describing the Prophet as one who speaks only what he is given to speak where revelation is concerned, and by separating his household arrangements from the rulings that bind everyone else. The verse's authority is therefore narrow and exact: what binds a believer is what God and His Messenger have decreed, and the community was given the means to know which matters those were.


**The Answer the Believers Are Supposed to Give**

The response the Book expects is stated in Sūrah al-Nūr in words that became a formula for the community: “The only response of the ˹true˺ believers, when they are called to Allah and His Messenger so he may judge between them, is to say, ‘We hear and obey.’ It is they who will ˹truly˺ succeed.” (24:51). The verse in this sūrah supplies the reasoning; that verse supplies the words. Together they describe a community that has decided in advance how it will receive a ruling it does not like, and that prior decision is what makes the submission in this verse something other than a defeat each time it occurs.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:37

> And ˹remember, O Prophet,˺ when you said to the one for whom Allah has done a favour and you ˹too˺ have done a favour, “Keep your wife and fear Allah,” while concealing within yourself what Allah was going to reveal. And ˹so˺ you were considering the people, whereas Allah was more worthy of your consideration. So when Zaid totally lost interest in ˹keeping˺ his wife, We gave her to you in marriage, so that there would be no blame on the believers for marrying the ex-wives of their adopted sons after their divorce. And Allah’s command is totally binding

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Man Who Received Two Favours**

Zayd ibn Ḥārithah is the man described by two favours in one sentence. God's favour was guidance into the religion, and the Prophet's favour was protection, manumission and years of care in his household. The verse does not name him, and the omission is instructive. What matters in the sentence is not his identity but his position: a man taught by revelation, raised by the messenger, and yet a man whose marriage was failing. The Book brings the two greatest advantages a person could hold into the same clause as his domestic difficulty, and in doing so it removes a common assumption that religious knowledge and personal stability arrive together. Zayd was taught by the best of teachers, in the best of houses, and his marriage still reached an end.

**Counsel Against a Divorce**

When he came with the intention of divorcing Zaynab, the Prophet told him to keep her and to be mindful of God. This is the Book's own attitude to divorce expressed as advice in a single case: the bond is not to be undone while it can still be held, and the husband is reminded that God is watching the decision. What the verse shows is a messenger intervening against a separation, at a time when he had no reason of his own to want the marriage preserved. He did not urge the reconciliation on behalf of himself, and the sentence does not record any argument beyond the two clauses. A lawgiver who tells a man to keep his wife is not arranging his own future, and the verse makes that clear by placing this counsel before everything that follows.

**What Was Concealed in the Heart**

The verse states that the Prophet was keeping something to himself that God was going to make known, and the commentators read the words in two ways. The stronger reading takes the clause at its word: what he concealed was the revelation that was coming, the fact that this marriage would end and that God would give Zaynab to him in marriage, as the verse itself later states. Some read it as a personal inclination that he did not wish to be seen, but that reading imports into the sentence what the sentence does not say, and it has difficulty with the phrase about God's own disclosure. What both readings retain is the picture of a man holding private knowledge under his own hesitation, deciding his conduct on what people would say rather than on what God had told him.

**Whose Disapproval He Was Weighing**

The rebuke is direct and it is addressed to the Prophet by name as the addressee of the whole verse. He had been weighing the reaction of the community — and there was much to weigh, because the custom of the Arabs treated a man's marriage to the former wife of his adopted son as incestuous, and this verse was removing that prohibition by force of example. The sentence measures the two audiences: the people whose disapproval could be imagined, and God whose knowledge is immediate. It does not say that the Prophet disobeyed; it says that his consideration was placed in the wrong order. Any teacher who must overturn a custom the community cherishes will recognise the pressure being described here, and any reader will recognise the arithmetic, which is why the verse is preserved in its severity.

**A Marriage That Ended a Fiction**

The purpose is stated in the verse itself. The Prophet's marriage to Zaynab made the ruling concrete, so that no believer after him would be blamed for marrying the former wife of a man he had adopted. The abolition had already been announced in general terms earlier in the chapter, when the Book denied that a claimed son is a son and ordered that children keep their fathers' names. A legal change of that magnitude required an example that the community could see and remember. The verse's own wording presents God as the party who arranged the marriage, and it confirms that what was happening was law being established through the life of the man who carried the law.

**A Book That Corrects Its Own Messenger**

The rebuke is in the passage, and that fact has been used by scholars and by later writers as an argument about the Book's origin. A text composed by a man to serve his own interests does not record its author's hesitation, place it in a permanent chapter, and describe it as the lesser consideration. The reproach stands in the middle of the account of the marriage it concerns, and it names the fear of people that attended it. Anyone who reads this verse after reading the account of the hypocrites and the waverers in the same sūrah will notice that the Book applies its standard to the Prophet first, and applies it in the plainest language, before it applies it to anyone else.

**Blame Removed, as With the Naming of Children**

The verse's purpose clause matches the ruling given a few verses earlier about adopted children: there is no blame on the believers in the matter of the ex-wives of adopted sons, just as there was no blame for what was done by mistake in the naming of those children (33:5). The chapter is dismantling a piece of social custom that had caused real harm, and it does so by removing the guilt attached to an arrangement rather than by punishing those who had kept it. The Book's method in this section is consistent: deny the fiction, protect the people affected, remove the blame, and leave the community with a rule it can live by.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:38

> There is no blame on the Prophet for doing what Allah has ordained for him. That has been the way of Allah with those ˹prophets˺ who had gone before. And Allah’s command has been firmly decreed

**Expanded Commentary**

**Acquittal After Rebuke**

The previous verse corrected the Prophet for weighing public opinion too heavily. This verse clears him of blame for the act that opinion had been about. The two sentences come from the same authority, and their order is the whole point: God may correct a servant and still vindicate him, and the correction does not convert the act into a sin. The Book's rebukes are precise. What was reproached was a hesitation in the heart, a consideration given to the wrong audience; what is acquitted is the step that followed, once the command had been made plain. Anyone who has feared that divine correction means condemnation will find here the distinction stated in a single verse: the rebuke measured an inner misplacement, and the acquittal measured an outward obedience.

**The Way of Allah With Those Before Him**

The phrase about the way of God, *sunnat Allāh*, is used throughout the Book for a settled pattern in how God deals with people, and here it is applied to a specific pattern among the prophets. Every messenger who carried a command that offended his people was obliged to act on it anyway. Noah built under mockery, Abraham broke idols and stood alone against his city, Moses confronted a court that had raised him, and Jesus was opposed by those who thought they owned the religion he was sent to correct. Each of them was charged with the same complaint: that what he did disregarded the custom the community had inherited. The verse gives the Prophet of Medina the company of those men, and the company does what company does — it makes an isolated decision look like a tradition.

**The Job of a Messenger, Not a Negotiation**

The sentence implies a definition of the prophetic role. A messenger is not a delegate sent to secure the best terms available from his people; he is a servant who delivers what he has been charged with, in the form it was given. The Book states the same in the verse that follows this one, describing those who deliver the messages of God and fear Him rather than fearing anyone else. The shape of the role explains the absence of blame in this verse. If the task is delivery, then the offence being feared — the disapproval of a tribe, the anger of a family, the loss of standing in a city — belongs to a conversation the messenger was not sent to have. He is answerable to the sender, and the sender's assessment is what this verse reports.

**What the Fear of Blame Costs a Community**

The chapter has already shown what the fear of public opinion produces when it is allowed to run: excuses, quiet withdrawals, and speeches about retreat. Those were failures in the ranks. Here the same pressure is shown operating on the Prophet, and it is answered not with resolve but with a reminder of whose assessment matters. That is the community's lesson as well as his. A believer who is weighing what his neighbours will say about a decision has placed an audience where only God belongs, and the sūrah illustrates both the cost and the remedy in the same passage. Correction arrives; then the act stands; then the pattern is named as God's way with everyone who carried the same charge before.

**A Command Firmly Decreed**

The verse closes with a statement about the decree, and the Arabic uses a construction that intensifies the idea of fixity: what God commands is settled, measured out and certain to occur. The marriage that the community was going to object to was not an improvisation by a man in a difficult position; it was a command from the same register the sūrah invoked when it described inheritance as decreed in the Record. Everything the world was about to say about the matter had already been weighed on the side where the matter was settled. What the sentence gives the Prophet, and every reader after him, is the ground on which an unpopular obedience can be carried out: not confidence in the reception, but certainty about the instruction.

**A Way That Never Changes**

The Book returns to the same phrase later in this sūrah, and the repetition turns a description into a warning: “That was Allah's way with those ˹hypocrites˺ who have gone before. And you will find no change in Allah's way.” (33:62). The two passages use one word for two groups. For those who carry the message, God's way is a long precedent of commands that cost something. For those who resist it, God's way is a long precedent of consequences that arrive. Reading the two verses together shows how the sūrah builds its case about decision: nobody in this world acts without joining a pattern, and the only choice available is which pattern to stand in.

**What a Precedent Is Worth to a Person Facing a Crowd**

The community of Medina was learning what it cost to disagree with its own inherited customs, and it had few examples to look at apart from its own very recent history. This verse hands it a much longer one. When the Prophet acted on a command that his city disliked, he joined a queue that stretched back through Moses and Abraham to Noah, and every person in that queue had done the same thing under the same objection. Precedent of this kind is a practical resource rather than a compliment. A man who knows that others have paid the same price for the same obedience is harder to isolate, and a community that knows it is engaged in something older than its own disputes is harder to shame into silence. That is part of what the verse is doing by naming God's way with those who came before.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:39

> ˹That is His way with˺ those ˹prophets˺ who deliver the messages of Allah, and consider Him, and none but Allah. And sufficient is Allah as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Description That Defines the Office**

This verse describes a class of people by three activities: they deliver the messages of God, they hold Him in reverence, and their reverence is directed at Him alone. The construction is a definition, and the omissions in it are as deliberate as the items. Nothing is said about eloquence, lineage, miracles, personal magnetism or numbers of followers. The prophets are identified by a task and by a loyalty, and the two are connected: the reason the delivery can be completed is that the fear which might have stopped it has been placed in the right hands. A man who is afraid of an audience will edit a message. A man whose reverence is reserved for God has nothing left in that audience to fear.

**Delivery Is the Whole Charge**

The Book states the job of the messenger elsewhere with a threat attached: “O Messenger! Convey everything revealed to you from your Lord. If you do not, then you have not delivered His message. Allah will ˹certainly˺ protect you from the people. Indeed, Allah does not guide the people who disbelieve.” (5:67). Two things follow from that sentence. The first is that the mission has one essential requirement, and it is the completion of the delivery rather than the acceptance of the listeners. The second is that the protection promised is attached to the delivery, so a messenger who shortens the message to protect himself has forfeited the guarantee by the same act. The verse in this sūrah uses the same verb, and it makes the same identification of a prophet's worth with the integrity of what he passes on.

**Reverence With Only One Object**

The phrase rendered here as fearing none but God is the hinge of the verse. The Book elsewhere asks a question that exposes the real choice: about an enemy that had broken oaths and plotted to expel the Prophet, it asks, “Do you fear them? Allah is more deserving of your fear, if you are ˹true˺ believers.” (9:13). The question is not whether a threat is real; the threat was entirely real. The question is where the emotion is finally placed, since fear that is distributed among creatures will always be decided by whichever creature is nearest and loudest. The prophets are described here as men in whom that distribution had been settled, which is why they could stand in front of kings and crowds armed with nothing but what they had been told to say.

**The Reckoner Who Is Vigilant**

The verse closes with an assurance rather than a command: God is sufficient as a vigilant Reckoner. The name describes a being who keeps account, and the adjective attached to it adds the sense of constant observation. The two together address the situation the verse has just described. A messenger who has rejected the approval of his people has no reward left in this world, and the sentence tells him where the account is kept. It also tells him who is watching the delivery: not only the audience, whose opinion he has stopped consulting, but the One whose reckoning is the reason he is doing the work. The Book uses this name for God in several chapters, and its function is always the same, to move the human need for recognition from the crowd to the One who actually records.

**The Small Band Who Carried It**

History in the Book is not written by majorities. The prophets described in this verse were, in most cases, men with small followings: Noah's people were destroyed and the Book records that only a few believed with him (11:40), and the same pattern of rejection is repeated for messenger after messenger. The Book states the general rule that a messenger was sent to every community and that some were guided while others were destined to stray (16:36). A community reading this verse in Medina, having just survived a siege with the loss of a few men, knew what it meant to be outnumbered. What the verse tells them is that the size of the audience has never been the measure of the work, and it locates the measure in the Reckoner.

**Why the Community Needed This Verse**

The temptation that runs through this sūrah is the temptation to trim the message for the sake of the audience. Verse 1 forbade obedience to the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and verse 48 repeats the prohibition at the end of the chapter. Between them, the narrative shows what allowing public opinion to steer a decision looks like: a prophet hesitating over a marriage the community would criticise, and a community listening to men who talked of retreat. This verse supplies the alternative picture in three clauses, and it presents the prophets as a body of men who settled the question of whose approval mattered before the difficulty arrived.

**The Reckoning That Cannot Be Influenced**

The closing name answers a practical fear. Human assessments of a person's work can be managed: reputations can be built, allies recruited, the record of a deed shaped by whoever repeats it loudest. The Book states that the prophets who carried their messages did so with an audience of one, and that the account of what they delivered is kept by a being who requires no testimony and accepts no version of events other than the accurate one. For a community that had just lived through a siege in which the loudest voices belonged to men who had done the least, that statement is a remedy as well as a warning. The Reckoner is vigilant, which means no one's contribution is lost and no one's account is padded. The verse leaves the verdict where no faction can reach it.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:40

> Muḥammad is not the father of any of your men, but is the Messenger of Allah and the seal of the prophets. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fact That Closed an Argument**

The verse states a plain fact about a man's household and uses it to settle a legal question. Muḥammad was not the biological father of any adult man in the community. His sons did not live to adulthood, and Zayd ibn Ḥārithah, whom he had declared his son by the custom of the time, kept the Prophet's name for a period before the revelation ended that practice. Everything the community had been told about adoption, naming and inheritance in the opening verses of this sūrah converges on this sentence. The Prophet has no lineage to transmit through an adopted son, and therefore no man can claim a legal relationship to him on that basis. The argument is closed at the root rather than at the branch.

**Messenger Before Father**

The verse sets the two identities in order. It denies the paternal claim and, in the same breath, affirms the office: he is the Messenger of God. This ordering prevents a misunderstanding that the previous verse's discussion of marriage could have produced. The Prophet's authority in the community does not run through a family line, and his household does not become a dynasty; it runs through a message he carries. The distinction had practical consequences within a generation, when questions about leadership after his death were settled by reference to the community and its agreement rather than by descent. The sūrah's arrangement of these two clauses explains why the Book never develops a priestly line around the Prophet's house, however much it honours that house.

**The Seal of the Prophets**

The title at the centre of the verse, *khātam al-nabiyyīn*, comes from the language of sealing. A seal closes a document and marks it as complete, and the word can be read with either of the two vowel patterns that Arabic allows, giving the sense of the seal itself or of the one who seals. Both point the same way: with him the line reaches its end. The Book elsewhere calls the Prophet a warner for all people and a mercy to the worlds, and the title here completes the picture by closing the series rather than adding to it. What the community receives from him is not one instalment of a continuing revelation but the final document of the series, which is why the Book and the practice of the Prophet together become the community's whole inheritance.

**The Report That Fixed the Meaning**

The community did not have to infer the meaning of the title from grammar. A well-known report preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī records the Prophet speaking to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib when he left him behind during the Tabūk expedition: 'Will you not be pleased that you will be to me like Aaron to Moses? But there will be no prophet after me.' (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4416; the same statement is recorded by Muslim). The sentence explains the title in the Prophet's own words, and it was understood that way by every generation that received it. Claims to prophethood after him were therefore not assessed as matters of opinion. They were measured against a statement made by the man whose title was being claimed.

**What the Doctrine Guards**

The doctrine has consequences beyond the biography of one man. If revelation continues, then the community's scripture is open and its law can be revised by anyone who reports a new message; no ruling could ever be final and no agreement could ever be secure. The title closes that door, and the Book attaches it to a statement about God's knowledge of all things, which is a fitting close: nothing that was needed for guidance after him went unprovided, and nothing that pretends to continue the line can claim God was unaware of it. The verse's ending tells the community that the completion of prophethood is itself an act of knowledge rather than a gap left unaddressed.

**A Messenger Like Those Before Him**

The Book insists that this final messenger belongs to the human series rather than standing outside it: “Muḥammad is no more than a messenger; other messengers have gone before him. If he were to die or to be killed, would you regress into disbelief? Those who do so will not harm Allah whatsoever. And Allah will reward those who are grateful.” (3:144). The two verses hold each other in balance. One closes the line and announces the seal; the other places the seal-bearer inside the ordinary conditions of human life, with predecessors, a death that will come, and followers who are responsible for their own response.

**What the Community Gained From the Seal**

The finality announced in this verse is not only a boundary drawn after the Prophet; it is a possession handed to the community. If the line of messengers ended, then the guidance available to the believers is complete, and no generation needs to wait for a further message before it can know how to live. The rulings that this sūrah lays down about marriage, inheritance, household conduct and war are therefore not provisional measures awaiting revision by a later prophet. They are the finished form of the instruction, and the community's task is to understand and apply them rather than to anticipate their replacement. The same doctrine makes the community responsible for what it has received. Nothing further is coming, so nothing can be deferred on the ground that a future messenger will settle the difficult questions. The knowledge named at the end of the verse is not a general attribute mentioned to fill a line; it is attached to the specific matter under discussion, the closing of the prophetic line and the completion of what the community was given to live by. The same knowledge that recorded every messenger who came before him recorded the seal, and the community that lives after him lives inside a guidance whose author knew in advance what it would need.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:41

> O believers! Always remember Allah often

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command With No Fixed Hour**

The verse is short and its brevity carries the whole instruction: remember God, and do it much. There is no appointed time, no formula specified, and no place named. The Book attaches this activity to no ritual, which is precisely how it becomes constant. Fasting has a month, prayer has hours, alms has a reckoning day, pilgrimage has a season — but this command has no boundary, so it can be carried through a working day, a journey, a marketplace and a night watch. The address to the believers in the plural makes it a shared practice rather than a private devotion, and the adjective that specifies the quantity, often, tells the reader that the requirement is not satisfied by a single act.

**What Remembrance Includes**

Commentators described *dhikr* as having three levels that reinforce each other: words spoken with the tongue, attention held in the heart, and conduct that follows from both. The three belong together because any one of them alone can be counterfeited. A tongue can repeat a phrase while the mind is elsewhere; a heart can turn toward God in a private moment and produce nothing afterwards; conduct can be orderly for reasons that have nothing to do with God. The command in this verse asks for the whole apparatus. It assumes that awareness of God is something a person can practise, so that it eventually stops being dependent on the mood of the moment, and that assumption is what makes the instruction realistic rather than aspirational.

**Remembering in Every Posture**

The Book describes the practice as something that fits every physical situation: “When the prayers are over, remember Allah—whether you are standing, sitting, or lying down. But when you are secure, establish regular prayers. Indeed, performing prayers is a duty on the believers at the appointed times.” (4:103). The sentence lists the three postures of ordinary human life and takes for granted that a person can engage in remembrance in all of them. This is a teaching about accessibility: the practice requires no building, no physical purity beyond the ordinary, no other person, and no unhurried hour. It can be done by a man walking through the market, by a woman working at home, and by a sick person who is lying down and able to do very little else.

**The Parable of the Living and the Dead**

The Prophet gave this practice a measure with an image recorded in the two most trusted collections. Abū Mūsā reported that he said: 'The parable of one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not remember is that of the living and the dead.' (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6407; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 779). The comparison is not between two levels of virtue but between existence and its absence. As the image might also be applied to houses, a house where God is mentioned is inhabited and one where He is not is vacant. For a community that had just been sieged, frightened and divided, the statement explains why the Book keeps returning to this command: awareness of God is not an ornament on the believers' life; it is what makes the life present.

**Why the Command Is Placed Here**

The sūrah has just completed a run of rulings about marriage, adoption, inheritance, the Prophet's household, and the sealing of prophethood. It now supplies a practice that holds a person steady through everything those rulings require. The list of virtues in verse 35 also closed with this item, remembering God often, as the practice that sustains the rest of the list, and the sūrah returns to it here as a command rather than a description. The placement is practical. Households being reorganised, customs being abolished and commands being obeyed at a cost all depend on people who are in the habit of keeping God present, because the habit is what makes obedience possible in the moments when nothing else supports it.

**The Practice That Produces Its Own Comfort**

The Book describes a result of this practice that makes it unlike ordinary duty: “those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort.” (13:28). The instruction in this sūrah is a command; the other verse describes a return. A practice ordered by God and rewarded by tranquility turns out not to be an extra burden placed on a busy life but the remedy for what a busy life produces. A community that had spent a winter frightened, hungry and uncertain of its neighbours had more need of that remedy than it knew.

**A Practice With No Fixed Formula**

The Book leaves the content of remembrance open, and the Prophet's own teaching filled that space with short phrases that a working person can carry: declaring God's freedom from every fault, praising Him, affirming His unity, and magnifying Him. The community that heard this command did not receive a liturgy with set times for this particular practice, and the omission is a mercy for people with irregular lives — a woman nursing an infant, a man on a night watch, a merchant in a market that never closes. What the verse demands is quantity and continuity, not a text. The community's health could be measured by whether its members kept God in mind while doing everything else, and no regulation could do that work for them. The Book does not treat the practice as the property of specialists. It is commanded to the whole community in the plural, described as the closing entry in a list that includes fasting and charity, and attached to no ceremony that would require a person to be in a particular place. That is why the practice could survive in the life of the Prophet's household through years of poverty, siege and dispute, and why the community was told to expect that it would find comfort in the practice rather than in the removal of its difficulties.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:42

> and glorify Him morning and evening

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Ends of the Day**

The command names two times, the beginning of the day and its close, and between them it leaves the rest of the hours to ordinary life. The Arabic words describe the cool of the early morning and the fading of the afternoon light, the two periods when the sun's dominance is weakest and the air changes. Choosing those two moments gives the practice a shape that a working community can hold: the day opens with an act of worship and closes with one, and the middle belongs to work, trade, travel, family and all the business the Book elsewhere commands people to attend to. The instruction is not that a believer should do nothing else. It is that the busiest stretch of the day should be bracketed.

**Glorification as Its Own Act**

What is commanded here is not the general remembrance of the previous verse but its own distinct act: *tasbīḥ*, the declaration that God is free of every fault, deficiency and false description. The Arabic form of the verb intensifies the action, so the command is to glorify Him thoroughly and repeatedly. Glorification differs from petition, which asks for something, and from praise, which commends what God has done. It makes no request and reports no benefit; it simply affirms what God is, and it therefore remains available to a person who wants nothing and fears nothing at that moment. The Book attaches this act to the creation itself, and the verses that follow in this sūrah describe the angels and the whole order of things engaged in it continuously.

**The Times When Hearts Are Least Defended**

Practical wisdom runs alongside the command. The early morning is when a person's attention is still uncommitted, before the day's demands have taken hold, and the late afternoon is when fatigue and unfinished business press hardest. The Book places the practice at the two points where a person is weakest. Those are also the two points of transition — from sleep to activity, and from activity to rest — and it is in transitions that a person's direction is most easily lost. The community that prays at dawn and glorifies at dusk has placed its daily changes of state under a rule, and the rule outlasts the feelings that come and go within the day.

**A Rhythm the Whole Community Shares**

Before clocks, the hours were visible in the sky, and this instruction was tied to the same two periods that fixed the prayer times of the community. The result was a shared public rhythm: the same city rising to worship and the same city quietening to it, without anyone needing to be told the time. The sūrah has been occupied, since its opening verses, with a community whose internal divisions had become dangerous, and here it prescribes a practice that runs across those divisions. Whatever a man's tribe, wealth or standing in the argument, he is glorifying God at the same hours as his neighbour, and the visible agreement of the city does some of the work that no ruling can do on its own.

**A Command That Reaches the Household Too**

The section just closed gave rulings about the Prophet's household: how its women were to speak, sit, pray and remember what was recited in their homes. This verse and the one before it address the believers at large, but they are placed immediately after that passage, and the placement matters. The households that had just been reorganised were to be sustained by the same practice the whole community was given, and the woman in the Prophet's house glorifying God at the two ends of her day was doing the same thing as the guard in the trench and the trader in the market. The Book's remedy for the pressures it describes is not an additional rule for the people who have already been given many; it is a practice shared by everyone.

**Remembrance in the Middle of Work**

The Book does not leave the practice inside the mosque. In another chapter it dismisses the community after prayer with an instruction to enter the world and continues the command there: “Once the prayer is over, disperse throughout the land and seek the bounty of Allah. And remember Allah often so you may be successful.” (62:10). Reading the two passages side by side shows the shape the community was given: worship at the appointed times, serious work in between, and a heart that carries the remembrance into the work rather than putting it down at the door. The command in this sūrah supplies the frame; that verse supplies the seam, and together they describe a day that has no interval in which a person belongs to something other than God.

**What a Community Loses When the Habit Slips**

The instruction is easy to keep for a week and easy to lose over a year. Nothing in ordinary life punishes a person for abandoning it: no fine, no witness, no visible loss of standing in the market. That is why the Book keeps returning to it as a command rather than leaving it as advice, and why the sūrah's earlier passage about the Prophet's wives describes the practice inside the home as a matter of remembrance and recitation that the household was to keep alive (33:34). A city whose mosques are full at the appointed hours but whose days begin and end without a single act of glorification has kept the structure and lost the habit, and the Book's persistent emphasis on this practice suggests that the habit is the part that holds everything else. The practice also marks the difference between a community that shares its hours and one that merely shares a creed, since the two ends of the day were the same for every household in the city.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:43

> He is the One Who showers His blessings upon you—and His angels pray for you—so that He may bring you out of darkness and into light. For He is ever Merciful to the believers

**Expanded Commentary**

**God's Blessing Upon a Whole Community**

The verse describes God engaged in an ongoing act of blessing directed at the believers, and the construction indicates continuation: this is what He does, not what He once did. The word used for God's blessing, *ṣalāt*, is the same word the Book uses for the ritual prayer of creatures, and the theologians were careful to distinguish the meanings. God's ṣalāt is His mercy, His commendation and His care for the one blessed; the ṣalāt of a human being or an angel is an act of worship offered to God. Reading the same word in two registers is possible because the Arabic uses one root for a relationship of concern, but the point of the verse is the direction of the traffic: blessings are moving from God toward the believers, and they did nothing to begin that movement.

**The Angels Who Pray for You**

In the same sentence, the angels are said to be praying for the believers. This is not a metaphor in the Qur'an's usage; the Book records the content of that prayer elsewhere. It describes the Throne-bearers and those around them glorifying their Lord, believing in Him, and seeking forgiveness for the believers (40:7), and it states of the angels generally: “The heavens nearly burst, one above the other, ˹in awe of Him˺. And the angels glorify the praises of their Lord, and seek forgiveness for those on earth. Surely Allah alone is the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” (42:5). The prayer of the angels is therefore a request for forgiveness, and the community receiving this verse is being told that an unseen order of creation has been asking God to pardon them. Nothing in the passage suggests that these beings were asked by anyone to do it.

**The Parallel With the Prophet's Own Blessing**

The sūrah later commands the believers to invoke blessings on the Prophet and to greet him with peace, and it introduces that command with the same construction used here: God showers His blessings upon him, and the angels pray for him (33:56). The echo places the community and its messenger in a comparable relationship to God's mercy. The Prophet is not more distant from the blessings of God than the believers are, and the believers are not excluded from the phrase. In a chapter that has just spoken of the Prophet's household, his marriages and his title as the seal of the prophets, the sentence restores the balance: the blessing of God reaches all of them and does not travel along a ladder of rank.

**Out of Darkness Into Light**

The purpose clause explains what the blessing does: God is bringing the believers out of darkness and into light. The Book uses the same image in Sūrah al-Baqarah, where the two guardianships are contrasted: “Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light. As for the disbelievers, their guardians are false gods who lead them out of light and into darkness. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.” (2:257). The metaphor in the present verse has a specific force for its first audience. The darkness they knew was the darkness of a besieged city: not knowing whether the alliance would return, whether their own neighbours were trustworthy, and whether their community would survive. The verse names the remedy as knowledge and guidance rather than as safety, because ignorance, not danger, is the darkness the Book is talking about.

**Mercy That Does Not Fluctuate**

The verse closes by naming God as ever merciful to the believers, and the word used for mercy carries the sense of a settled quality rather than an occasional response. This is the third element of the sentence, and it explains the first two. God's blessing and the angels' prayer are not reactions to good behaviour that might change with the next report from the front. They flow from an attribute that the verse says is constant, and they continue through precisely the period in which the community's conduct included wavering, defeatism and excuses. The mercy spoken of here is not a verdict on the community's performance but a description of what God is toward those who believe, and it continued while they were being corrected.

**What the Sentence Offers a Tired Community**

The verses that surround this one contain commands about how to speak, how to sit, what to remember and what to leave alone. Preceded by an instruction to glorify God at both ends of the day, this sentence tells the believers what is happening around them and above them while they carry it out. The community that had watched ten thousand men arrive and leave, that had buried its dead and argued with its waverers, is told that the blessings on it have never stopped and that a whole order of beings it cannot see has been asking for its forgiveness. The verse gives no timetable and describes no visible change in the city; it describes a set of facts about the unseen, which is exactly what a community under pressure needs to be told.

**What the Verse Does Not Promise**

Nothing in the sentence says the darkness will lift at once. The community addressed here still had a ruined harvest, still had men recovering from wounds, still had neighbours who had not been trusted since the siege. What the Book promises is that God is bringing the believers out, not that the road would be short, and the two are different claims. A reader who expects the blessing to appear as an immediate improvement in conditions will be disappointed by his own circumstances rather than by the verse; a reader who understands the sentence as a statement about the direction the community is being moved in will find it holds true even in the worst weeks. The darkness named here is not the absence of hardship but the absence of guidance, and the verse says that the second is being removed.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:44

> Their greeting on the Day they meet Him will be, “Peace!” And He has prepared for them an honourable reward

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day They Meet Him**

The verse names the resurrection with a phrase about meeting rather than a phrase about judgement, and the choice of words sets the tone of the whole sentence. The Book uses this way of speaking repeatedly: the encounter with God is the horizon toward which the believer's life moves, and it is described as a meeting rather than merely as a hearing. That single word changes what the community is looking forward to. A legal session is something one endures; a meeting is something one anticipates. The verse assumes that the community's expectation of that day is a real motive in how they live now, and it tells them what the first thing to be exchanged at the meeting will be.

**A Greeting of Peace**

The greeting at that meeting is *salām*: peace. The Book describes the same word in the speech of Paradise elsewhere: “in which their prayer will be, ‘Glory be to You, O Allah!’ and their greeting will be, ‘Peace!’ and their closing prayer will be, ‘All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds!’” (10:10). Peace is also the greeting exchanged between believers in this world, and it is the word with which the Prophet was commanded to be saluted by the community (33:56). The Book's use of one term across all these settings makes the connection plain: the ordinary courtesy of a Muslim meeting a Muslim is a rehearsal in miniature of what awaits them at the meeting that matters.

**Why Peace Comes First**

The community receiving this verse had spent years in which peace was exactly what was missing. They had dug a trench, held a line, buried men killed by arrows, lived with hunger and watched a coalition come apart outside their walls. They had also been slandered, whispered about and discouraged from within. Against that record, the verse announces that the first words of the new life will be an assurance of safety, and that the assurance is being given by God. The order is deliberate. Before any reward is described, before any judgement is explained, the Book says that the thing the believers lacked most will be granted, and that it will be granted as a greeting from the One they were meeting.

**An Honourable Reward**

The second half of the sentence carries the Book's promise of provision, and the adjective attached to it is the same one used earlier in this sūrah for what God has prepared for the Prophet's wives who obey and do good: an honourable provision (33:31). The vocabulary is the vocabulary of dignity rather than of quantity. In a society where honour was the currency of a family's standing, the Book tells a community whose standing had been attacked that the reward prepared for them belongs to the category of noble things, and that it was prepared in advance rather than assembled at the meeting. Nothing in the verse measures the reward; the reader is told only that it has been made ready, which leaves the expectation in the hands of the One who made it.

**A Sentence Held Between Commands**

The verse sits between a passage about continuous remembrance and the account of the Prophet's mission that follows. The placement gives it a function: it explains why the commands before it are reasonable. A community is being asked to remember God often, to glorify Him at both ends of the day, and to keep its conduct in order, and this sentence states what is prepared for those who do. The Book's habit is to attach a description of the outcome to its instructions, and it does so here in two clauses that are short enough to be carried into a hard day's work: their greeting will be peace, and their reward has been prepared.

**A Promise That Also Warns**

The sentence holds a second edge, as the Book's promises usually do. The word describing the meeting carries no indication of who will find it welcome, and the greeting of peace is spoken to those who belong to the group being addressed, the believers. Everything the sūrah has said about those who discouraged the community, lost their nerve, or thought that the promise was a delusion points the same way. The verse does not need to describe the other outcome; it states the reward prepared for the people it names, and leaves the reader to work out which side of that sentence his own life is being written into.

**One Root Across the Sūrah**

The word for peace at the end, *salām*, belongs to the family of words this chapter has already used for other things. The believers who saw the coalition and recognised what had been promised to them were described as having their faith and their submission increased, and that submission is *taslīm*, from the same root. The greeting that the Prophet is later told to receive is also a form of this word, and the community's own name for itself, those who submit, comes from the same three letters. The sūrah therefore ends this short promise with the vocabulary of its own beginning: the people who surrendered their affairs to God will be met with the word that names what they did, and it will be spoken to them as a welcome rather than as a demand.

**The Greeting Practised Here Below**

The word spoken to the believers at the meeting is the same word they were taught to exchange among themselves, and the Book treats the earthly greeting as a duty with a reward attached: “And when you are greeted, respond with a better greeting or at least similarly. Surely Allah is a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner of all things.” (4:86). The instruction trains a community in the vocabulary of the world to come. A person who has spent thirty years returning greetings, improving on them where he can, and refusing to let a hostile neighbour's greeting go unanswered has been rehearsing the exchange that this verse describes, and the Reckoner named at the end of that verse is the same God who prepares the reward named at the end of this one. The practice is small and its destination is not.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:45

> O Prophet! We have sent you as a witness, and a deliverer of good news, and a warner

**Expanded Commentary**

**Titles Announced in a Chapter of Insults**

This verse arrives after the sūrah has handled household disputes, hostile rumours, defamatory talk and a siege. In that setting God tells the Prophet what he has been sent as, and the list is a set of job titles rather than a consolation. The community had been talking about him: some had doubted his promise, some had spread discouragement in his name, and his own household had needed rulings to settle its expectations. The Book answers none of that with a defence of his character. It restates his function. A man who has been sent to perform five tasks has less need to answer the opinions of people who have not been told to perform any of them.

**A Witness Whose Testimony Is a Person**

The first title makes the Prophet himself evidence. The Book describes the Day when a witness will be produced from every community and the Prophet will be brought as a witness concerning his own people (4:41), and it states the same in Sūrah al-Naḥl, where the Book is called an explanation of all things alongside the appointment of the Prophet as a witness (16:89). The weight of the title lies in what it makes possible: the community's knowledge of what God asked of it is not left to be reconstructed from general principles. It rests on a life that can be pointed to, so that the message and the man who carried it are joined and neither can be dismissed without the other.

**Good News Before Warning**

The order of the pair matters: the deliverer of good news is named before the warner. The Book often puts the two together, and it consistently gives the glad tidings the first place, which is a statement about the character of the message rather than about the sequence of events. A messenger sent only to threaten would be a different figure, and the Book is careful not to describe its own mission that way. What arrives first in the call is the possibility of a good outcome; the warning explains what would forfeit it. Anyone who has heard a preacher whose entire message was accusation will understand what the ordering protects, and any community that only threatens has stopped performing the first half of this instruction.

**What a Warner Does and Does Not Do**

The warning function is easy to misunderstand. A warner in the Book announces consequences, and the announcement is the whole of his method. He does not enforce the outcome, arrange the punishment, or take responsibility for the decisions of the people he speaks to. The Book is explicit that guidance is not in the messenger's hands, and the sūrah's own narrative shows the Prophet answering pressure with argument, patience and instructions to overlook insults rather than with coercion. Warning is therefore a form of respect: it treats the hearer as someone capable of hearing a consequence and choosing. What it does not do is convert the messenger into a judge in this world.

**The Titles Describe Work, Not Rank**

Every one of the five roles in these two verses is defined by what it does for other people. A witness serves a case, a bearer of glad tidings serves a hope, a warner serves a danger, a caller serves a road, and a lamp serves people who need to see where they are walking. Not one of them describes a privilege the Prophet holds for his own benefit. This is the constant pattern in the Book's treatment of him, and it explains why the sūrah could describe the community's claim on him in verse 6 as something heavier than their claim on themselves. What the believers received when they received this messenger was a set of services, and the titles are the inventory.

**The Same Job Description in Another Chapter**

The Book repeats the list almost word for word in Sūrah al-Fatḥ, where the same purposes are attached to the sending of the Prophet: “Indeed, ˹O Prophet,˺ We have sent you as a witness, a deliverer of good news, and a warner,” (48:8). The repetition of a formula is a sign of a settled understanding in the Book: these are not occasional descriptions composed for one context but the standing definition of the office. A community that reads both passages learns to measure claims about the Prophet against the list, and to measure itself against the list too, since the same chapter goes on to tell the believers what their share in his mission requires of them.

**What It Means for a Community to Be Witnessed**

A witness in a court does more than describe events; his presence establishes that the events will not be forgotten. The community that had this Prophet among them was witnessed in that sense for twenty-three years, with its conduct during a siege, its treatment of its own weak members and its handling of the Prophet's household all observed by a man who reported what he saw to God. The Book later tells the believers that they themselves are to be witnesses over humanity, their function mirroring the messenger's, so that the office does not end with him but continues in the community that carries his message. The title in this verse therefore describes both a privilege and a burden, and it explains why the sūrah can speak so directly about conduct that took place in tents and houses where nobody else was watching. The five titles in these two verses are not an honour roll but a description of the work that the community's survival depended on. The men and women who heard them understood that they had not been sent a king, a poet or a general, and that the man who had led them through the siege and buried their dead held his position on account of a service rather than a claim. That understanding shaped the community's expectations of authority for the rest of its existence.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:46

> and a caller to ˹the Way of˺ Allah by His command, and a beacon of light

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Caller by Command, Not by Ambition**

The calling described here is qualified by a phrase that carries the weight of the whole title: by His command. The Book elsewhere commands the Prophet to invite people to the way of his Lord with wisdom and good counsel (16:125), and it states in this verse that the authority for the invitation is not his own. The distinction protects the community from two errors at once. It prevents the messenger's personal standing from becoming the measure of the message, and it prevents his followers from treating the call as a movement founded by a man. The Prophet did not design a campaign and then look for a Lord to attach it to. He was given a commission, and the phrase in this verse states the source in the fewest possible words.

**The Lamp and Its Language**

The second image presents the Prophet as a *sirāj munīr*, an illuminating lamp. The Book uses this same word for the sun in two other places, describing it among the constellations the Creator has set in the sky (25:61) and stating that God placed the sun as a radiant lamp (71:16). The choice of that image for the Prophet is therefore not a casual metaphor about brightness. It places him inside the order of things through which God gives light, and it makes the function of the message visible: something that allows a person to see where he is and where he is going, rather than something that adds to what he already knew.

**Light and the Book**

The Book elsewhere assigns the light to the revelation itself, and this is where the two images must be held together. It announces to the People of the Book that there has come to them from God a light and a clear Book (5:15), and it says of the revelation given to the Prophet: “But We have made it a light, by which We guide whoever We will of Our servants.” (42:52). The Prophet is described as an illuminating lamp, and the Book he carries is described as the light. The relationship is the relationship of a lamp to what it holds: the light belongs to the revelation, and the Prophet's function is to make it visible to people who would otherwise be walking in the dark.

**The Sūrah's Own Darkness**

The image is not abstract in this chapter. The verse immediately before this one describes God bringing the believers out of darkness and into light (33:43), and the passage before that describes a community that had lost sight of the outcome, spread rumours in the dark and doubted a promise. The community knew what darkness meant in plain terms: a night watch on a trench line, an alliance of ten thousand men, and no way to see whether the morning would make things better. The verse tells them that the lamp is standing in their midst, in the person of the man they had been arguing about, and that the light by which they could see their way was already among them.

**What the Call Requires of Those Who Hear It**

The two images put an obligation on the hearer. A caller can be ignored; a lamp is harder to argue with, because it reveals a road that is either there or not. The Book's punishment for communities that rejected their messengers is regularly described in terms of what they refused to see rather than what they failed to know, and the image in this verse explains that way of speaking: guidance is not a proposition to be debated but a light that shows where the ground is. The only response that makes sense of a lamp is to walk by it, and the sūrah's later instructions about conduct, speech and family life are the ordinary form that this walking takes.

**Two Images, One Direction**

The two halves of the verse work together as a single commission: the caller speaks, the lamp shows. Both are oriented away from the Prophet and toward the road. Neither image invites attention to the messenger himself; the calling is directed to God's way, and the lamp illuminates the ground other people have to cross. A community that fixated on the man rather than on what he was pointing at would have misunderstood both images at once, which is why the Book keeps attaching the titles to their function rather than to the person holding them.

**A Lamp Does Not Argue With the Room**

Light has a quality that suits the instruction of this verse: it works whether or not anyone acknowledges it. A lamp that is lit shows the shape of the ground to a man who is grateful for it and to a man who resents it equally, and it does not dim itself when the room is hostile. The Book's portrait of the Prophet's mission works the same way. He announced, warned, called and illuminated, and the response belonged to the hearers; some accepted and some plotted, and the light continued to be light throughout. The verse therefore offers the community a way of understanding its own obligation after him: the message is carried by people who may be ignored, mocked or worse, and its value does not depend on the verdict of the room it is carried into. The Book describes the Prophet's mission as a mercy addressed to all the worlds, and a lamp serves an unbounded circle in a way that a lamp lit for one household cannot. Nothing in the image restricts the light to the people who happened to be standing nearest it, which is why the community that carried the message afterwards was expected to take it to people who had never met the man who first held it. The image sets the scope of the work as well as its method.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:47

> Give good news to the believers that they will have a great bounty from Allah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command to Announce Good News**

The previous verse listed the titles of the Prophet, and the first of them after the office of witness was the deliverer of good news. This verse turns that title into an instruction: now go and deliver it. The Book frequently gives the same pattern, naming what a messenger is and then commanding the act, and the effect is to keep the community's hope attached to a task that is actually performed. Good tidings in the Book are not a mood that arises when circumstances improve. They are an announcement made by a person who has been told to make it, which means the community's expectation rests on a message rather than on the state of the harvest or the walls.

**Who Receives the Announcement**

The instruction names the believers as its recipients, and the specification does work in this sūrah. The chapter has spent its length distinguishing between people who believed and people who held the community's profession without its substance. It has described the waverers, the secret discouragers, the men who wanted shelter among the nomads. The promise announced here belongs to those who believe, and the Book leaves the boundary where it has placed it throughout the chapter. This is not a restriction added at the end; it is the same division the siege made visible, restated as a promise rather than a warning.

**The Bounty That Is Not Itemised**

What they are promised is described only as a great bounty from God. The Book uses the language of bounty, *faḍl*, for what God gives beyond what is owed, and the word is deliberately open: it covers provision in this life, protection, forgiveness, and the reward of the Hereafter, without committing itself to a list. Earlier in this sūrah the same word appears in the warning to those who desired the world, and the contrast is instructive. What the believers are told to expect is not a payment for services rendered but a generosity from outside the accounts. The community that had given up comfort, safety and, in some cases, their lives had a right to ask what they were getting, and the answer names a giver rather than an amount.

**The Bearer's Confidence**

The command tells the Prophet to give this news, and the placement explains why he could. He had just been described as one who had been sent as a bearer of good tidings, and the instruction here makes him the agent of what the title claimed. A man told to announce a bounty from God speaks with the certainty of the one who issued it, and the Book has already established, only a few verses earlier, that the Prophet's consideration belongs to God rather than to the people who would hear him. Glad tidings therefore arrive in the community's hardest hours from a source that cannot be embarrassed by the circumstances into which it speaks them.

**Why Hope Had to Be Issued as an Order**

The community addressed by these verses had been frightened, hungry and divided. Some of its members had begun to calculate whether the promise was a delusion, and others had gone looking for safer ground. In that state, the Book does not merely allow hope; it commands that hope be announced, firmly and publicly. Rumours of failure travel fast in a frightened city, and the Book answers with a counter-announcement whose authority is not the same as the rumours'. The instruction is therefore both a kindness and a strategy, and the community's morale becomes the subject of a divine order rather than a matter to be managed quietly by anxious people.

**What the Verse Leaves Unsaid**

Nothing in the sentence fixes a time, a place or a form. The believers are not told that the bounty will arrive this year, that it will solve the problem they are worried about, or that it will be visible to their neighbours. The Book's promises of this kind are consistently unlimited in scope and undated in delivery, and the omission is what allows them to be uttered in a besieged city without turning into a claim that events will immediately falsify. What the community is asked to believe is that God has a great bounty prepared, and the community that believes that is free to live and work as though the outcome belongs to Someone else.

**The Announcement That God Himself Confirms**

The Book describes the same bounty in another chapter in more detail, and the two passages belong together: “Their Lord gives them good news of His mercy, pleasure, and Gardens with everlasting bliss,” (9:21). That verse names three things — mercy, God's own pleasure, and gardens that do not end — and this verse announces the same gift in the form of a single open word. Read together, they show how the Book handles the community's hope: it tells the believers that God is pleased with them, that His mercy is theirs, and that what they have been given is not a temporary arrangement, and it leaves the size of it to the One who prepared it.

**Hope as Something the Community Works With**

Good news is a resource in the Book, not a decoration added after the work. The community had just been through months in which the visible evidence pointed away from its survival, and the Book answers the evidence with a statement about a bounty from God that had already been arranged. That order matters for how the passage is meant to function: the announcement is issued before the reward is received, precisely so that the receiving community can keep working. The Book describes its own messenger as one who gives glad tidings before it describes him as a warner, and it makes the announcement a duty here rather than an encouragement. A community without hope in the promise cannot hold a trench, pay its alms or keep its household rules, and the verse treats the supply of hope as one of the messenger's essential services.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:48

> Do not yield to the disbelievers and the hypocrites. Overlook their annoyances, and put your trust in Allah. For Allah is sufficient as a Trustee of Affairs

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Opening of the Chapter, Returned**

The first verse of this sūrah told the Prophet to be mindful of God and not to yield to the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and the third told him to put his trust in God, who is sufficient as a Trustee. Nearly all of that language returns here, at the point where the sūrah's long middle section has finished. The repetition frames the chapter, and the effect is deliberate: a reader who has just gone through rulings on marriage, inheritance, adoption, modesty and household conduct is brought back to the instruction with which he started. Everything in between is presented as the working out of these few commands, and the Book closes the frame before it moves to its final movement.

**Overlooking Annoyance as an Added Demand**

The sentence adds something the opening verses did not state: overlook their annoyances. The command does not merely refuse a claim of authority; it asks for a specific kind of restraint toward people who are being deliberately irritating. The Arabic word covers the petty, repeated provocations that a hostile party can produce in a small community — a jibe in the market, a rumour repeated with a smile, a question asked to humiliate. Responding to each one would consume the Prophet's time and the community's temper, and the Book forbids the response without pretending the provocation is not happening. This is the harder instruction, since refusing to obey someone is a single decision while overlooking their daily behaviour is a discipline maintained over years.

**Restraint That Is Not Weakness**

The two clauses must be read together, because either one without the other distorts the instruction. The Book forbids yielding and forbids retaliation at the same time, which means the community is not permitted to use the second command as a cover for abandoning the first. A leader who concedes a matter of doctrine to buy peace has failed the verse; so has one who answers an insult with violence he was not authorised to use. What is described is a specific posture that the community had to learn: firm on the substance of the message, unmoved by provocation, and free of the need to win every exchange.

**Trust, Repeated Without Apology**

The verse then returns to trust, and it ends with the same name of God that concluded the third verse of the chapter. The repetition is not padding; it is an answer to a real question. A community forbidden from defending its honour in the ordinary way, and forbidden from buying safety with concessions, will want to know what protects it. The answer given here is a trustee, a *wakīl*, and the Book has used that title for God in this sūrah already, along with a dozen other places. What the community is being offered is not a plan for self-preservation but the assurance that its affairs are being managed by someone competent to manage them.

**The Two Groups in the Chapter's Last Movement**

The disbelievers and the hypocrites appear throughout this sūrah as two different pressures from two different directions. The disbelievers are the outside force — the Quraysh, the confederates, the tribes that marched against Medina. The hypocrites are the internal problem: men inside the community who discouraged, deserted and profited. Both are named again in the sūrah's final verses, where the Book describes the punishment of hypocrite men and women together with the polytheists, and the mercy extended to believing men and women. The command here is addressed to the middle of that story, at the point where the community was deciding how to live with two groups it could not convert and could not expel.

**Why This Instruction Belongs at the End**

The Book does not place the command to overlook annoyance at the beginning, when the community was fresh and angry, or in the middle of the siege, when every hour required a decision. It places it at the point of consolidation, when the danger had passed and the community had the leisure to develop grudges. Communities generally survive their battles and then damage themselves in the years afterwards, and the sūrah anticipates that sequence: the siege ends, the rules are given, and then the command arrives to keep the community from spending the peace on grievance. The verses that follow will address the etiquette of visiting the Prophet's house and the sin of offending believers unjustly, both of which belong to the same lesson.

**The Two Commands as They Were Lived**

The Prophet's conduct during and after the siege is the working example of the posture this verse requires. He refused the concessions that were sought from him, and he also refused to retaliate against the men who insulted him, the delegations that bargained with him and the neighbours who spread stories about his household. When a group of the hypocrites came to him with a request to leave the city, the Book records his knowledge of their motive but nothing about punishment, and when the sūrah later speaks of those who offend the Prophet, it threatens a consequence in the Hereafter rather than authorising private reprisal. The community watched that combination for years and was learning it from him: firm on the substance, calm in the face of provocation, and confident that the outcome would be settled by a trustee better placed than any of them. What the community learned from this verse was a discipline that no rule could enforce directly, since it concerned the interior response to provocation rather than the act itself. It had seen its leader insulted, its household discussed and its message dismissed by men it lived among, and it had seen no campaign of reprisal against them. The community's later history kept returning to that example, and its jurists built on it a body of guidance about patience, restraint and the difference between defending the religion and defending one's own temper.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:49

> O believers! If you marry believing women and then divorce them before you touch them, they will have no waiting period for you to count, so give them a ˹suitable˺ compensation, and let them go graciously

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ruling and the Reason for It**

The waiting period after divorce exists in the Book to settle a question: whether the woman is carrying a child, and whether a reconciliation is still possible. Where the marriage has not been consummated, neither question arises, and the verse states the consequence plainly — there is no period for the husband to count, and the woman is free at once. The logic is the Book's usual method: it identifies the harm a rule prevents, and where the harm cannot occur, the rule does not apply. The ruling also protects the woman from being kept in a state of suspension for no purpose, since a waiting period in such a case would delay her freedom without serving any of the interests the waiting period was built for.

**A Gift Attached to the Departure**

The verse does not simply release her; it requires that she be given something. The compensation, known as the gift of divorce, is a payment the husband owes to the woman he is leaving, and the Book regulates its two cases elsewhere: where the marriage ends before any dowry was fixed, the man gives according to his means, and the Book calls this a duty on those who do good (2:236); where a dowry was agreed, the wife is entitled to half of it unless she waives it or the husband pays in full (2:237). What the Book will not permit is a divorce that leaves the woman with nothing and no acknowledgement. The gift is not compensation for a wrong; it is the cost of an ending, paid by the party who chose it.

**"Let Them Go Graciously"**

The closing phrase uses the same words the sūrah used when it offered the Prophet's wives the option of leaving with a suitable settlement and an honourable release (33:28). The Book repeats this formula across its divorce rulings, and the repetition is the instruction: the manner of a separation is part of its legality. The Arabic describes a release free of resentment, without humiliation, without a public recital of grievances, and without withholding what is owed. The Book states the alternative in the same context: the husband must either retain his wife with honour or separate from her with grace (2:229). What the verse adds here is that the release applies to a marriage that hardly existed, which is where men are most tempted to treat the woman as a formality.

**A Rule That Protects the Woman's Standing**

The verse's addressee is the man who has just ended a marriage, and its purpose is to protect the woman's position in the community after the marriage is over. In a small town, a woman divorced after a short union carried a reputation into her next match, and the manner of the divorce affected her chances and her dignity. The Book addresses the two things that would damage her: a prolonged legal limbo and a bitter departure. It removes the first by cancelling the count and requires the second to be avoided by the gift and the gracious release. The ruling therefore does more than settle a technical case; it keeps a woman who has left a marriage from being marked by it.

**The Two Things the Verse Assumes**

Two assumptions sit under the sentence, and both are worth stating. The first is that marriage in this community is dissolved for ordinary reasons rather than only for scandal, so the law must provide for endings that are nobody's crime. The second is that a woman's consent to marriage matters, since the verse describes the dissolution of a bond that both parties entered and does not treat the woman as a transferable property. The community that received this ruling was one in which women's marriages could be arranged by families, and the Book's careful provision for the outcome of a failed marriage keeps the woman's interests in view throughout the transaction rather than only at its beginning.

**A Chapter That Regulates Exits as Carefully as Entries**

This sūrah has handled a marital offer to the Prophet's wives, a marriage in the Prophet's household that ended in divorce, the ruling on a wife whose husband had cut her off by an oath of repudiation, and now the case of a marriage dissolved before consummation. In every instance the Book tells the community what may not happen to a woman on her way out. The chapter that began with the community's survival through a siege ends up legislating about the smallest details of a marriage's end, and the two subjects are connected: a community's standards are visible in how it treats people at the moments when it has power over them, which is precisely the moment a divorce produces.

**What the Book Asks of the One Who Chose to Leave**

The obligation is placed on the person who made the decision, and the Book does not soften it with apologies about means. A man of limited resources gives what he can, but he gives something, and the Book states that a reasonable compensation is an obligation on the good-doers (2:236). The instruction's logic is simple: the man who ends a marriage has taken a decision that affects someone else's life, and the costs of that decision belong to him rather than to the person he leaves. What is required is a payment, a release, and a manner of speaking that allows both parties to continue living in the same town without either of them being diminished by the ending.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:50

> O Prophet! We have made lawful for you your wives to whom you have paid their ˹full˺ dowries as well as those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession, whom Allah has granted you. And ˹you are allowed to marry˺ the daughters of your paternal uncles and aunts, and the daughters of your maternal uncles and aunts, who have emigrated like you. Also ˹allowed for marriage is˺ a believing woman who offers herself to the Prophet ˹without dowry˺ if he is interested in marrying her—˹this is˺ exclusively for you, not for the rest of the believers. We know well what ˹rulings˺ We have ordained for the believers in relation to their wives and those ˹bondwomen˺ in their possession. As such, there would be no blame on you. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Expanded Commentary**

**What the Verse Makes Lawful**

The verse lists four categories of women lawful to the Prophet: wives whose dowries have been paid, women in his possession whom God has given him, the daughters of his uncles and aunts who migrated with him, and a believing woman who offers herself to him without dowry. Each item answers a question the community had been raising about the Prophet's household, and the list reads as a legal clarification rather than a personal privilege. A community that had been whispering about his marriages is given the categories in order, with the conditions attached to each, and the verse's own reasoning is that God knows the rulings He has laid down for the believers concerning their wives and their dependants. Nothing in the household was being left to improvisation.

**The Daughters of His Relatives, With a Condition**

The third category names the daughters of the Prophet's paternal and maternal uncles and aunts, and it attaches a condition that is easy to overlook: they must have emigrated like him. The condition excludes relatives who had remained in Mecca among the disbelievers, and the exegetes discussed whether it referred to the migration to Medina specifically or to holding the faith of the migrant community. Either way the verse ties the permission to a shared commitment rather than to blood alone. Nearness of kinship no longer established a marriage tie by itself; membership in the community of the migration did. The ruling follows the sūrah's constant theme, that loyalty to the message orders relationships that kinship had previously ordered on its own.

**The Woman Who Offers Herself**

The fourth category concerns a woman who gives herself to the Prophet in marriage without a dowry, and the verse states that this is for him alone and not for the rest of the believers. The permission is narrow and the restriction is stated in the same sentence, which prevents the ruling from becoming a general licence in a community where dowry was the recognised requirement of a marriage contract. What the community learned from the provision is not that dowries are optional but that the Prophet's position carried rulings of its own, and that the Book was willing to state them openly rather than leaving the household to be judged by the arrangements that applied to everyone else.

**Why the Prophet Had Rulings of His Own**

The verse allows the natural question — why would the messenger have permissions and obligations different from the community's — and answers it by pointing to the rulings God has laid down for the believers concerning their wives. The Prophet's household was not exempt from the law; it was subject to a law whose terms were given for it specifically, because the community's most closely watched household could not be governed by rules that had been designed without reference to its public role. Earlier verses imposed obligations on the Prophet that were not imposed on the community at all, and this verse provides permissions that are not extended to anyone else. The two must be read together to see the shape of the office.

**The Categories the Book Names**

Two of the four items concern bondwomen, and the Book names the category without apology and legislates within it. Slavery was an entrenched institution of the ancient world, and the revelation dealt with it by regulating the treatment of those held in it, defining their rights, setting the atonement for wrongs done to them, and opening the door to manumission for those who chose it. The verse's phrase about those whom God has granted you places the relationship inside the framework of God's provision rather than inside the framework of ownership as an absolute claim. Later Muslim jurists built a large body of rules on these texts to protect dependants, and the sūrah's own concern in the surrounding verses is the treatment of wives, orphans and the weak.

**"No Blame on You" and the Two Names of Mercy**

The verse closes with the removal of blame and with the names of forgiveness and mercy. The removal of blame answers the criticism that had been circulating in Medina about the Prophet's marriages; the community had been told about them for months, and its talk was not harmless. The two names at the end tell the reader how the revelation treats the whole subject. The rulings were given, the criticism was answered, and the matter was closed with the declaration that God is forgiving and merciful rather than with a condemnation of the people who had spread the talk. A household that had been the subject of the community's gossip is left in the hands of the One who knows both its arrangements and its critics.

**The Ruling for Everyone Else**

The Book supplies the general law for the community in another chapter, where it sets out the limits of plurality and the requirement of justice: “If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺, then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four. But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession. This way you are less likely to commit injustice.” (4:3). Read beside this verse, that passage shows where the community's law came from and why the Prophet's own permissions were stated as exceptions. The general ruling was given to protect the rights of orphans and to limit injustice; the Prophet's case was defined separately, so that nobody could claim his particular arrangements as a model for his own household.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:51

> It is up to you ˹O Prophet˺ to delay or receive whoever you please of your wives. There is no blame on you if you call back any of those you have set aside. That is more likely that they will be content, not grieved, and satisfied with what you offer them all. Allah ˹fully˺ knows what is in your hearts. And Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing

**Expanded Commentary**

**Freedom in the Household Division**

The verse grants the Prophet discretion in how he divides his time among his wives and in whether he returns to a wife he had set aside. The permission is stated without the usual conditions attached to the treatment of wives elsewhere in the Book, and the commentators connected it to the special circumstances of the household: the Prophet was carrying responsibilities no other householder carried, and the community's demands on him did not stop at the door. The verse is a ruling, not a licence to neglect the obligations that apply to other men, and the Book states those obligations elsewhere in language that is unsparing. What the permission recognises is that the Prophet's situation required room to move.

**The Purpose Given: Contentment Rather Than Grief**

The verse states why the arrangement is beneficial, and the reason is entirely about the feelings of the women: they would be more likely to be content, not grieved, and satisfied with what they were all offered. This is a remarkable thing for a legal text to prioritise. The Book does not justify the ruling by pointing to the Prophet's convenience, to the community's needs, or to the general good of the household; it points to the emotional consequences for the wives. The verse therefore assumes that a woman's grief in a household is a matter of real weight, worth adjusting the arrangements to avoid. The community receiving this ruling learned that the inner life of a marriage belongs to the law's concern, not merely to the parties' private feelings.

**Calling Back Those Who Were Set Aside**

The permission to restore a wife who had been set aside is the second half of the ruling, and it addresses a specific cruelty the Book is careful to prevent: the practice of leaving a woman in a state in which she is neither maintained nor released. The verse removes the sting of the correction by allowing the Prophet to reverse it at any time, and it takes for granted that the possibility of return is what makes an interim arrangement bearable. Nothing is said about the reasons that might lead him to set a wife aside; the mercy is placed on the possibility that she can be taken back, which protects her from a permanent verdict pronounced in a moment of difficulty.

**What God Knows About the Hearts in That House**

The sentence turns from legal permission to the interior of the household, and it does so without warning: God fully knows what is in your hearts. That knowledge is not only a comfort for the wives; it is a statement about the reckoning that governs the arrangements just described. The Prophet's discretion is bounded by the fact that the feelings of the women affected are known to God with more accuracy than they can be expressed. The verse therefore allows a flexibility of policy while removing any possibility of a private account with oneself, and it does the same for the community that reads the verse: what is permitted in a household is still measured by a knowledge that no one can manage.

**The Attribute Most Forbearing**

The names that close the verse — All-Knowing and Most Forbearing — answer the emotional complexity the ruling has created. Forbearance is the quality of one who does not punish promptly, who gives time, who takes account of weakness without ignoring it. Combined with full knowledge, it describes an authority that sees everything and is still patient with what it sees. The verse has just legislated about a house where several marriages coexisted and where the ordinary frictions of household life were unavoidably public. The names at the end tell the reader that the author of the ruling understands the difficulty of being a person inside it, and He is not in a hurry to settle accounts.

**What the Community Took From the Prophet's Household**

The families of Medina could hardly claim that their own domestic arrangements were private when the Book was legislating about the Prophet's, and the effect was a standard that could be applied in every home. The Book tells other husbands what justice between wives requires: “You will never be able to maintain ˹emotional˺ justice between your wives—no matter how keen you are. So do not totally incline towards one leaving the other in suspense. And if you do what is right and are mindful ˹of Allah˺, surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” (4:129). The two passages share a realism about what men are capable of and a concern for the woman left hanging. The community could not dismiss the requirement as one designed for a Prophet's exceptional duties, because the concern it expresses is the ordinary concern of every household. The community's own marriages were being judged by the same standard the Book applied to this household, and the standard was not equality of nights but the absence of a woman left in suspense. That is a demanding measure, because it asks a man to notice states of mind that no one else can see and to count them as part of his obligation. The Prophet's household provided the case that made the measure public, and the ruling in this verse shows what the measure looks like when it is applied by someone with the power to arrange things as he pleases and the instruction to keep the women content rather than grieved.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:52

> It is not lawful for you ˹O Prophet˺ to marry more women after this, nor can you replace any of your present wives with another, even if her beauty may attract you—except those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession. And Allah is ever Watchful over all things

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Limit Placed on the Household**

The verse closes the question of the Prophet's marriages. After a series of rulings about his wives, their provision, their standing and the arrangements of his household, the Book states that no further marriages are lawful for him and that he may not replace any of his present wives with another. The ruling is given in the same voice as the permissions that preceded it, and it has the effect of settling the household's future. The wives who had just been offered a choice between the world and God were told, in effect, that the women who stayed would remain; the household would not change around them. A community that had spent months discussing the Prophet's marriages was given the answer it needed to hear.

**"Even if Her Beauty May Attract You"**

The clause is an admission of how ordinary attraction works and it makes the prohibition harder rather than easier. The Book does not pretend that the Prophet was beyond being drawn to a woman's beauty; it names the fact and then states the limit, which is a more demanding instruction than a rule built on the assumption that no desire would arise. Any legal text that acknowledges the strength of the thing it is prohibiting is telling the reader that the prohibition is real rather than theoretical. The community learned from this sentence that restraint in a household is not a matter of not feeling anything; it is the management of feelings that the Book treats as an unavoidable part of human life.

**How the Limit Has Been Read**

Commentators differed over the reach of the prohibition. One reading takes the sentence at its widest: after this revelation the Prophet was forbidden to marry any woman at all, whether adding to his household or replacing a wife. Another reading, noticing the mention of replacing, allows that the prohibition was specifically aimed at the practice of exchanging one wife for another, and that the general ruling was given by the command itself. The difference turns on how the phrase about the present wives is attached to the two clauses, and the disagreement is not about the Prophet's conduct, since none of the readings describes him taking another wife after this period. What the community received from the passage was a settled limit either way.

**What the Ruling Gave the Household**

The practical consequence was security. The wife who had chosen to remain in the Prophet's house, on the terms set out earlier in the sūrah, could not thereafter be replaced by a woman of greater beauty or better connections. The choice verses had placed the household's expectations in the open, and this ruling fixed the terms on which the women who stayed would keep their position. The Book had already given them a title that made them mothers of the believers and hence unavailable to any other man; this verse added the corresponding assurance that their place in their own house was not temporary. The two rulings together made the position of these women a settled matter rather than an arrangement that depended on favour.

**The Exception Named Without Comment**

The exception for women in the Prophet's possession is stated in a few words, and the Book lets it stand beside the general limit without further explanation. What the exception shows is that the ruling was concerned with marriage as a contract with its public consequences, not with the status of dependants already inside the household. The community learned that the law distinguishes relationships by their legal character, and the verse's structure keeps that distinction visible: the limit governs new contracts, and the exception concerns what is already established. In a passage closing a long discussion of marriage, the precision is the point, since misreading the scope of a ruling is how communities reach conclusions the text never stated.

**The Name at the End**

The verse closes by naming God as ever Watchful over all things, and the choice of that title fits the subject. Household arrangements of the kind discussed in this passage are conducted in private, and the community's interest in them produces both gossip and concealment. The name tells the reader that nothing in a house is outside the field of observation, including the considerations that lead a man to prefer one wife to another. The same kind of statement closed the earlier ruling about the division of time, where God was described as knowing what is in the hearts. This sūrah legislates for a marriage in full view of the fact that its subject matter is the most private part of a life.

**The Wives Who Are Their Mothers**

The limit on new marriages must be read with the earlier declaration that these women are the mothers of the believers: “The Prophet has a stronger affinity to the believers than they do themselves. And his wives are their mothers. As ordained by Allah, blood relatives are more entitled ˹to inheritance˺ than ˹other˺ believers and immigrants, unless you ˹want to˺ show kindness to your ˹close˺ associates ˹through bequest˺. This is decreed in the Record.” (33:6). The two verses explain one another. The Prophet's marriages were not a private matter for a family; they created relationships that the whole community was bound by, since the women in question became mothers to every believer, and the community's care for them became part of its obligations.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:53

> O believers! Do not enter the homes of the Prophet without permission ˹and if invited˺ for a meal, do not ˹come too early and˺ linger until the meal is ready. But if you are invited, then enter ˹on time˺. Once you have eaten, then go on your way, and do not stay for casual talk. Such behaviour is truly annoying to the Prophet, yet he is too shy to ask you to leave. But Allah is never shy of the truth. And when you ˹believers˺ ask his wives for something, ask them from behind a barrier. This is purer for your hearts and theirs. And it is not right for you to annoy the Messenger of Allah, nor ever marry his wives after him. This would certainly be a major offence in the sight of Allah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Three Rules of a Guest**

The verse regulates how the community behaves in the Prophet's house, and it does so in three stages. Do not enter without permission or, if invited for a meal, do not arrive early and sit waiting for the food to be ready. If you are invited, come at the time you were asked for. When you have eaten, leave rather than remaining for conversation. The rulings concern ordinary conduct, and their subject is the household of a man who could not defend his own time. The Book does not ask the community to be generous to the Prophet in return for what he has given them; it asks them to stop treating his door as a public meeting place, and it treats the failure to do so as a failing of the community rather than a discourtesy to be tolerated.

**The Occasion: A Wedding and Guests Who Stayed**

The reports connect the verse to the Prophet's marriage to Zaynab bint Jaḥsh. Anas ibn Mālik, who served in the Prophet's household for years, described the occasion: the Prophet gave a wedding banquet with bread and meat, the guests came and ate, and then some of them remained, sitting and talking while the Prophet waited. Anas reported that he went out with the Prophet and returned with him more than once, and that the guests were still there, until the verse was revealed. He also said, of all people, he knew this verse of the barrier best (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7421; the report of the lingering guests is also recorded at Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4792). The ruling therefore grew out of a specific evening in a specific house, and the community preserved the details.

**God Says What a Guest Could Not Be Told**

The sentence at the centre of the verse is one of the most human in the Book: the Prophet was too shy to ask them to leave, but God is never shy of the truth. The Prophet's restraint was not weakness and not indifference; he simply would not humiliate a guest, even a guest who was imposing on him. So the instruction was given from above, and the community was told in advance what its own conduct had made necessary. The verse's lesson about shyness is worth extracting: a person's good manners may not be used as a licence by others, and where a matter of truth needs to be stated and no one will state it, the Book states it plainly on the injured person's behalf. The Prophet was spared an awkward conversation by a revelation.

**The Barrier for the Household**

The verse then gives a rule for approaching the Prophet's wives: ask them from behind a barrier. The instruction is attached to a reason, that this is purer for your hearts and theirs, and the reason is decisive for how the rule should be understood. It is not a statement about the women's capacity or standing; they taught, transmitted and were consulted by the community for decades after this ruling. It is a protective measure for the hearts of both parties, acknowledging that repeated unfenced contact between men and women in a small city produces attachments and suspicions that the community is better without. The verse states its purpose rather than leaving the reader to infer it, which keeps the rule tethered to the harm it prevents.

**The Annoyance the Community Was Not Allowed to Cause**

The verse names one specific offence and then generalises it: it is not right for you to annoy the Messenger of God. The Book had already told the Prophet to overlook the annoyances of the disbelievers and the hypocrites (33:48), and here it forbids the believers from adding their own. The two commands belong together, and they place the community's conduct toward the Prophet in a category of its own. A leader may be criticised on matters of public interest, and the community did criticise and question openly throughout this period. What the verse prohibits is the wear caused by carelessness — the lingering guest, the household pestered, the wife approached without protection — which is the kind of annoyance that a man of good manners cannot complain about.

**A Marriage Forbidden After Him**

The verse closes with a prohibition: no man is ever to marry the Prophet's wives after him, and it describes the act as a grave offence. The ruling completes the declaration that these women are the mothers of the believers (33:6), and it protects them from being pursued after his death as political matches with connections to the Prophet's name. The framing is legal and permanent, and it settled a question that might otherwise have been raised within the community within a few years. The Book gives the reason at the level of gravity rather than of mechanism, and by declining to explain it further, it leaves the community with a settled boundary rather than a matter to be renegotiated by each generation.

**The Barrier and the Cloak**

The sūrah later gives the community the outward counterpart of this instruction, and the two passages belong together: “O Prophet! Ask your wives, daughters, and believing women to draw their cloaks over their bodies. In this way it is more likely that they will be recognized ˹as virtuous˺ and not be harassed. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” (33:59). The barrier regulates a place, the household of the Prophet; the cloak regulates public space, where any believing woman might walk. Both are addressed to the prevention of harassment and of the attentions the Book thinks will follow from unprotected contact. Read together, they show a consistent approach: the community's protection of its women is arranged through rules of conduct, and the rules are attached to reasons rather than issued as bare commands.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:54

> Whether you reveal something or conceal it, surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Without a Condition Attached**

The verse is bare and complete: whether a person makes something visible or hides it, God knows everything. There is no threat written into the sentence and no promise, and the omission is part of its force. The statement does not depend on the reader's circumstances, his intentions or his excuses; it holds for the man who has just left the Prophet's house after overstaying and for the woman who watched him go. The Book places a statement of this reach immediately after a set of rules about private conduct, which is where the human mind is most inclined to think of concealment as a solution. The sentence removes that option before anyone can reach for it.

**Why It Follows the Rules of the Household**

The commands that precede this verse concern things that happen when nobody is looking. A guest who arrives early and sits, a visitor who approaches a wife to ask a question, a man who keeps a thought about a household he has been visiting: all of these take place in a setting where only the people present can know what occurred. The Book has legislated for the visible part, and it now states that the visible part is not the part that matters most. The warning is placed at the join between the community's conduct and its interior life, and it tells the community that its compliance will be measured by something other than the reports its members give each other.

**The Sūrah's Recurring Statement About the Hidden**

This is not the first time the chapter says something of the kind. Early in the sūrah the Prophet was told that God is aware of what the believers do (33:2), and the account of the siege reported what people were concealing behind their words: some entertained conflicting thoughts about God (33:12), and it said of the Prophet himself that he concealed something which God was going to reveal (33:37). Later in the chapter the ruling about the division of the household ends with the statement that God fully knows what is in the hearts (33:51). The verse here is the plainest of the series, and the repetition across the sūrah shows that the hidden is one of its central subjects rather than an incidental warning attached to a few rules.

**Concealment and the Health of a Community**

The verse does not forbid discretion. The Book requires modesty, it protects reputations, and it warns against prying into what is not one's business. What is being denied is the idea that a matter becomes harmless because it has been covered up. In a small community, most serious damage begins with conduct that the doer assumed was unobserved and with intentions that were never spoken, and the Book's method is to make the unseen part of the same account as the seen part. A person who understands this verse cannot divide his life into a public section in which he obeys and a private section in which he does not, because the same knowledge governs both and there is no division to exploit.

**What the Verse Offers a Person Alone**

The sentence holds two things for a reader who takes it seriously. It is a warning, because nothing done in secret is unrecorded, and it is a consolation, because nothing endured in secret is unrecorded either. A believer who keeps his conduct in order when nobody sees him and gains nothing for it in the community's estimation has not been overlooked, and the Book makes that point repeatedly. It is the same logic the sūrah used when it described the reward prepared for the household that obeyed quietly and the punishment prepared for the conduct that the community never suspected. The verse ends the section by assuring the reader that the account is being kept by the only witness who misses nothing.

**The Same Declaration in Sūrah al-Baqarah**

The Book states the principle in an early chapter in terms that include the hearts themselves: “To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Whether you reveal what is in your hearts or conceal it, Allah will call you to account for it. He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.” (2:284). The two verses together show how the Book handles the interior: it claims full knowledge of it, it attaches accountability to it, and it leaves the outcome to God's decision rather than to a fixed calculation. Placed at the end of a passage about how a community behaves in its neighbour's house, that claim is what gives the rulings their weight.

**The Account That Includes the Unspoken**

The Book attaches accountability to what is hidden deliberately, and it does so consistently rather than in this verse alone. The sūrah states the same fact at its beginning when it tells the Prophet that God is aware of what the community does (33:2), and it returns to it in the middle of the household rulings when it says that God fully knows what is in the hearts (33:51). What this closing sentence adds is the removal of the last refuge, the idea that a matter which nobody witnessed has no record at all. A community that understands this verse stops managing appearances and starts working on intentions, and it stops expecting the private parts of its life to remain outside the account that the Book keeps describing. The verse ends a section of rules about manners in a house by naming the witness in every house.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:55

> There is no blame on the Prophet’s wives ˹if they appear unveiled˺ before their fathers, their sons, their brothers, their brothers’ sons, their sisters’ sons, their fellow ˹Muslim˺ women, and those ˹bondspeople˺ in their possession. And be mindful of Allah ˹O wives of the Prophet!˺ Surely Allah is a Witness over all things

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Barrier and Its Exceptions**

The previous verses required the community to address the Prophet's wives from behind a barrier. This verse names the people the requirement does not cover, and the list is a list of close relatives and household members: fathers, sons, brothers, nephews on both sides, believing women, and dependants. The ruling shows how the barrier was understood. It was never a rule about women being hidden from the world; it was a rule about the boundary between a household and the public, drawn so that ordinary family life could continue inside it. The exegetes noted that the list follows the degrees of kinship that marriage is forbidden within, which is the same list the Book gives in Sūrah al-Nūr when it permits women to relax their covering at home.

**Who Is Included, and Why the List Is Necessary**

The list is precise about which male relatives are exempt, and the precision protects both sides. A nephew or a brother may enter the house without the barrier because the relationship is one the Book has made permanently unmarriageable, so that no question of temptation arises in the way it does with other men. The mention of believing women in the same list recognises that the barrier was also a protection within the community of women, and the final item covers the household's dependants, whose daily work required movement through the house. The Book states the exemptions rather than leaving them to inference, which is characteristic of its handling of the subject: the community is told exactly where a rule stops, so that nobody has to guess.

**Relatives and the Temptation to Be Casually Selfish**

The verse has a second function that the community could have missed: it removes a refuge. A household can be closed to strangers while remaining entirely open to relatives, and not every relative is harmless in the way the list assumes. The Book closes the door on that by naming the category of person for whom the barrier lapses as one defined by a fixed legal relationship, rather than by the family's comfort with the individual. In doing so it protects the women from the pressure of hospitality, which is exactly the pressure the earlier verses had described in the case of visitors who lingered. The community's standards were not to be settled by who had grown up with whom.

**Be Mindful of Allah: The Command That Returns**

The verse does not leave the exemptions to stand alone. It adds the address to the wives: be mindful of God. The instruction had been given to the Prophet at the opening of the sūrah, and it returns here in a domestic setting, where the community's rules have been spelled out and the remaining work is interior. What the Book is doing is separating two things that are easily confused. The list of relatives defines what is permitted. The command to be mindful of God governs the way a person behaves within what is permitted, and it is a reminder that a household's conduct cannot be fully regulated by a list of names. The wives are given both, and the second is the harder of the two.

**The Witness Over All Things**

The verse closes with the statement that God is a witness over everything, and this is the same closing move that the previous verse made in different words. The book's reasoning is consistent: where conduct happens behind closed doors, the community's knowledge ends and a different kind of knowledge takes over. A woman who keeps a generous distance from a relative she could technically receive unveiled is not doing so for the community's benefit, and nothing in the visible record would register her restraint. The name at the end of the verse tells the reader that this part of the account, the part that no neighbour can see, is being kept by a witness who requires no report.

**A Rule That Expected Ordinary Life**

The exemptions in this verse are a statement about what the Prophet's household was meant to be. A house in which a woman can see her father but not her nephew, or can be visited by her sister but must be separated from the servant who carries water, is not a protected home; it is an unworkable one, and unworkable rules produce either constant violation or a household withdrawn from the world. The Book chooses a third path: it draws the boundary at the legal relationships that cannot be marriages and leaves the rest of the family intact. The community learned from that decision that modesty rules are not intended to abolish family life, and the sūrah's own legislation about a woman's standing, her teaching role and her own accountability confirms the reading.
**A Household Where the Rules Had to Be Human**

Read as a whole, the section that runs from the offer to the wives through the barrier and this list of exemptions is one of the most detailed treatments of a home in the Book. It covers what a wife may choose, what she is owed, how she speaks, where she sits, who may see her, and what she is to remember. The details are not an accident of the Prophet's position; they are what it takes to run a house that is also a public institution. The community learned from that house how the ordinary privacy of a Muslim family is to be protected while still being governed by the revelation, and the list of exemptions in this verse is the clearest sign that the protection was intended to be liveable.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:56

> Indeed, Allah showers His blessings upon the Prophet, and His angels pray for him. O believers! Invoke Allah’s blessings upon him, and salute him with worthy greetings of peace

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence With Three Parties**

The verse names God, the angels and the believers, and it gives each a part in the same act. God blesses the Prophet; the angels pray for him; the believers are commanded to invoke blessings and to greet him with peace. The structure is the same one used twelve verses earlier, where the community was told that God showers His blessings upon the believers and His angels pray for them (33:43). Read together, the two passages show that the Prophet is not elevated out of the community's ranks into a separate order of being. He receives the same blessing from God and the same angelic prayer, and the difference is that the community is commanded to join in on his behalf.

**What God's Blessing Means**

The word *ṣalāt*, when its subject is God, describes His mercy, commendation and care for the one blessed. When its subject is a creature, it describes worship offered to God. The scholars were careful about the distinction because it safeguards the doctrine: God does not pray to anyone. The sentence therefore says that God honours His Prophet and shows him mercy, and that the angels, whose standing was described in this sūrah as one of continuous glorification, are asking God to do exactly that. The Book reports the same angelic concern for the believers in other chapters, where the angels seek forgiveness for those on earth (42:5), and the pattern is consistent: the unseen order of creation is portrayed as actively favourable to the guided and to the one who guides them.

**A Command to the Community**

The verse then turns to the believers with two instructions: invoke blessings on him, and salute him with worthy greetings of peace. The Arabic verb for invoking blessings is the same root used for God's action in the first clause, which means the believers are being told to perform toward the Prophet the creature's form of what God does toward him, without either act being the same thing. The command is in the plural and addressed to the whole community, which makes it a collective practice rather than a private devotion, and the greeting attached to it, peace, is the word the Book uses for the greeting exchanged among believers and for the greeting of Paradise itself.

**What the Believers Receive for It**

The practice is not left without a return. Abū Hurayrah reported that the Prophet said, 'Whoever sends one blessing upon me, Allah sends ten mercies upon him.' (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 408). The statement is worth placing beside the command in this verse, because it shows the practice running in both directions: the believer honours the Prophet, and God honours the believer for doing it. A community instructed to bless a man who had spent decades serving it is not being told to give something away; the Book gives back more than is offered, and the Prophet's own report describes the return without qualification.

**Why the Prophet Is Blessed Rather Than Worshipped**

The sentence is the clearest possible statement of the distinction the community's practice rests on. God blesses the Prophet, the angels pray for him, and the believers bless and salute him. Nowhere is the Prophet asked to bless the believers in return, and nowhere is he addressed in prayer. The Book had already placed him inside the human series in this sūrah when it described him as the messenger of God and the seal of the prophets, and it had described his death as a possibility in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān (3:144). The honour of this verse and the humanity of the man go together, and the community's ritual practice of blessing him was designed to keep both in view.

**A Practice Without a Season**

The command has no fixed time and no material requirement. The blessing on the Prophet is spoken in the ritual prayer and outside it, alone and in company, by a man walking to work and by a woman in her house, which means the community carries his name through the day in a way that no festival or gathering could achieve on its own. The Book attaches the practice to the whole community rather than to a class of specialists, and it asks for speech, not for any particular act of devotion. A community instructed to honour its messenger in this way will find that the habit keeps the memory of the message close, and that the greeting of peace attached to the blessing has a way of setting the tone of the day in which it is said.
**The Greeting That Accompanies the Blessing**

The verse pairs the blessing with a greeting of peace, and the pairing matches the community's everyday practice: a Muslim meeting a Muslim says peace, and the greeting carries the assurance that the other person's safety matters. Directing that word to the Prophet places him inside the community's ordinary courtesy rather than above it, since the same word is exchanged between neighbours, between strangers and between the members of a household. What the command produces, when it is practised over years, is a community in which the messenger is present in ordinary speech rather than only in formal settings, and in which his name is spoken with the same warmth a person uses for those he wishes well.
**A Practice That Keeps Him Present**

The instruction has had a long life in the community's devotional life. The blessing on the Prophet is spoken in the ritual prayer itself, at weddings, at gatherings, and in ordinary conversation, and it is one of the few practices the Book commands that has no fixed time and no material requirement. What it accomplishes in the community's life is a specific kind of presence: the man who carried the message is remembered as a person and not only as a source of rulings, and the community's relationship to him is expressed as gratitude rather than as doctrine. The verse asks for two things, a blessing and a greeting of peace, and both of them are forms of speech that anyone can offer, whatever his means or his circumstances.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:57

> Surely those who offend Allah and His Messenger are condemned by Allah in this world and the Hereafter. And He has prepared for them a humiliating punishment

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Offend" as a Category, Not a Slip**

The Arabic verb used here describes causing harm or injury, and when it is attached to God it means something specific: to treat what He has made sacred as a thing to be violated. The verse is not about a person who said something wrong in a careless moment. It describes an established conduct, the kind of deliberate injury that the sūrah has been documenting for its whole length: bargains with enemies over the content of the message, discouragement spread among believers, guests who wear out a household, slander against those who cannot answer it. The Book gathers all of it into one category and states the consequence in a sentence, and the gathering is part of the point. Offences that look unrelated from the outside are one thing at their root, because they all involve a claim to treat the messenger or his Lord as something other than what they are.

**Condemned in This World and the Next**

The verse names two arenas of consequence. In this world the offender is condemned, and the Book has shown in this sūrah what that looks like in practice: exposure. The men who discouraged others during the siege were identified by revelation while they were still among the believers, their reasoning was published, and their excuses were shown to be what they were. That is a specific kind of worldly punishment, and it is worse than an ordinary penalty in one respect. A man can survive a fine or a beating and keep his standing, but a person whose interior has been publicly described has lost the ability to manage how he is seen. The sentence states that this exposure belongs to God's judgement rather than to the community's vigilance.

**A Humiliating Punishment and Its Fit**

The punishment in the Hereafter is described with the word for humiliation, and the choice matches the offence. A person who offends God and His messenger is generally trying to enhance his own standing, whether in front of a tribe, a court or a crowd of like-minded people. The Book's answer is that the enhancement is temporary and the humiliation is the settled outcome. It states the same correspondence elsewhere, where the people who were too proud to worship God are told that they will enter Hell humiliated (40:60), and the sūrah itself will report the offenders of the city being seized and killed wherever they are found (33:61). The Book does not treat humiliation as an arbitrary penalty; it treats it as the reversal of a specific ambition.

**Where the Line Is Drawn**

The community had to know what the verse did and did not cover, and the Book supplies the distinction in two directions. It commands the Prophet in this same sūrah to overlook the annoyances of the disbelievers and the hypocrites (33:48), which means not every irritation caused by an opponent is the offence described here. On the other side, the verse that follows this one speaks of those who abuse believing men and women unjustifiably, so that the protection of this sentence extends outward from God and His messenger to ordinary Muslims. What is being defended is not a man's sensitivity but the standing of the message, and the community's later jurists read the passage in that light when they distinguished disagreement from deliberate injury.

**The Two Who Are Named Together**

The verse joins God and His Messenger in a single object of offence, and the Book does the same in the warning that closes the sūrah's account of the trust, and in the command to obey. This joining is a claim in itself. It says that the message and its bearer cannot be separated, so that a person cannot honour God while treating His messenger as a negotiable figure. The community had watched men try precisely that during the siege: they spoke well of God and questioned what His messenger had promised. The verse closes the gap they had been trying to open, and it does so by naming the two in a single sentence with a single punishment.

**How the Community Used the Warning**

The verse is addressed to those who offend, and the community's jurists read it as a limit on its own conduct as much as on the conduct of its opponents. Nothing in the sentence authorises a private prosecution, and the sūrah shows how the Prophet handled the men who insulted him and the group that questioned his household: he overlooked the annoyance where he could, and where the matter had to be answered, the answer came from revelation rather than from his temper. The Book's warnings about offences against the Prophet therefore served two purposes in the community's life, describing the gravity of the offence and restraining the believers from taking the reckoning into their own hands. Both readings were necessary if the community was to keep its balance as its power grew.
**What the Warning Does Not Do**

The verse does not authorise the community to enforce the punishment itself. No procedure is described, no offender is named, and nothing in the sentence directs the believers to take action against anyone. The sūrah's own account of the Prophet's conduct is the practical commentary: he answered insult with restraint, refused to punish the men who bargained over doctrine, and left the reckoning to God. The Book consistently keeps this separation between the announcement of a consequence and the community's authority to impose it, and the passage here is a statement about what God has settled rather than a licence for the believers to settle scores.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:58

> As for those who abuse believing men and women unjustifiably, they will definitely bear the guilt of slander and blatant sin

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Extension of the Warning**

The previous verse protected God and His Messenger from deliberate injury. This verse extends the same protection to ordinary Muslims, men and women alike, and it does so in the language of law. The offence has two elements: abuse, meaning words or conduct that harm a person's reputation or dignity, and the qualification that the abuse is without justification, meaning the accusation is not only damaging but false or unwarranted. The Book states the consequence in terms of guilt carried rather than of punishment inflicted, and it names two charges, slander and blatant sin, which the offender will be bearing at the reckoning.

**What the Offence Costs the Community**

The Book treats reputation as a form of property that cannot be taken without consequence, and it legislates for the subject in detail. The sūrah of light requires four witnesses for an accusation of sexual immorality and prescribes a penalty for those who make the accusation without producing them (24:4), and later in this sūrah the Book will threaten the rumourmongers of Medina directly (33:60). The rules are not a luxury of settled times. During the siege, the community's morale depended on its members' confidence in one another, and a lie circulated about a believer's conduct did more damage than a lost skirmish. The Book's protection of the individual therefore protects the community's cohesion at the same time, which is why the rulings on slander stand beside the rulings on war in this chapter.

**Men and Women Named Together**

The verse specifies believing men and believing women, which matches the pattern of this section of the sūrah. The community's women were the particular target of the talk that circulated in Medina, and the Book's response is to name them in the same sentence as the men and to attach the same protection to both. The scholars drew from this verse that the injury caused by false accusation does not vary with the standing of the person accused; a believing woman's reputation is protected by the same rule as a believing man's. The Book states the two together, and it does not rank the harms.

**Slander and Blatant Sin as Two Charges**

The two words at the end of the verse are not synonyms. The first is the burden of the falsehood itself, the lie that has been told about someone who did not do what was alleged. The second is the wider offence of the act that produced it, which covers the malice that drove the speaker, the audience that was misled, and the harm that followed. The Book lays both on the person who started it, and it does so with an emphatic construction that leaves no room for the excuse that the story was merely passed along. A community that understood this verse would be careful about repeating what it could not verify, which is exactly what the Book demands elsewhere when it tells the believers to verify reports before acting on them (49:6).

**Why the Word "Unjustifiably" Matters**

The qualification protects the legitimate work of public accountability. A community must be able to name wrongdoing, to give testimony, to report a crime and to warn others about a person who harms them. None of that is covered by this verse, because the abuse described here is unjustified. What the Book forbids is the false charge, the rumour with no basis, the insult aimed at a person's dignity for reasons of faction, envy or amusement. Reading the qualification as a loophole would invert the verse; the Book treats a truthful accusation as a duty and a false one as a sin, and the difference rests on whether the claim is true and whether stating it serves a legitimate purpose.

**The Duty to Verify**

The verse does not stand alone in the Book's treatment of talk. Sūrah al-Ḥujurāt adds the instructions that surround it, requiring the believers to verify a report before acting on it and warning them against suspicion, spying and backbiting (49:12). The two passages describe one ethos. A community that repeats what it has not checked becomes a machine for manufacturing injury, and the Book shuts the whole mechanism down at the source by attaching the guilt of slander and blatant sin to the person who starts the talk. What is being protected by these rules is not merely a few reputations; it is the trust that allows a community to function, which in the case of Medina had been severely tested by a season of rumour.
**Why the Sūrah Protects Reputation in the Middle of a War**

The placement of this verse beside the account of a siege and a list of rulings about marriage is not accidental. A community with an external enemy cannot afford an internal climate in which every member suspects his neighbour of something he has only heard about, and the Book addresses that climate directly. The rules on slander in Medina's legislation were part of the community's defence, and the sūrah's repeated concern with the hidden and the spoken, from the waverers in the trenches to the rumourmongers of the city, shows that the Book treated the second danger as the more corrosive of the two. Armies depart; the words a community has used about its own members remain.
**The Belt of Protection Around the Ordinary Believer**

Read with the preceding verse, the passage draws a line around three things: God, His Messenger, and the ordinary Muslim man or woman. The community that received these verses had spent a winter in which all three had been attacked, and the Book's remedy was to state the standing of each and the consequence of injuring it. The verse does not ask the believers to be suspicious of one another, and it does not hand them a mechanism for enforcement. It tells them where the account is kept, and it tells them that the person they are talking about is a believing man or woman whose injury has an owner.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:59

> O Prophet! Ask your wives, daughters, and believing women to draw their cloaks over their bodies. In this way it is more likely that they will be recognized ˹as virtuous˺ and not be harassed. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command Given to the Community Through the Prophet**

The verse instructs the Prophet to speak to a group that goes well beyond his own household: his wives, his daughters, and the believing women. The command is therefore a public measure rather than a domestic one, and its inclusion of the whole community of women makes the practice a standard for Medina rather than a privilege attached to one family. The ruling is directed to the men who are responsible for the household, since the Prophet is told to ask these women to adopt the practice, and it presumes a community in which families took responsibility for one another's conduct. The Book's method here is consistent with the rest of the sūrah: it regulates conduct at the point where a decision is actually made, which in this society meant the head of a household.

**The Cloak and the Way It Was Worn**

The garment named is the *jilbāb*, an outer covering worn over normal clothing when a woman left her house. The exegetes described it as a sheet or wrap drawn over the head and down over the body, and commentators differed about the exact manner of draping without disagreeing about the garment's function, which was to cover rather than to display. Umm Salamah reported the effect of the command on the city: 'When the verse "That they should cast their outer garments over their persons" was revealed, the women of the Anṣār came out as if they had crows over their heads by wearing outer garments.' (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4101, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī). The image is the report of an eyewitness to the first generation that received the instruction, and it describes immediate compliance rather than gradual adaptation.

**Two Purposes Stated in the Same Clause**

The verse gives its own reasons, and both concern what happens in public space. The first is recognition: a woman dressed this way is identified as a believing woman who is not inviting attention. The second is protection from harassment, which the Book names as the expected outcome if the practice is ignored. The order matters. Recognition is the means and safety is the result, which tells the reader that the garment was not designed to hide women from society but to announce where they stood in it. The Book's realism is worth noting in the same breath: it assumes that a woman moving through a public street may be approached, and it addresses the danger rather than pretending that the danger does not exist.

**Addressed to the Men Who Caused the Problem**

The verse does not ask the women of Medina to reform the men who harassed them, and it does not treat harassment as a matter of the woman's responsibility. It protects the community's women by removing an excuse from the men who would approach them, and it leaves the offence where it belongs. The Book will shortly take up the offenders directly, naming the hypocrites, the sick in heart and the rumourmongers of the city as the target of a threat. Read together, the two passages place the burden of the problem on the men who created it, while giving the believing women a means of being recognised before a word is exchanged. The instruction is protection, and its counterpart is a warning to those from whom the protection was needed.

**What the Verse Does Not Do**

The verse does not confine women to their houses. The command assumes that women go out, walk to the mosque, visit, trade and travel, and it addresses how they should appear while doing so. It does not prescribe a colour, a design or a single approved garment, and it does not specify the parts of the body in detail here; those details come from the practice of the Prophet, from the parallel instruction in Sūrah al-Nūr (24:31) and from the long discussion of the jurists. What the Book states is the principle, that a believing woman's dress should make her identity and her disinterest in being approached clear. The community's law grew out of that principle rather than out of the single word used in this verse.

**A Practice Confirmed by the Community's Practice**

The instruction was taken up in the community's life within the first generation, as Umm Salamah's report shows, and it was understood from the beginning as a standard for believing women rather than as a rule for the Prophet's family alone. The Book had already given the community a general principle about modest dress in Sūrah al-Nūr, and the ruling here supplied the public counterpart for the street and the market. The scholars derived from the two passages the ordinary requirements of covering and the emphasis on the outer garment, and the community's practice across its long history has kept the principle in view even where the style of clothing has changed from place to place.
**Forgiveness at the End of a Public Rule**

The verse closes with two names, All-Forgiving and Most Merciful, and their placement is worth attention. The command concerns appearances in public, where the pressures on a community are constant and where compliance is often imperfect. Some women in Medina would struggle with the garment, some families would adopt it slowly, and some people would use the standard as a weapon against women rather than as a protection for them. The names at the end tell the community that the matter is held in mercy, and they also set the tone for the community's own enforcement: the Book commands a manner of dress and then names the forgiveness of God, which leaves no room for a campaign of humiliation against women who fell short.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:60

> If the hypocrites, and those with sickness in their hearts, and rumour-mongers in Medina do not desist, We will certainly incite you ˹O Prophet˺ against them, and then they will not be your neighbours there any longer

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Groups, One City**

The verse names three parties, and each has appeared earlier in this sūrah in a different setting. The hypocrites were the men who professed faith and worked against the community, and the Book described their discouragement of the fighters and their hope to be away among the nomads. Those with sickness in their hearts were named during the siege as the men who spread doubt, and they appear in the verse about the Prophet's wives as the men whose attention the women were instructed not to invite. The rumourmongers are those who carried stories through Medina, and the verse gives them no other description because their work is done with words. Three different methods, one effect, and the Book gathers them into a single sentence because the city's survival depended on the same thing in each case, the community's ability to trust itself.

**"If They Do Not Desist"**

The warning is conditional, and the condition is not that the groups change their beliefs but that they stop their conduct. The Arabic verb means to restrain oneself, to leave off an activity, and the Book's usual pattern with the hypocrites of Medina is visible in it: the door out is available, and its price is the cessation of the harm rather than a public confession. The community was not asked to hunt for hidden opponents, and the men in question were not told to identify themselves. They were given the option that a community under threat can reasonably offer, and the Book kept the offer open for as long as the conduct continued.

**What It Means to Be Incited Against Them**

The threat is directed to the Prophet, and it promises a change in his stance rather than an immediate punishment: the community's leader will be moved against them, and their position in the city will end. The verse does not describe the mechanism, and the Book's later rulings on the treatment of the hypocrites show how carefully the community proceeded, since the Prophet continued to accept their presence in the mosque and their contributions for years. The Book itself explains one reason for that restraint in another chapter, where the community is told not to drive people to extremes by harsh treatment (4:? ). What the sentence establishes is that the patience of the community had a limit that God had set, and that the limit was being approached.

**The Loss of Neighbourhood as a Sentence**

The closing clause describes the consequence with the word for neighbour: they will not remain your neighbours in the city any longer. The choice of that word rather than a word for exile or death is deliberate, because it measures the offence in the currency of ordinary life. The men in question had been living in the same neighbourhoods, sharing markets, wells, streets and mosques with the people they were undermining. The sentence tells them that the arrangement is not a right; it is a relationship, and a relationship can be ended by the party who is being injured. The community would eventually expel those who persisted, and the wording shows that the removal was framed as the end of a shared life rather than as an act of hostility for its own sake.

**Why the Sūrah Says This Now**

The warning comes after the passage on the cloaks and before the final passage about the Hour, and the placement is practical. The rules about dress were designed to remove an excuse from men who harassed women, and the verse immediately identifies the men who would be affected by the rule. A community that had legislated about modesty in public space needed to know that the danger being addressed was not a hypothetical one and that the offenders were known. The sūrah has spoken about the offence against believing men and women in general terms; this verse gives the offence a home address, and it tells the city that the stories, the harassment and the discouragement came from people its members saw every day.

**What the Community Learned About Hidden Enemies**

The verse teaches a lesson that the community needed beyond the immediate threat: opponents inside a city are identified by their conduct rather than by their opinions, and the Book names the conduct precisely. Talk, discouragement, the deliberate stirring of fear: these are activities that can be observed by everyone, and the Book describes the men by what they did rather than by what they secretly believed. That approach shielded the community from the temptation to judge intentions it could not see, and it gave the Prophet a clear standard when he had to decide how to treat people who claimed loyalty while working against the community. The standard remained conduct, verifiable and public.
**A Warning That Preserves the Community's Peace**

The sentence holds a quieter benefit for the community as well. By naming the conduct and stating that it would be met with a decision, the verse relieved the believers of the need to identify and pursue their hidden opponents themselves. The matter was taken out of private hands and attached to the Prophet, which is where the Book consistently places such decisions. The community's part was to endure what could not be changed and to keep its own conduct clean, while the men who were spreading the harm were told plainly that their neighbours' patience was not indefinite and that the account was being kept by someone who knew exactly who they were.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:61

> ˹They deserve to be˺ condemned. ˹If they were to persist,˺ they would get themselves seized and killed relentlessly wherever they are found

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Follows the Warning**

The previous verse gave the offenders a condition: leave off, or the community will be moved against them. This verse describes what the outcome would look like. The Book states it in harsh terms and does so without the diplomatic framing of the earlier sentence, which tells the reader that the patience described there was a decision rather than a lack of capacity. The community had the means to end the problem, and the sūrah is clear that the limit existed in the offending rather than in the responding. What is being prevented by the warning is the outcome described here, and the Book gives the offenders the opportunity to avoid it in the plainest possible way.

**Condemned in Their Standing**

The first word of the verse describes a state rather than an act: they are cursed, expelled from the mercy that the community lives inside. The Book uses this language for those who have removed themselves from a covenant, and the previous passage described the warning in terms of neighbourhood, which is a social fact. This verse adds the spiritual fact and places the two together. A man who spends his days undermining the community he lives in has, in the Book's account, left two homes at once, and the sentence makes clear that the second departure is the more serious of the two.

**What Is Described and What Was Done**

The description of being seized and killed should be read against the record of the community's practice. The Prophet did not carry out a campaign of assassination against the men who mocked him, questioned him or spread stories about his household; the reports of the period show him receiving them, arguing with them and, on occasion, forgiving them. The verses belong to the same Book that told him in this very sūrah to overlook the annoyances of the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and that commanded the believers to verify reports and to avoid suspicion. The sūrah therefore states a limit that would be reached if the conduct continued, and it leaves the enforcement in the hands that had set the limit.

**Disorder as the Real Offence**

The verse does not describe these men as heretics or as members of an enemy tribe. It describes them as people whose conduct would make life in the city impossible if it were allowed to continue. The Arabic phrase rendered as relentlessly carries the sense of being put to flight, of being driven out of the city's settled life. That is the key to the passage: the punishment is described in the language of removal from a place rather than in the language of religious correction. What the community was protecting was its ability to live as one body with a shared future, and the Book, which had just legislated for the protection of women in the streets, states that those who make public life unsafe forfeit their place in it.

**The Legal Reading the Community Took**

The scholars read these verses as a description of a category rather than as a licence for individuals to act. The men they describe are those who join an external enemy and act against the community from inside, and the community's later law assigned the authority to deal with them to the political leadership, with conditions attached and evidence required. Private reprisal was excluded by the very structure of the sentence, which addresses the Prophet and speaks of enforcement by an authority. The community's history shows how rarely the limit was reached, since the Prophet's practice was to tolerate the presence of the men this sūrah exposes as long as they kept within the bounds of city life.

**The Limit and the Mercy Before It**

Two verses before this one, the Book had given the offenders a way out that cost them nothing but the abandonment of their practice, and this verse tells the reader what the refusal of that offer would produce. The sequence is characteristic of the sūrah. It warned the confederates through a wind before it described their dispersal, it corrected the Prophet over a matter of consideration before it acquitted him over the act, and it gave the rumourmongers a condition before it described the sentence. The Book's severity is repeatedly preceded by an offer, and the offer is usually as generous as the circumstances allow, which is itself part of what the community was meant to learn about how authority should behave.
**The Contrast With the Verse Before It**

The chapter places the command about the cloaks, the warning to the rumourmongers and this description of the outcome in a single movement, and the sequence teaches a lesson about the protection of the weak. The Book does not merely instruct women how to dress and leave it there. It identifies the men who made such a measure necessary, warns them, and states what their persistence would cost them. Read in order, the passage treats the community's vulnerable members as people whose safety was a matter of public law rather than private discretion, and the harshness of this verse is the measure of how seriously the Book took the offence against them. The verse therefore describes a limit rather than a policy, and the community's history records how long the limit could be postponed by restraint. A city that keeps its patience in the hands of a leader who prefers mercy has an option that a city governed by private revenge does not, and the Book's warnings were addressed to men whose conduct could still be abandoned.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:62

> That was Allah’s way with those ˹hypocrites˺ who have gone before. And you will find no change in Allah’s way

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Law Rather Than an Incident**

The verse states that what the previous passage described was not a new policy devised for Medina. It was the way God has dealt with this kind of conduct throughout history, and the Book attaches that claim to a wide sweep of preceding communities. The hypocrites of Medina were being told that their method was old, their predecessors were numerous, and the outcome of the method had been settled long before they arrived. The Book's habit of placing a community's crisis inside a pattern of earlier crises serves a specific purpose: it removes the sense that the community's situation is exceptional, and it removes the hope that an exception will be made for persistent offenders.

**The Same Way, Two Directions**

This sūrah has used the phrase before, and the earlier use concerned the prophets. Verse 38 stated that acquittal for doing what God has ordained was the way of God with the messengers who came before, and this verse states that the treatment of those who undermine the community is the same way in the other direction. The two together describe a single order in which obedience and its opposite each meet a settled response. The Book does not present these as two separate patterns that a reader must balance; it presents one pattern with two faces, and it applies the same word, *sunnat Allāh*, to both. Nothing in the arrangement is improvised, and nothing in it is negotiable.

**"You Will Find No Change"**

The second sentence is a flat statement about the stability of that order. The Book repeats it in several chapters, where it tells the reader that the divine way does not shift and that no community can negotiate a different outcome by being modern, powerful or numerous. For the community of Medina, the sentence carried a warning and a comfort at once. It meant that the men who were spreading harm could not count on God's restraint becoming permanent, and it meant that the community which held its ground could not be abandoned by a change of policy. The same phrase had described the destruction of the deniers in earlier sūrahs, and it is used here to describe the stability of the whole arrangement.

**A Textual Note About the Closing Words**

The words that close the verse in the received text pose a problem for a reader of this translation, because they appear to open the subject that the next verses treat at length. Commentators and editors have taken them as a heading or as a transition rather than as part of the preceding sentence, since the verse's own argument about the way of God has ended with the statement about the absence of change. The next passage turns to the question of the Hour, and the words serve as an introduction to it. What matters for the reading of this verse is that the sentence about God's way is complete in itself, and the phrase that follows it announces the topic the sūrah is about to take up.

**Why the Chapter Moves to the Hour From Here**

The movement from the fate of the rumourmongers to the question of the final Hour is not a change of subject. The passage has been about accountability: conduct that escapes the community's notice, injuries done with words, people who assume that their standing protects them. The question of the Hour is the question of when the reckoning that governs all of this becomes visible, and the sūrah answers it by placing the whole matter in the hands of God. The warning to the offenders and the question about the end of the world belong to one argument, and the Book joins them in a single breath, as it often does when it moves from the conduct of a city to the end of the world.

**A Statement That Also Restrains the Community**

The doctrine of this verse carries a practical instruction for the believers who would read it later: since the way of God is settled and the accounting belongs to Him, the community's business is its own conduct rather than the settlement of every score. The Book made the pattern visible in the Prophet's own practice, since he continued to live among the men this passage exposes and to accept their presence until their conduct ended. What the community inherited from the verse was a discipline of patience exercised alongside a firm grasp of what was true, and the pairing of that patience with the question of the Hour in the same sentence kept the reckoning in view without ever handing it to private hands.
**The Pattern That Judges Every Generation**

What the verse leaves the community with is a form of judgement that does not depend on its own enforcement. The believers in Medina were told that they did not need to secure every outcome, since the way of God would do that work in its own time and had already done it for the communities that preceded them. The men whose conduct this sūrah exposed would face an accounting that no community could carry out, and the community's task was to keep its own life in order while that accounting ran its course. The Book closes the passage the same way it closes the whole sūrah, with the reckoning left in the hands that had arranged everything from the siege onward. The chapter's last movement begins from here, moving from the stability of God's way to the reckoning of the last day, and the two subjects belong together. A community that believes the pattern is fixed will not exhaust itself trying to force outcomes, and it will not treat the patience of God as permission to do as it pleases.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:63

> People ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the Hour. Say, “That knowledge is only with Allah. You never know, perhaps the Hour is near.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question People Kept Asking**

The people of Mecca and Medina asked about the Hour repeatedly, and the Book records the question in several chapters. Their interest was practical rather than speculative: if the world was going to end, a person wanted to know how much time remained for the decisions he was deferring. The question also carried an element of challenge, because a threat of judgement is easier to dismiss when its date is unknown and its arrival can be doubted. The Book answers the question the same way in every chapter, and it answers it here in a single sentence that removes the subject from human hands: the knowledge belongs to God alone.

**An Answer That Refuses to Speculate**

The sentence does not offer a sign, a calculation or a general expectation of nearness. It states the location of the knowledge and stops, and the Book treats every attempt to fix the date as an overreach. In Sūrah al-Aʿrāf the Prophet is told to say that the knowledge is with his Lord, that He alone will reveal it when the time comes, and that the questioners ask as though he had full knowledge of it (7:187). The Book's consistency on this point is a form of protection for the community: a religion whose followers are kept from date-setting cannot be discredited by a failed prediction, and the community's energy stays where the Book puts it, on conduct rather than on calendars.

**"Perhaps the Hour Is Near"**

The second sentence gives the hearer something to do with the question. The Arabic construction expresses possibility and expectation at once, and the Book uses it to keep the community's sense of nearness alive without naming a date. The Prophet himself is told to say it, which means the uncertainty is shared rather than resolved for the messenger. What the sentence produces is a specific kind of seriousness: a person who considers that the end may be close lives differently from one who assumes he has years to spend, and the Book prefers that the community live with the contingency rather than with a schedule it could plan around.

**What Nearness Does to Ordinary Life**

The Book attaches practical consequences to the expectation of a near end. It tells the community that a person's deeds are the only thing he will carry, and it warns against the long-term project that becomes the whole of a life — the storehouse a man builds and then leaves (102:1-2), the fields a man tends as though he will live forever. The community of Medina was in the middle of building something durable: farms, a city, a legal order and a state. The verse does not cancel that work. It places it under the awareness that the same God who gave the community its victories would one day bring the whole arrangement to an end, and that no achievement would be able to argue with the Hour when it came.

**Why the Messenger Was Kept From Knowing**

The Book is explicit that the knowledge was withheld even from the messenger, and the withholding has a purpose that concerns the community's relationship to him. If a messenger knew the date, his followers would treat him as a source of information about the future rather than of guidance for the present, and the community would organise itself around the timetable. The Book keeps the messenger in the position of a warner, which is the title this sūrah gave him a few verses earlier, and a warner announces a danger without appointing its hour. The community that accepts the limitation follows a man who warns them about what he does not know the time of, and its obedience is built on trust in God rather than on the messenger's access to hidden things.

**A Question That Reveals What a Person Is Doing**

The Book treats the demand for a date as a symptom rather than as curiosity. When people asked the Prophet about the Hour, the question usually came from men who were postponing a decision until they could see the outcome, and the answer removed the postponement by removing the knowledge. The community of Medina had its own reasons for wanting certainty about the future: the confederates might return, the harvest was uncertain, and the political situation of the city changed from month to month. The verse tells the community that the future will not be disclosed to help anyone plan, and that the obligations it has been given are to be carried out under uncertainty. That condition is not a defect in the arrangement; the whole doctrine of the trust in this sūrah presumes it.
**The Same Question in Another Chapter**

The Book treats the question at its fullest in Sūrah al-Nabaʾ, where it opens by describing people who are asking one another about the great tidings of the coming event, and then spends the chapter describing the arrangement of the world rather than the date of its end. The pattern is consistent across the Book. Where the question is put, the answer gives information about God's power, the certainty of the resurrection, and the character of the reckoning, and it leaves the timing with God. The community addressed in this sūrah was asking in the middle of a siege and a series of rulings; the answer it received turned it back to the obligations it had been given, and that is where the chapter leaves the subject. The verse therefore trains the community in a discipline that every generation after it has needed: to work for a future it cannot see, on the strength of an instruction it can.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:64

> Surely Allah condemns the disbelievers, and has prepared for them a blazing Fire

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Doubters**

The previous verse removed the question of timing from human reach. This verse states what is certain about the matter regardless of when it occurs: those who rejected the message are condemned and a fire has been prepared for them. The community that had been arguing with the disbelievers of Mecca for years, that had faced their armies and their trade boycotts and their negotiations over the content of the message, is told that the outcome of the dispute is settled. The sentence is not addressed to the Meccans, who would not read it; it is addressed to the believers, and it gives them the one piece of information about the future that they actually needed for the decisions they had to make.

**What Condemnation Means Here**

The word describes a decision already made rather than a rejection that might be reconsidered. The Book uses it for those who have deliberately turned away from a message they understood, and it states elsewhere the condition that accompanies it: the condemnation falls on those who persist in denial, and it does not apply to a person who has not yet decided. The community had members who had been disbelievers a few years before — Abū Sufyān's allies were still fighting them, but some of their own leaders had once opposed the Prophet — which means the sentence cannot be read as a verdict on everyone who had ever rejected the message. It is a statement about the settled end of a settled denial.

**A Fire Prepared, Not Improvised**

The Book describes the punishment as having been made ready, using the same construction it used for the rewards promised to the believers and the forgiveness promised to the household. Heaven and hell in this vocabulary are not reactions that appear at the moment of judgement; they are arrangements already in place. The effect on the reader is to remove the notion that God must decide what to do with each person at the last minute, as though there were room for negotiation. For a community under pressure, the assurance has an edge: the men who were trying to end its existence, and who seemed to be succeeding for a time, were already accounted for, and the arrangement had been made before the siege began.

**Why the Passage Turns From Society to the Last Day**

The sūrah has spent most of its length on the community's present: its household, its visitors, its rumourmongers, its dress, its marriages, its defence. At this point it lifts the whole discussion onto the plane of the final reckoning, and the shift is the Book's way of answering the problem the community faced. The men who spread stories about believing women were not punished at once; the men who bargained over doctrine kept their lives and their property; the community's dead were lying in graves while their attackers went home. The passage exists to say that none of that is the final accounting, and it says so immediately after the community has been given rules about its own conduct.

**The Following Verses and the Shape of the Reckoning**

This verse is the first of a short sequence that describes the end of the disbelievers in a rising intensity, from their condemnation and the fire, to their perpetual dwelling there, to their faces being turned over in the flames, to the words they will say when they see what they have earned. The Book pauses long enough in that sequence to record their speech, which is a notable decision, since it means the reader is given the voice of the men whose fate has just been described. The effect is instruction rather than spectacle: the community hearing the verse is being shown what people will say when it is too late to say anything useful, and the words are recorded for the benefit of those who still have time.

**Why the Passage Names the Punishment Before the People**

The Book could have stated the fate of the disbelievers in one verse at the end of the chapter. Instead it unfolds the description across several, and each one adds something the community needed to understand about the world it was living in. This verse establishes that the outcome is settled and prepared. The verses that follow establish that it is permanent, that it will be visible, and that the people who arrive there will explain how they got there. Taken as a set, they provide the community with a complete picture of the alternative to the guidance it had received, which is a form of instruction: a person who can picture the end of a road is less likely to walk down it out of habit or inattention.
**The Certainty That Sustains Ordinary Duty**

The verse gives the community something to work with in the years when nothing is visibly settled. The believers of Medina were running a city and a religion that had enemies on every side, and a good deal of their work produced no immediate result. The Book's answer to that condition is not a promise of quick vindication but a statement about certainty: the condemnation is in place and the fire is prepared. A community that understands the sentence can go on with its obligations without needing to see the men who oppose it answer for their conduct, and it can hold its own conduct to a standard even while its opponents appear to be winning. The sentence also fixes the order in which the two facts are held: the condemnation is stated before the fire, and the fire is described as prepared rather than as imposed in the heat of the moment. A community that reasons about its opponents in that order will neither underestimate the seriousness of denial nor mistake the patience of God for indifference to it.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:65

> to stay there for ever and ever—never will they find any protector or helper

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Things Stated About the Arrangement**

The verse adds two facts to the sentence before it, and both concern permanence. The inhabitants remain there without an end, and they find no protector or helper. The two elements work together, since a person abandoned in a difficulty but able to appeal to someone would still have a route out, and a person with no route out but a limited term would still have an end to endure toward. The Book removes both, and it does so with the emphatic vocabulary of duration that it uses consistently for the fire and for the reward of Paradise. The community that had been told that the hour might be near is now told what the reckoning will look like for the people whose conduct it had spent a chapter documenting.

**No Protector, No Helper**

The Book has used these two names earlier in this sūrah in an entirely different setting. When the Prophet was told to ask the waverers who could shield them from God if He intended harm or mercy, the verse ended with the statement that they would never find a protector or helper besides God (33:17). The vocabulary returns here, and the reader is meant to connect the two. The men who calculated their safety by siding with the strongest army, and the men who thought a wall could protect them from the decree, are shown the outcome of a condition in which nothing at all is available to shield a person. The same names that describe God's exclusive adequacy elsewhere describe the absolute destitution of the people who rejected Him.

**Why the Fire Is Described by What It Lacks**

The description through absence is the Book's way of stating the scale of the loss. The fire is not merely a punishment; it is the condition of being without everything that a person would turn to in trouble. No relative will intervene, no community will speak for him, no ally will repay the support he once gave. The Book states the same fact in other chapters in terms of the relationships that end, where a person's kinship, wealth and followers cannot answer for him, and it records the despair of people who offered every substitute they could think of and were refused. The two names in this verse condense that whole catalogue into five words.

**The Address to a Community That Had Both**

The community of Medina knew what protectors and helpers were worth, because it had recently depended on both. It had protected itself with a trench, with alliances among the tribes of the city and with the decision of men who stood in a line at night. The verse does not tell the believers that such means are useless; the sūrah's own account of the siege shows God working through them. What it says is that the means are available in this world and not in the next, and that a person who has relied on them to the exclusion of the commands he was given will arrive at the reckoning with nothing to call on. The community that read this verse lived with that distinction every day.

**Permanence and the Community's Warnings**

The Book's warnings about eternal duration have engaged the theologians for centuries, and their discussion is worth a sentence here. Some held that the punishment of the fire is unending for those who die in denial, and they cited these verses as their proof; others argued that the mercy of God ultimately encompasses all things, and they cited passages about the extent of that mercy. The verses in this sūrah state the duration in the clearest possible terms, and the Book states the same for the reward of the believers. What the community took from both is a seriousness about the outcome that does not permit the present life to be treated as the whole of the account.

**The Two Absences and the Two Presences**

The Book's descriptions of the fire are mostly descriptions of what is missing: no shade that refreshes, no food that nourishes, no companion who helps, no protector and no helper. Against them, the same Book describes Paradise almost entirely in terms of presence — the presence of God's pleasure, of peace, of brothers and companions, of gardens and rivers. The contrast is deliberate in a chapter that has spoken so much about community, since the punishment described here is the loss of exactly what the community of Medina had spent its years building: people who would stand with a person and defend him. A believer reading the verse learns what happens to the relationships that this sūrah has been ordering, when they are severed at the only point where no repair is possible.
**What the Sentence Gives a Reader Who Still Has Time**

The verse's function in the chapter is not to settle the fate of the people mentioned but to inform the conduct of the people reading it. The Book records the end of the disbelievers in unsparing terms, and it does so beside instructions about speech, modesty, marriage and honesty, so that the two halves of the passage illuminate one another. The reader who understands that this is what abandonment looks like has a reason to keep the small duties the sūrah has been laying down, and the community in which those duties are kept is one in which no member will find himself without a helper on the day the helpers are gone. The Book's language of permanence is used for both destinations without qualification, and the community was expected to read both sentences as literal.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:66

> On the Day their faces are ˹constantly˺ flipped in the Fire, they will cry, “Oh! If only we had obeyed Allah and obeyed the Messenger!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Image and What It Does**

The verse describes the faces of the condemned being turned over in the flames, and the image conveys a specific helplessness. A person can protect his face with his hands, and the Book shows the condemned unable to do even that; the feature by which a person is recognised and through which he faces the world is the part being exposed to the fire. The Book uses the face for the whole person throughout its descriptions of the reckoning, where the saved are described as having their faces brightened and the condemned as having theirs darkened. The punishment therefore begins with the loss of what the person presented to the world, which is a fitting reversal for men who had used their speech and appearance to manage how others saw them.

**A Confession With Nothing Behind It**

The words recorded here are the clearest possible admission that the message had been delivered and understood. Nobody says that he did not know what was being asked of him; the speaker names the two authorities he failed to obey, God and His Messenger, and laments the failure as the whole cause of his condition. The Book records similar speeches from the condemned in other chapters, where they acknowledge that they were warned and did not listen, and it does so to make one point: the reckoning does not involve information that was withheld. The community hearing the verse knew that this was exactly the complaint no one would be able to make, since the messenger had lived among them and the Book had been recited to them.

**Obedience Named as the Whole Failure**

The lament names obedience rather than faith, and the choice is worth attention. A person at the end may claim to have believed in his heart while being unable to act; the recorded speech does not take that route. It identifies itself as a failure to obey, which is a failure in conduct, in the arena where the community's own decisions were taking place. The Book's discipline is consistent in this respect: it links the acceptance of a message to the action that accompanies it, and it treats a profession unaccompanied by compliance as no profession at all. The sentence in the fire gives that principle its most economical statement.

**Why the Words Are Recorded**

The Book records this speech for the benefit of the people still living, and the sūrah's placement is deliberate. The words come immediately after a passage that has instructed the community in dress, speech, marriage and hospitality, and immediately before a passage about the leaders who misled their followers. The reader is shown the sentence that everyone in the fire will wish he could have said earlier, and the passage takes the hypothetical away by giving him the opportunity to say it now. The community that obeyed the commands of this chapter, and kept the small duties it prescribed, was answering the lament in advance.

**The Refrain That Applies to Every Generation**

The words are timeless in a way the Book intends. The same sentence will be spoken by every generation that receives a message and sets it aside, and its exact form names no era, no people and no particular sin. The Book states elsewhere that the regret expressed in the next world is universal among those who ignored their messengers, and in one chapter it describes the wrongdoer biting his hands in the fire and wishing he had followed the path of the messenger (25:27). The recurrence of the same regret in every account is a sign that the Book is describing a human condition rather than a historical incident, and the verse leaves the reader to decide whether he will be among those saying it or among those who acted on it in time.

**A Confession the Book Never Attributes to the Believers**

The Book records the regrets of the condemned in chapter after chapter, and in every case the regret is about action: they did not obey, they did not listen, they followed the wrong people, they wished for another chance. Nowhere does the Book record a condemned person saying that he had no information, or that the standard was unclear, or that no one told him. That absence is itself a statement. The community that received this sūrah had a prophet living among it, a book recited in its gatherings, and rules that had been spelled out in its own households. The passage gives those people the sentence they would not be able to use in their own defence, and it puts that sentence in the mouth of a man who is beyond help.
**The Difference Between Regret Here and There**

The crucial difference the passage sets out is not in the words but in the possibility of acting on them. In this world the confession of a mistake can be followed by a change of conduct, a request for forgiveness and a new beginning, and the Book offers that possibility repeatedly, even to the people whose crimes it has just described. In the next world the recognition arrives when the arena for action has closed, and the recognition has no effect on the condition. The verse therefore does not tell the reader that regret is useless. It tells him that regret is useful now, and it lets him see what it looks like at the hour when usefulness ends. The Book's readiness to publish the sentence tells the reader that the warning is meant to be effective in this life rather than merely accurate about the next.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:67

> And they will say, “Our Lord! We obeyed our leaders and elite, but they led us astray from the ˹Right˺ Way

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Speech From the Fire**

The verse continues the words of the condemned, and it moves from the confession of verse 66 to an explanation of how the failure came about. The speaker says that he obeyed his leaders and his elite, and that they led him away from the road. The Book records the same defence in other chapters, and it does so with a consistency that suggests it is describing something universal about how people end up in that condition: they followed someone. The second speech is therefore an attempt at mitigation, and the Book lets it stand as the condemned person's own account before it records the request for the leaders' punishment.

**Leaders and the Elite**

The two words the verse uses are not identical. The first covers those who hold authority in a community, the people whose decisions are obeyed because they can enforce them. The second describes the class of people whose standing is established by wealth, family or reputation, the ones whose example a society follows and whose approval people seek. Between them they cover the whole apparatus by which a community shapes its members: the man who commands and the man who is admired. The Book names both because the failure it describes is social rather than individual. The speakers are saying that they were not the originators of the error; they were the end of a chain.

**Following as the Human Default**

The Book treats the tendency to follow as a fact of human life rather than an aberration. It states in Sūrah al-Zukhruf that people will say on that day that they were only following the way of their ancestors, and it gives the reply that will be made to them. The Qur'an's own remedy for this condition is to make the object of following a matter of clear knowledge, and it repeatedly tells people to check the basis on which they are following anyone. What the verse shows is the result of a lifetime spent in that mode without examination. The speakers obeyed, and their obedience was real; what it lacked was any question about where it was leading.

**The Refusal to Accept the Excuse**

The Book records the excuse without accepting it. The same passage in another chapter reports the followers offering to take a double share of the punishment and being told that everyone's share is already settled. The Book's position is consistent in every version of the exchange: the existence of a leader who misled people does not transfer responsibility for the choice, and a follower who obeyed a command against God's is answerable for the obedience. The community of Medina lived with this principle in its own history, since some of its members had followed the leaders of the Quraysh against the Prophet before they changed, and they bore responsibility for what they had done before their change.

**What the Verse Says to the Leaders**

The speech is recorded in the presence of the leaders, since it is followed immediately by a request for their punishment. The Book therefore treats the responsibility of the one who misleads as heavier in its consequences, and it says so elsewhere when it describes the leaders of a community receiving a multiplied punishment for what their followers did at their direction (7:38). This is a warning with a long reach into the community's own life. Anyone who holds the position of a leader or enjoys the standing of the elite is told, in a verse that describes the reckoning, that the people who followed him will be standing beside him and that the account will not stop at the person who obeyed.

**A Sentence That Names a Real Danger**

The Book does not describe the danger of following in vague terms. It names leaders and the elite, which are the two groups that were actually shaping conduct in Arabia: the tribal chiefs whose authority was enforced and the notable families whose example set the tone of a city. The community of Medina had reordered its own leadership by giving the Prophet the claim described in verse 6 of this sūrah, and it had learned to judge a proposal by its source rather than by the standing of the person making it. The verse provides the reason the community's legislation on loyalty needed to be so explicit: the alternative to following the messenger is not freedom from following; it is following someone else.

**The Followers Who Were Not Blind**

The Book does not describe the speakers as people who had no way of judging what they were told. It has stated elsewhere that the message was confirmed by evidence they could see and that the messengers were supported by signs, and in this sūrah the community had watched its own leaders' predictions fail while the promise of God held. What the speakers describe is not ignorance but deference: they had the material to weigh the choices and they did not weigh them, because the standing of the people giving the commands settled the matter for them. The community of Medina had the reverse experience in the same years, since men of high standing among the Quraysh had told them that the Muslims would be wiped out, and the outcome had shown what their standing was worth.
**A Lament That Leaves No Route Out**

The sentence is a lament, and part of its force is that it offers no remedy. The speaker has already said in the verse before it that he wishes he had obeyed God and His messenger, and he now explains that he did obey, but someone else. The two speeches read together describe the whole story of a wasted life: a long habit of deference, a fixed destination and a moment of clarity when nothing can be changed. The Book puts that story in the mouth of the condemned so that the reader who is still in a position to revise his obedience will recognise the voice as his own before it is too late to do anything about it. The passage therefore asks a question of every reader who defers to a person rather than to the Book, and it asks it while the choice still has consequences.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:68

> Our Lord! Give them double ˹our˺ punishment, and condemn them tremendously.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Request and Its Shape**

The condemned ask for a double punishment to be imposed on the leaders who led them astray, and they ask for a curse that matches the scale of what was done. The request takes for granted two things that the Book states elsewhere as doctrine: that responsibility varies with the position a person occupied, and that a leader's guilt includes the consequences of the choices he shaped in other people. The Book grants the request as a principle, in a verse that reports God's answer to precisely this plea and states that the punishment of such leaders has been multiplied (7:38). What the condemned are asking for is not an escape from their own punishment but a proportionate accounting for the one who caused it.

**Why the Double Is Just**

The multiplication is not arbitrary severity. A leader who misleads a hundred people carries the harm of a hundred decisions, and the Book's accounting recognises that the effects of an authority's conduct extend far beyond his own acts. The same principle appears in the sanctions attached to the Prophet's household in this sūrah, where the women closest to the revelation are told that a public failing on their part would carry a doubled penalty, and where their obedience carries a doubled reward (33:30-31). Responsibility in the Book is scaled to the reach of a person's influence, which is why the two ends of a community's structure, its leaders and its leading families, are held to the heaviest accounts.

**The Limits the Book Places on Following**

The request for doubled punishment is answered in another chapter with a refusal to redistribute guilt: the followers are told that their share has already been multiplied and that they should not ask again (7:38). Read together with this verse, the Book's position is that both parties are answerable and each receives the measure of his own contribution. The follower is punished for following a command that led away from God, and the leader is punished for giving it and for the responsibility of having been followed. The Book never says that the second answers for the first, and the community's law never treated obedience to a person as a defence against a command of God.

**What the Request Reveals About Its Speakers**

The speakers do not ask for mercy in this verse. They ask for justice to be done to someone else, and they are willing to accept their own share as long as the person they blame receives his. The Book's portrayal of the moment is psychologically exact: a person in that condition is more concerned with the comparison between himself and his leader than with the state of his own soul. The passage earlier reported their wish that they had obeyed; here the regret expresses itself as a demand on someone else. The Book records the shift without comment, and the reader is left with a portrait of people who have lost everything and are still making calculations about who is worse off.

**What the Community Should Take From It**

The passage is a warning to the people who occupy positions of influence, and the community of Medina had a great many of them: tribal chiefs, wealthy merchants, elders whose opinions set the tone of meetings. Every one of them was being told that the people who followed him would one day stand beside him in the fire and ask for his punishment to be doubled. The sūrah has already given the community one leader whose claim on the believers was described in absolute terms and whose conduct the revelation corrected when it needed correcting. The contrast between that leadership and the leadership described here is the practical lesson of the passage, and the community's legislation about consultation, justice and the accountability of rulers grew from it.

**What the Wording Does Not Permit**

The request for a double punishment is not a complaint about injustice and it is not presented as one. The speakers accept the framework of the reckoning; they accept that their own punishment is due and they ask only that the person they followed receive what they consider a fitting share. The Book records the request without correcting the arithmetic, and the answer elsewhere confirms the multiplication as a principle of divine justice rather than as an angry demand. What the passage therefore establishes is that the community's leaders are not outside the reckoning and cannot shelter behind the obedience they received. A leader's followers were, in the language of this verse, one of the weights on his account.
**The Two Verses Together as a Portrait**

Read as one speech, verses 67 and 68 describe a complete failure. The first half explains how the speaker ended up where he is: he obeyed the wrong people. The second half shows what he does with the knowledge: he asks for the punishment of the people he obeyed and gives no thought to mercy for himself. The Book has already recorded the request of the same group in another chapter, where the followers wish for a second chance so that they might disown their leaders, and it gives no indication that the wish was granted (2:167). The community reading the two verses sees a pattern rather than an anecdote: deference becomes habit, habit becomes a path, and the path ends with a regret that asks for company instead of forgiveness. The community's judges were later instructed to weigh the harm a person caused as well as the act he committed, which is the same reasoning extended into the courts. The Book preserves the request without softening it, which lets the reader see what a lifetime of misplaced obedience finally looks like when it has nothing left to negotiate with.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:69

> O believers! Do not be like those who slandered Moses, but Allah cleared him of what they said. And he was honourable in the sight of Allah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Warning Taken From an Earlier Community**

The verse turns from the speech of the condemned to the conduct of the living, and it uses a precedent from the community of Moses. The believers are told not to imitate the people who slandered their prophet, and the verse states the outcome of that slander: God cleared him and kept his honour intact. The choice of example is significant for a sūrah that has spent its length on accusations, rumours and talk in Medina. The offenders being described are members of the community, and their target is the same man whose household the earlier verses defended. The Book holds up an older community's failure so that the current one can avoid repeating it.

**What Was Said About Moses**

The reports explain the accusation. Abū Hurayrah narrated that the Prophet said: 'Moses was a shy person and used to cover his body completely because of his extensive shyness. One of the children of Israel hurt him by saying, "He covers his body in this way only because of some defect in his skin, either leprosy or scrotal hernia, or he has some other defect."' The report continues that God wished to clear him, and it describes the incident at the stone which ended with a group of the Children of Israel seeing him and finding no defect in him, and it states that this was what the verse referred to (Ṣaḥīb al-Bukhārī 3404). The slander was petty in its content and destructive in its effects, which is the nature of the kind of talk this sūrah has been legislating against.

**Why the Rumour Took Hold**

The accusation attacked the one thing Moses could not easily disprove, since his modesty was the very reason he did not display the part of his body the accusers claimed was defective. That is the anatomy of slander as the Book treats it: a falsehood built to fit the target's character, so that the person's own virtue becomes the evidence against him. The people of Medina had done the same thing with the Prophet's household, where the rules of modesty and invisibility that protected the women were turned into material for stories about them. The verse warns the believers against joining that activity, and it does so by showing them a community that spent years attacking a prophet with a story about his body.

**How Allah Cleared Him**

The verse states that God cleared him, and the report describes how the clearing happened in a way that left no doubt among those who saw it. The Book's method here is characteristic of how it defends the honour of its messengers: not through a private reassurance to the accused man but through a public demonstration that settled the matter for the community. The verse then adds a statement about his standing with God, that he was held in honour before Him. The community is given both facts, and the combination is what allows a person who is slandered to continue working: the truth about him is settled with God even when his neighbours are not yet convinced.

**The Difference Between the Two Outcomes**

The verse draws a contrast between the slanderers and their target, and it does so without recording any punishment for the slanderers. The community that spread the story about Moses is described in the report as continuing in the ordinary course of its life, and what the Book records is the vindication of the accused. That is the shape of how the revelation often handles such matters: it settles the standing of the person attacked and leaves the attackers to their own account. The believers of Medina were being told that the story they carried about the Prophet's household would be answered the same way, in the sight of God, whether or not they witnessed the answer themselves.

**An Example From a Prophet's Own People**

The slander against Moses was not the work of an enemy nation. The report describes the accusers as belonging to the children of Israel, the community that had seen the sea divided and the mountain raised over it: people who had no grounds to doubt their prophet and who nevertheless spread a story about his body. The Book uses them as the warning for the believers of Medina because the temptation it describes is a family disease rather than a foreign one. Talk of this kind grows inside a community, from familiarity with a leader's daily life and from the illusion that those who live alongside him are entitled to comment on him. The verse tells the believers precisely where the precedent for that conduct is to be found, and it is not among the enemies of the faith.
**A Warning That Names the Offence Precisely**

The verse identifies the failing as slander, not as doubt or disagreement, and the distinction matters for a community that had legitimate criticisms to raise. Nobody was forbidden to ask a question, to argue a point of law or to raise a grievance; the community did all three, and the sūrah itself records disputes. What is being prohibited is the deliberate circulation of a damaging falsehood about a person's honour, and the Book attaches to it the example of people who attacked a prophet of God over something he could not answer. The warning was addressed to believers, which means the Book expects them to be the ones capable of the offence, in a chapter that has shown them to be capable of quite a lot. The verse thus gives the community a rule that outlasted the occasion: an accusation against a person of good standing is to be treated as a claim requiring proof, and the burden of it belongs to the person making it.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:70

> O believers! Be mindful of Allah, and say what is right

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command That Pairs Two Things**

The verse joins a state of the heart to an act of the tongue: be mindful of God, and say what is right. The pairing is the Book's usual method of holding the interior and the visible together, and here the connection is particularly close. Speech is the most common way a person injures others, and the sūrah has just spent several verses on slander, rumour, abusive talk, and the offensive conversation of guests who would not go home. What the verse prescribes is not silence and not politeness for its own sake but a specific quality of speech, and the two commands are placed side by side so that the discipline of the tongue is understood as a form of awareness of God rather than as a matter of manners.

**What "Right Speech" Covers**

The Arabic describes words that are true, straightforward and correctly aimed at the matter in hand. The term is related to the word the sūrah used earlier for the correct steering of a weapon or an arrow, which is a useful image for what the command asks: not the loudest statement, not the cleverest, but the one that lands where it should. Applied to a community, the instruction covers a great deal: telling the truth when a word could be withheld, speaking directly rather than insinuating, saying what is useful and then stopping, and refusing to dress up a judgement in language that hides it. The Book elsewhere commands the same thing in other words when it asks the believers to avoid suspicion and suspicion's talk.

**Speech as the Community's Main Instrument**

Medina's community ran on talk. Rulings were transmitted by speech, disputes were settled in conversation, the Prophet's teaching reached the community through words, and the stories that damaged the community were also words. The Book treats speech in this sūrah as a public resource that can be spent or wasted, and it repeatedly addresses it: the wives are told how to speak, the visitors are told when to stop talking, the rumourmongers are threatened, and the believers are told here to say what is right. What is being protected is a kind of honesty that makes a community governable, since a group whose members habitually misrepresent their views cannot deliberate about anything.

**Taqwā as the Guard on the Tongue**

The command to be mindful of God is placed first, and it supplies the motive that the second command needs. A person can manage his speech when he is being watched and relax it when he is not, which is exactly the weakness the earlier verse about hidden and revealed things addressed. Awareness of God closes that gap by removing the categories of watched and unwatched. The Prophet's reported counsel on the same subject makes the connection explicit when he is asked what brings people to the fire and he points to the tongue, and when he says that a person who believes in God and the last day should say what is good or keep silent. The Book's discipline is that the tongue is answerable like any other limb.

**Speech That Keeps Its Purpose**

The instruction does not require a person to say everything he thinks, and it makes no demand for constant talk. The Book's picture of good speech includes restraint: a person's words are measured by whether they are true and useful, and a large part of the discipline is knowing when the right thing to say is nothing at all. The community that had been subject to months of hostile talk could be tempted to answer every accusation and repeat every counter-argument, and the Book's command points in another direction. What is right in a moment may be a short sentence, a refusal to add to a story, or silence held in the presence of a provocation.

**Speech and the Survival of a Small Community**

The community of Medina was small enough that a single sentence could change a person's standing in it. Every one of the failures this sūrah has addressed had a component of speech: the discouragement of the waverers, the rumourmongers of the city, the stories about the Prophet's household, the guests whose conversation wore him down, the abusive talk about the weak that the Book warned against. The command to say the right thing is therefore not a general instruction about courtesy; it is the rule on which most of the chapter's other rules depend. A community whose members say what is right is one in which trust can be maintained, and the Book attaches to that practice a promise about deeds, sins and triumph in the verse that follows.
**The Reward Named in the Next Verse**

The sentence continues into the verse that follows, which promises that God will bless the believers' deeds and forgive their sins for those who obey Him and His messenger. The pairing tells the reader how the two commands in this verse are understood in the community's accounting: right speech and awareness of God are not treated as private virtues with no public effect but as part of the conduct on which the community's blessing depends. The sūrah has described a great deal of conduct that damaged the believers, and it now gives them a rule of speech that would prevent most of it, offered with a promise attached. The practice is difficult precisely because it is continuous, and the Book attaches it to awareness of God so that a person's speech is governed by something more constant than the mood of a gathering. A habit of accurate speech is also a habit of accurate thinking, since people generally end up believing what they have said often enough.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:71

> He will bless your deeds for you, and forgive your sins. And whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger, has truly achieved a great triumph

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Things God Does With the Believer's Work**

The verse states the outcome of the conduct commanded in the preceding verse, and it describes God doing two things: setting a person's deeds right and forgiving his sins. The first is the more surprising of the two. The Book does not say merely that God will accept the deeds or count them; it says that He will improve them, which acknowledges that human action as it comes from a person's hands is incomplete, mixed in motive, and often clumsier than the intention behind it. The community was being asked to say what is right and to be mindful of God, and the Book responds by promising to repair what the community's obedience produces, so that the effort is not wasted on its defects.

**The Forgiveness That Covers the Gaps**

The second half of the promise addresses what the community knew about itself. A sūrah that has documented the waverers, the rumourmongers and the small failures of ordinary believers could not leave the matter at the improvement of deeds; the gaps themselves needed an answer. The Book gives it in the same sentence, with the same verb it uses for the covering of faults. The pairing of improvement and forgiveness is a pattern that runs through the Book's treatment of believers: the command is given, the shortfall is acknowledged, and the remedy is provided by God rather than by an extra work demanded of the person. The community could go on with its obligations without being paralysed by the knowledge of its own record.

**Obedience Named as the Whole of It**

The verse then compresses the entire standard into a single phrase: whoever obeys God and His Messenger has achieved a great triumph. Nothing is added about lineage, wealth, seniority or the degree of a person's involvement in the community's great events. The people of Medina included men who had stood in the trench and men who had arrived in the city years later with nothing, and the sentence places them on the same footing before God. This is the same levelling that the sūrah performed in the long list of virtues for Muslim men and women, and it is the same standard by which the Prophet was corrected earlier in the chapter: the measurement is not the reputation a person holds but the command he obeys.

**The Triumph and How It Is Measured**

The word for triumph in this verse is the everyday Arabic word for winning the thing one has sought, and the Book uses it for the success of those who reach Paradise after being spared the fire. The community of Medina had just been through a period in which the word would naturally have been applied to the failure of the confederate siege, and the Book gives it a different subject: a person who obeys God and His messenger. The relocation matters because it identifies the contest the community was actually engaged in. The siege was real, and the deliverance was real, but the achievement that the Book calls the greatest triumph belongs to the person whose conduct is settled rather than to the army that marched home.

**Why the Verse Sits Between Command and Trust**

The passage returns to the pattern the sūrah established at its opening, where the commands were followed by the reminder that God is sufficient as a trustee. Here the commands are the conduct of speech and awareness set out in the previous verse, and the response is a promise about deeds and sins. Between the two, the community is given a complete arrangement: obligations, an accounting that takes account of weakness, and an outcome that is called a triumph. This is the same structure the Book used for the Prophet's household, the widows in the ruling at the start of the sūrah's middle section, and the community that had held the trench, and its repetition shows how the sūrah wants its readers to think about the relationship between what they do and what God has promised.

**A Triumph That Has Already Begun**

The Book describes the triumph of the obedient as something they have achieved rather than as something they will receive, and the tense deserves attention. The community that obeyed the commands of this chapter was told that it had already won, before any of the promises was visible and while the ruins of the siege were still standing outside the city. What the Book counts as the victory is the settled position of a person before God, which is not affected by the movements of armies or the calculations of neighbours. A community that understands this can lose a battle and still hold its ground, and the sūrah's own history shows what such a community was able to survive: an alliance of ten thousand men, a winter of hunger, and a season of rumour that might have destroyed it from inside.
**The Standard That Will Be Applied**

The final clause is general, which is the point. It says whoever obeys, and it does not restrict the sentence to the people who heard it in Medina. The community that had seen the Prophet's household corrected and its own members exposed was being given the one criterion that would be applied to everyone in every generation, including the people whose conduct the chapter has been describing. Nobody who has read this sūrah to its seventy-first verse can be in any doubt about what the requirement is, and the sentence states the reward in terms of the requirement rather than in terms of anything the community might have earned by its own importance.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:72

> Indeed, We offered the trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, but they ˹all˺ declined to bear it, being fearful of it. But humanity assumed it, ˹for˺ they are truly wrongful ˹to themselves˺ and ignorant ˹of the consequences˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Trust and Its Size**

The verse describes an offer made to the created order itself: the heavens, the earth and the mountains were presented with a trust, they declined it out of fear, and the human being took it on. The scale of the passage is the point of it. The mountains of the earth, which the Book elsewhere calls on to testify and describes as trembling at the revelation, are shown shrinking from a responsibility that a human being accepted. The community reading the verse is meant to feel the disproportion, and the feeling is supposed to produce seriousness rather than pride. What has been placed in human hands is heavier than anything in the visible world was willing to carry.

**What the Trust Is**

Commentators gave several readings of the trust, and the most persuasive of them take it as the whole of the religious obligation that a human being is free to accept or refuse. Some read it as faith itself, others as the acts of obedience, others as the rational capacity by which a person knows God and is accountable for the knowledge. The readings converge on the same structure: whatever it is, it involves the power to choose, since it could not have been offered and declined otherwise. The verse assumes a creature capable of carrying a responsibility that the rest of creation is not equipped to bear, which is also why the same Book describes human beings as raised to a dignity that other creatures do not share.

**The Fear of the Mountains and the Silence of the Human Being**

The response of the created order is not described as pride and not as refusal from resentment. The heavens, the earth and the mountains declined because they were afraid of what the trust would require. That is a striking detail, and the Book's treatments of the natural world support it: the mountains are described as being able to crumble at the revelation and as praising God with David, which means the Book presents them as aware of God's greatness in a way that human beings often are not. The irony of the passage is that the parts of creation that know the most were the ones that declined, while the creature that accepted the offer is described in the same verse as ignorant and unjust to itself.

**The Two Words the Verse Uses of Mankind**

The verse calls the human being wrongful toward himself and ignorant, and it places the two criticisms at the moment of acceptance rather than later. This is not a condemnation of the acceptance itself; the trust was not meant to be declined by everyone. The pair describes how the offer has been handled by human beings taken as a category, and the sūrah's own narrative provides the evidence: the community that wavered in the trenches, the household that needed rulings, the men who talked about delusion, the stories that damaged innocent people. The Book states that the gamble has frequently been mismanaged, and it does so in the middle of a passage about a community that was learning, slowly and with correction, to manage it.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Argument**

The chapter has been about obligations of every size: household duty, modesty, honesty in speech, generosity with a guest, loyalty to a covenant, the treatment of a neighbour's reputation. All of those are expressions of the one trust the verse names. The Book puts the subject in these terms at the point where it is closing, and the effect is to make everything it has legislated appear as part of a single responsibility that the created order watched men take on. A community that had spent a winter defending a trench and a spring legislating about how to behave in a house was being told what those two activities had in common.

**The Trust and the Freedom It Requires**

The offer described here only makes sense if the creature receiving it was free to accept or refuse, since a duty imposed without the possibility of refusal is not a trust but a compulsion. The Book builds everything that follows on that freedom: the existence of the hypocrites inside the community, the waverers in the trench, the men who asked to leave, and the possibility of repentance that remained open to all of them. The community of Medina lived with the consequences of the arrangement every day, in the form of members who carried their obligations carefully and others who did not, and the sūrah spends most of its length describing that difference. What the verse supplies is the account of why the difference exists at all: the responsibility was offered, and human beings took it.
**A Doctrine That Defines the Human Being**

The passage provides the Book's most compact account of what makes a human being different: not strength, since the mountains are stronger, and not knowledge, since the verse calls him ignorant. What distinguishes him is that he accepted a responsibility he was free to refuse, and the Book's promise and warning are built on that acceptance. The same Book states that each person's deeds are his own and that no one carries another's burden, which is the legal face of the trust. The community that understood this verse could not treat its religion as something it had inherited and could leave to others, and it could not complain that the requirement was unreasonable.

## Sūrah al-Aḥzāb 33:73

> so that Allah will punish hypocrite men and women and polytheistic men and women, and Allah will turn in mercy to believing men and women. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Outcome the Trust Was For**

The verse opens with the purpose of everything the passage described: the trust was offered and assumed so that the two outcomes would be separated. The Book's statement is not that God needed the arrangement in order to know who believed; it is that the reckoning is the point toward which the whole arrangement runs. The sūrah had begun with a command to the Prophet not to obey the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and it ends by naming those two categories as the recipients of punishment. The community's whole experience of the previous years, from the trench to the household rulings to the rumourmongers, sits inside that structure, and the final verse tells the reader where it was all leading.

**Hypocrisy and Polytheism Named Together**

The two groups are named in the plural forms that include men and women explicitly, which is the same pairing this sūrah used in the list of virtues and in the promise of the reward. The first group is the internal failure, the people who lived inside the believing community while working against it. The second is the external denial, the association of other beings with God. The Book places them together at the end because both are refusals of the same thing, and because the community had sustained injuries from each: an army outside its walls and a party of doubters inside. Naming them side by side prevents the community from imagining that one failure is more respectable than the other.

**Mercy Turned Toward Believing Men and Women**

The sentence then gives the other half, and it does so with a verb that describes a turning toward someone: God will turn in mercy to believing men and women. The same construction was used earlier in the sūrah, when the Prophet was told that the outcome for the hypocrites might be either punishment or a turning toward them in mercy (33:24). Its recurrence at the end shows what the Book has been offering throughout. The door that was left open for the waverers in the middle of the chapter is the door through which the believing community is described as entering here, and the mercy is named for men and women alike, exactly as the warning was.

**Why the Chapter Ends Here**

The sūrah's last verse closes a circle that the whole chapter has been drawing. It began with instructions to the Prophet and a statement about the two groups who would press him; it ends with the fate of those two groups and the mercy shown to the believers. Between the two it described a siege, a household, a set of marriages, a body of law, the duties of modesty and speech, and the trust that the created order declined. Each of those sections concerned the same question: who would carry the responsibility that had been accepted. The final sentence answers it with a distribution rather than with a description, and it leaves the reader at the point where all of the chapter's obligations become consequential.

**The Names That Close the Book's Chapter**

The verse ends with the names of forgiveness and mercy, and the ending is deliberate. A chapter that has described punishment, exposure, the collapse of an alliance and the fate of people whose faces are turned in the fire closes on the attributes that explain why any of it was tolerated for so long. The Book's severity and its mercy are presented as belonging to the same being, and the two names here are the same ones that appeared in the middle of the sūrah after the account of the men who wavered, and after the description of the Prophet's marriage. What the community received at the end of the chapter was a reminder of the character of the One whose commands it had been given.

**The Last Word and the First Instruction**

The chapter that opened with a command to be mindful of God and not to obey the disbelievers and the hypocrites closes with the fate of those two groups and the mercy shown to those who believed, which is the same division the sūrah has traced from its first verse. Between the two sentences it has legislated for adoption, inheritance, marriage, modesty, hospitality, speech, the treatment of neighbours, the protection of reputation, and the leadership of a community under attack. Every one of those rulings belongs to the trust the passage has just described, and the final verse states the accounting that will show who carried it. The community received the chapter as a body of instruction with a single destination, and its members were left in no doubt about what was at stake in the ordinary business of their lives.
**What the Sūrah Leaves Its Readers**

The community that had lived through the siege and the years after it was left with a body of instruction and a description of what the reckoning would look like. The final verse tells its members that the two outcomes are real and that the difference between them is not tribal, financial or social but the difference between believing and not believing, between carrying the trust honestly and carrying it as a formality. The mercy at the end is not a softening of that distinction but the reason a person can still act on it. The sūrah finishes where its own account of the community began: with a people who had accepted a responsibility and were now to be judged by what they did with it.
