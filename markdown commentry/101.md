# Sūrah al-Qāriʿah (Chapter 101) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Striking Calamity and the Lightness of the Scales**

Al-Qāriʿah, "The Striking Calamity" or "The Sudden Terror," is the one-hundred-and-first chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 11 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with three explosive, echoing, rhythmic strikes: *“Al-Qāriʿah! What is al-Qāriʿah? And what can make you know what is al-Qāriʿah?”* (101:1–3). *Al-Qāriʿah* signifies the catastrophic, deafening blow that strikes human ears and hearts with terrifying suddenness, knocking the universe out of its established order. In eleven concise, unforgettable lines, Sūrah al-Qāriʿah delivers two profound cosmic images: humanity transformed into scattered, fluttering moths, and the monumental mountain ranges reduced to carded, weightless flakes of colored wool. It establishes the ultimate judicial mechanism of the Hereafter: the weighing of human deeds upon the cosmic Scale (*al-Mīzān*), dividing humanity between the pleasant life of Paradise and the bottomless, fiery abyss of *Hāwiyah*.

**The Structural Architecture and the Motherhood of the Pit**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Qāriʿah moves across three distinct movements:
1. The Heavy Scales and the Pleasing Life (6–7): The judicial verdict of righteousness: *“Then as for him whose scales are heavy [with good deeds], he will be in a pleasant life (*fī ʿīshatin rāḍiyah*)!”*
3. The Light Scales and the Abyss of Hāwiyah (8–11): The tragedy of moral bankruptcy: *“And as for him whose scales are light, his mother / refuge will be an abyss (*fa-ummuhū hāwiyah*)! And what can make you know what that is? It is a Fire, intensely hot (*Nārun ḥāmiyah*)!”*

**The Motherhood of the Abyss: Ummuhū Hāwiyah**

The most terrifying psychological and poetic image in Sūrah al-Qāriʿah is contained in verse 9: *Fa-ummuhū hāwiyah* ("his mother will be the abyss"). Exegetes note that a mother (*umm*) is the ultimate sanctuary to which a frightened child runs for warmth, shelter, and protective embrace. For the wicked sinner whose scales are light of good deeds, the only sanctuary left to embrace him is *Hāwiyah*—the bottomless, blazing pit of Hell! The Fire becomes his "mother"—clutching him to its boiling breast and never letting him go. Sūrah al-Qāriʿah warns humanity to invest in heavy, substantial deeds of faith, prayer, and charity today, lest tomorrow we stand before the Scale with empty hands.

## Sūrah al-Qāriʿah 101:1

> The Striking Disaster

**A Name That Hits Before It Explains**

*Al-qāriʿah* stands alone as a sentence. No verb, no oath, no “and.” The word is a feminine active participle from *qaraʿa*, to strike so that a sound comes, to knock, to beat. A *qāriʿah* is that which slams and deafens. Early listeners heard a blow, not a calendar term. Some take it as one of the names of the Day of Judgment, alongside *al-ḥāqqah*, *al-ṭāmmah*, *al-ṣākhkhah*. The sūrah is Makkan, placed by many just after *Quraysh* (106) in order of revelation, and it takes its own name from this first word. Naming the Day by its sound is a method: people who will not think about justice may still flinch at a crash. The verse is that flinch, written.

**Not a Definition, a Blow**

Verses 2 and 3 will ask what it is and what will make you know. Verse 1 refuses to define. It names. Definition would let the mind file the event under “eschatology” and move on. A bare name with a definite article treats the event as already known in rumour and now pressed against the ear. The definite *al-* can mean the well-known striking, the striking you have heard of. Makkan preaching had already spoken of a day. This sūrah picks the acoustic quality of that day and makes it the title. Compare *“The Inevitable Hour!”* (69:1). Same syntax of shock: a noun slammed onto the recitation.

**Calamity in English, Strike in Arabic**

“The Striking Disaster” tries to keep both the hit and the ruin. *Qāriʿah* in ordinary speech can be a disaster that befalls a people. In this sūrah the disaster is not a local raid or a famine. The following verses will show people like moths and mountains like wool. The scale is the earth. Still, the root remains a knock. Doors are knocked. Hearts are knocked. The sky, in other sūrahs, is split. Here the first fact is auditory-tactile. A religion that only talks about inner peace has no organ for this verse. The Book begins this chapter with a blow because some truths arrive as blows. Many Makkan openings use *wa-* oaths. This one does not. The absence is a choice. An oath would place the Day among other signs. A lone vocable places the Day over the listener with no mediation. Calling it *al-qāriʿah* does not cancel *yawm al-dīn* or *al-sāʿah*. Each name is a pedagogy. *Dīn* stresses requital. *Sāʿah* stresses the appointed hour. *Qāriʿah* stresses the impact that cannot be talked down. A community that fears poverty more than that impact has inverted the names.

## Sūrah al-Qāriʿah 101:2

> What is the Striking Disaster

**A Question That Refuses a Small Answer**

*Mā al-qāriʿah* repeats the name and puts *mā* in front of it. *Mā* asks for the nature of a thing, not its location on a map. The question is God's, not a student's. It teaches the listener how large the gap is between the word he has just heard and any picture he can form. Similar questions structure other sūrahs: *“What is the Inevitable Hour?”* (69:2); *“What is the Night of Glory?”* (97:2). The pattern is: name, then “what is it?”, then “what will make you know?” The mind is not allowed to say “I have this.” The Day is not an item in a list of Makkan themes. It is an unknown that has been named only to show that the name is not possession.

**Repetition Is Emphasis, Not Stutter**

Some modern ears hear repetition as filler. Classical Arabic hears it as *taʾkīd* and *tahwīl*, confirmation and making huge. Preachers who paraphrase verse 2 into “this is a serious event” have replaced a question with a slogan. The sūrah wants a mind that does not yet have a picture, because the pictures in verses 4–5 will be strange, and the fire in verse 11 stranger.

**What Is It — Against Casual Eschatology**

People then and now speak of the last day the way they speak of weather: a topic, a threat for children, a date they do not believe. The question breaks that register. If you have to be asked what it is, you do not have it. Knowledge of the Hour in the Book is of two kinds: that it is coming, which is required faith, and when it is, which is withheld. *“People ask you about the Hour. Say, ‘That knowledge is only with my Lord’”* (7:187). Verse 2 is not asking for the clock. It is asking for the *māhiyyah*, the what-it-is, which also exceeds the creature. The following verses will give marks, not an essence. Tafsīr on 1–3 notes that the true nature of the *qāriʿah* is difficult or impossible to comprehend, which is why the next lines describe symptoms rather than a definition. That is accurate. A definition would be a kind of control. Symptoms — moths, wool — are what a human eye could bear to be told.

**How to Recite a Question God Asks**

The reciter is not confused. God is not confused. The question is a teaching tool aimed at the hearer. Tone should not be a quiz-show lilt. It should be the tone of a size being opened. After verse 1's blow, verse 2 is the first echo. The echo is interrogative. Together they say: you have been struck by a name; do you even know what hit you? The third verse will raise the same point by addressing “you” directly. This second verse still speaks of the thing in the third person, as an object too large to be owned by a “you” yet.

## Sūrah al-Qāriʿah 101:3

> And what will make you realize what the Striking Disaster is

**The Formula That Marks Creaturely Limit**

*Wa-mā adrāka mā al-qāriʿah* is a known Qur'anic mould: *mā adrāka mā X* — what has given you to know what X is? It appears with the Inevitable Hour (69:3), the Night of Glory (97:2), the crushing Fire in *al-Humazah* (104:5), the Sijjīn (83:8), and others. *Adrāka* is from *dirāyah*, a knowing that reaches. The question implies that nothing in your present equipment has delivered the knowledge. You are being told that you do not know, by the only one who does. The “you” is first the Prophet, as in many such addresses, and through him every listener. The Prophet's not-knowing of the essence is not a defect in prophecy. It is a boundary prophecy itself teaches. Because this formula admits a limit, verses 4–5 will not say “the *qāriʿah* is such-and-such a definition.” They will say: a day when people are like moths and mountains like wool. Those are *ʿalāmāt*, marks. Readers who treat verses 4–11 as a full science of the Hour have missed verse 3.

**Addressing the Prophet Without Reducing the Verse to Biography**

When God says *mā adrāka* to Muḥammad, He is not scolding him for ignorance in the ordinary sense. He is installing the Hour beyond even prophetic claim except as conveyed *waḥy*. *“They ask you as if you had full knowledge of it. Say, ‘That knowledge is only with Allah’”* (7:187). This verse is that doctrine in the vocative mould. Biographical curiosity — what exactly did he know that day — is the wrong hunger. The right hunger is to let the formula work on us: if he is told he has not been given to know the essence, we certainly have not.

**What Makes One Realize**

*Mā adrāka* can be heard as “what could possibly make you know?” The implied answer is: only God's further speech, and even that will be marks. Revelation is the only *idrāʾ*. Reason can infer that a just Lord will judge. Reason cannot produce moths and carded wool and a mother called abyss. Those arrive by this sūrah. Verse 3 is therefore also a claim about the necessity of revelation for last things. Natural theology does not reach the *qāriʿah*. It may reach a bare “judgment of some kind.” The strike, the moths, the wool, the scales, the *hāwiyah* — those are given. Verses 1–3 are one drum: name, what is it, what will make you know what it is. Only now will images be allowed. That delay is the pedagogy of *tahwīl*. A sūrah that opened with a landscape would have been a painting. This sūrah opened with a limit. Mā adrāka in 101:3 is that boundary installed inside a sūrah of images.

## Sūrah al-Qāriʿah 101:4

> ˹It is˺ the Day people will be like scattered moths

**Moths, Not an Army**

*Yawma yakūnu al-nāsu ka-al-farāsh al-mabthūth* — a day people are like moths scattered. *Farāsh* are moths or butterflies, light, many, drawn to a flame, without formation. *Mabthūth* is spread, strewn. The simile is humiliation of human self-image. People like to imagine the last day as a parade of tribes, banners, ranks. This verse gives insect panic. *“On the Day you see it, every nursing mother will abandon what she is nursing, and every pregnant woman will deliver her load, and you will see people as if they are drunk, though they are not drunk”* (22:2). Same bewilderment. *“They will emerge from the graves as if they were swarming locusts”* (54:7) is a cousin image, though that verse is often tied more tightly to the rising itself. Here the moths are the human condition under the strike.

**Accusative Day and an Implied Verb**

The Arabic *yawma* is accusative. Grammarians supply a verb: “mention” (*udhkur*), as Ālūsī notes, or “it strikes” from the root of *qāriʿah*, as al-Zamakhsharī suggests: it strikes on a day when… The implied verb keeps verse 4 from being a new independent definition. It is still the *qāriʿah*, now given a time-face. The strike is a day, and the day's people are moths. Syntax and image work together: even the grammar of the sentence is a blow that needs a verb to be supplied, as the event will supply a reality the mind cannot conjugate in advance.

**Scattered Means No Leader, No Path**

Moths do not keep ranks. *Mabthūth* denies the social structures people trusted: clan, city, market, even congregational lines. Each moth is a small self in a swarm that is not a community. The Book elsewhere restores ranks for judgment — rows, books in right or left hands. This verse is an earlier moment, the shock before the sorting, or the sorting experienced from inside panic. Either way, pride of assembly dies. The *jamʿ* that horses penetrated in *al-ʿĀdiyāt* is here a swarm with no centre the moths can choose.

**Why Moths and Not Lions**

A lion simile would have preserved majesty. Moths are drawn to destruction by a tiny love of light. That may be extra, but it is not alien: people of *takāthur* are drawn to glints. The primary sense remains lightness and scatter. Mountains in the next verse will lose weight; people here have already lost mass in the simile. The *qāriʿah* inverts the weights. The moth-image must not be used to cancel responsibility. Panic is the atmosphere, not the verdict. Verses 6–11 will still weigh deeds. Being like a moth is what the Day feels like, not a claim that no one knew the way in the life before.

## Sūrah al-Qāriʿah 101:5

> and the mountains will be like carded wool

**The Firm Things Go Soft**

*Wa-takūnu al-jibālu ka-al-ʿihni al-manfūsh* — the mountains will be like wool carded, teased, fluffed apart. *ʿIhn* is coloured wool; *manfūsh* is pulled until the fibres separate. A mountain in ordinary life is the emblem of staying. The Qur'an itself uses mountains as pegs: *“He placed onto the earth firm mountains so it would not shake with you”* (16:15); *“He placed onto the earth firm mountains, standing high”* (21:31). This verse takes the emblem and cards it. What held the earth will not hold itself. *“On the Day the earth and the mountains will shake violently, and the mountains will be like dunes of shifting sand”* (73:14); *“when the mountains are blown away”* (77:10); *“when the mountains are set in motion”* (81:3); *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the mountains. Say, ‘My Lord will blast them into dust’”* (20:105). Verse 5 is that dossier in one simile.

**Wool Is Homely on Purpose**

Carded wool is women's and shepherds' work, a domestic image. The sūrah could have said exploding rock. It said wool. The homely word makes the unthinkable thinkable without making it small. God names Himself as the one whose “hands” will do that to peaks. The colour in *ʿihn* (some lexica: wool of mixed colours) may hint at the various rocks looking like dyed fibres once the structure is gone.

**People as Moths, Mountains as Wool**

Verse 4 and verse 5 are a pair. The moving things (people) become weakly flying. The still things (mountains) become weakly sitting, a pile of fibre. Together they say: no created refuge. You cannot hide in the crowd (moths are a crowd and still helpless). You cannot hide in the landscape (the landscape is wool). *“On the Day the earth will be changed to another earth and the heavens as well”* (14:48) is the same loss of the old map. *Al-Qāriʿah* gives the loss in two pictures a child can remember. That is Makkan method. Carding is not random mess; it is a known process that unmakes a felted mass. *“The earth will shine with the light of its Lord, the record will be laid open, the prophets and witnesses will be brought forward”* (39:69). A theology that only sees destruction and not the court that follows has stopped at verse 5 and ignored 6–11.

**What This Does to Human Building**

People build against mountains, on mountains, of mountain stone. The verse does not forbid building. It forbids treating the built and the geological as the last shelter. *“Do you feel secure that He Who is in the heaven will not cause the earth to swallow you up as it quakes?”* (67:16). Wool is a quiet word for swallow. Recite it slowly. The tongue should feel the softness that the eye of that Day will see in what is now granite. Carded wool is the homely version of that blast; both unmake the pegs of 16:15.

## Sūrah al-Qāriʿah 101:6

> So as for those whose scale is heavy ˹with good deeds˺

**After the Strike, a Balance**

*Fa-ammā man thaqulat mawāzīnuhu* — as for the one whose scales have become heavy. The *fāʾ* moves from cosmic pictures to personal outcome. Moths and wool were the world. Scales are the person. *Mawāzīn* is plural of *mīzān*, a balance. In other verses *mīzān* is the cosmic and legal balance God set: *“He raised the heaven and set the balance”* (55:7); *“Indeed, We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and with them We sent down the Scripture and the balance”* (57:25). The plural here is the weighing of a life, each person meeting weights. *“We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least”* (21:47). *“Those whose scale is heavy, they are the successful”* (7:8; 23:102). Verse 6 belongs to that family.

**Heavy with What**

The translation adds “with good deeds.” The Arabic says only “whose scales are heavy.” Tafsīr fills the pans with *ḥasanāt*, and that filling is correct, because a scale heavy with sins is not the success this contrast is about. The next verses will name the light scale as the path to the abyss. So heaviness here is the heaviness of accepted good. Faith itself has weight. The famous card of *lā ilāha illā Allāh* in the hadith of the scrap of paper is reported in the Musnad and the Sunan; the point, however it is graded in a given chain, matches this verse: some goods outweigh piles. Do not invent numbers. Do hold that God can make a small deed heavy and a large show light. *Man* is singular; *mawāzīn* is plural. Each individual meets a weighing that may be spoken of as multiple pans, multiple deeds, or the majesty of the instrument. Most say the plural is for the deeds being weighed or for the greatness of the event. The experience is individual. No clan's total rescues a light pan.

**So As For — A Fork, Not a Blend**

*Ammā* begins a two-way split that verse 8 will complete. The sūrah does not offer a third pile called “average.” People like a foggy middle. The Book, at this speed, does not. There will be more nuance in other texts about those who mix deeds. This sūrah is a first teaching: heavy or light. Live so that the pan goes down.

**Heaviness Is the Opposite of Moths**

Moths are light. The saved person is heavy. The sūrah's images talk to each other. To be heavy on that Day is not to be a mountain of ego; mountains are wool. It is to have a weight God counts. The believer seeks that weight in secret charity, kept prayer, and a tongue that does not card other people's honour. Heavy mawāzīn in 101:6 is that justice felt as mass, not as a slogan.

## Sūrah al-Qāriʿah 101:7

> they will be in a life of bliss

**A Life, Not a Moment of Relief**

*Fa-huwa fī ʿīshatin rāḍiyah* — he is in a life that is pleasing. *ʿĪshah* is a way of living, a livelihood of the whole person. *Rāḍiyah* is an active participle: a life that is pleased, or a life with which the liver is pleased. Both directions of pleasure are wanted: the person is pleased, and the life itself is a pleasing thing. The verse does not yet say “garden.” It says a quality of life. That is wider and in some ways stronger. Gardens in other verses give water, shade, fruit, spouses. This verse gives the inner and outer fit of a life that no longer fights its Lord or itself.

The moths were not content. The lover of gains in *al-ʿĀdiyāt* was not content. *Riḍā* is the rare state the Book pairs with God's own pleasure: *“Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him”* (98:8; 5:119; 9:100). A *ʿīshah rāḍiyah* is the human side of that pair spoken as environment. It is not numbness. It is a life that has nothing left to chase in the *takāthur* sense. The heaviness of the scale has produced lightness of heart. That paradox is the local theology: heavy pan, easy living.

The *fāʾ* in *fa-huwa* is the *fāʾ* of consequence. Heaviness is not a badge worn in hell. It causes a life. Deeds are not ornaments; they are causes under God's acceptance. The verse is short because the contrast with verse 9 needs speed. Short is not thin. “Life of bliss” in English can sound like a travel ad. *ʿĪshah rāḍiyah* is a technical mercy: a pleasing mode of being, ongoing. The tense is not a flash. *Huwa fī* is a located present in that world.

Makkan sūrahs often delay furniture. *Al-Qāriʿah* will spend more words on the abyss than on the bliss. That imbalance is a warning style, not a claim that the garden is poorer than the fire is rich. The fire needs more description because pride does not believe it. The bliss needs only a quality-word because the soul already hungers for *riḍā* even when it chases the wrong objects. Name the true object as a pleasing life, and the hunger knows.

**Who Is “He”**

*Huwa* goes back to *man* in verse 6, the one whose scales are heavy. Individual again. Families may enter together by other verses' mercy, but this line keeps the unit of salvation as the weighed person. A community can help a person become heavy. It cannot step on the pan for him at the end. The life of bliss is therefore prepared now as a personal mass of accepted work, then enjoyed as a personal — and, elsewhere, communal — living. This verse's job is the personal result. Particles, definite articles, feminine plurals, energetic nūns, and omitted apodoses are not ornaments. They are how the threat or the oath actually works. A reciter who only translates into a slogan has dropped the tools. Classical iʿrāb on a verse like this is not a hobby for specialists. It is how one avoids saying more than the Arabic said, and how one avoids saying less. Where the translation uses brackets, those brackets are tafsīr.

## Sūrah al-Qāriʿah 101:8

> And as for those whose scale is light

**The Other Pan**

*Wa-ammā man khaffat mawāzīnuhu* — and as for the one whose scales have become light. The fork begun in verse 6 is completed. *Khaffa* is to be light, unweighty, easy to lift. In this court, easy to lift is ruin. The world taught the opposite: travel light, keep options, do not be weighed down by duty. The last day reverses the proverb. Lightness is not freedom. It is lack of accepted good. *“Those whose scale is light, they will have doomed themselves for wrongfully denying Our signs”* (7:9); *“And those whose scale is light, they are the ones who have lost themselves, remaining in Hell forever”* (23:103). Verse 8 is the same fork's first half; verse 9 will name the home.

**Light Because Empty of Good, Not Because Sins Weigh Nothing**

A question in kalām and tafsīr: are sins themselves placed as weight, or does the pan of good simply fail to go down? Both pictures exist in the tradition. This verse does not draw the diagram. It states the outcome: the scales, as they concern this person's success, are light. A life of show, of *kanūd*, of moth-like scattering in this world already, produces a pan that does not count. Some deeds look heavy here — wealth, sons, raids — and weigh nothing there if they were not for God. *“We will turn to whatever deeds they did and make them scattered dust”* (25:23). Dust is light. Wool is light. Moths are light. The sūrah's images have been training the ear for this adjective. *Ammā… wa-ammā* is a closed choice in this sūrah's rhetoric. Other texts speak of people of the heights, or of a mixed record. Those texts are true in their places. Importing them here to soften verse 8 is a refusal of this sūrah's job. This sūrah wants a listener who fears a light pan tonight. Again *man* singular, *mawāzīn* plural. The light ones are still each a person. Mass disbelief does not average the pans. A culture can be light together and still be weighed apart. That is why reform of the self is not delayed until the culture is heavy. The moth-swarm will not be weighed as a swarm. The verse uses a perfect verb: *khaffat*, have become light. The becoming happened in the life that already ended. Nothing in the line suggests a surprise change of mass at the last second without a cause in works and faith. Mercy can still forgive, and God can still make a late repentance heavy. *“And those whose scale is light, they are the ones who have lost themselves”* (23:103)

## Sūrah al-Qāriʿah 101:9

> their home will be the abyss

**A Mother Called Hāwiyah**

*Fa-ummuhu hāwiyah* — his mother is *hāwiyah*. *Umm* is mother, source, the place one returns to, the thing that embraces a child. To call the abyss a mother is a terrible kindness of language: the only embrace left is a fall. *Hāwiyah* is from *hawā*, to fall, to drop. It is a pit that is itself a falling. The English rendering of this verse (101:9) captures the refuge-sense of *umm* without the shock of “mother.” Both should be held. The one whose scale is light is not only sent to a prison. He is returned to a parent that is a drop. The image inverts the first refuge of human life.

A house can be left. A mother, in the old idiom, is origin and return. *Umm al-qurā* is Makkah as mother of towns. *Umm al-kitāb* is the source of the Book. Here *umm* is the final belonging of a weightless person. He belongs to a fall. Commentators also say *umm* can mean the brain, the *umm al-raʾs*, so that the line would mean his skull-crown is a fall — he is thrown on his head. That reading exists. The mother-as-refuge reading is stronger because the sūrah is naming a dwelling, and verse 10 will ask what it is, as one asks about a place. Either way the body is not honoured.

“Their home will be” in English makes the abyss real estate. Arabic makes it kinship. Identity after the weighing is not “I was a merchant of Quraysh.” It is “my mother is a pit.” That is why the verse is short and unforgettable. People spend lives building names. This line replaces the name with a relation to a fall. *“Hell will be their resting place”* (3:197) and similar verses use beds and dwellings. This verse uses a mother. It is among the harshest metaphors in the last *juzʾ*.

A light scale and a falling mother are one physics. What has no weight falls. The verse is almost tautological in a physical parable, and that is its justice: the outcome matches the mass. No extra cruelty needs to be imagined on top of the match. The fire in verse 11 will add heat to the fall. Verse 9 is the gravity. Together they are a complete ruin: drop and burn.

**Do Not Soften Umm**

Some well-meaning teachers skip “mother” because it hurts family feeling. The hurt is the point. The Book takes the safest word in a child's vocabulary and yokes it to a pit so that a grown man will flinch. Flipping the metaphor into a joke is a different sin. Recite it as a sentence that should not be easy in the mouth. Then go back to verse 6 and work for heaviness while the pans are still in this world. Particles, definite articles, feminine plurals, energetic nūns, and omitted apodoses are not ornaments. They are how the threat or the oath actually works. A reciter who only translates into a slogan has dropped the tools. Classical iʿrāb on a verse like this is not a hobby for specialists. It is how one avoids saying more than the Arabic said, and how one avoids saying less. Where the translation uses brackets, those brackets are tafsīr.

## Sūrah al-Qāriʿah 101:10

> And what will make you realize what that is

**The Formula Returns, Now Aimed at the Pit**

*Wa-mā adrāka mā hiyah* — and what will make you know what it is? The same mould as verse 3, but the object has changed. There it was the *qāriʿah*. Here it is *hiyah*, “she/it,” the *hāwiyah*. The sūrah teaches that not only the Day as a whole exceeds you, the particular home of the light-scaled exceeds you too. People think they know fire because they have burned a finger. This question denies that a finger is enough. The next verse will still use the word fire, but only after the denial. Knowledge of Hell is revealed knowledge, not kitchen knowledge.

*Hiyah* is a feminine pronoun standing for *hāwiyah* or for the *umm*. The shortness is a gasp. After “his mother is a *hāwiyah*” the recitation does not rush to a textbook label. It asks again. The pedagogy of verses 1–3 is reused so that the second half of the sūrah has its own awe, not merely a leftover awe. A preacher who explains Hell with too much confidence, as if he had walked it, has not recited verse 10.

Speech about other people's damnation can become cheap. This question makes cheap speech look ignorant. You do not even know what “that” is. Fear it for yourself; do not use it as a stone. The Prophet is again the first “you,” and his not being given the full essence is a limit we inherit. We are given enough to flee, not enough to gloat.

The sūrah now has two unknowns: the Strike and the Abyss. In between sit moths, wool, heavy, light, bliss. The structure is: huge unknown, marks, fork, second unknown, last mark (fire). That is a complete short sūrah. Verse 10 is the hinge of the last movement. Without it, verse 11 would sound like a simple synonym. With it, verse 11 is an approximation offered after a confession of inexactness.

**Realization Will Not Be Theoretical**

*Mā adrāka* in this life is answered by revelation. In the next life the light-scaled will know by entry. The question is therefore also a delay: you may refuse to know now; you will not refuse then. The wise take the revealed approximation (scorching Fire) as enough to turn. The foolish wait for the empirical class, which is the *hāwiyah* itself. Verse 10 is the last courtesy before the approximation is given. Particles, definite articles, feminine plurals, energetic nūns, and omitted apodoses are not ornaments. They are how the threat or the oath actually works. A reciter who only translates into a slogan has dropped the tools. Classical iʿrāb on a verse like this is not a hobby for specialists. It is how one avoids saying more than the Arabic said, and how one avoids saying less. Where the translation uses brackets, those brackets are tafsīr.

## Sūrah al-Qāriʿah 101:11

> ˹It is˺ a scorching Fire

**Nār Ḥāmiyah After a Question**

*Nārun ḥāmiyah* — a fire intensely hot. Two words close the sūrah. *Ḥāmiyah* is from *ḥamiya*, to be burning hot, to rage with heat. The translation “scorching Fire” is right. After verse 10's “what will make you know,” these two words are not a claim to have mapped the pit. They are the permitted approximation. It is fire, and the heat is extreme. Arabic could have added layers of furniture. The sūrah chooses heat as the one quality. The *qāriʿah* struck; the *hāwiyah* burns. Sound at the opening, temperature at the close.

Hell is *nār* in many verses, *jaḥīm*, *saʿīr*, *ḥuṭamah*. Each name is a face. *Ḥāmiyah* stresses temperature rather than crushing (that will be *al-Humazah*) or blazing brand. The light-scaled person's mother-pit is, at least, a heat that does not relent. *“Taste then, for you had thought you would not be brought back to Us”* is another sūrah's taunt. Here there is no taunt-sentence. There is a predicate. The abyss *is* a scorching fire. Identity, not simile. Verse 4 used “like” for moths; verse 5 used “like” for wool. Verse 11 does not say “like fire.” It says fire. The similes were for this world unmade. The last home of the loser is not “like” something. It is the thing.

Bliss had one short verse. Makkan warning often weights the close toward fear. That is not because mercy is smaller. It is because the audience of an early sūrah is an audience that does not yet fear. Ending on heat leaves the ear burned. Recitation in tarāwīḥ that chatters into the next sūrah without a pause has wasted the two words.

*Al-Humazah* will say the fire *rages over the hearts*. This verse does not. Keep them distinct. Here the fire is the identity of the *hāwiyah*, a place-heat. There the fire is specified as reaching the *fuʾād*. Both can be true of one Hell. This sūrah's last job is to answer “what is she?” with heat, so that “mother” cannot be heard as a cool dark well. A fall into a well might be imagined as quiet. *Ḥāmiyah* refuses quiet.

**The Whole Arc in Eleven Verses**

Name of the blow; what is it; you do not know; people as moths; mountains as wool; heavy scales; pleasing life; light scales; mother-pit; you do not know; scorching fire. That is a complete eschatology for a memory that must carry it without a book in hand. The sūrah is early teaching in its most portable form. Acting on it means working for weight now. The fire is not a metaphor for bad moods. The verse did not say metaphor. It said *nār ḥāmiyah*. Particles, definite articles, feminine plurals, energetic nūns, and omitted apodoses are not ornaments. They are how the threat or the oath actually works.
