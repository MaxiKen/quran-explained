# Sūrah al-Raḥmān (Chapter 55) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:1

> The Most Compassionate

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Chapter That Begins With a Name**

Most sūrahs open by addressing a listener, issuing a command, or swearing an oath. This one opens with a single word standing alone as a sentence: *al-Raḥmān*, the Most Compassionate. Arabic permits a nominal sentence whose predicate is understood, and the effect is that the Name carries the whole weight by itself. Nothing is said yet about what mercy does. The sūrah first establishes who mercy is, then spends the rest of its length showing what mercy has done. The order is the argument. Teaching, creating, measuring, feeding — all of it arrives as evidence, and the first word states the claim that the evidence exists to prove. That claim is also the title of the chapter, and in Makkah it was a contested title. The idolaters had been told to bow before the Most Compassionate and had answered with a question instead of a prostration: "What is 'the Most Compassionate'?" (25:60). The sūrah does not answer with a definition. It answers with a list of gifts.

**A Name No Creature May Carry**

Al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm grow from the same root, r-ḥ-m, and they are not interchangeable. Al-Raḥīm names mercy in action, and it can be predicated of a human being. Al-Raḥmān names mercy as identity, and it cannot be predicated of anyone but God. Arabic morphology backs the distinction: the pattern *faʿlān* expresses fullness and overflow rather than a single act, so the Name does not say that God happens to be kind; it says that mercy is what He is. The Book treats the two Names as one Lord under two aspects: "Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names." (17:110). The verse settles a dispute among the earliest Muslims about which Name to use in prayer by refusing to choose, and it ends the argument by naming both. The sūrah then does something more demanding. It takes the Name its audience had rejected and shows, favour by favour, how much it has given them.

**Mercy Recorded Before Anything Was Made**

The sūrah's opening Name is not a mood. It is a written commitment, and the Prophet ﷺ described when it was written: "When Allah completed the creation, He wrote in His Book, which is with Him above the Throne: My mercy prevails over My wrath." (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3194; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2751). The record is placed above the Throne, which is the seat of rule, and its content puts mercy ahead of anger in the order of divine action. The Qur'an states the same priority in the words Allah spoke to Mūsā: "My mercy encompasses everything." (7:156). Nothing is left outside that sentence, not the believer, not the sinner, not the animal or the plant. What the anger of God does is measured and targeted; what His mercy does is total in scope and prior in time. A chapter that begins with *al-Raḥmān* is therefore not beginning with a soft word. It is beginning with the governing principle of the whole created order, announced before a single creature is mentioned.

**The Compassionate Occupies the Throne**

Mercy in this sūrah is not opposed to authority; it is authority in action. The Qur'an joins the Name directly to the Throne: "the Most Compassionate, ˹Who is˺ established on the Throne." (20:5), and again: "˹He is˺ the Most Compassionate! Ask ˹none other than˺ the All-Knowledgeable about Himself." (25:59). The second verse is instructive because it turns a question about the throne into a question about the One seated on it, and it sends the asker to God for the answer. Early scholars affirmed the words and left the manner of the seating to God; later theologians read it as mastery and control. Both readings leave the sūrah's point untouched: the ruler of the heavens is the Compassionate, so the exercise of power is a form of care. In the list that follows, the sun and moon keep time, the sky is lifted, the balance is set, and the earth is spread out — all of it administration, all of it mercy to creatures who could not survive without it.

**A Title That Rests on a Weak Report**

Many Muslims know this chapter as "the Bride of the Qur'an," and the title is usually traced to a saying attributed to the Prophet ﷺ: "For everything there is a bride, and the bride of the Qur'an is Sūrat al-Raḥmān." The wording is beloved and the report behind it is not sound. Al-Albānī classed it as *munkar*, an objectionable narration, in *Silsilat al-Aḥādīth al-Ḍaʿīfah* (1350), and the scholars who examined the virtues attributed to particular sūrahs found no authentic chain for it. The chapter does not need the title. Its own opening word, a Name reserved to God alone, is a stronger honour than a bride's; and its text has been recited in every mosque since the first generation without depending on a report about its beauty. The sound material about this sūrah is of a different kind: the Prophet ﷺ did recite it, and the response he received for it was, by his own account, better than the response of the people around him.

**A Name Addressed to People Who Had Sinned**

The mercy announced in the first word is not reserved for the deserving, and the Book says so in language that leaves no room for doubt: "Say, ˹O Prophet, that Allah says,˺ 'O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins. He is indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.'" (39:53). The address is to people who have already gone too far, and the Name used of God in the verse that follows is the same one that opens this sūrah. That is what makes the opening word a promise rather than a description. If the chapter had begun by listing obedience, only the obedient would have read it as good news. By beginning with the Name, the sūrah puts the entire created order — sun, moon, herbs, trees, water, ships, and both multitudes of listeners — under a mercy that is already in place before anyone has done anything to deserve it. The rest of the chapter then asks what has been done with it.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:2

> taught the Quran,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Deed Named After the Name**

The Name is followed immediately by a verb, and the verb is not *created* or *commanded* but *taught*. Arabic gives this verb two objects, a person and a thing, and the verse states only the thing: the Quran. The learners appear in the next two verses, first as a species and then as speakers. The delay is not carelessness. It puts the Book in view before the people who receive it, so that revelation is presented as something God does before it is presented as something humans are given. That is a claim about priority. In the order of the sūrah, the mercy named in the first verse expresses itself first as instruction, and the physical world that fills the middle of the chapter comes after. The Quran makes the same point about its own arrival: "Read, ˹O Prophet,˺ in the Name of your Lord Who created—created humans from a clinging clot." (96:1–2). In the first verses revealed, the command to read stands in front of the reminder that the reader was made.

**A Book Outranks a Harvest**

Every blessing listed in this chapter is real: fruit, dates, grain, fragrance, water, ships. The ranking is nevertheless fixed by the first item, and the first item is a book. Guidance is named before provision because provision alone cannot tell a creature what it is for. Food keeps an animal alive; the Quran tells a human being why being alive matters. The difference becomes concrete in the Makkan years, when the first Muslims were taught to recite what they received while their circumstances were difficult. They were the poorest and least secure people in the city, and the gift named first in this sūrah belonged to them completely. The sūrah does not treat revelation as one blessing among many of equal size. It treats it as the blessing that makes the rest legible, because a mind that knows the Giver receives bread and dates and water as gifts rather than as accidents.

**An Answer to the Charge That a Man Taught Him**

Makkan opponents had an explanation for the Quran that avoided God altogether, and the Book quotes it: "And We surely know that they say, 'No one is teaching him except a human.'" (16:103). The reply given there is linguistic — the man they point to speaks a foreign tongue, while the Quran is Arabic of unmatched clarity. Some commentators held that this verse of al-Raḥmān supplies the other half of the reply. The question of who teaches is answered by the first word of the sūrah: the Compassionate teaches. The charge assumed an ordinary chain of instruction, a tutor passing text to a student. The sūrah answers by naming the teacher before it names the lesson, and by placing the teaching of the Book in the same breath as the creation of the sun and the setting of the balance. Sources agree that the claim of human instruction was known in Makkah; the sūrah's opening verses deny it without arguing about intermediaries.

**Made Easy, Which Is Itself a Mercy**

The Quran describes its own accessibility as a deliberate act rather than an accident of style, and the chapter before this one repeats the point four times as a refrain: "And We have certainly made the Quran easy to remember. So is there anyone who will be mindful?" (54:17). The same claim appears in another sūrah: "Indeed, We have made this ˹Quran˺ easy in your own language ˹O Prophet˺ so perhaps they will be mindful." (44:58). Memorability is designed, not incidental. Reciters carry the whole Book in their memory, and the community's methods of teaching have relied on that for fourteen centuries. The Prophet ﷺ set the value of the activity plainly: "The best of you are those who learn the Quran and teach it." (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5027). Learning and teaching are named together as one honour, which is what this verse implies when it credits the teaching of the Quran to God and then hands the practice to human beings.

**Teaching Before Creating**

The order of the first four verses is worth stating plainly: mercy, then teaching, then creation, then speech. Many accounts of humanity begin with the body. This one begins with what the body is for. The Qur'an elsewhere describes Adam being given the names of things as his privilege, a rank that made the prostration of the angels meaningful. Knowledge precedes obligation across the sūrah's opening, and it precedes it in revelation as well: the Book was taught before the laws were imposed. That sequence defines the divine method as instruction rather than experiment. A person is told what is true, then asked to live by it. When the later verses of this chapter ask the two multitudes which favour they deny, the question assumes a creature that has been informed. Being taught is the first item on the list, and denial is a response to information, not to ignorance.

**A Finished Verb Describing a Gift That Continues**

Arabic uses the past tense here, *ʿallama*, taught. The form states an act that is complete, and its completeness is the point: the Quran has already been given, and nothing further is being withheld. A reader waiting for a second revelation will wait in vain, because the verb is closed. Yet the same construction in Arabic can describe an established fact rather than a moment in time, which is how the grammarians explain the tense of the surrounding verses — created, taught, set the balance — describing what God does as a settled feature of the world. The practical effect of the finished verb is that the learning is now the community's work. The Prophet ﷺ described who does that work, and the answer is not the scholars alone: the best people are those who learn and then teach. The Book that was taught to one man in Makkah is carried forward by students who become teachers, generation after generation, without a new revelation. The verse names the act that started the chain and leaves it running.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:3

> created humanity,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation Stated After Instruction**

The verse places the making of human beings after the teaching of the Quran, and the sequence is not accidental. Revelation is described as if it were waiting for a creature able to receive it. The Book presents humanity as made for knowledge, not merely capable of it; the capacity is the point of the construction. Other passages assemble the same two facts in a different order. In the sūrah of the Clot, the command to read is followed at once by the reminder that the reader was made from a clinging clot, so that instruction and creatureliness appear side by side. Here instruction comes first and creatureliness second. Both arrangements deny that humans are simply another kind of animal with an unusual brain. The sūrah describes a being whose intellect is not a by-product of the body but a reason for it, and the favours listed later — speech, the balance, the harvest — are the equipment of that purpose rather than substitutes for it.

**The Womb Carries a Name Derived From the Merciful**

The root r-ḥ-m gives Arabic three words at once: *raḥmah*, mercy; *raḥim*, the womb; and *al-Raḥmān*, the Name that opens this sūrah. The overlap is not a coincidence of letters, and the Prophet ﷺ built a ruling on it: "Verily, the womb derives its name from the Merciful. Allah said: I will keep good relations with one who keeps good relations with your relatives, and I will sever relations with one who severs relations with your relatives." (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5987; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2554). The kinship tie is therefore not a social convention that religion later blessed. It is treated in the hadith as carrying the name of God, which is why the Qur'an commands attention to it in the same sentence as attention to Him: "And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties." (4:1). There is no softer image in the language for dependence on God than the womb. The Creator who made the human being here shows what kind of making it was.

**One Man, or Every Human Being?**

Commentators disagreed about the scope of the word. A few took it as a reference to Adam, the first of the species, and one report applied it to the Prophet ﷺ, since he is the one the Book is addressed to. Most held that it refers to all human beings, and their grounds are textual. The verse that follows describes speech being taught to the one just created, and speech is not the property of a single man; the refrain that runs through the sūrah addresses humans and jinn as two great multitudes; and the blessings that follow, from the light of the sun to the fruit of the earth, are shared by everyone who lives. The general sense is the stronger reading because it accounts for the whole sūrah, while the specific readings explain only the first line. Nothing is lost if a reader notices Adam here, as long as the same favour is seen in every face.

**Made, Then Taught: the Shape of a Human Life**

Being created is only the first half of the description. The next verse completes it with taught speech, and the pairing recurs elsewhere in the Book as a definition of what a person is. Adam's privilege in the Beginning was not strength or size but the names of things; he was made a learner and given the rank of one. The formation of the body happens in stages rather than in an instant, and the Qur'an points to that as something to think about: "when He truly created you in stages ˹of development˺?" (71:14), and elsewhere: "then We brought it into being as a new creation. So Blessed is Allah, the Best of Creators." (23:14). A creature that arrives slowly and learns slowly is being given time, and time of that kind is mercy. The sūrah's list of favours begins with two verbs of teaching and one of making, and the sequence outlines a life: called, made, instructed.

**A Creature That Can Deny the Gift**

The refrain of this sūrah asks which favour is being denied, and the question only makes sense because the recipient of mercy can refuse it. The refusal is itself evidence of what the creature is: a being that could not deny anything if it had not been given both the gift and the freedom to weigh it. The Qur'an repeatedly treats denial as a misuse of something received rather than as a condition outside God's care, and it warns against confusing long life with approval: "Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment." (3:178). In this sūrah the argument never becomes a threat in the early verses. It states the gifts with patience, one after another, so that the reader counts before answering. Being created is the first thing that a person would have to deny in order to answer the refrain at all.

**A Species, Not a Chosen Few**

The verse names humanity as a category, and the Quran's word for the species carries a reputation. "And He has granted you all that you asked Him for. If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never be able to number them. Indeed humankind is truly unfair, ˹totally˺ ungrateful." (14:34). The same word that receives the gift of creation in this sūrah is the word the Book uses to describe ingratitude elsewhere, and the two uses are not in tension. They state the shape of the arrangement: the favour is given to the species as it actually is, not to an ideal version of it. Nobody qualifies for existence by good behaviour, and no lineage, tribe, or community owns the privilege. That universality is what gives the refrain its force later in the chapter. If creation had been granted only to the pious, the question about denied favours would apply to a few people. Because it is granted to humanity, every listener is included in the accounting, and the first item being counted is the life that makes the counting possible.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:4

> ˹and˺ taught them speech.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Speech Was Named Before Power Was**

The list of favours continues with a faculty rather than a possession. Creation and instruction belong together, and this verse states what the instruction produced: articulate speech. Human beings are the only creatures on earth that ask questions about their own existence, and the asking depends on language. The sūrah gives the ability to speak the same weight as the ability to receive revelation, because the second depends on the first. Speech lets a person report what happened, describe what is not present, name a moral error, and hear a command and repeat it. Every covenant in the Book rests on that machinery. The verse also implies a limit, since the faculty is described as taught. It was not won by effort, invented by the first human, or earned by any generation since. A person receives a language before being able to evaluate it, which is how knowledge begins for everyone.

**Bayān: Speech and Also Clear Proof**

The word rendered speech is *bayān*, and its root means to appear, to separate one thing from another, to make something plain. The word therefore covers more than the mechanics of talking. It is used in the Book for the clarity of revelation itself: "This is an insight to humanity —a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing." (3:138). The translation renders *bayān* there as insight, and the choice shows the range of the term. A faculty that can state a fact and a capacity that can tell truth from falsehood are two sides of one gift. Classic commentators read this verse as a reference to God teaching Adam the names of things, and as a reference to the moral discernment that lets a person recognise what is permitted and what is forbidden. Both readings survive, and they do not compete: naming things truly and judging them rightly are the same skill applied to different objects.

**The Names That Were Taught in the Beginning**

The scene behind the verse is narrated in the sūrah of the Cow: "He taught Adam the names of all things, then He presented them to the angels and said, 'Tell Me the names of these, if what you say is true?'" (2:31). The angels answered that they knew only what they had been taught, and added: "You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise." (2:32). Two facts stand out in that exchange. First, knowledge is transmitted, not generated; even the angels describe theirs as received. Second, the distinction given to the human being was a name for everything, which is to say the ability to classify, order, and speak about the world with precision. Adam's rank among the angels rested on vocabulary. A sūrah that lists speech as a divine favour is recalling that scene and treating it as an honour that every human being still carries, whether or not the carrier thinks about where it came from.

**Many Tongues, One Faculty**

Languages differ, and the Book calls the difference a sign: "And one of His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the diversity of your languages and colours. Surely in this are signs for those of ˹sound˺ knowledge." (30:22). The same faculty that allows a community to build a shared world also walls it off from its neighbours. The Quran does not treat the diversity as a punishment, as the story of Babel does in another tradition. It lists it with the diversity of colours, side by side, as evidence of a Maker who works in plurality. The practical consequence is that the Book itself had to be carried across languages, and translation became a religious duty rather than a betrayal. The first verse of revelation addresses a reader in a specific tongue; the last command of the Prophet's mission addresses nations. Between the two sits the ordinary, universal ability to be taught how to speak.

**A Gift Received Before It Could Be Refused**

Dignity is granted before it is deserved, which is the pattern in this sūrah: "Indeed, We have dignified the children of Adam, carried them on land and sea, granted them good and lawful provisions, and privileged them far above many of Our creatures." (17:70). Carrying people on land and sea is a fact of the physical world, and speech is a fact of the mind, but the two are gifts of the same order. Nobody earns a mother's tongue. No child decides which grammar will organise its first thoughts, and no adult remembers learning what a question is. The Quran describes this debt repeatedly and then draws a conclusion that is stated with unusual sharpness: "If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never be able to number them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful." (16:18). The two halves of that verse belong together. Being unable to count the favours is the human situation, and the forgiveness named in the second half is the mercy offered to those who counted badly.

**The Faculty That Can Be Turned Against Its Giver**

The same capacity that makes revelation receivable makes rejection expressible. The verb the sūrah will use in its refrain, *kadhdhaba*, means to call something a lie, and it is a speech act; it requires a tongue. A stone cannot disbelieve, and a tree cannot slander its Maker. The Makkan objection quoted earlier in this commentary — that a human being was teaching the Prophet ﷺ — was itself a sentence, constructed with the grammar God taught. This is the peculiar exposure of the creature described in the verse. It can take the gift of *bayān*, the very tool that proves the generosity of the Giver, and use it to construct an argument against Him. The Quran does not treat that possibility as an accident. It treats it as the cost of making a being that can understand, and it warns that words will not be free: measured speech is rewarded, and every careless sentence is recorded. A sūrah listing favours is also, quietly, a sūrah about accountability for what is said.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:5

> The sun and the moon ˹travel˺ with precision.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Lamps and a Clock**

The word rendered precision is *ḥusbān*, a reckoning, from the root that gives Arabic its word for arithmetic. The verse does not merely note that the sun and moon move. It says that they move according to a number, and it places that number inside a chapter about mercy. A reckoning is what you get when nothing is left to whim. The same idea appears where God describes the purpose of the arrangement: "He is the One Who made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases, so that you may know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺." (10:5). And the sūrah of the Cattle states the fact in almost the wording of this verse: "He causes the dawn to break, and has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing." (6:96). The word used of the heavenly bodies is a bookkeeping word, which is why the sūrah can move from the sky to the market scales without changing its subject.

**The Calendar That Prayer Depends On**

Timekeeping is not a side benefit for Muslims; it is scaffolding for the whole religious year. The five daily prayers are fixed by the sun's movement across the sky, and the fast of Ramadan and the pilgrimage are fixed by the lunar month, beginning with a sighting of the crescent and ending with another. A person cannot pray on time or fast the right number of days without the precision named in this verse. That is a striking design: the obligations of a community are tied to two bodies that cannot be talked into changing, and the calendar is not owned by any ruler, scholar, or institution. This is also why the verse belongs to the list of favours rather than to a lecture on astronomy. Without *ḥusbān*, worship loses its schedule, agriculture loses its seasons, and travellers lose their bearings on land and at sea. Order in the sky is the first service the heavens render to the humans living underneath.

**Bodies That Run, Not Gods That Rule**

The sun and moon were worshipped across the ancient world, and the Book repeatedly removes them from the altar without insulting them. The command is explicit: "Do not prostrate to the sun or the moon, but prostrate to Allah, Who created them ˹all˺, if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺." (41:37). Abraham's argument in the sūrah of the Cattle makes the case from observation: when the night covered him he saw a star and said, "This is my Lord!" but when it set he said, "I do not love things that set." (6:76), and he reached the same verdict about the moon and then the sun: "But again when it set, he declared, 'O my people! I totally reject whatever you associate ˹with Allah in worship˺.'" (6:78). Setting is the test, and the two great lights set every day. This sūrah gives the sun and moon a place in its list of favours and never gives them a title. They are employees in a system, and the system is the mercy.

**Each Body Runs to an Appointed Term**

Precision does not mean permanence. The Book says of both lights that they are subject to an end: "He has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term." (13:2), and again: "And He has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term. He is truly the Almighty, Most Forgiving." (39:5). Another verse names the sun's own tomb, so to speak, in a single line: "The sun travels for its fixed term. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing." (36:38). The two ideas — an exact course and a fixed end — appear in the same breath in this sūrah's universe. A clock that will stop is still a clock, and the stopping is part of the honesty of the created order. The sun measures out a life that is not endless, including the lives of those who worship it.

**Balance in the Sky, Balance in the Hand**

The next verses in this sūrah move from the two lights to the raising of the sky, and from there straight to the scales used in trade. That transition is not abrupt; it is the same argument in two places. If measurement holds the sun and moon in their courses, then measurement is not an arbitrary human invention but a pattern the human being is asked to copy. The sūrah puts the point three times in a row — the balance set, the command not to transgress it, the order to weigh fairly — before returning to the earth and its fruit. Astronomy and commerce are therefore treated as one subject. A trader who shortens a weight is breaking the pattern that keeps the sky in place. Al-Ṭabarī records that the balance is read by most exegetes as justice, and by some as the revealed law itself. Either way, the scale is set by God in the sky before it is set on any counter on earth.

**One Course for Everyone Under It**

The sun and moon keep time without reference to the character of the people they serve. Their precision falls on the just and the unjust in equal measure, and the Quran treats that impartiality as a reason for shame rather than for confidence: "Indeed, We have dignified the children of Adam, carried them on land and sea, granted them good and lawful provisions, and privileged them far above many of Our creatures." (17:70). Dignity comes with carriage; the same sky moves over every traveller, and the same calendar measures the life of a tyrant and the life of his victim. A gift that arrives before merit is a stronger claim on gratitude than a wage. The sūrah's list makes that claim by accumulation. It does not argue that the two multitudes owe God thanks; it lays out what has already been handed to them and lets the arithmetic do the work, and the two bodies in this verse are the first items large enough for anyone to see without being told.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:6

> The stars and the trees bow down ˹in submission˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**What Prostration Means When the Subject Is a Tree**

A tree cannot place its forehead on the ground, and the verse is not asking anyone to imagine that it does. *Sajdah* in this context means unbroken compliance with the decree that made it. The sun and moon keep their courses, the herbs push up out of the earth, the palm and the vine yield what they were built to yield, and none of them deviates by choice or by accident. The Book treats this as worship: "Do you not see that to Allah bow down ˹in submission˺ all those in the heavens and all those on the earth, as well as the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, and ˹all˺ living beings, as well as many humans, while many are deserving of punishment." (22:18). Note the last clause. In that list, human beings are the only category split in two, and the split is what this chapter's refrain will spend forty verses addressing.

**Najm: Sky-Stars or Stemless Plants?**

Commentators divided over the first noun. Mujāhid, al-Ḥasan, and Qatāda took *najm* in its usual sense and read the verse as the stars of heaven bowing, paired with the trees of earth. Ibn ʿAbbās, Saʿīd b. Jubayr, al-Suddī, and Sufyān al-Thawrī read it as vegetation that has no stalk or trunk, so that the verse names two kinds of earthly plant, the soft-stemmed and the hard-stemmed. Ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī preferred the second reading, and the reason is structural: *shajar* is defined as plants that stand on trunks, so the pairing works best if *najm* means the plants that do not. Against that, the same noun in the sūrah of the Cattle carries its astronomical sense. The stronger case belongs to the plant reading, because the verse pairs two nouns in one grammatical breath, and because a list of four created things bowing is more coherent than a list of two stars and one set of trees. Both readings, however, end in the same act of submission, which is why nobody's creed turns on the choice.

**The Highest Thing and the Lowest Thing Agree**

The verse cuts across the whole scale of creation. It starts at the top with the two great lights and then names whatever grows out of the ground, so the sūrah covers the distance between the sky and the soil in three short lines. Nothing is exempted, and nothing is too small to be mentioned. This is one of the reasons the chapter reads like a catalogue: it is assembling witnesses. The proudest object visible to a human being and the most ordinary shrub in a field are described with one verb, and the verb is a posture of submission. For a Makkan audience accustomed to thinking in terms of powerful allies and weak clients, that flattening is a correction. Status does not determine obligation. The tree does what it was made to do, and so does the sun; the ranking of created things has nothing to do with whether they answer to their Maker.

**Two of Them Bow, and the Form Is Deliberate**

The Arabic verb is dual, *yasjudān*, bowing by two. The dual form is not idle grammar. It runs through this sūrah as a structural device: the refrain addresses two multitudes, and the gardens of the hereafter appear in pairs. A dual verb gathers a pair into one sentence, so two subjects share a single act, and the same construction serves a second function here. It signals that the two nouns are being treated as a matched pair rather than as examples chosen at random. Arabic uses the dual for pairs and also for the two members of a class, which allows the verse to be read as two categories bowing rather than two specimens. So the grammar itself supports the exegetes who read the line as vegetation of both kinds, and it leaves the whole category of growing things — every root, stem, leaf, and fruit — inside the submission the verse describes.

**Bowing Without a Promise of Reward**

There is a difference between the prostration of a plant and the prostration of a person, and the difference is the whole point of the verse's placement here. The stars and the trees are obedient without being promised anything for it; there is no garden waiting for the palm tree, and no accounting for the herb. The human being is the one creature told that a prostration will be weighed, rewarded, and remembered. That arrangement is the mercy under discussion. The sūrah has spent five verses listing gifts that arrive without anyone's effort, and now it points to a creation that answers God without being asked to hope for anything. What the two multitudes will be asked about in the refrain is not whether the sun kept its course, but whether they recognised a system of gifts that even the trees obey. The trees have no refrain to answer and no denial to make.

**Full Measure, Given Without Being Asked**

A field is not stingy. The wheat that ripens in it carries far more grain than the seed that was planted, and it does so whether or not the sower deserves it; the tree yields its fruit without negotiating a price. That is the sense in which the verse's prostration is also a form of justice — the created thing hands over what it was made to hand over. Against that background, the Book's attack on cheating in trade lands harder: "Woe to the defrauders! Those who take full measure ˹when they buy˺ from people, but give less when they measure or weigh for buyers." (83:1–3). The contrast is not between religion and commerce. It is between a creation that gives in full because that is its nature, and a creature that withholds by choice. The stars and the trees, and whichever of the two readings of *najm* is preferred, are doing exactly what the fraudulent trader refuses to do: delivering the whole of what is due, and doing it without an audience.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:7

> As for the sky, He raised it ˹high˺, and set the balance ˹of justice˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Roof Put Up Without Visible Pillars**

The verse reports two acts in one line, and the first is architectural. The sky is raised, which means it is held in position above the earth as a cover rather than left as a void. The Book states the same fact where it describes the structure of the world: "It is Allah Who has raised the heavens without pillars—as you can see— then established Himself on the Throne." (13:2). What hangs over a person's head is not empty space but a ceiling with a purpose. It holds the air that is breathed, it carries the rain, and it screens out what would otherwise make the earth uninhabitable. The sūrah of the Cow puts the two together in a sentence about the same mercy: "˹He is the One˺ Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you." (2:22). A raised sky is therefore already a provision for creatures that had not yet been named when it was raised.

**A Balance Set in the Same Breath as the Sky**

The second act in the verse is stranger than the first, and its placement is the reason it is strange. Lifting a roof is an act of engineering; setting a balance is an act of law. The verse joins them without a conjunction that distinguishes them, and the exegetes took notice. Most read the balance here as justice itself, established as part of the order of the world the way gravity is. Some read it as the revealed law, the *sharīʿah* by which disputes are settled. Others read it as the Quran, since the Book elsewhere names itself in the same list: "and with them We sent down the Scripture and the balance ˹of justice˺ so that people may administer justice." (57:25). A few pressed the literal reading, the scales of the marketplace, on the strength of the two verses that follow. The literal sense is the weakest on its own and the easiest to reconcile, because the physical scale is how justice is practised by traders.

**A Fixed Standard, Not a Negotiable One**

The verb is *waḍaʿa*, to place or set down, and it describes an act that puts something where it stays. A balance that is set is not a preference that shifts with the generation or the market. This is the force of the word in a chapter that has just named the two bodies keeping time by number. Both images say the same thing: there is a measure in the world that no human being made and no human being may amend. Justice in the Quran is not a social contract negotiated between parties who each give up something. It is a fact of creation, older than the people who will be judged by it, and the command in the next verse is to stop transgressing it rather than to agree on it. That is why a chapter concerned with gratitude can move into commercial ethics without changing the subject — the same Maker who lifted the sky set the standard for what is owed.

**The Same Word for a Scale and a Soul**

Arabic uses the root w-z-n for weighing grain and for weighing conduct. The double usage is not a metaphor that the language borrowed from the market; the Book treats it as one operation described at two levels. A merchant who adds a stone to his weights has done something to the order of the universe, and a judge who favours a relative has done the same thing in a different room. The sūrah reinforces this by naming the balance three times in three consecutive verses and drops the word in no other part of the chapter. Repetition of that density in a text that rarely repeats is a form of emphasis: the reader is meant to notice that the thing being mentioned a third time is a scale. Nothing in the list of the sun, the moon, the stars, and the plants is pushed this hard.

**What the Balance Does for People Who Cannot See God**

Justice is the one divine attribute ordinary people can operate. Nobody can raise a sky, and nobody can set a moon in its course, but anybody with two hands can hold an honest scale. The sūrah gives the created order as evidence of the Creator's mercy in the verses before this one, and then hands the human being a share in the same activity. That share is the mercy inside the command. A merchant who weighs fairly is copying, at his own scale and in his own shop, the pattern that keeps the sun and the moon on course. The next verses will forbid the opposite in three ways — do not transgress, weigh justly, do not give short measure — and the prohibition is generous before it is stern. To be told exactly what fairness is, in a world where the strong write the rules, is itself a favour.

**The Roof Above and the Rule Between**

The two halves of the verse are a pair of protections. One is above the head and the other is between people, and both are described in the same line because both are necessary for the same reason. A raised sky makes the earth survivable; a set balance makes it liveable. Without the first there is no harvest, and without the second there is no market, no trust, and no peace between neighbours who must exchange what they grow. The sūrah never presents law as a burden imposed on human beings from outside. It presents it as the second of two shelters, given by the same hand that gave the first. This is why the verse's grammar couples them so tightly. The sky and the balance are the same kind of gift, and the Creator who provided one did not leave the other to chance.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:8

> so that you do not defraud the scales.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Purpose Clause Attached to a Cosmic Act**

The verse opens with a particle of purpose, so that. It explains why the balance was set in the line before, and the explanation turns the chapter from description into address. God did not place justice in the world as an ornament to be admired; the goal stated here is behavioural. Grammarians also allow the line to be read as a prohibition standing on its own, "do not transgress in the balance," and both readings reach the same demand. The choice between them changes nothing practical: whether the words state the reason for the balance or command respect for it, the human listener is the one being spoken to. What is remarkable is the scope assigned to a rule about trade. A clause about short weight is attached to the raising of the sky, as if the two belonged in one sentence because they do, and the sūrah is written as though the reader ought to see that without being persuaded.

**What Overstepping Actually Means**

The Arabic verb here is *ṭagha*, and the root is about overflowing a boundary. It is used of water leaving its channel, of a person exceeding a limit until he thinks he is above the law, and of a king who governs without restraint. The word choice is exact: the sin described is not arithmetic error, it is a boundary crossed. A merchant who trims a weight has decided that the limit does not apply to him, which is the same decision made by every tyrant in the Book, only in miniature. The sūrah's language then gathers both offenders into a single moral category. The man who cheats a customer out of a handful of dates and the ruler who seizes a province are committing the same act of overstepping, and the Quran refuses to grade them by the size of the damage. What makes both serious is the claim they make about themselves.

**The Merchant and the King Commit One Offence**

Commentators divide the verse between two readings — tyranny and oppression on one side, withholding what is due on the other — and the division reflects the range of the root rather than a disagreement about meaning. Oppression is overstepping when you have power; short measure is overstepping when you have a shop. The single verb covers both because the underlying move is identical: taking more than is yours, whether by force or by a scale that lies. The Quran habitually treats economic honesty as a test case for social justice, since it is the one form of theft that leaves no visible wound. A customer cannot see the missing weight, and the damage is spread across hundreds of small transactions. This is why the command is stated so plainly in a chapter of worship, without a doctrine attached and without a punishment named.

**The Version of the Same Rule Outside This Sūrah**

The rule has a second home in the Quran, in the story of a prophet sent to a trading town. Shuʿayb addressed his people with the words: "O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him. And do not give short measure and weight. I do see you in prosperity now, but I truly fear for you the torment of an overwhelming Day." (11:84). The order of that speech is instructive. The command to worship comes first, and the commercial warning follows immediately, as though the two are parts of one law rather than different departments of life. Shuʿayb's people were prosperous, and the verse says so, which means cheating had been rewarded for a long time before a prophet arrived. The same pattern holds in this sūrah: the gifts arrive before the accounting does, and the mercy of the delay can look like approval to a person who has not read the rest of the chapter.

**Why a Chapter About Favours Talks About Scales**

A reader might expect this sūrah to be entirely praise and meditation. Instead it interrupts its survey of the sky and the harvest to regulate weights for the third line in a row. The reason lies in what the chapter is counting. Every item listed so far is a gift given without payment: teaching, creation, speech, sunlight, seasons, fruit. A creature that accepts such gifts and then refuses to give what is owed to another creature has contradicted the whole arrangement. The balance in the sky is a picture of how God deals with what He owns, and the balance on the counter is how a human being deals with what he has been given. Cheating, in that light, is not merely unfair; it is a misreading of the universe the chapter has been describing. The verse does not expand on that.

**Honesty as the Visible Half of Gratitude**

Gratitude in the Quran is rarely left as an inner state. It is given things to do: pray, give, forgive, and deal honestly. The verse is one of the clearest instances, because it ties the invisible virtue of thankfulness to the visible act of handing over the full weight. There is no way to fake the second. A heart that recognises the Giver will hand over what the buyer paid for; a scale that has been altered announces the state of its owner more accurately than any statement of belief. That is why the sūrah gives the subject three consecutive verses, with this one in the middle as the explanation of the other two. The command not to overstep is the moral translation of the raised sky and the set balance, and the verse after it supplies the positive form: give full weight, and do not fall short.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:9

> Weigh with justice, and do not give short measure.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command Stated Twice, Once Positively**

Legal material in the Quran usually pairs a prohibition with a positive order, and this verse is a clean example: weigh with justice, and do not give short measure. The first half tells the listener what fairness looks like in action; the second closes the door on the shortcut. Neither half is redundant, because a rule expressed only as a prohibition leaves the honest trader guessing what standard he has met, while a rule expressed only as an ideal leaves the cheater room to argue about degrees. Together they define a finished obligation. The structure also carries a warning about how the violation typically happens. Cheating rarely takes the form of adding weight for oneself; it takes the form of a scale that reads slightly heavy when the merchant sells and slightly light when he buys, and the verse names both directions by naming the shortfall.

**Justice as an Active Duty, Not an Absence of Theft**

The word rendered justice here is *qisṭ*, and it belongs to the vocabulary of giving each party what is owed. That framing matters because fairness in a market is not a passive condition. Buyers and sellers do not meet as equals: the trader knows his scale, his goods, and his margins, and the customer knows the price. Justice means the informed party carries the burden. The verse places the duty on the one who holds the balance, which is to say on the one with the information and the instrument. Nothing is said about the buyer's ignorance as an excuse for either side, and nothing suggests that a fair outcome can be produced by good intentions alone. The command is procedural: use the scale honestly, and do not shave the amount. Two sentences, one for the method and one for the outcome.

**Why the Balance Is Mentioned Three Times**

This is the third reference to the balance in three verses, and the density is unusual even for a book that repeats for emphasis. Commentators noted it and drew the natural conclusion: justice belongs among the foundations of the created order, not among its optional refinements. The chapter's subject is mercy, and mercy without justice becomes favouritism. The Quran makes that point in a way that leaves no room for private preference, since the scale that was set in the sky does not care who is standing near it. A reader who counts the mentions in this passage will also notice that they increase: the balance is set, then defended, then used. The progression moves from the act of God to the act of man in three steps, and the third step is this verse, where the reader is told to pick up the instrument.

**A Report About the Foundation of the World**

A saying is widely quoted for this passage: that the heavens and the earth are founded upon justice. The wording circulates as a prophetic report, and the chains for it are not established in the way the sound collections require; the idea it carries is nonetheless one the Book states repeatedly in its own words, as when justice is named alongside the sending of messengers and the revelation of the Book. [UNVERIFIED: the saying "the heavens and the earth are founded upon justice" is widely circulated as a hadith, but no authentic chain with a collection and number could be confirmed; it is therefore mentioned only as a reported saying and no citation is given.] What can be said on the strength of the text itself is stronger than the report. The sūrah sets the balance beside the raised sky in a single verse, and it repeats the word until the reader cannot miss it.

**Short Measure as Theft From the Invisible**

A scale that is a little light does its damage quietly. There is no injury to point at, no property to recover, and no witness who can prove the difference. The offence is compounded across transactions and across years, and its victims are strangers who never learn what happened. This is why the Quran gives the subject an entire sūrah of its own, opening with a woe pronounced on those who measure carefully when they buy and give less when they sell. Short measure is also, for the same reason, easy to rationalise. Nobody feels like a thief over a few grams. The verse closes that avenue by stating the standard without a tolerance: give full weight, and do not fall short. The chapter's earlier mention of the sun and moon running by number belongs here too, since precision is what the created order already practises.

**A Standard the Weak Can Invoke**

The practical worth of this command lies in who is protected by it. Anyone can be cheated, but the poor are the easiest to cheat and the least able to object, since a complaint requires time, standing, and a witness. A revealed rule about weights gives the weakest party in a transaction a claim that does not depend on his influence. The sūrah's placement of the rule is therefore not a digression from its theme. It is a specific application of mercy, delivered in the middle of a survey of cosmic favours, and it shows what gratitude is expected to look like in a shop. The next verses return to the earth and its produce, and the reader who has just been told to weigh fairly is expected to see the harvest differently.

**What an Honest Scale Makes Possible**

A balance is one of the few instruments a person cannot verify on his own. A buyer can taste a fruit, test the cloth between his fingers, and smell the oil, but he cannot see what happens inside a merchant's weights unless he has his own. Trust in measurement is therefore a form of trust between strangers, and it is the condition of every market larger than a family. A community in which nothing can be weighed without suspicion has to trade with people it knows, and that limits it to a village. The command to weigh justly is therefore a gift before it is a duty: it creates a space where a person can deal with someone he has never met, in a city he does not come from, and be treated as a participant rather than as prey. Mecca's livelihood as a caravan town depended on exactly that space, which is why the sūrah's audience would have heard the command as a description of their own economy under the rule of the balance God set in the sky.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:10

> He laid out the earth for all beings.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ground Prepared Before the Residents Arrived**

The verse turns from the ordering of the sky to the preparation of the ground, and the verb means to spread out, extend, and make level. What is described is not the creation of matter but the fitting of a surface for habitation: an expanse wide enough to walk on, level enough to build on, and stable enough to farm. The Book states the same provision in a verse about the whole world and its supply: "˹He is the One˺ Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you." (2:22). The order of events inside that sentence is the point. The floor is prepared, the roof is placed, the rain is sent, and only then does the fruit appear. Every one of those steps precedes the arrival of the people who benefit from them, which is the pattern this sūrah keeps repeating.

**Who Are the Beings Meant Here?**

Commentators offered three answers to the question of who lives on this spread-out earth. One reading took the word as a reference to the domesticated animals that serve human beings, since the Arabic noun can carry that sense. Another took it as everything that crawls or walks upon the ground, including wild creatures with no relation to humans at all. A third read it as human beings and jinn together, the two multitudes this sūrah addresses throughout. The general reading is the safest, and it fits the surrounding verses, where the next line names fruit and grain and the following verse names fragrance — provisions that no single species consumes alone. Wild animals, insects, and birds all live on the same surface, and the verse describes a floor laid for a household larger than the one which reads the text.

**The Earth as a Servant, Not a Possession**

The chapter's habit is to describe the physical world in terms of service. The sun tells time, the sky covers, the balance measures, and here the ground is spread so that creatures may stand on it. None of these things are objects lying around; each is doing something for someone. This is why the Book can describe rain as a delivery: "We send fertilizing winds, and bring down rain from the sky for you to drink. It is not you who hold its reserves." (15:22). The last clause dismantles any claim of ownership. Reserves of water are held by someone other than the water's users, and the users are the guests of the arrangement. A chapter counting favours does not treat land as property with a price. It treats it as a table already set, and the human being arrives as a guest who has been given a place to sit.

**A Shift From the Spiritual to the Bodily**

Commentators observe that the first nine verses of the sūrah supply nourishment for the soul — teaching, revelation, speech, and the moral balance — and that from this verse the chapter turns to food. The turn is deliberate and it is not a descent. A book that cares about the mind would be incomplete if it ignored the body that carries it, and the Quran never treats physical need as an embarrassment. The sequence also mimics the way a person actually lives: instruction is received, conduct is regulated, and then the day's bread has to be found. By placing the bodily provisions after the moral ones, the sūrah keeps the order of priority clear without denying either. The earth is spread out for beings who have already been told what justice is, and the harvest that follows is offered to people who have been asked to treat each other fairly.

**What the Word for Spreading Carries**

Arabic lexicography gives the root d-ḥ-w the sense of extending and flattening something, the way a surface is smoothed so that it can be used. The classical commentaries understood it that way, and the verse's concern throughout is habitation rather than geometry. What matters for the argument is that the action is done for the sake of someone. A surface is spread so that something may rest on it; land is levelled so that people may live and plant and walk. Later readers have sometimes tried to derive a shape claim from the word, and the attempt is unnecessary and fragile. The plain sense of the Arabic is about suitability: this ground has been made usable, which the following verses demonstrate by naming what grows out of it and who eats.

**A Floor, Four Rows of Provision, Then a Question**

The verse is the first of a small block. After the floor come fruit, then date palms and grain, then fragrance, and after these four items the sūrah will stop and ask both multitudes a question. The arrangement is like a room prepared for guests: the ground laid, then dish after dish brought out, and then silence while the host waits for a word of thanks. Reading the block this way explains why the chapter spends so little time on argument in these verses. It is not making a case; it is setting a table. What follows the table is a question that has been repeated in every generation since it was first recited, and the reader who has been watching the food arrive is the one expected to answer it.

**A Provision That Does Not Wait for Merit**

Nothing in the spread-out earth asks whether its occupants believe in God before it feeds them. Rain falls, seed sprouts, and herds graze for communities that never heard of a prophet and for creatures that have no religion at all. The Quran states the sustaining relationship as a fact about God: "That is Allah—your Lord! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. ˹He is˺ the Creator of all things, so worship Him ˹alone˺. And He is the Maintainer of everything." (6:102). Creation and maintenance stand in one sentence there, with worship commanded between them, and the order is the argument. A creature that is both made and fed is being asked to draw the obvious conclusion, and provision that arrives before acknowledgement is the ground of the request. If the ground withheld its yield until people deserved it, the earth would be barren and the question this sūrah asks would have nothing to stand on. The universality of the gift is what makes the rebuke possible without unfairness.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:11

> In it are fruit, palm trees with date stalks,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Item Named Is Pleasure, Not Survival**

The list of earthly provisions opens with *fākihah*, fruit, and the word comes from a root that means to take delight in something. Bread keeps a person alive; fruit is eaten because it is good. The sūrah gives the pleasant category the first position, ahead of the staple crops that appear in the following verse, and the ordering is a statement about the Giver. A creator concerned only with keeping creatures running would have supplied calories and stopped. This chapter's list includes flavour, and it includes it first. The same pattern appears in the descriptions of the gardens later in the sūrah, where the rewards named are not merely sufficient but various, paired, and beautiful. Mercy here is not the minimum required to sustain life. It is an abundance that has been shaped for enjoyment, and the reader is invited to notice that before being reminded of anything he owes.

**The Date Palm as the Standard of the Good Life**

The date palm holds a place in Arabian life that requires some translation for readers who buy dates in plastic trays. It was food, sugar, and medicine; its trunk was timber, its fronds were roofing and rope, and its shade made an outdoor room in a land without much of it. For the Arabs of the Peninsula it stood above all other fruit, and the sūrah names it right after the general category, as though the general word needed its best example immediately. The phrase rendered date stalks refers to the sheath that holds the cluster of ripening dates at the top of the tree, a protective casing that keeps the fruit from spoiling before it is ready. One detail describes an entire agricultural system: the tree, the fruit, the wrapping that preserves it, and the harvest that follows.

**Delight and Necessity Occupy the Same List**

The movement from fruit to grain follows a line that runs from luxury toward necessity, and the chapter does not rank them morally. Both are called favours in the verses that follow, and both will be counted when the question about denial is put to the two multitudes. This is a quiet correction of the way people usually assess their circumstances: the comfortable things are treated as extras, and the boring things as the real provisions. The sūrah has no such hierarchy. It never suggests that the one who receives dates and the one who receives wheat are receiving different grades of mercy. The list simply accumulates, and the accumulation is the argument. A reader who counts will find that his ordinary months, the ones without anything memorable in them, are made of these verses.

**A List That Names Anatomy, Not Just Produce**

The chapter's precision is worth watching. It does not say that the earth produces food in general; it names fruit, then a specific tree, then the sheath that protects its cluster. The next verse names grain together with its husk, and the aromatic plants that grow beside the field. This is the vocabulary of somebody describing a farm rather than a concept of fertility, and it belongs to a book that everywhere presses its readers toward observation. The instruction to look at what grows is not framed as an aesthetic exercise. It is framed as a way of reading evidence. A person who has actually looked at a palm cluster and noticed the sheath has done the same thing the sūrah is doing, one line at a time, and the result is that gratitude stops being a vague sentiment and starts being a response to specifics.

**Provision That Keeps Its Own Appointed Time**

Fruit ripens when it ripens. A farmer can plant, water, prune, and wait, and none of those activities give him control over the day the harvest arrives. The sūrah's word for the palm's produce is tied to a season, and the chapter itself has just described the sun and moon running to a fixed reckoning. The two facts belong to one system. Agriculture is the place where human effort meets a schedule it did not write, which is why the farming passages of the Quran so often end in praise or rebuke rather than advice. When a harvest comes in, the grower has been paid by someone else's timetable. Anyone who has waited through a dry year knows how thin the line is between the earth spread out for creatures and the earth withholding what it was asked for.

**The Table Set Before the Question**

This verse belongs to the small block that follows the spreading out of the earth, and the block ends with a question addressed to humans and jinn together. Before that question arrives, the reader has been shown four things: the floor, the fruit, the staple crops, and the fragrance. The sequence has the shape of hospitality, in which the guest is given a place and then food without being asked for anything first. The sūrah's method in its opening third is therefore not exhortation but provisioning. It tells the listener what he has already been given, in the order in which he usually notices these things, and only then turns to address him directly. The gratitude it asks for later is a response to a table that has already been laid.

**The Harvest Still Needs a Farmer**

The verse names what grows and says nothing about who plants it, because the chapter's subject is the Giver and not the labourer. The two are not in competition, and the Book states the relationship elsewhere without embarrassment: "Allah ˹alone˺ caused you to grow from the earth like a plant." (71:17). Human work in that sentence is real and still secondary. Somebody prepares the ground, prunes the palm, and gathers the clusters, and none of those activities can manufacture a single date. Agriculture teaches the lesson in a way that nothing else teaches it, because the farmer puts in months of effort and then watches something happen that he did not cause. A harvest is the clearest ordinary picture of a creature cooperating with a provision it receives, and the sūrah places the palm and its stalks on the page as evidence that the cooperation has been going on successfully for as long as people have lived on the ground.

**What the List Leaves Out**

The inventory given in these three verses is not a diet. Water is not mentioned, and neither is meat, milk, or fish, though all of them belong to the provisions the earth and sea supply. The word translated favours, *ālāʾ*, is a plural without a stated limit, and the sūrah's lists are samples chosen for effect rather than a register of everything a person consumes. The omission of water from the list is worth a moment: it is the substance without which nothing else on the list can be eaten, and its absence forces the reader to notice how arbitrary a complete catalogue would be. The chapter is counting in a way that is meant to be interrupted. Four items drawn from ordinary life are enough to make the point that the list can never be finished, and the question that arrives in the next verse is asked about all of them at once.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:12

> and grain with husks, and aromatic plants.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Staple, the Casing, and the Scent**

This line closes the first block of earthly provisions with two items that are not of the same kind. The first is grain together with its husk, which is the food that can be stored and carried and which feeds a household through the year. The second is aromatic plants, which supply no calories at all. Their inclusion is the kind of detail that makes the sūrah memorable, because it refuses to define provision as nutrition. A person is fed by bread and pleased by scent, and the chapter that has already given pleasure the first position in its list keeps the category open at the end. What the verse names, in effect, is a complete ordinary life: the sack in the storeroom and the fragrance in the air of the house. Both are gifts, and both end up in the same verse.

**What the Two Nouns Could Mean**

Commentators disagreed about the second noun, *rayḥān*, and the disagreement is worth reporting because the reasons on each side are lexical. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have taken it as every green plant, on the strength of the root's association with freshness. Al-Ḥasan read it as the fragrant things a person wears or smells, which is the ordinary sense of the word in Arabic poetry and speech. A third reading connected it to provision for animals, since the related word for fodder belongs to the same field of meaning. The disagreement does not change the sense of the verse. Whether the reference is to green growth in general or to scented plants in particular, the point stands that the list of provisions includes something whose only use is that it is pleasant. That is a deliberate design in a sūrah about mercy, and it is the reason the interpreters had to reach for a category broader than food.

**Provision Named With Its Packaging**

The verse does not say grain; it says grain with husks. The addition is not decoration. A husk is what allows a harvest to survive the interval between the field and the kitchen, and a book that names the casing is describing a supply system rather than a meal. The same instinct appears in the previous verse, where the palm's cluster is mentioned with the sheath that holds it, and the two cases together show a consistent interest in the parts of a plant that exist for preservation. Storage is one of the great mercies of settled life, and the Quran's treatment of bread, grain, and harvest assumes that people plan beyond the day. Nothing in these verses assumes scarcity as the normal condition of existence. The picture is of abundance that arrives with the means to keep it.

**Fragrance as Evidence of a Generous Order**

Scent has no survival function in the way that food and water do, and that is exactly why it belongs in this list. It shows that the order of the world has been arranged with an eye to pleasure and not only to function. Flowers serve the plant's reproduction, and the fact that they also please the human nose is a surplus that the chapter counts as a favour. The Qur'an refers to the gardens of the hereafter as scented and shaded, and the worship of the mosque has always involved perfume, incense, and clean clothing. The Book's attitude toward the senses is consistently positive, and it never treats delight in created things as a distraction from the Creator. A person who stops to smell a plant growing by a wall has done something the sūrah itself invites.

**The Straw That Feeds the Animals Too**

The husk has a second life. Once the grain has been threshed, the straw and chaff that remain are used as fodder for animals and bedding for their stalls, and the word used in this verse carries that agricultural sense as well. The earth was spread out in verse ten for all beings, not for human beings alone, and this verse quietly supplies the rest of the household. The ox that works the field and the goat that gives milk eat what the harvest leaves behind. That arrangement is a closed loop of provision that no merchant designed, and the sūrah's inclusion of it among the favours given to two multitudes should be read as a reminder that the accounting is not limited to people who can read a text.

**The First Block Ends, and the Earthly List Is Complete**

With this line the survey of the earthly table is finished. Ground, fruit, palms, grain, fragrance: five items, and then the sūrah stops. The next verse is not another provision but a question, and the contrast between the ordinary contents of the list and the size of the demand is what gives the question its weight. A reader who has followed the verses carefully now holds a short inventory of things he has used without thinking about them since childhood. The sūrah will ask, in a formula that repeats dozens of times, which of these he denies. Nothing has been exaggerated and nothing has been invented to make the case stronger, which is precisely the case. The list is a plain description of the world as it is, and the question is what a person does with a plain description.

**Grain That Can Travel**

Of everything named in this chapter's earthly list, grain is the only item built to keep. Fruit spoils, fragrance fades, and even dried dates have a season; a sack of grain can be carried for weeks and sold at the end of the road. That single property shapes the world the sūrah addresses. Mecca lived on caravans, and its livelihood depended on goods that could survive the journey to Syria in the north and Yemen in the south. The husk named in this verse is what made the trip possible, since it shields the seed from damp, insects, and the handling of porters. A provision that can travel is a provision that connects strangers, and the chapter that has just spoken about honesty in the scales is now naming the produce those scales were built to measure. The trade routes of the ancient world rest on two facts that the verse states in four words: grain, and its husk. Portability turned a harvest into a livelihood for people who never planted it.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:13

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you ˹humans and jinn˺ both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Asked Thirty-One Times**

This line appears thirty-one times in the sūrah, and it does more work than any other sentence in the Quran. It is addressed in the dual: *rabbukumā*, your Lord, both of you, and *tukadhdhibān*, you two deny. The two parties are human beings and jinn, and the sūrah identifies them explicitly when it first introduces the question. Nothing in the phrasing allows a reader to hear it as addressed to someone else. The interrogative is built on the preposition *bi*, so the question is really about instrument: by which of your Lord's favours do you deny? The form is close to a challenge, and it assumes that the answer is available in the listener's own history. The favours have been named one after another for twelve verses, and the question arrives as a request for an accounting of them.

**What the Verb of Denial Covers**

*Kadhdhaba* means to call something a lie, and the sūrah uses it because denial of a favour is a form of false statement about its source. A person who attributes the harvest to his own skill, the rain to the cycle of the winds with no giver behind it, or the Quran to a human teacher, has made a claim about where these things came from and has thereby called the sūrah's account false. The denial does not have to be total. Commentators noted that it can also describe the person who accepts God while rejecting one of His attributes, or who affirms the Creator and dismisses the resurrection, or who praises the mercy of this world while refusing to believe in the judgement the same mercy implies. The question is therefore not aimed only at atheists. It is aimed at anyone who takes the gifts and edits the record.

**Why the Two Multitudes Share One Address**

The Arabic dual bundles human beings and jinn into a single audience, and the sūrah keeps them together from here to the end. This is a statement about moral order: the jinn are not spectators watching the human test, and human beings are not the only community accountable for what they do with revelation. The Book elsewhere describes jinn listening to the Quran and carrying its news back to their people, and it holds them to the same law. The effect on the human reader is worth noticing. A question that includes an invisible nation alongside one's own cannot be answered on the grounds of local custom, since the standard is being applied to a species with entirely different capabilities. Favours are counted against the creature receiving them, and the two species are instructed to answer together.

**The Jinn Answered While the People Were Silent**

The Prophet ﷺ recited this sūrah aloud and the Companions with him said nothing, and the report of what followed is preserved: he told them that he had recited it to the jinn on the night he met them, and that their response was better than the response of the people in front of him. He described the pattern — at every occurrence of the question about denied favours, the jinn said, "None of Your favours do we deny, our Lord." The narration is recorded by al-Tirmidhī, who graded it ḥasan, and by al-Ḥākim, who judged it sound by the criteria of al-Bukhārī and Muslim. Whether or not a reader accepts the grading, the report preserves the practice the verse is asking for: the question is meant to be answered aloud, and the answer it expects is a confession.

**A Question That Expects Only One Answer**

The form of the question is rhetorical but the requirement is real. Nothing in the sūrah suggests that a denial can be argued in good faith, because the favours listed are not claims about the unseen; they are the ordinary conditions of being alive. A person is asked, in effect, whether he contests the sunlight, the fruit, or the fact that he can speak. The silence of the Companions in the reported scene is telling, and it is not the silence of dismissal. It is the silence of people who have no counter-argument and have not yet thought of the right word. The verse is designed to produce that moment of hesitation in every reader, and it repeats itself precisely because the effect wears off after the first hearing.

**Every Favour Is Counted Separately**

The refrain is placed after specific favours rather than after the sūrah as a whole, and the placement turns one general question into a series of particular ones. It follows the mention of creation; it follows the creation of the jinn; it will follow the two seas, the ships, the destruction of the world, and the gardens in which the righteous will live. Each time, the listener is meant to answer for that one item. The method matches the way denial actually works in a person's life. Nobody rejects the totality of mercy in a single decision; they accept most of it and quietly decline one part, usually the part that asks something of them. The sūrah's question returns until every part has been faced, and it does not accept a summary answer.

**A Question Asked of Two Communities That Cannot Meet**

Human beings and jinn share the address, and in the ordinary course of life they do not sit together to discuss it. The sūrah makes them partners in a single moral accounting, and the Quran describes the second community as divided in the same way the first is: "Among us are those who are righteous and those who are less so. We have been of different factions." (72:11). The words are those of the jinn themselves, reporting their own condition after listening to the Quran, and they end with a division that maps onto human experience: "And among us are those who have submitted ˹to Allah˺ and those who are deviant. So ˹as for˺ those who submitted, it is they who have attained Right Guidance." (72:14). A reader who grasps that the question has two audiences, one of them invisible, understands that it cannot be answered with a tribal defence. There is no human lineage that answers for a jinn, and no jinn may plead that the law was only sent to human beings.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:14

> He created humankind from ˹sounding˺ clay like pottery,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Body Made From Baked Earth**

The word rendered sounding clay is *ṣalṣāl*, and the term describes clay that has dried until it rings when it is struck, the sound of a fired vessel knocked by a knuckle. The second image tightens the picture: *fakhkhār*, the potter's clay, the material of jugs and bowls. Commentators as early as Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, and Qatāda glossed the first word as dry mud, and Ibn Kathīr took the two descriptions together as a single portrait of earth that has been shaped and then hardened in heat. The Quran is not being vague about human material origins here. It is naming a specific manufacturing process, and it does so in a chapter whose other verses have been listing the produce of the same ground. The creature being described was made out of the topsoil it will later farm.

**The Stages Behind the Word Clay**

Other verses describe the same creation with different materials, and the commentators assembled them into a sequence. There is dust, then a viscous clay, then molded mud, and then dried clay like pottery, at which point the body is ready for the spirit. The Book states the final stage in the sūrah of the Ranks: "Indeed, We created them from a sticky clay." (37:11), and it names the first stage in the story of Adam where the angels are told what is coming. Read together, the verses describe a process rather than a single act, and the process is slow and unglamorous. Nothing in it resembles the birth of a god or the forging of a hero. A human being is described as work that passes through the stages of a craft, and the craftsman is God.

**Why the Noblest Creature Is Made of the Cheapest Material**

The choice of material is a standing argument against pride, and the Quran uses it that way. The same humanity that has been dignified and given dominion is told, a few verses later and in many other places, what it is made of; the combination is designed to prevent both self-hatred and arrogance. Clay is not a substance anyone boasts about. It is what feet walk on, what rain turns to mud, what the potter takes for nothing from the riverbank. That God should shape the being He addresses with His own words out of that material is a claim about value that has nothing to do with the material's price. What makes a human being noble in this account is not what the body is made of, but what it was made to carry.

**The Vessel and the Breath**

The second image in the verse, pottery, points forward to something the chapter does not mention yet. A vessel exists to be filled, and the human body in the Quran is described precisely in those terms: "So when I have fashioned him and had a spirit of My Own ˹creation˺ breathed into him, fall down in prostration to him." (38:72). The molding comes first, and the breathing comes after, and the prostration of the angels follows the breathing rather than the molding. This order is the answer to any reading of the verse that treats the material as the whole story. The clay is the condition of the gift, not the gift. A potter who forms a jar and then fills it with something precious has not honoured the clay; he has honoured what the clay was made to hold.

**A Material That Rings, Breaks, and Returns to Dust**

The word *ṣalṣāl* also carries a sound, which is unusual vocabulary for a creation account. Baked clay is resonant, brittle, and easily shattered, and every one of those properties is true of the human body as the Quran describes it. It is noisy with speech, quick to break, and destined to return to the ground it came from. The sūrah has already named speech as a divine favour in its fourth verse, and the material chosen here is the kind that can produce a voice. The fragility is not hidden or softened. It is placed in the same verse as the act of creation, and the reader who takes clay seriously has already learned the lesson that the Book returns to whenever it wants to puncture a claim of immortality.

**An Origin Story Used Against Arrogance**

This verse has a history of use. Iblīs refused to prostrate to Adam and gave as his reason the material difference between them: fire against clay. The Book records that refusal as the first act of pride in the created order, and the argument has been repeated by human beings in every generation since, with different definitions of fire. Every claim of superior blood, superior class, or superior nation is a version of the same sentence. The verse answers it before it is spoken by describing the origin of the human being in a way that leaves nothing to brag about, and the fact that the description comes from the same chapter that calls the human being the recipient of the Quran keeps the balance. Low origin, high calling: that is the shape of the creature whose favours are about to be counted.

**Clay Taken From the Ground the Reader Walks On**

The material described in the verse is not exotic. It is the same earth the audience tills, walks over, and sweeps out of its houses, and every other passage that describes human creation draws on the same substance: "Indeed, We created man from sounding clay moulded from black mud." (15:26). The Quran's material vocabulary for the human being is deliberately ordinary, and the ordinary quality is the teaching. A person is made of what he stands on, and the Book expects him to notice that the ground beneath his feet and the body above it are relatives. The lesson has practical consequences in the chapters on humility, burial, and purity, and it is one reason the traditions of the Prophet ﷺ discouraged boasting about ancestry. Everything a human being claims as his own prestige is a claim about dust, and the sūrah's next verse will say the same thing about the other creature, whose material is brighter and no more self-made.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:15

> and created jinn from a ˹smokeless˺ flame of fire.

**Expanded Commentary**

**What the Arabic Phrase Permits**

The phrase is *mārij min nār*, and the translation renders it as a smokeless flame. The commentaries record a range of meanings within that phrase. Some read the word as fire that has been mixed with something, so that the jinn are made from a substance in which fire is an ingredient rather than the whole. Some read it as pure flame from which the smoke has separated. Others take it as a unique kind of fire different from what burns in a hearth or a furnace. The translation's choice captures the most common sense: flame without the smoke that accompanies ordinary combustion. The disagreement is about the quality of the material, not about the conclusion. In every reading, the jinn are creatures made of something, and the something is created.

**Fire That Leaves No Residue**

The absence of smoke is not a trivial detail in a desert culture, where fire served for cooking, warmth, and light, and where smoke was the visible evidence that something was burning. A smokeless flame is energy that produces its effect without leaving a trace. That image suits a creature described in the Book as swift, subtle, and largely unseen. The Quran names the mobility of the jinn in other places, and it describes one of them offering Solomon a task and completing it faster than a person could stand up. The material chosen for the jinn matches their function in the created order just as the clay chosen for human beings matches theirs. Slow, heavy, visible, and made to endure; quick, light, unseen, and made to pass through the world without leaving a mark.

**Two Creatures Named Side by Side**

The verse places the origin of the jinn immediately after the origin of the human being, in a single breath, and this is the first time the sūrah has named the two together. It will name them together another thirty times before it finishes. The pairing is the foundation of the refrain. A question addressed to two multitudes assumes that both are accountable, and that assumption rests on the fact that both were made and both were given something to answer for. The Book states the purpose of the pairing without ambiguity: "I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me." (51:56). Creation and purpose are joined in that sentence exactly as they are joined here, and the jinn's unusual substance gives them no exemption from either.

**The Nation That Listened to the Quran**

The Quran reports the jinn's encounter with revelation in their own words: "Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'It has been revealed to me that a group of jinn listened ˹to the Quran˺ and said, 'Indeed, we have heard a truly amazing Quran.'" (72:1). The same passage records them carrying the message home, warning their people, and describing what they had heard about the punishment of those who reject it. The point of those verses for this sūrah is that the refrain's second audience had actually answered. When the Prophet ﷺ recited this chapter, the response he received from the jinn was better than the silence of the human listeners with him. The verse here is the earlier half of that story: before the jinn could accept or deny anything, they had to be made.

**Fire and Earth as Two Tempered Natures**

Clay and fire are the two materials of a workshop, and the sūrah has set them in adjacent verses. Clay is shaped and fire is what hardens it; fire is contained and clay is what contains it. Both are ordinary substances that no one in the audience considered divine, and neither could be claimed by an ancient pantheon, since every household used both daily. The contrast between the two creations also describes two ways of existing in the world: one slow and visible, one fast and hidden. The Quran never treats the difference as a ranking. Neither creature is described as nobler for its material, and the demand placed on both in the verses that follow is identical.

**Why the Version of Creation Matters Here**

A chapter that is about to ask which favours are denied needs to establish what the two audiences are. The answer given here is the plainest possible: both are made. Fire does not belong to the jinn the way a kingdom belongs to a king, and clay does not belong to a human being the way a farm belongs to a farmer. The Bounties listed in the preceding verses were handed to creatures who did not produce their own substance, and now the text names the substance for both species, one verse each. The refrain that follows is not a change of subject. It is the natural consequence of the two creation statements, since a creature that did not make itself can hardly claim the gifts of its Maker as its own.

**The Word 'Created' Rules Out Every Rival Claim**

The verse does not say that the jinn came from fire as though fire were their parent or their god. It says they were created, which places them on the same side of the line as the human being and the tree. The distinction matters in a world where unseen powers were routinely treated as partners of God or as forces that could be negotiated with, and the Quran spends considerable space correcting that habit. The jinn describe their own situation without any claim of divinity: they are divided into the righteous and the deviant, they hear revelation, they believe or refuse, and they face the same consequences as human beings. The substance they are made of gives them unusual abilities and no status. A creature that must be created, that must choose, and that can be wrong is not a candidate for worship; it is a fellow servant, and the sūrah names it in the same breath as the rest of the created order.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:16

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain Turns a List Into a Conversation**

By this point the sūrah has named the Quran, the human being, speech, the sun, the moon, the plants, the raised sky, the balance, the earth, the harvest, and the creation of two kinds of intelligent creature. Then it stops and asks a question. The pause is the point. A list read straight through becomes scenery, and scenery stops being noticed; a list interrupted by the same sentence becomes an interrogation of the reader's own accounts. The Quran uses the same rhetorical move in another sūrah, where the formula is put in a slightly different form: "Now, which of your Lord's favours will you dispute?" (53:55). The shift from denial to dispute in that verse shows what the question is really doing. It is not asking for information about the favours. It is asking the listener to explain why he has not been changed by them.

**Everything Named So Far Is Still on the Table**

The refrain stands here after two creations have been described, the human being from clay and the jinn from fire, and the pairing matters. The favours that have been counted are not luxuries distributed to a few; they are the conditions of being a creature at all, given to both multitudes whether they asked or not. The timing of the question also matters. It arrives before the passage on the two seas and long before the descriptions of the gardens, which means the listener is being asked to answer a question about what he can already see: the ground, the food, the daylight, and his own body. Nothing in this first accounting depends on accepting a doctrine or hearing a prophet. A person standing anywhere on the earth at any period of history has received the favours the question is asking about, and that is why the question can be put to the two great multitudes together.

**How Repetition Works on a Resistant Listener**

A sentence heard once can be filed away. A sentence heard thirty-one times in one sitting becomes impossible to ignore, and the sūrah seems designed for recitation rather than silent reading. This chapter is recited aloud in prayer in long stretches, and the refrain lands differently at each return, depending on what has come before it. The effect is not the irritation of a repeated slogan; it is the pressure of an unresolved question. Each occurrence follows a specific favour, and each return raises the same demand: give an answer about this one. The listener who has just agreed about the harvest is confronted with the sea, then with the ships, then with the destruction of the world, and each time the question finds him with something new to explain. Repetition of that kind is a form of patience on the part of the text, not a failure of variety.

**Two Multitudes, One Standard, No Exceptions**

The Arabic dual in this verse binds human beings and jinn into a single audience, and there is no clause anywhere in the sūrah that lifts the requirement from one of them. No favour is listed as belonging to a particular species, and no answer is certified as sufficient for one audience and not the other. The Quran gives the reason for the union in a sentence free of ambiguity: "I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me." (51:56). Creation and purpose are joined, and the two species are joined with them. Knowing that a second, unseen community shares the accounting removes two possible escapes at once. A human being cannot argue that the testimony of one nation is limited by its circumstances, and a jinn cannot argue that revelation was addressed to somebody else. Both are being asked about the same sunlight and the same ground.

**Denial Does Not Have to Be an Argument**

The verb covers denial in speech, but the practical form of denial in most lives is quieter than a debate. A person can take every listed favour and simply fail to connect it to its Giver, month after month, without ever forming a sentence that says God is not behind it. The Quran treats that inattention as a serious act, which is one reason the refrain does not ask whether the listener has denied these things with his mouth. It asks which of them he denies, and the answer is often the one he has never thought about. Commentators noted that the denial can also be selective: a person accepts the existence of God while rejecting one attribute, one command, or one consequence, and the refusal to grant that single point constitutes the denial the verse is describing.

**The Question That Cannot Be Answered by Changing the Subject**

Nothing in the sūrah offers a way around the question. It cannot be answered by citing the sins of someone else, by appealing to one's lineage or community, or by pointing to the difficulty of the times, because the subject is what the listener has received. The only two responses available are the ones the book records from the two sides of its own story: the jinn's answer, that none of the favours is denied, or the silence of the people who had no answer ready. The sūrah waits for one of these. It has been asking since its first verse, and it will keep asking until its last, when it closes with a statement of blessing rather than another question. The listener who has followed the chapter from the beginning already knows which answer it is inviting.

**Whose Favours Are Being Counted**

The refrain says *your Lord's* favours, and the possessive is doing precise work. The gifts belong to the Giver before they are handed over; they are His, lent to the two multitudes for a lifetime and claimed as His in the very sentence that lists them. Arabic marks this with a pronoun attached to *rabb*, so the question is literally about which of the Lord's own benefits the listener is calling untrue. That framing removes a common evasion. A person can tell himself that his advantages are his own property, to be used as he likes, and the sentence quietly denies it: what he is spending was never his. The Qur'an keeps the same grammar in other places, where provision is described as being sent down and returned to its owner, and the effect is consistent. Gratitude in this chapter is not a compliment offered to a benefactor. It is a recognition of ownership, and the accounting follows from it.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:17

> ˹He is˺ Lord of the two easts and the two wests.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Naming the Limits to Name Everything Between**

Arabic has a rhetorical habit of mentioning the two ends of a range in order to include what lies between them, and this verse uses it. Two east and two west stand for every place and every moment at which the sun rises and sets. The Quran uses the singular form of the same phrase elsewhere, in Mūsā's address to Pharaoh: "˹He is˺ the Lord of the east and west, and everything in between, if only you had any sense." (26:28). It uses the plural in an oath by the points of sunrise and sunset: "So, I do swear by the Lord of ˹all˺ the points of sunrise and sunset that We are truly capable" (70:40). Singular, dual, and plural describe the same lordship at three scales, and no contradiction arises, because the subject is one Lord and the horizons are His in every direction and at every hour. The dual here adds something the others do not: a pair. This sūrah works in pairs throughout.

**What the Two Easts Are**

The classical explanation, recorded by Ibn Kathīr and repeated in the tafsīr literature, is that the two easts are the two extreme points of sunrise in the year: the furthest north the sun rises at the summer solstice and the furthest south at the winter solstice. The two wests are the corresponding points of sunset. Between those extremes the rising and setting points migrate gradually each day, which is why the Quran can also speak of many easts and many wests. An alternative reading, favoured by some modern commentators, takes the pair as the eastern horizons of the earth's two hemispheres, where sunrise and sunset occur simultaneously. The solstice reading has the stronger claim, since it accounts for the dual in ordinary Arabic usage and explains why the Quran can describe the same phenomenon with a plural in another verse.

**A Migration Nobody Watches**

The movement between the two extremes is one of the most consequential things in human life and one of the least noticed. The point of sunrise creeps north and then south by small increments, and the change accumulates into the seasons that decide when crops are planted, when herds are moved, and when the sea can be sailed. A person who spends a year watching the same horizon will see the sun use the whole width of its two limits, and the year that the sūrah has already connected to the moon's reckoning is measured out by this oscillation as well. The verse turns a fact of astronomy into a claim about ownership. Nobody arranged the tilt that produces the two extremes, and nobody maintains the range within which the sun keeps arriving; the One who owns the extremes owns the days between them.

**The Argument Against a Local God**

The first audience of this sūrah lived among shrines and tribal deities whose authority was tied to a valley, a stone, or a season. A Lord of the two easts and the two wests has no such address. He cannot be carried in a caravan or left behind when a tribe migrates, and no rival sanctuary lies outside His jurisdiction, because there is no horizon that is not His. The Quran makes this argument repeatedly against the idea that divinity can be parcelled out, and it does so here with geography rather than with logic. If the rising and setting of the sun happen at His command from one end of the year to the other, then no quarter of the world stands outside His rule, and no traveller moves beyond it.

**A Chapter That Does Everything in Pairs**

The dual form in this verse belongs to the sūrah's most visible structural feature. Two multitudes are addressed, two creations are described, two seas will be merged, two gardens will be promised to the God-fearing and two more placed below them, two springs will flow in each, and two kinds of fruit will hang in each. The pair of horizons in this verse and the pair of worlds in the next two verses set the pattern. Duality in the created order is not an accident to be smoothed over; it is the sūrah's way of showing that the world is made of matched things that answer to one Lord. A reader who notices the pattern can see why the refrain is addressed to two parties rather than to one. The Book is not dividing the audience. It is showing that every pair it names belongs to the same owner.

**The Favour Inside the Claim of Ownership**

Being owned by the Lord of both horizons is good news, and the sūrah places the verse between two occurrences of the refrain so that the reader hears it that way. A world without a lord of the extremes would be a world of competing powers, each with its own territory and its own demands, and travellers would pass from one jurisdiction into another at every border. The verse instead describes a single authority that holds the whole circuit of the sun, which means that the same rule applies in every country and the same mercy arrives on every morning. The two easts and two wests are not a threat issued to the two multitudes. They are the extent of the generosity the next refrain is about to ask them to acknowledge.

**A Course That Never Steps Outside Its Bounds**

The sun's path is one of the most obedient things a human being can observe. It does not arrive early in summer to shorten the harvest, and it does not stay hidden in winter until the stores run out. The two east and the two west name the boundary within which it keeps its appointment without exception, century after century, for every generation that has ever watched it. The sūrah has already used the word balance for the standard set between people, and the sky here practises a version of the same fidelity. That is why the verse belongs among the favours rather than among the warnings. To live under a course that can be relied on is to be able to plan a sowing, a journey, and a fast; a world whose sun wandered unpredictably would be unlivable for the creatures the chapter keeps naming, and the reliability is itself a gift.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:18

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Asked Immediately After the Horizon**

This is the third occurrence of the refrain, and it arrives at the end of a verse about the two east and the two west. The placement is deliberate. The favours counted so far have included the Book, the body, speech, the sky, the balance, the harvest, and the creation of two kinds of intelligent being. The horizon is the first item on the list that no one can miss and no one can own. A person may attribute his education to his own effort, and a merchant may attribute his profit to his skill, but nobody arranged the sunrise. The question therefore falls at the point where the excuse of personal merit has nothing to work with any more. What is being denied, if anything is being denied, is something that lands on the whole world every morning without being asked.

**Explaining a Favour Is Not the Same as Denying It**

There is a difference between describing how something happens and deciding that no one is behind it, and the Quran does not ask its readers to abandon the first. This sūrah itself has just described the sun and moon as moving by a reckoning, which is a statement about mechanism. The Book treats the order of the heavens as a system with measurable regularities, and it does not think the description of a system diminishes its Maker. What the refrain rejects is the further step in which a regular process is treated as a self-sufficient cause, so that the question of why anything exists at all is answered by pointing to the process. The old idols were replaced by new ones when that step was taken, and the verse keeps its edge, because the denial it names can be committed by anyone who explains everything and gives thanks for nothing.

**A Gift That Falls on the Ungrateful Too**

The sun rises over cities that mock the idea of a Creator and over villages that have never heard of one. It dries the mud of a battlefield and ripens the grapes of a monastery. The sūrah's list of favours is not addressed to the deserving; it is addressed to the two multitudes as they actually are, including the deniers whose denial the question anticipates. That arrangement is the mercy underneath the rebuke. A gift that is withdrawn the moment it is not thanked would not be mercy at all; it would be a transaction. The fact that the provision continues while the denial continues is what gives the refrain its patience, and it also explains why the verse can be recited year after year without losing its force. The evidence for the accusation is the same as the evidence for the generosity.

**The Accounting That Happens Every Day**

The verse is a question about a single morning as much as about a lifetime. Each sunrise repeats the favour, and each repetition gives the listener another chance to answer. The Book elsewhere ties the believer's day to gratitude in the same way, and the practice of the dawn prayer places the first act of the day before the first task of the day. The sūrah does not wait for a final judgement to conduct its audit. It holds the accounting in the present tense, where the listener still has the day in front of him and the answer still costs him nothing but honesty. Repetition is what makes that possible; thirty-one times, the text hands the question back and waits.

**An Answer That Can Be Spoken Aloud**

The report of what the Prophet ﷺ heard from the jinn turns on a spoken response, and it shows that the question has a form of reply. They said, at each occurrence, that they denied none of their Lord's favours. The Qur'an records the same instinct in the opening of another sūrah of praise, where the command is not to argue but to glorify: "Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High," (87:1). Praise is the alternative to denial, and the two are mutually exclusive in this chapter's arrangement. A listener who has been taking without noticing is now being offered the simplest possible remedy: say what is true about where the gifts came from, and the question loses its charge. Nothing more is demanded at this point in the sūrah. The favours are on the page; the accounting is a sentence.

**The Pattern That Will Not Let a Reader Settle**

The refrains will continue, and this one establishes how a reader should expect to meet them. Each will follow a favour or a scene, and each will return the listener to the same ledger. The sūrah is teaching a habit as much as delivering a message. In a life where the harvest, the sea, the ship, and the garden arrive in their seasons, the question about denial arrives with them, and the person who has answered thirty-one times in one recitation is being trained to answer in the ordinary hours when no one is reciting. This verse is short, and its work is repetition. The chapter is building a reflex of gratitude, and it does so by refusing to let a single favour pass without a response.

**Denial by Comparison**

There is a form of denial that never mentions God and never denies the sunrise: the habit of measuring one's own portion against another person's and concluding that the distribution has been unjust. The Book treats that mood as a rejection of what has actually been received, and it keeps answering it with lists. The refrain in this sūrah works against the habit directly, because every occurrence names a favour that the complainer holds in common with the person he envies: the same sky, the same water, the same sun on both rooftops. Comparison cannot survive that framing for long, since the terms are universal. What a person has been given is counted from the beginning of the chapter, and the neighbour he resents is living under the same count. The verse asks him to stop auditing someone else's ledger and answer for his own, which is the only account he can actually read.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:19

> He merges the two bodies of ˹fresh and salt˺ water,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Seas Brought Together and Kept Apart**

The verse presents a meeting and the next verse presents a boundary, and the two must be read as one statement. Fresh water and salt water flow into each other at every river mouth on earth, and they remain distinguishable afterwards, which is what makes the pairing remarkable enough for a chapter of favours. The Quran states the same fact in the sūrah of the Criterion: "And He is the One Who merges the two bodies of water: one fresh and palatable and the other salty and bitter, placing between them a barrier they cannot cross." (25:53). Another passage names the two bodies and then names what people take from them: "The two bodies of water are not alike: one is fresh, palatable, and pleasant to drink and the other is salty and bitter. Yet from them both you eat tender seafood and extract ornaments to wear." (35:12). The pairing serves the same purpose each time. What is being described is a provision that requires two opposites to coexist.

**The Verb That Means to Set Loose**

The Arabic verb *maraja* carries the sense of letting something loose, releasing it to move freely, and the noun form describes a mixture. Commentators noticed the tension in the sentence itself: the waters are described as meeting and mingling, and the following verse says they do not cross a barrier. The resolution they gave is that the two bodies are allowed to touch, and the properties that God assigned to each are what hold them apart. The classical exegetes explained the barrier as God's decree, which is a statement of ownership rather than a description of a wall. Nothing physical is named in the text, which is why the boundary can be read at several levels, but the plain sense concerns the water people drink and the salt they sail on, and the plain sense is where the sūrah begins.

**What the Meeting Supplies**

Provisions that come from two seas that cannot mix are among the most ordinary facts of a coastal life. Estuaries where rivers empty into the sea are the richest fishing grounds on any coast, because the mixing of nutrients from the land with the water of the ocean feeds the young of countless species. Salt is harvested where sea water is trapped and dried, and pearls and coral come from the saline water that supports them. The sūrah will name the pearls in a few verses, and the ships in the verse after that, so the passage is already moving through a sailor's inventory: the meeting of waters, the barrier between them, the ornaments they yield, and the vessels that cross them. A merchant in Mecca who financed caravans to the sea would have recognized this list as a description of his own trade.

**Allegorical Readings and Their Grounds**

Some commentators extended the two seas to mean the sky and the earth, or this world and the next, with the barrier standing for the decree that separates them. Others, following the same habit, read the pair as formal religious knowledge and spiritual knowledge, which meet in a person and remain distinct, with the intelligence serving as the barrier that keeps either from overwhelming the other. These readings are not what the verse says, and their authors did not claim that they were. They are applications built on the structure of the sentence: two things that meet without merging, and a boundary that preserves both. The literal reading carries the exegetical weight, because the verse sits inside a passage about the actual sea, and the sea is what the pearls and the ships in the following verses come from. The allegories survive as homilies rather than as interpretations.

**A Sign That Concerns Salt, Not Metaphysics**

The chapter's method in this section is to point at things a person can verify with his own senses, and the meeting of fresh and salt water belongs to that method. Anyone who has stood at the mouth of a river has seen the brown current push into the green sea and then fade. The favour being named is not the existence of a mystery but the existence of a stable arrangement in which two necessary supplies survive contact with each other. Drinking water and the salt sea are both indispensable, and the boundary between them is what keeps either from being ruined by the other. The sūrah asks the two multitudes to notice that arrangement before it asks them anything, and the noticing is the answer the question is looking for.

**Why the Barrier Is Part of the Favour**

A reader who thinks of boundaries as restrictions will misread the next verse. The barrier between the two waters is not a wall built to keep creatures away from something; it is the condition under which both supplies continue to exist. If the seas merged completely, the fresh water of the world would be lost to the salt, and if fresh water displaced the sea entirely, the oceans that produce pearls and carry ships would disappear. The sūrah names the mixture first and the boundary second because the boundary is what makes the mixture useful. The same logic governs the balance, the measured orbits, and the appointed terms of the chapter's earlier verses. Limits in this Book are frequently the mechanism by which mercy is delivered, and the two waters are one of the plainest examples a person can watch from a riverbank. A sailor who knows where the fresh water ends and the salt begins can find the river mouth in poor visibility, and a farmer who knows how far the tide reaches can plant accordingly. The boundary is information as well as protection, and the men who worked these coasts used it every day without asking who had set it.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:20

> yet between them is a barrier they never cross.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Boundary Nobody Built and Nobody Breaks**

The verse names something that has no visible substance and yet holds two large bodies of water apart. *Barzakh* means a partition, a thing standing between two others, and the text gives it no material. The classical commentaries supplied explanations: dry land separating a river from the sea, or the decree of God preventing either body from overwhelming the other, or a hidden power by which each keeps its own nature. Al-Qurṭubī described the barrier as belonging to the power of God rather than to any physical object, and Ibn Kathīr took it as a divine will that prevents corruption of one by the other. The Quran's word choice keeps the barrier in the realm of command rather than construction, and this matters for the argument of the chapter, which has been describing order as something God says and maintains.

**The Dual That Points at Two Subjects**

The Arabic verb here is dual, which means the sentence could be read in more than one way. Most commentators took the two subjects to be the two seas, so that the verse says the waters do not overrun the boundary set for them. Others read the dual as the two multitudes addressed throughout the sūrah, human beings and jinn, so that the boundary is between the fresh and the salt, and those who might cross it are the creatures who use both. The first reading fits the grammar of the surrounding verses and the second fits the sūrah's refrain. The disagreement is not resolved, and it does not need to be, because the two readings describe the same arrangement from different sides: a limit that the created things obey, watched by creatures who are supposed to notice.

**The Same Word Used for the State After Death**

*Barzakh* appears in one other place in the Quran, where it names the interval between death and the resurrection: the barrier behind which the dead remain until the appointed day. The lexical connection is worth stating because it shows how the Book uses the word. A barzakh is a partition that separates two conditions without abolishing either of them, and it is not a wall that can be climbed or negotiated. Between the two waters, the partition preserves the identity of each; between this life and the next, it preserves a person in a state from which return is impossible. The two uses share the same idea of a limit set by God that no creature can rewrite, and the sea verse teaches the reader the word before the verses about death put it to its final use.

**What the Barrier Preserves**

The barrier is described as being for the good of both bodies of water, and the ordinary observation behind the verse confirms it. Salt water does not turn fresh rivers brackish along their whole length, and fresh water does not dilute the ocean to the point where its coral and pearls die. Each body keeps the properties that make it useful. The chapter's habit is to present such arrangements as favours rather than as laws, and the two seas fit the pattern exactly. A person drinks from the river and eats from the sea, and both are available because neither has consumed the other. The sūrah's insistence on limits across its whole length — the measured sun, the set balance, the appointed term — reaches one of its clearest physical expressions in this verse about water that stays where it was placed.

**Oceanographers and the Ancient Text**

Modern descriptions of what happens where fresh water meets salt water use the language of density and salinity, and the boundary is not a line but a zone in which the two waters mix slowly under ordinary conditions. Nothing in the verse competes with that description and nothing in it depends on later science to be intelligible. A fisherman who has worked an estuary knows that the character of the water changes sharply and predictably, and the Quran's audience included seafarers who knew the coast of the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean well enough to find the statement unremarkable as observation and remarkable as a claim about its source. The verse takes a fact of their working lives and asks them who set the arrangement, which is precisely the question the refrain repeats.

**The Refrain That Follows This Barrier**

The next line will return to the question about denied favours, and the placement is instructive. What precedes it is a statement about a limit that fish, currents, and salt all obey without argument. The reader who has just been told that the waters keep within their bounds is being prepared to answer for his own behaviour with respect to bounds. The sūrah does not draw the comparison out loud here; it puts the boundary on the page and then asks the question, leaving the listener to notice that the created order is being credited with an obedience that rational creatures have not yet matched.

**The Same Boundary Put to the Reader as a Question**

Another sūrah states the barrier in the form of a challenge, listing the works in question one after another: "Or ˹ask them,˺ 'Who made the earth a place of settlement, caused rivers to flow through it, placed firm mountains upon it, and set a barrier between ˹fresh and salt˺ bodies of water? Was it another god besides Allah?' Absolutely not! But most of them do not know." (27:61). The verse gathers four provisions into one sentence and then puts the decisive question about ownership at the end, exactly as this chapter does with its refrain. The agreement between the two passages is in the argument, not simply in the imagery. Both treat the boundary between fresh and salt water as a piece of evidence, and both use it to press the listener toward a conclusion he is expected to reach on his own. The reader of this sūrah has just been shown the same evidence in a different order, and the refrain that follows is the question of ownership asked in its shortest form.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:21

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain Stops at the Shore**

This occurrence of the refrain is placed after the two seas and before the pearls that come out of them, and the position makes water the subject of the question. Of all the favours named so far, water is the one that people use most often and credit least. It is drawn, carried, drunk, poured for washing, and given to animals dozens of times a day by people who have never once said where it came from. The question interrupts that routine. It does not arrive after a miracle or a disaster; it arrives after an arrangement so ordinary that a listener who has been reciting the chapter may pass over it. The sūrah's practice throughout the passage is to make the reader pause at the plainest items, and the plainest item in the verses just finished is the water that keeps tasting of nothing.

**A Question Built on an Impossible Choice**

The Arabic interrogative *ayy* asks the listener to pick one thing out of a set. Which of these favours do you deny? The construction forces the mind to try to select, and the attempt is what makes the verse work. A person who begins to name one favour he has refused to acknowledge will usually find that he cannot isolate it from the others. The fresh water is tied to the rain, the rain to the winds, the winds to the atmosphere, the atmosphere to the earth's rotation, and the rotation to a reckoning. Every candidate points back to the whole, and the whole is credited to the same Lord. Commentators read the question as rhetorical for that reason. It is not seeking information; it is arranging the listener's knowledge in a way that leaves him with one coherent answer.

**The Kind of Denial the Question Exposes**

Denial of water is rarely a statement. It is the absence of a sentence that was never formed, and the ordinary human pattern is to consume a gift for fifty years without ever acknowledging it. The Book treats that pattern as the most serious charge in its vocabulary of ingratitude, because it combines full use of the gift with total disregard of the giver. The refrain's repeated verb covers the person who says the favour came from nature, from his own effort, or from nobody at all; it also covers the person who says nothing and never thinks about it. In the passage just finished, both seas obey the boundary set for them without a word, and the two multitudes are being asked to give an answer that water and salt are not required to give.

**Water as the Ordinary Measure of Mercy**

The Quran repeatedly uses water as the clearest evidence of provision, from the rain that revives dead ground to the rivers that flow beneath gardens. It uses it because thirst is a fact everyone has felt and because the remedy arrives on a schedule nobody controls. The sūrah of the Criterion calls the two bodies of water fresh and palatable, salty and bitter, and then states the boundary between them. The pairing in this chapter follows the same logic: a sweet supply and a salt supply, a barrier between them, and ornaments and food out of both. The question attached to all of it is whether the creature who drinks twice a day has credited the arrangement to anyone. Nothing in the passage depends on philosophy or on accepting a doctrine about the unseen, and nothing in it is aimed at specialists.

**From the Sky to the Shore, the Question Widens**

The first three occurrences of the refrain stood after the creation of the human being and the jinn and after the two horizons. This one stands after the meeting of the seas, and the scope of the favours being counted has broadened from the sky to the entire circuit of land and water. The list is still recognizable in a single day of a listener's life: he wakes under the sky, walks on the ground, eats a harvest, crosses a river, and tastes salt in his food. The refrain is placed at every point where that ordinary day becomes visible, which is the function it will serve for the rest of the chapter. The question stops a person at the shore and asks him to account for the water, because the water is where the accounting is easiest to avoid.

**A Refrain That Teaches by Repetition**

Repetition is the sūrah's pedagogy, and this verse shows it working. A reader who has heard the question four times in twenty verses no longer meets it as a novelty; he meets it as a return. The effect of a return is different from the effect of an argument: it waits for the listener instead of pressing him, and it gives him the chance to answer differently the next time it comes. The jinn answered at every occurrence, according to the report preserved from the Prophet's recitation, and the practice they modelled is the one the structure invites. The question is short, and its brevity is deliberate. It can be recited by a child, answered by anyone, and repeated until it becomes the habit of a mind that has learned to notice what it has been given.

**Water Among the Book's Great Signs**

The Quran lists water among the items it calls signs, and the two seas in this passage are one instance of a wider pattern. The sūrah of the Cow gathers a series of them in one verse: "Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth; the alternation of the day and the night; the ships that sail the sea for the benefit of humanity; the rain sent down by Allah from the skies, reviving the earth after its death..." (2:164). Rain, rivers, and the meeting of fresh and salt water appear in that list beside the ships and the stars, which is exactly how this chapter treats them. The two words, favour and sign, describe the same objects from different angles. A favour is what a creature receives; a sign is what the creature is meant to read. The refrain turns the reader from the first category into the second by asking him to acknowledge what he has been given, and it does so at the water's edge because water is the clearest case of a favour that everyone uses and nobody studies.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:22

> Out of both ˹waters˺ come forth pearls and coral.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ornaments From the Same Water That Feeds**

The verse draws luxury out of the two bodies of water that the previous verses described, and it makes the drawing out a provision. The same sea that supplies food also supplies jewellery, and the Book lists both together where it describes the sea's subjection to human beings: "It is He Who made the sea subservient, that you may eat fresh meat from it and extract ornaments to wear." (16:14). The pairing of meat and ornament in that verse is the same pairing found here, and the point is that provision is not limited to survival. A person is fed and also decorated; the second need is not treated as frivolous or as evidence of worldliness. The Quran's lists routinely include the beautiful alongside the necessary, and this verse joins a chapter about mercy to a case of pure luxury in a single line.

**What the Word Marjān Names**

The second noun, *marjān*, is understood by some commentators as small pearls and by others as large and valuable ones. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī record both positions among the early authorities, and the disagreement turns on usage rather than on any difficulty in the sentence. The Arabic lexicon supports the wider sense of coral or of the precious beads that come from the sea, and the translations differ accordingly. What the disagreement shows is how closely the two nouns in the verse are related. Pearls and coral can be read as two grades of one commodity, or as two different products of the same water, and neither reading changes the sentence's work. The verse is naming the sea's most valuable yields, and the ambiguity about which grade of pearl is meant only underlines that value.

**Who Brings These Things Forth**

The verb can be read as a statement that the two waters produce pearls, or as a passive construction: pearls and coral are brought forth from them. The second reading leaves the agent unstated, which in Arabic is a way of pointing to God without naming Him, and it makes the sentence fit the sūrah's pattern of describing provision without dwelling on the mechanism. Both readings are defensible and the commentators acknowledged them both. The difference matters only if a reader wants to know where agency is located in the sentence. The Book's habit elsewhere is to credit the act to God directly: it made the sea subservient, it brought the two bodies of water together. This verse can be heard in that same voice, with the pearls arriving as something done rather than something that merely happened.

**A Merchant's Verse in a Merchant City**

Pearls were the most expensive commodity in the world the sūrah was first recited into, and the divers who gathered them were among the poorest workers on the coast. The trade that carried them from the Gulf and the Red Sea to the markets of Syria and Iraq was the same trade that made Mecca's caravans profitable, and a Meccan listener would have known exactly what the verse was naming. The Book does not sentimentalise the trade, and it does not attack it either. It places the pearls in the list of provisions and lets the question of the refrain fall on the whole chain: the diver, the broker, the caravan, and the buyer who wears the ornament. Everything in that chain is a favour received, and the verse names the last item first because it is the one that shines.

**The Pearl as a Wound Repaired**

A pearl forms inside an oyster when a foreign particle lodges in its tissue, and the animal coats the irritant in layer after layer of a substance that hardens into lustre. The process is a defence against injury, and its product is one of the ornaments this verse names. The detail is worth stating because the sūrah's interest lies in the ordinary and the overlooked, and this particular gift is manufactured out of discomfort. The Quran does not mention the mechanism, and no part of the verse depends on the reader knowing it. But the fact belongs to the kind of observation the chapter keeps inviting: the thing that the wealthy wear around their necks began as a wound in a shellfish, and the sea was arranged so that the wound would produce something a human being would consider beautiful.

**Beauty Named as a Category of Provision**

A chapter that had listed bread in the previous verses now lists jewellery, and the sequence treats both as gifts of the same order. The Quran's view of adornment is stated elsewhere without apology, since clothing, perfume, and ornament are described as things God has brought out for His servants. The ascetic instinct that treats beauty as a distraction from the Creator is not the sūrah's position here. The seas were made to produce pearls, and human beings were made to want them, and the desire for beauty is credited to the same arrangement as the hunger for food. The refrain that follows this verse will ask about both, and the question will be easier to answer for the ornament than for the food, since most people have at least thanked someone for a gift they could see.

**The Divers Who Never Wore the Pearls**

The men who brought the pearls to the surface were usually the poorest people on the coast. They dived on a held breath, worked without equipment, and were paid a fraction of what a finished necklace would fetch in a city they would never visit. The sūrah names the pearls as a provision and does not describe the labour, which is a reminder that the favours listed in this chapter are not distributed evenly among the people who handle them. The Quran is aware of that gap and addresses it elsewhere with rules about honest wages, fair measure, and the treatment of workers. What the verse establishes here is the origin of the ornament: it comes out of the water, not out of the market, and the ocean that produced it is named as belonging to the same Lord whose favours the two multitudes are asked about. A merchant who understood the verse would have understood that his inventory had a giver, and that the chain which delivered it included men whose share of the blessing was a wage.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:23

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question After the Pearls**

This occurrence of the refrain follows the verse about pearls and coral, and it is placed there on purpose. The favours immediately before it are not bread and water but ornament and luxury, and they are the kind of gift that people are most likely to treat as their own achievement. Nobody feels proud of the rain, and many people feel proud of the necklace on a table. The question arrives at precisely the point where ownership is easiest to claim, and it asks the same thing it has asked after the sun and the sea. The sūrah does not drop the luxury items from the list because they are less essential. It counts them and then asks, which keeps the accounting honest about the range of what people actually value.

**Gifts for the Eye as Well as the Stomach**

A book concerned only with survival would have stopped at grain and water. This chapter keeps adding categories: fragrance, then pearls and coral. The inclusion says something about the One who gives, and something about the creature that receives. Human beings are made to enjoy colour, shine, and scent, and the Quran treats that constitution as intended rather than as a weakness to be corrected. The Prophet ﷺ accepted gifts of perfume and wore it, and the descriptions of the gardens later in this sūrah include brocade, green cushions, and beautiful companions. Adornment in this Book is a feature of the created order, not a concession to human vanity. The refrain's question therefore covers the whole range of pleasure a person has experienced, not only the portion that kept him alive.

**What the Question Asks of the Poor**

A reader with nothing to wear might reasonably ask what pearls have to do with him. The sūrah's answer is contained in who is being addressed: the two multitudes, without distinction of wealth, and the favours named across the chapter include the ones nobody can be deprived of, such as daylight, breath, and speech. The pearls are named as evidence of what the sea contains, not as a required possession, and the same chapter will describe gardens in which the reward is greater than any ornament. The question is not whether a listener owns the pearl. It is whether the goodness of the order that produced it is credited to its source, and a poor man who has watched the sun rise is as equipped to answer that as a rich man with a chest of jewels.

**Repetition as Formation**

The refrain is training a habit, and the placement of this occurrence is part of the training. Each return finds the listener in a different frame: after the sky, after the seas, after the pearls. What is being built is a reflex of noticing, the kind that surfaces when a person opens his eyes and sees a colour he had stopped seeing. Scripture uses repetition for this purpose because human attention does not hold a lesson for long, and the sūrah's thirty-one occurrences are spaced so that no listener can settle into a single mood. The verse is the same sentence as the twelfth and the sixteenth, and its function changes each time it is heard because the material around it has changed. That is the whole method of the chapter in miniature.

**The Word the Question Uses for Favours**

The noun in the refrain, *ālāʾ*, is the plural of a word for a benefit or a benefaction, and it carries the sense of something given freely rather than earned. The question therefore does not ask about wages, rewards, or rights. It asks about gifts, and the distinction is the reason the interrogative is so hard to answer honestly. A wage can be claimed as a matter of entitlement, and the earner can name the work that produced it. A gift has no such defence, and the more valuable the gift, the less the recipient can say about why he received it. Every item counted so far in this chapter falls on the gift side of that line, and the pearls are the clearest case, since no human being made the oyster or the water it lived in.

**A Question That Cannot Be Answered Back**

The listener who wants to argue with the verse has nothing to work with. He cannot claim he was never given anything, since he is breathing, and he cannot claim he produced what he has, since the elements of his body were arranged before he existed. The refrain's power comes from that combination of an exhaustive list and a single-word question. It returns often enough that a person will eventually notice that he has been answering it all his life without speaking, in the way he uses what he has been given. The pearls in the verse above are worth a moment's thought for exactly that reason: they are the least necessary item in the chapter so far, and they are counted like everything else.

**No Scholar Is Required to Answer**

The argument of this chapter has never depended on education, and this verse shows why the refrain can be put to the whole world. A person does not need to read, own property, or follow a philosophical argument in order to notice that the sea produced something beautiful and that he had no part in arranging it. The Book elsewhere addresses the same point to the unlettered as readily as to the learned, and it never suggests that a simple person's gratitude is worth less than a scholar's proof. The pearls were chosen as an example that would sit in the hand of a listener; the water and the harvest were chosen for the same reason. What the question asks is not an analysis of the universe but an acknowledgement of a gift, and every human being who has ever held something valuable knows how to make that acknowledgement. The verse lets the reader do it before the chapter proceeds to harder matters.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:24

> To Him belong the ships with raised sails, sailing through the seas like mountains.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ships Named as a Sign of Ownership**

The verse opens with a possessive construction: to Him belong the ships. Nothing is said about the sailors, the builders, or the merchants who finance the voyage, and that silence is the point. Human skill is real and the Book does not deny it; it places the whole enterprise under the ownership of the One who made the water and the wind. The same framing appears where the Quran lists the sea among the signs: "Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth; the alternation of the day and the night; the ships that sail the sea for the benefit of humanity; the rain sent down by Allah from the skies, reviving the earth after its death..." (2:164). In that verse the ships are a sign, not merely an industry. The chapter of al-Raḥmān takes the same object and puts it in a list of favours, which is another way of saying the same thing.

**What the Word for These Ships Carries**

The translation renders *munshaʾāt* as ships with raised sails, and the word can be read in more than one way. Taken as a passive participle from the verb meaning to raise or bring into being, it describes vessels whose sails are lifted, and the image that follows — like mountains — supports that reading, since a sail seen from a distance is a mass standing above the water. Some commentators took the word as referring to everything that has been brought into being, so that the verse would mention all created things before returning to the ships. The first reading is the more natural in a passage about the sea, and the commentators who preferred it noted that the sails of a large vessel do resemble a landmark on the horizon. The second reading is possible and adds a wider reference, but it does not displace the ordinary sense.

**Sails That Look Like Mountains**

The comparison in the verse works because a large ship under full sail is the tallest moving object a coastal person would ever see. From a harbour, a fleet arriving is a range of hills on the water; from a small boat, a merchantman is a wall that comes with the wind. The simile also carries a warning that the sūrah does not state: the same mass that carries goods across the sea can sink beneath it. Everything the chapter names is a creature, and the ships are no exception. What protects them is not their size but the laws that govern the water they float on, and the reader who has been following the chapter knows who set those laws. A mountain that floats at the command of its owner is a stronger image of dependence than a mountain on land.

**Navigation Is Built on the Reckoning of the Sky**

The ships in this verse depend on the astronomy of verse five. A vessel that sails beyond the sight of a coastline needs the sun, the moon, and the stars to know where it is, and the precision of their movement is what makes an open-water route possible. Sailors of the pre-modern Indian Ocean and the Red Sea navigated by the positions of the stars, and the lunar calendar that governs the pilgrimage and the fast was also the calendar that governed the sailing season. The sūrah has already asked the reader to notice that the sun and moon travel with a reckoning. The ships in this verse are the practical consequence, and the chapter's movement from the sky to the sails is a chain of provision rather than a jump between subjects.

**The Sea as a Road Nobody Built**

Human beings did not construct the ocean or the winds that cross it, and no one had to negotiate a treaty with the water for passage. The currents and the seasonal winds were already in place, and the whole history of maritime trade is the story of people discovering a road that existed before they did. The Quran treats that as a standing invitation to gratitude: the sea was subdued for human benefit, and the ships merely use what was provided. The chapter's list has been moving through things a person receives and uses without making: light, water, harvest, ornament, and now transport. The ships are the one item on the list that human beings actually build, and the verse still places them in the same category, because the material and the medium belong to someone else.

**A Chapter That Counts Movement Among Its Gifts**

Being able to leave home is a favour that people rarely count. The sūrah lists the ships between the pearls and the dissolving of the world, and the placement gives travel a place among the provisions of ordinary life. A listener whose livelihood came from moving goods would have heard the verse as a description of his trade; a listener who never left his valley would have heard it as a window onto a larger creation. Both are addressed by the same question that follows, and both can answer it, because the wind that fills the sails blows on everyone and belongs to no one. The ships are named as His, and the seas they cross are His, and the refrain will ask the two multitudes what they make of that.

**The Purpose Clause Attached to the Sea**

Other passages describe the subjection of the sea and state why it was done. One verse reads: "Allah is the One Who has subjected the sea for you so that ships may sail upon it by His command, and that you may seek His bounty, and that perhaps you will be grateful." (45:12). Another puts the ships and the winds in the same sentence: "And one of His signs is that He sends the winds, ushering in good news ˹of rain˺ so that He may give you a taste of His mercy, and that ships may sail by His command, and that you may seek His bounty, and perhaps you will be grateful." (30:46). The two verses end in the same place, with gratitude named as the intended result of a working harbour. Trade in that framing is not a distraction from worship; it is one of the arrangements through which a creature receives what it needs, and the profit a caravan makes is inside the category of bounty rather than outside it. This sūrah's ordering of the same material reaches the identical conclusion through its refrain: the sea is His, the wind is His, the ship is His, and the question is what the two multitudes will say about that.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:25

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain After a Human Achievement**

The ships in the previous verse are the first item in the chapter that human beings actually make. The sun rises without assistance, the sea meets the river without a plan, and the palms grow without a nursery, but a vessel with sails is a work of hands. The refrain arrives here more sharply for that reason. It asks the two multitudes which favour they deny immediately after naming something they built, and the question is aimed at the point where pride has the most natural foothold. The chapter does not accuse the shipwright of stealing anything. It reminds him who owns the water, the wind, and the timber, and it treats the ship as a favour rather than as an accomplishment. A person who can answer the question honestly about the thing he made himself has learned to answer it about everything else.

**The Sailor's Dependence Is Easily Seen**

Nobody has ever pretended that his boat was self-propelled. A vessel moves because wind fills the sail or oars pull the water, and the crew spends its day managing forces it did not create and cannot control. The ship is therefore a picture of the human situation that a farmer, a trader, and a king all share without noticing it on land. The sea removes the illusion of autonomy for a few days at a time, which is one reason sailors across cultures have been more willing than their neighbours to speak about providence. The Qur'an uses the voyage in exactly that way, listing the ships among the signs and pairing them with the rain and the winds. To be on the water is to feel what the chapter keeps stating: the creature is carried by arrangements it did not set up.

**Why the Prosperous Are the Hardest Audience**

The sūrah's audience included merchants whose caravans and ships had made them comfortable, and the refrain's question is hardest for such listeners. Comfort produces the impression that the arrangement is deserved; the caravan arrived, the accounts balanced, the profits came in, and no one stopped to ask who had supplied the weather. The Book addresses that impression repeatedly when it warns the wealthy against reading their wealth as a verdict on their standing with God, and it does so with the same vocabulary of gifts and denial that this chapter uses. In this sūrah the warning is delivered as a question rather than as a rebuke, which leaves the listener to supply the answer. A person who has just been told that the ships are God's, and not his, must decide what to do with that sentence in his own ledger.

**Gratitude as a Discipline for Success**

The refrain's contribution to the successful listener is a habit of mind that fits the shipping lanes as well as the mosque. Gratitude in the Qur'an is not a mood that arrives on its own; it is a discipline that has to be practised in the middle of activity. A merchant who has learned to say that the voyage was provided is protected from two failures at once: the arrogance of the man who credits himself, and the stinginess of the man who is afraid to lose what he thinks he owns. The same discipline turns the money he has into something he holds in trust, which changes what he does with it. This is why the sūrah keeps attaching its question to things that people are likely to be proud of, and why the ships occupy a place in the list between the pearls and the passing away of the world.

**Borrowed Skill and Borrowed Breath**

The shipwright's skill is not excluded from the list of gifts. A person who reads well, steers by the stars, or joins two planks so that they hold in a rough sea has a set of abilities that arrived without his choosing them. The Book includes the faculties in its accounting, and the sūrah's fourth verse already named speech as a divine gift, which is the same argument applied to the intelligence that built the vessel. If the capacity to learn a trade is part of the provision, then the trade and its product are downstream of the gift, and the question about denial applies to the whole chain. Nothing in this turns the sailor into a passive object. He works, plans, and risks, and the verse still places what he produces among the favours his Lord owns.

**The Question That Keeps Count of Human Hands**

The sea passage in this sūrah is not finished with people. The ships come back into the chapter's description of the worlds to come only as images, and the reader is left with the plain statement that a vessel of a size to be mistaken for a mountain is owned by its Creator. The refrain follows immediately, and the placement treats the whole maritime economy as one more item on a list that began with a name, moved through the sky and the harvest, and has reached the harbour. The question does not ask the listener to stop trading. It asks him to notice, on the quay as much as the shore, whose sea he is crossing and whose wind is in his sails.

**The Winds That Fill the Sails Are Themselves a Sign**

A merchant who sailed the Red Sea depended on a wind he could not summon, and the Book treats that wind as a deliberate provision rather than as a feature of the weather: "And one of His signs is that He sends the winds, ushering in good news ˹of rain˺ so that He may give you a taste of His mercy, and that ships may sail by His command, and that you may seek His bounty, and perhaps you will be grateful." (30:46). The verse lists three effects of a single act: rain for the land, sail-power for the sea, and the chance to seek a living. All three arrive from the same source, and the sentence names the aim, which is gratitude. Read beside this sūrah, the passage shows how ordinary the Quran's logic is. It does not present the wind as a mystery but as a service performed for creatures that never asked for it and seldom acknowledge it. The crews of the vessels in this verse made their living from that arrangement, and the refrain that follows asks them what they made of it.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:26

> Every being on earth is bound to perish.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence That Ends the Whole List**

The chapter has been counting provisions, and this verse states that every recipient of them will die. The Arabic construction is emphatic and general: it covers everything that lives on the earth, and it uses a word for living beings rather than for human beings alone. The sentence does not qualify itself. It does not limit the verdict to the wicked or except the righteous, and it draws no distinction between the two multitudes the refrain has been addressing. Anyone who had been following the list of favours and feeling the weight of the question now receives a further fact about himself: the account will be closed at short notice. The sūrah moves from what has been given to how long the receiver lasts, and the second fact is the frame around the first.

**Who Is Included in the Verdict**

Commentators read the verse as applying to every living creature on the face of the earth, human beings and jinn included. Some extended it to every created thing that exists in this world, and the Arabic supports a wide reading of the word for being. The point on which all agree is that the verdict is not restricted to one species. The jinn, who had just been named as the second audience of the refrain, are mortal by the same sentence, whatever their abilities; the animals, which have never denied anything and never affirmed anything, are covered; and the human being, who has been given speech and understanding, is covered with everything else. The only entity the sūrah excludes is named in the next verse, and the exclusivity of that exception is what makes it worth the reader's attention.

**Why Death Enters a Chapter of Favours**

A list of gifts followed by a statement of universal death may look harsh, and the chapter's structure presents it as a mercy instead. Mortality limits the power of every tyrant, dissolves every monopoly, and corrects every arrangement that treats a human being as permanent. It also makes the accounting unavoidable, since an account that is never closed can be postponed forever. The Book uses the same move elsewhere, in a verse read wherever Muslims bury their dead: "Every soul will taste death." (3:185). In this sūrah the fact is placed at the centre, between the favours of the created world and the gardens promised to the God-fearing, so that the reader sees the gift, the limit, and the reward in one frame. What dies is the recipient; what has been received has already been recorded.

**The Grammar of Passing Away**

The Arabic verb describes something leaving, ceasing, and coming to nothing, and its noun form became the classical term for the annihilation of everything other than God. The Book uses the vocabulary in connection with the transience of wealth, power, and life, and it sets the whole created order against the permanence of its Lord in one of the most quoted verses of the Quran: "And do not invoke any other god along with Allah. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. Everything is bound to perish except His ˹perfect˺ Being. All authority belongs to Him. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned." (28:88). The terms of that verse and this one agree. What is passing belongs to a world; what remains is a Being, and the sūrah has already told its readers who that is with the Name that opens it.

**A Verse Recited in Difficult Rooms**

These two verses are among the most familiar lines in the Quran for anyone who has sat with the dying or stood at a grave. They are short, and their brevity is one of the reasons they can be said aloud when a person has no strength for a long passage. The verse does not say that the deceased perished alone or that the loss is permanent; it states the universal fact and leaves the next line to name the One who remains. That is the shape of the consolation the chapter offers. Grief is not treated as a denial of mercy or as a failure of faith; it is treated as the natural consequence of loving creatures that pass away, and the sūrah answers it by pointing past the creature to the One who is not subject to the same verdict.

**What the Statement Does to the Rest of the Chapter**

The remaining lines of the sūrah describe gardens, springs, fabrics, and companions, and every one of them is read differently after this verse. They are not the continuation of the world the reader knows; they are what comes after it has been dissolved. The chapter's movement from provision to extinction to reward is the whole of its architecture, and this verse is the hinge. Everything the two multitudes have received, everything they have denied or acknowledged, and everything they have built on the earth, including the ships, the balance, and the harvest, will be taken out of the account by death. What will be left is the record of what was done with the gifts, and the sūrah turns to that subject after establishing, in one sentence, that nothing else survives.

**A Parable of the Harvest That Withers**

The Book repeatedly uses the fate of vegetation to make the same point as this verse, and one parable states it almost step by step: "And give them a parable of this worldly life. ˹It is˺ like the plants of the earth, thriving when sustained by the rain We send down from the sky. Then they ˹soon˺ turn into chaff scattered by the wind. And Allah is fully capable of ˹doing˺ all things." (18:45). The imagery is drawn from the farming country the sūrah has already described. Green growth after rain is the strongest impression of permanence a desert dweller receives, and it lasts one season before it dries and blows away. The verse in this chapter makes the same comparison without the illustration. A listener who has watched a field go from green to straw knows exactly how long the world's tenure looks when it is measured against a few summers, and the sūrah is asking him to apply that knowledge to himself before the next verse names the One who is not subject to it.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:27

> Only your Lord Himself, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain ˹forever˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The One Exception to a Universal Rule**

The previous verse says that everything living on the earth is passing away, and this verse names the single exception. Arabic marks it with a construction known to the grammarians as a restriction: the exception is placed before the verb, so that the sentence reads as a declaration that only one subject stands outside the verdict. Nothing is said about angels or prophets or any other being; the verse leaves no category open. The word rendered as Himself is *wajh*, the face, which in Arabic usage also means the self or the presence of someone, and the classical commentators understood it as the Being of God rather than a part of Him. The effect is that the sūrah's whole universe of creatures ends in one remaining subject, and the remaining is stated as a fact about who He is rather than as a decision He might revise.

**Majesty and Honour as a Pair**

The two attributes in the verse, *jalāl* and *ikrām*, are the terms that give this line its resonance in Muslim devotion. Jalāl is the greatness that produces awe, and ikrām is the generosity and honour that produce gratitude; the two together describe a Lord who is both overwhelming and beneficent. The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have encouraged the recitation of this pair as part of the remembrance of God, and the Qur'an itself uses the same two words again at the very end of the sūrah, where the naming of the Lord is called blessed. The pairing is precise for this chapter because everything in it has been both awe-inspiring and generous. The sky, the seas, the harvest, and the judgement all belong to the first term; the mercy that begins the chapter with a name belongs to the second.

**What Permanence Means for Everything Else**

The Book states the relation between the enduring Lord and the transient world in a verse that is among the most frequently recited in Islam: "Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining. Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth... His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him. For He is the Most High, the Greatest." (2:255). The names at the start of that verse answer the statement in this one. The One who remains is the Living who sustains everything else, and the preservation of the heavens and the earth is described as a task that does not tire Him. Creatures pass away, and the order that holds the rest of creation together is maintained by a Lord who does not.

**The Axis of the Chapter**

Commentators noted the position of these two verses at the centre of the sūrah and treated them as its pivot. The first half has described the created world and its provisions; the second half will describe the gardens and the judgement; and between the two stands the statement that everything created is passing away and only the Lord endures. This is the doctrinal sentence that keeps the chapter's lush descriptions of water, harvests, pearls, and gardens from becoming a picture of permanent material happiness. The blessings of this world are real, and they are on loan. The verse states the terms of the loan before the sūrah goes on to describe what is given without end to those who are loyal to the Giver, and the reader who misses this line will misread everything after it.

**Why the Consolation Works**

The doctrine of divine permanence sounds abstract until it is applied to what people actually lose. Everything that has been named in this chapter as a gift is also something that can be taken: the sun moves toward its appointed term, the sea can drown a crew, the harvest can fail, and the body that enjoys all of them decays. A chapter that ended its list with the statement that everything perishes would be bleak if it stopped there. The verse gives grief a place to stand. What the reader loves is passing, and the One who gave it is not, which means that what has been entrusted is still in the hands of the One who can restore it. The gardens that follow in the second half of the sūrah are introduced on the strength of this permanence.

**The Attribute That Ends the Sūrah Too**

The final verse of the chapter repeats the two words of this verse in a declaration of blessing, and the echo is deliberate. The sūrah opens with the Name of the Compassionate, passes through its list of favours, states the mortality of everything, and closes by blessing the Name of the Lord who possesses majesty and honour. The same phrase that answers the question of what remains amid universal dissolution also concludes the whole recitation. The architecture makes the reader's final thought the same as the middle of the chapter: whatever the world does, and whatever the two multitudes answer, the One whose majesty and honour are unending is the fixed point around which everything else in the sūrah turns.

**Why the Verse Names the Lord Rather Than God**

The verse says the Face of your Lord remains, where other passages in the Book speak of the Face of God. Commentators gave a reason connected to this chapter's subject. Lordship, *rubūbiyyah*, is the attribute under which provision, ownership, and care are exercised, and the sūrah has spent a long passage counting provisions. Naming the Lord in this verse keeps the reader inside the theme of bounty and rule rather than shifting the frame to the pure divine name. Al-Rāzī made a related point about the grammar of the exception: had the verse intended to say that nothing whatsoever survives except the Face of God, it would have needed a different construction, and its actual wording states a positive fact about the One who remains rather than a subtraction from the whole. What the reader holds, then, is a statement about ownership that continues after everything owned has gone. The Lord of the harvests is still the Lord when there are no harvests.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:28

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Asked Just After a Funeral Sentence**

This occurrence of the refrain follows the declaration that every living being on the earth will pass away, and the placement gives the question a new edge. The favours being counted now include the information itself: the two multitudes have been told that their stay is temporary, and being told is a mercy. A person who receives a warning about the end of a journey can arrange his affairs; a person who is surprised by it cannot. The sūrah therefore puts the question after the announcement rather than before it, and the sequence says something about the nature of revelation. The Book's habit is to describe the end of things in plain language and treat that description as a benefit, because a creature that knows what it is facing can still do something about it.

**A Warning Counted Among the Gifts**

Most systems of thought treat mortality as bad news, and the Qur'an does not pretend that death is pleasant. It does treat the advance notice as a favour, and it places the notice in a chapter whose theme is mercy. The reasons are visible in the chapter's own list. Everything named as a provision is temporary, so a person who attaches his whole hope to the provision is building on something that has been scheduled for removal. The early verses gave him the sun, the sea, the harvest, and the pearls; this verse adds the timetable. The two halves belong together, and both are gifts, because the same Lord who provided the good things has also announced honestly how long they last and what comes afterwards.

**Denial at the Level of How a Person Lives**

Nobody argues with the statement that everything dies. The denial the verse addresses is practical rather than verbal: a person plans for decades as though he were permanent, avoids the subject, and treats the mention of death as an intrusion. That way of living is a form of calling the announcement untrue, however firmly the person would affirm it with his tongue. The sūrah's question catches the listener in that gap. It has just said that all things pass away, and it asks whether he denies any of the favours, including the warning he has just received. The Arabic verb covers the response of the man who hears the sentence and quietly proceeds as though he had not, which is the ordinary condition of most people most of the time.

**Two Multitudes Under the Same Sentence**

The dual address includes the jinn, and the previous verse's verdict covers them, since the word for living beings on the earth applies to all creatures that inhabit it. The classical discussions of the jinn's lifespan were conducted on this basis: they die like human beings, whatever their abilities and however long their lives may run, because the general statement of the Qur'an leaves them no exemption. The exception named in the verse just before this one belongs to God alone. A community of creatures that is powerful, long-lived, and invisible to human eyes is still a community of creatures, and the refrain asks them the same question it puts to the humans. Two audiences, one mortality, one accounting.

**How the Question Lands in Difficult Rooms**

When these verses are recited beside someone who is dying, the refrain that follows them does not function as an accusation. The person in the room cannot do anything about the favours he has received in the past, and the question is not a demand for a report. Read in that setting, the verse asks the living to say what is true about what they have been given while they still can, and it gives them the sentence to say. The jinn, according to the report of the Prophet's recitation, answered at every occurrence that they denied none of their Lord's favours. That answer is what the verse invites from a person standing at the edge of a loss, and the sūrah supplies both the question and the words for the reply.

**A Refrain That Measures the Weight of Gratitude**

The chapter has now asked the question seven times, and after the statement about universal death it is asking about something more than the harvest. What is being counted is the whole condition of a creature that is fed, protected, carried over the sea, warned of its end, and then offered something beyond the end. The favours named in the first half of the sūrah were provisions for a temporary stay, and the reader has just been told that the stay is temporary. The question about denial therefore reaches the last and largest gift on the list, which is that a creature scheduled to pass away has been told what happens next and invited to prepare for it.

**The Messenger Was Subject to the Same Sentence**

The Book applies mortality to the Prophet himself in plain words, and the verse is worth recalling beside this passage: "You ˹O Prophet˺ will certainly die, and they will die too." (39:30). No messenger, king, or saint has been exempted from the sentence pronounced in the previous verse, and the community that grew up around the Prophet ﷺ understood his death as the same event that awaits every listener of this sūrah. That fact cuts against two errors at once. It prevents any devotion from attaching to a mortal in a way that belongs to God alone, and it prevents despair when the mortal does die, since the permanence the chapter then names belongs to the One whose Face remains. The refrain that follows this statement of universal death is therefore addressed to the two multitudes in their full condition: dependent, temporary, warned, and still able to answer.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:29

> All those in the heavens and the earth are dependent on Him. Day in and day out He has something to bring about.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Every Creature in Both Realms Asks**

The verse gathers the two realms into a single audience and reports something they have in common. Those in the heavens and those on the earth are all entreating Him and all dependent on Him; the angels, who are higher in the created order by every measure the Book gives, are included without distinction. Commentators noted the difference in the way the two groups ask. Those in the heavens plead on behalf of those on the earth, while those on the earth plead for themselves. The result is a creation in which supplication runs in two directions at once, with the higher realm interceding for the lower and the lower asking for its own needs. The Quran states the relation between creature and Creator without flattery on either side: "O humanity! It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy." (35:15).

**Dependence That Does Not Need Words**

Not every creature asks in a language, and the verse does not limit its statement to those who pray. Commentators observed that even a person who never addresses God directly is making a request by the sheer fact of his existence, since he lives on what he did not supply and cannot survive for a moment without continued provision. The plant asks for rain by opening its leaves; the infant asks without knowing the words. Human beings are the only creatures that can miss the point, and the verse quietly returns them to the company they share with everything else. The sūrah has already described the prostration of the stars and the trees in an earlier verse. What is added here is that the prostration of need is universal, and that no creature in either realm is exempt from it, including the ones who are unaware of making it.

**The God Who Is Never Finished**

The second half of the verse states that He is occupied with a matter every day, and the expression rules out the idea of a Creator who finished His work and withdrew. The Book states the same fact in the greatest of its verses about God: "Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him... His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him." (2:255). Commentators connected this verse directly to that one, and some of them contrasted it with the image of a God who rested on the seventh day from the work He had done, which is how the creation account in the Book of Genesis ends. In the Islamic account, the work never stopped. If the sustaining activity were suspended for the blink of an eye, the created order would collapse, and the verse treats that dependence as a permanent arrangement rather than as an occasional intervention.

**What the Daily Tasks Are**

The tasks named by the early authorities are the ones a reader can recognise from his own life. Mujāhid reported from the Successor ʿUbayd ibn ʿUmayr that the affairs of the day include answering the one who supplicates, giving to the one who asks, removing hardship, and curing the one who seeks a cure. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have listed creating from nothing, providing, causing life and death, giving power and taking it away, and granting abundance or restriction. Qatāda added freeing captives and enriching the poor. These are statements of Successors and Companions about the verse rather than prophetic words, and their agreement on the shape of the answer is what makes them useful. What the list describes is providence in its ordinary, daily form. Someone is forgiven, someone's illness ends, a family's fortune rises, and another's falls, all within the same twenty-four hours that this verse calls a day. The chapter that has been counting cosmic favours now names the small ones that arrive with them, and the range is the point. The Maker of the two easterly horizons is described as attending to the specific complaint of a servant.

**A Disclosure That Never Repeats**

Ibn ʿArabī read the verse as a statement about God's self-disclosure, and his reading deserves mention for what it adds to the ordinary sense. In his account, the Lord never shows Himself in the same form twice, either to two people or to one person on two occasions, so that each day's matter is genuinely new and not a repetition of a previous one. This is a Sufi reading, and it rests on a principle rather than on the plain sense of the sentence. What it preserves is the freshness the verse implies. A God whose activity is described as daily is not administering a fixed programme; the chapter's own opening word, the Compassionate, has already said that the operation of the world is personal, and the Sufi reading simply presses that claim into the furthest corner it can reach.

**Why Dependence Is Stated as a Comfort**

The verse is placed immediately after the declaration that every living creature passes away, and the placement turns need into good news. A person who must depend on someone for everything is in a desperate position if that someone is unreliable, hostile, or uninterested. The sūrah has spent a long passage establishing that the One on whom the two realms depend has been giving generously and continuously from the beginning, and it has just said that He does so every day. The two halves of the verse therefore belong together: the first states the need, and the second reports the activity that answers it. Between them they define the position of every creature, and the position is not degradation. To be dependent on the Compassionate is the condition under which the favour of existence is delivered, and the refrain that follows asks how the recipients are treating it.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:30

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question After the Verse of Daily Work**

This occurrence of the refrain follows the statement that the Lord is occupied with a matter every day. The connection is direct. The favours being counted are not only the fixed provisions of land and sea; they include the renewed activity by which each one of them arrives. Grain does not simply exist; it is produced on a schedule, in a particular field, in a particular year, by a process that nobody has ever controlled from beginning to end. The question that follows the verse about daily tasks is asking about that renewal, and it is addressed to the two multitudes in the same dual form as every other occurrence. A reader who answers it honestly is saying something about today as well as about the past, since the mercy being acknowledged is the one still in motion while the words are being recited.

**Favours That Arrive While the Recipient Sleeps**

The verse before this one describes a God who works every day, and the ordinary experience of a human being confirms that the work continues whether he notices or not. A person sleeps, and the night cools the ground; he wakes, and water is still in the well and air is still in the room. Nothing he did during the night maintained the arrangement, and nothing he could do would have improved it. The sūrah's question is aimed at that interval. It asks whether the favours which were renewed without his help are being denied, and denial in this context includes the assumption that the renewal is automatic. A process so regular that it never needs attention is precisely the kind of gift that stops being seen, and the chapter places the question at the point of maximum invisibility.

**Two Realms Named in One Sentence**

The verse that precedes this refrain named the inhabitants of the heavens and the earth together, and the question keeps them together in its dual address. The pairing has accumulated over the course of the sūrah: two multitudes, two horizons, two seas, two creations from clay and fire. By this point in the recitation, the reader has heard the dual form so often that it functions as a signature. Its effect is to prevent the listener from treating himself as the sole concern of the chapter. Everything being counted is counted for a community larger than the one listening, and the answer the verse expects is one that a jinn and a human being could give in the same words. The universality of the favours is matched by the universality of the question.

**Denial as Not Noticing Renewal**

The specific failure this refrain identifies is not the construction of an argument against providence. It is the failure to register that the arrangement is still running. A farmer who plants in spring and harvests in autumn has participated in a renewal he did not design, and a merchant whose ship returns has been carried by winds he did not commission. Neither of them says the renewal came from nowhere, and neither of them says anything else about it either, which is the ordinary form of the denial being described. The Arabic verb is strong and the everyday practice is quiet. The sūrah's method has been to hold the two together: it states the strongest possible case for gratitude and then asks a question that can only be answered by someone who has been paying attention.

**Why the Chapter Repeats Instead of Arguing**

A single well-constructed argument would have taken fewer verses than a refrain repeated thirty-one times, and the sūrah does not use one. The choice reflects what the chapter is asking for. An argument produces agreement, and agreement is not the response the question seeks; gratitude is a habit rather than a conclusion, and habits are formed by repetition. The Book uses the same technique in other places, where a phrase returns at intervals to structure a long passage and to give the listener a fixed point in a moving text. Here the fixed point is also the demand, so that everything named between one occurrence and the next is gathered into a single question. The reader who reaches the end of the chapter has been asked for an answer more than thirty times.

**Gratitude and the Knowledge of One's Own Need**

The verse before this one ends with the statement that every creature entreats God and that He is at work each day. The refrain takes the next step by asking what a creature does with what it has received. Between the two verses sits the whole subject of this sūrah: need on one side, provision on the other, and a question about the response. The Quran treats recognition of one's own need as the beginning of gratitude, and it treats the illusion of independence as the root from which denial grows. A person who believes he stands on his own has nothing to be thankful for, because he credits himself with his position. The verse is therefore asking about the answer to a fact he already knows: the favours were not produced by the recipient, and the One who produces them is still at work.

**An Answer the Reader Has Heard Before**

This refrain has now appeared eight times, and the two multitudes have been given ample opportunity to compose an answer. The report of the jinn's response to the Prophet's recitation preserves the words they used, and it shows that the question was designed to be answered rather than merely contemplated. The sūrah does not leave the listener in the position of a defendant without a statement. It has supplied the fact of the favours, the identification of the Giver, and the question itself, and the response it expects is a short one: none of them is denied. Everything after this verse in the chapter will describe what happens to those who give that answer and those who do not, and the reader has just been asked one more time to decide which group he belongs to.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:31

> We will soon attend to you ˹for judgment˺, O two multitudes ˹of jinn and humans˺!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence That Names Its Audience Directly**

Every occurrence of the refrain so far has addressed the two multitudes without identifying them beyond the dual pronoun. This verse names them. O two multitudes is the first time the chapter turns from a question to a direct summons, and the change of tone is unmistakable. The sūrah has spent thirty verses listing favours and asking the reader to acknowledge them. It now announces that the account itself is coming, and the announcement is delivered to the two species without an intermediary. The sentence is short, it is addressed to everyone who hears the chapter, and it contains no conditional clause. Nothing in it depends on the listener's willingness or his answer to the previous questions; the summons is issued to both audiences whether they acknowledge the favours or not.

**The Word for the Two Multitudes**

The Arabic term is *thaqalān*, the two weighty ones, from a root that gives the language its words for heaviness and burden. The commentaries offer two explanations of the name, and both are worth weighing. The first connects it to the substances from which the two species were created: human beings and jinn are made of clay and fire, which are heavy compared with the light from which the angels are described as made. The second takes the weight as moral: these are the two creations that carry responsibility, and the burden is the account they will owe for what they did with it. The second explanation accounts for the context better, since the verse is a summons to judgement and not a lecture on physics. A community is weighty in this Book when its choices matter, and the verse has just told the two communities that theirs are about to be examined.

**What It Means for God to Attend to Someone**

The verb rendered as attend translates an expression whose ordinary sense is to have leisure for something, to finish one task and take up another. Commentators pointed out that the word cannot be applied to God in its human sense, since no task keeps Him from any other, and they understood it as a statement that nothing about the resurrection and the reckoning will be delayed by any competing business. That is precisely the fear the verse removes. A creature that has been postponing an account may imagine that the One to whom it owes the account has more pressing matters. The sūrah answers that imagination in advance. The same Lord who is occupied with a matter every day has appointed a day on which the matter of these two multitudes will be settled, and the settling is not going to be crowded out.

**A Warning That Is Also an Assurance**

The word carries a second sense that the commentators noticed, and it runs in the opposite direction to the threat. If God is never too occupied to turn to someone, then He is never unavailable to a servant who calls on Him. The verse that follows the passage on daily tasks in another sūrah makes the same point about a different attribute: "And when My servants ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Me: I am truly near." The Nearness of the divine is one of the meanings the word can bear, and this verse has been read both ways. The reader who is being summoned to an account is at the same time being told that the One calling him is prepared to attend to him personally. The invitation to repent stands inside the announcement of judgement, rather than being deferred to a later chapter.

**The Judgement That Cannot Be Avoided**

The summons arrives in a chapter whose readers have just been told that everything living on the earth passes away. The two statements belong to one sequence: mortality, then permanence, then the accounting. Once the sūrah has established that the world's inhabitants do not last and that the Lord does, the only remaining question is what happens to the record of their actions, and this verse answers it. There is no interval in which the account can be lost, no court with a competing jurisdiction, and no defendant with the standing to refuse the summons. The Quran's descriptions of that day are consistent on this point, and the sūrah's own later verses will describe what the two multitudes meet when the heavens split apart and the record is opened.

**Why the Mercies Come Before the Summons**

The order of the sūrah is a deliberate arrangement of mercy before judgement. Thirty verses of favours precede the announcement that the account is coming, and the reader has been given the whole inventory before he is told that he will be asked about it. That sequence is repeated across the Quran, where warnings come after the provision of evidence, and the Book is emphatic that no community is destroyed without a prior message. The summons in this verse is therefore a further act of provision: the two multitudes are being told in advance about the appointment, and the interval between the announcement and the event is the space in which a person can change what the record will say. A court that publishes its schedule in advance is doing the defendant a favour.

**Where the Verse Sits in the Refrain Pattern**

The refrain will return immediately in the next verse, and this insertion between two occurrences of the question is deliberate. The question has been asking about the favours of the Lord; the summons names the Lord's authority over the two who are asked. Read together, the sequence tells the listener who is speaking and who is being addressed: the One who furnished the sea and the harvest is the One who will audit the use made of both. The chapter's structure alternates between provision and accountability for the rest of its length. Anyone who has followed the sūrah to this point has already learned the pattern, and this verse raises the stakes of the question that has been asked eight times without forcing the reader to wait for the answer.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:32

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Asked Between Summons and Judgement**

This occurrence of the refrain follows immediately on the announcement that the two multitudes will be attended to and brought to account. The placement is striking, because the question has just been surrounded by threat rather than by provision. The verses before it named the harvest, the sea, the pearls and the ships; this one is preceded by the announcement of the reckoning. The sūrah is asking the same question in a harder setting, and the effect is to test what the acknowledgement was worth. A person who said the favours were not denied while his hands were full of them is now asked the same thing while he is standing before the One who gave them. The question does not change its wording; the circumstances of the question change, and the circumstances carry the argument.

**Which Favour Is at Issue When a Warning Has Just Been Issued**

The most obvious favour in this position is the warning itself. The summons in the previous verse is not a punishment; it is an announcement delivered ahead of time, and the interval it creates is the space in which a person can still act. The Book treats that interval as a form of mercy throughout, and the refrain, arriving immediately after the announcement, asks the listener whether he will call this final notice a lie as well. The other favours are not withdrawn by the warning. The sun still rises, the sea still carries ships, and the harvest still arrives while the account is pending, because the provision and the summons come from the same Lord. The question therefore covers everything at once: the gifts, the notice, and the time still available.

**Two Multitudes Being Summoned Together**

The address remains dual, and at this point in the chapter the dual form has gathered a weight of its own. Two horizons, two seas, two creations, two audiences and now two defendants. The sūrah never allows either community to stand apart from the other in the accounting, and the Book reports the jinn accepting the same revelation and being divided by it in the same way people are. A human listener who has been following the pattern understands that the court is not a human institution and that no species has been granted a separate standard. The question is put to both, and the answer is expected from both, which is why the words of the refrain can be spoken by a member of either community without alteration.

**The Difference Between Being Told and Being Surprised**

The value of a warning depends on the time it leaves for a response, and the Quran's warnings are consistently early. The communities that were destroyed in earlier sūrahs were not destroyed without messengers, and the Book repeats the principle that a people is never punished before it has been informed. The verse's position in this sūrah makes the same point in miniature: the two multitudes are told that the account is coming long before they meet it. The refrain then asks what a listener does with the notice. Calling it a lie is one response, ignoring it is another, and acting on it is a third. The verse itself does not rank the responses. It asks the question and leaves the listener to notice that he has just been given something valuable, which is the opportunity to answer it with time remaining.

**A Question That Keeps the Ledger Open**

The refrain has now returned after the announcement of judgement, and the chapter will keep returning to it through descriptions of fire, gardens, and the end of the world. Its function at this point is to keep the ledger open. A reader might expect the sūrah to move from warning to verdict without further questions, and the Book does not do that. It asks again, which means the accounting has not yet been closed, and the interval in which the favours are still being counted is still running. Everything the two multitudes do with the question in that interval is being recorded in a book kept by the One whose days are all occupied, and the repetition of the refrain is the sound of the door being held open a little longer.

**How the Refrain Changes the Frightened Reader**

A question repeated under threat can produce either honesty or panic, and the sūrah's model for the right response is preserved in the report of the jinn. Faced with the same refrain, they answered that they denied none of their Lord's favours and gave thanks, and the Prophet ﷺ described their response as better than the silence of the Companions sitting with him. The two audiences in that report are instructive. The jinn were the ones being warned about judgement in this very passage, and their answer was neither an argument nor a plea; it was an acknowledgement. The refrain in this position produces that kind of answer from anyone who has understood it. Fear does not change the facts about what was received, and the honest response to the question remains the same whether the listener is comfortable or frightened.

**The Rhythm of the Refrain at This Point**

The verses of the sūrah are noticeably shorter from here, and the refrain therefore arrives more often. The chapter has moved from long passages on the created order into compact statements about the end of the world and the fate of the two multitudes, and the intervals between the questions narrow accordingly. The change of pace is part of the composition. A reader who has been following the argument in long stretches is now given the question at close range, and the effect is to prevent him from treating what follows as a description of distant events. The next verses describe the heavens splitting apart and the wicked being seized, and the refrain that follows each of them keeps the listener in the room. Where the pace is fastest, the question comes most often, and the two multitudes are given no space to forget it.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:33

> O assembly of jinn and humans! If you can penetrate beyond the realms of the heavens and the earth, then do so. ˹But˺ you cannot do that without ˹Our˺ authority.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Direct Address to Both Species by Name**

The sūrah has been addressing the two multitudes through the dual pronoun of the refrain. This verse calls them out by name: assembly of jinn and humans. The word rendered assembly denotes a group gathered together, and it is used elsewhere in the Book for the leaders of a people or a company of witnesses. Here it names the whole species of each kind, and the pairing makes the announcement comprehensive. No member of either community stands outside the address, and the two are treated as one gathering for the purpose of this challenge. The wording also removes an option that the refrain left open. A listener could have heard the dual pronoun as rhetorical; with the two species named, the audience is identified beyond argument, and the command that follows is issued to everyone who belongs to either group.

**The Challenge to Leave the Bounds**

According to the commentators, these words are spoken to people and jinn on the Day of Reckoning, when the angels have surrounded them. The challenge is to escape the domains of the heavens and the earth, and the impossibility of doing so is the sentence's point. Nothing in the created universe lies outside the region named, so the escape being proposed has nowhere to go. The verse does not describe a physical barrier with a gate; it describes the extent of a jurisdiction. A prisoner who is told he may leave if he can walk beyond the horizon has been told the truth about his position, and the two multitudes are being told the same thing about the authority under which they will be judged. There is no exit from the creation of God.

**Sulṭān: Authority and Proof at the Same Time**

The word translated authority carries two senses in Arabic, and both are relevant to the sentence. It means dominion or power, and it also means a proof, a warrant, or a decisive argument. Read with the first sense, the verse says the two species cannot leave the bounds without God's permission, and the permission will not be given. Read with the second, it says they cannot cross without a valid proof to justify their passage, and they do not have one. The two readings are not exclusive. The Quran uses the word in both senses elsewhere, and the ambiguity is the kind that enriches a sentence rather than weakening it. A creature being summoned to an account is being told that the only way out runs through a gate that is guarded by the authority that summoned him.

**What the Verse Says About Power in This Life**

Some commentators drew a second lesson from the sentence that applies to this life rather than to the next. If no one can pass beyond the regions of the heavens and the earth except by God's authority, then no human being can know or dominate anything within that creation except by the sanction of the One who made it. Knowledge, technology, and power are therefore not independent achievements; they operate inside a system that was set up before their possessors existed. The verse does not belittle human ability. It places it in a frame. The sūrah has already credited the shipbuilders with their vessels and the divers with their pearls, and it has credited the whole maritime economy to its Owner. This verse extends the same account to every capacity a creature may develop, and it names the limit beyond which none of them reach.

**A Bounded Estate Rather Than a Prison**

The imagery of the heavens and the earth as a single enclosed region has occasionally been read as a description of a closed universe, and that reading misses the purpose of the sentence. The bounds being named are the bounds of created things, and within them the chapter has described an abundance of life, movement, and provision. A creature that cannot leave its home is not necessarily confined if its home contains everything it needs and more. What the verse denies is the possibility of leaving the jurisdiction of the One who is owed an account, and that jurisdiction is coextensive with everything that exists. There is no neutral territory, no corridor out of creation, and no place where a summons cannot reach. The two multitudes are being told the size of the world they live in, and the measurement is the argument.

**Why the Challenge Is Aimed at the Powerful**

The verse uses a rhetorical form that presupposes confidence in its audience. It offers them an opportunity to do something difficult, which means the audience is expected to consider itself capable of difficult things. Jinn were described in the sūrah's earlier verses as created from a smokeless flame, and the Quran elsewhere reports their speed and their ability to reach the sky. Human beings were made from clay and given speech, understanding, and command over the earth. To this pair of capable species the verse issues a challenge whose only honest answer is that the challenge cannot be met, and the answer is the point. Creatures with unusual abilities are being shown the ceiling of those abilities before they are asked to give an account of how they used them, and the ceiling is the authority that made them capable in the first place.

**A Challenge That Also Defines the Creature**

The verse performs a further service for anyone who takes it seriously, because it states precisely what a created being is: an agent whose powers operate inside a domain that is neither of its own making nor within its own control. That definition is uncomfortable for a reader who measures freedom by the absence of limits, and the Book presents it as a fact rather than as a problem to be solved. The chapter has already described human beings as taught, provided for, dependent, and mortal. This verse adds that they are also contained. Nothing in the sūrah treats containment as a punishment. It is the condition under which a creature receives a world full of provision, and the authority that closes the exits is the same authority that opened the doors and set the table.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:34

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Refrain After a Failed Escape**

This occurrence of the refrain follows the challenge to pass beyond the regions of the heavens and the earth, and the order of the two sentences matters. The two multitudes have just been offered a chance to demonstrate their independence and have been told in the same verse that they cannot take it. Then the question arrives: which of your Lord's favours will you deny? A creature that has just been informed of its complete inability to leave its Creator's domain is now asked whether it credits the One who owns that domain with anything. The question is not an interruption of the threat. It is the conclusion of it, since a person who cannot escape has no reason to pretend he owes nothing.

**The Word for Authority Turned Into a Question**

The previous verse turned on the noun *sulṭān*, authority or proof, and stated that the two multitudes cannot leave without it. The refrain immediately asks about denial, and the two statements belong to one argument. To deny a favour in this context is to claim that the gift was not given, which is a claim about authority, since the gifts are distributed by the One whose permission governs every crossing of every boundary. Millions of people receive the day's provision and live as though the arrangement were a neutral fact of nature with no lord attached to it, and the verse asks them what they intend to do with that position. There is no neutral ground in the sūrah's universe. Everything either belongs to the Lord being described or is being treated as though it did not.

**Two Species That Cannot Outrun Their Record**

The address in the dual keeps the jinn and human beings together, and in this passage the pairing has a particular force. The jinn were made from a smokeless flame and are described in the Book as possessing abilities that human beings do not have; they reached the lower heaven to listen for news, according to their own report. Human beings have been given command over the earth and the seas, and the sūrah has just described their ships. Neither species can leave the created order, and neither can erase a record kept by the One who made them. The question is therefore asked of two creatures whose only genuine resource in the matter is their answer to it.

**The Favour Inside a Bounded Universe**

A universe with limits is a universe that can be understood, and the sūrah has been crediting its readers with the ability to read the signs inside it. A person who could pass beyond the heavens and the earth would be a creature without a home, and the Book treats the boundaries of creation as the conditions that make existence in it possible. The verse that states the impossibility of escape therefore also affirms a form of mercy: the two multitudes are held inside a world that has been arranged for them, and the boundary that prevents them from leaving is the same one that keeps the arrangement in place. The question of denial is put at that boundary, where the estate ends and its Owner is named.

**Powerlessness as a Correction of Pride**

Human beings find it easy to believe that their reach is expanding, and the jinn, according to the Book, made a comparable assumption about their access to the heavens. The sūrah offers both communities a demonstration that the reach has limits, and it does so without humiliating them. Nothing in the verse mocks the effort or denies the creatures their abilities. It simply states the extent of the estate and the authority under which it is held, and then asks about gratitude. Pride does not respond well to being told that it cannot leave, which is why the question follows so quickly. A creature that accepts its limits can answer honestly about the favours; one that does not will keep looking for the exit instead of reading the question.

**The Question That Needs No Argument Behind It**

The refrain's simplicity is one of its strongest features, and this occurrence shows why it works in a passage about power. No logical demonstration is offered, no chain of premises, and no appeal to authority other than the authority just named. The two multitudes are asked what they deny, and the question can be understood by a labourer and a philosopher in the same words. The sūrah has used the same method throughout: it lays out objects that anyone can see, then asks whether the One behind them is being credited. In a setting where the vanity of human power has just been exposed, that plainness is the appropriate instrument. The question does not need to prove anything. It needs an answer.

**The Pause Before the Fire**

The next verse will describe fire and smoke sent against the two multitudes, and the refrain sits directly before it as the last question asked in a scene of favour and warning. The pattern of the chapter means that the reader has heard the question ten times by now, each time attached to a different subject: water, pearls, ships, death, daily provision, judgement, escape. The fire that follows will make the question harder to answer casually and easier to answer honestly, which is the effect the sūrah's arrangement has been building toward. The favours have been counted and the limits have been named; what remains is the answer, and the sūrah has not yet finished asking. The order of the two verses says something about how the Book handles judgement: the description of the penalty is short and the question is long-lived, repeated long after the penalty has been described. The reader is meant to leave the scene with the question in his ear rather than with the fire in his imagination.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:35

> Flames of fire and ˹molten˺ copper will be sent against you, and you will not be able to defend one another.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Weapons Named in the Verse**

The first noun describes a flame, a flash, or a blast of fire that leaps without fuel. The translation renders it as flames of fire, and the commentators understood it as a fire that advances on the two multitudes when they are brought out for the reckoning. The second noun is disputed, and the dispute is worth recording in full. A group of early authorities took *nuḥās* to mean the smoke of the fire, which is how the word can be read in Arabic. Another group took it as liquid copper, molten metal, which is how the same word is used in the story of Solomon. Both readings are reported from al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, and both convey something of the scene: a punishment delivered in two forms at once, one of them luminous and the other opaque, with no gap in it through which the two multitudes might slip.

**The Copper That Was Once a Gift**

The word for molten copper appears once elsewhere in the Book, and it appears there as a bounty rather than as a punishment: "We caused a stream of molten copper to flow for him, and ˹We subjected˺ some of the jinn to work under him by his Lord's Will." (34:12). In that verse, copper is part of the provision given to Solomon, and the jinn themselves are employed in the work. In this verse the same material is turned against the jinn and their human partners. The contrast is exact and it belongs to the sūrah's method: everything the chapter has named as a gift can be converted into an instrument of judgement when the gifts are denied. What was a workplace for the jinn in this world becomes a hazard for them in the next, and the reader who notices the echo understands that the punishment is drawn from the same created order that supplied the favours.

**The End of Mutual Defence**

The verse closes with a statement that the two multitudes will not be able to help each other, and the Arabic construction puts the failure on the second person dual: you two cannot defend one another. In this world, alliances are the usual instrument of security. Tribes protect their members, the wealthy protect their clients, and the jinn and human beings have sought refuge with one another according to the Book's own account: "And some men used to seek refuge with some jinn—so they increased each other in wickedness." (72:6). The verse states that the arrangement collapses on the day it is needed most. There is no intercession that has not been authorised, no clan whose standing protects a member, and no partner whose strength can be transferred. The two who were addressed together throughout the sūrah stand together in the fire and cannot shield each other.

**Fire Against Creatures Made From Fire**

The jinn were described earlier in this sūrah as created from a smokeless flame, and the punishment here comes as fire. The Book does not explain the arrangement, and the commentators did not treat it as a paradox. Fire in the Quran is a creature that obeys its Lord, not a force with a will of its own, and the same fire that gives light and cooks food becomes unbearable when the One who made it commands otherwise. If anything, the correspondence makes the punishment harder to dismiss: a jinn that considers its own substance superior, as Iblīs did in the story of Adam, is shown that the substance provides no immunity whatsoever. The same logic applies to the human being, whose body returns to clay and cannot resist the fire that the clay of this world has been feeding for millennia.

**The Justice of a Matching Penalty**

The chapter has been listing favours; this verse names a penalty, and the two belong to one account. What is being punished is the denial of benefits the two multitudes have been receiving continuously, and the Book's descriptions of punishment typically match the offence in kind. Those who consumed the good things without acknowledging them are met with something they can neither consume nor ignore; those who relied on fire, or on the strength of allies, meet the failure of both. The verse does not dwell on the suffering. It states what is sent, states what will not work, and stops. The sūrah's restraint in describing punishment is a feature of its rhetoric throughout: the favours are elaborated at length, and the penalty is stated once, briefly, and then the question returns.

**Why the Refrain Follows Immediately**

The next verse is the refrain again, and its placement after this penalty is deliberate. Even here, the sūrah asks about denial rather than pronouncing a final verdict, which means the scene has not closed and the answer is still available in the logic of the passage. The question is not addressed to people already burning; it is addressed to everyone reading or hearing the chapter, and the fire is being described in advance so that the question lands with its full weight. Nothing in the text suggests that the punishment is a pleasure to God. The Book says in another place that He does not approve of disbelief for His servants, and the warning here is consistent with that: the two multitudes are told what is coming, told that mutual aid will not help, and then asked the same question that opens the whole account. The question's placement is itself an argument that the door has not been shut at the moment the fire is described, since the account for which the fire is the penalty is still open to correction in the life of the person reciting the chapter.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:36

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Asked Immediately After the Fire**

This occurrence of the refrain stands between the punishment described in the previous verse and the destruction of the heavens that the next one announces. It is asked at the hardest point in the sūrah so far, and it does not soften. The two multitudes have just been told that fire and smoke are sent against them and that no alliance will protect them; the question about denied favours is then put to them again. A reader may wonder why the sūrah does not simply pronounce judgement and close the account. The answer lies in what is being requested. What the verse wants from its audience is not a verdict but a recognition, and a recognition offered under threat is still worth making, while a verdict without it would end the conversation the chapter has been conducting since its first verse.

**Denial in the Presence of What Cannot Be Denied**

There is a specific kind of dishonesty that this refrain exposes when it is asked in a scene of punishment. It is the pretence that the gifts were never given. A person who has spent a lifetime using sunlight, water, food, and speech cannot credibly maintain at the end that he received nothing, and the question makes him say so out loud if he intends to persist in denial. The Arabic verb covers both a spoken lie and a lived one, and the setting removes the option of ambiguity. The sūrah does not ask whether the two multitudes are grateful; it asks which favours they deny, which forces them to name one or admit that none can be named. The question is engineered so that the only admissible answer is the one the jinn gave.

**Two Multitudes Facing the Same Assault**

The dual form, which has run through the whole chapter, reaches one of its most forceful uses here. A punishment that cannot be resisted by either species is described, and the question that follows is addressed to both together, in the same breath, as one audience. Neither may defer to the other. A human being cannot say that the question concerns the jinn, and a jinn cannot say that the accounting belongs to the humans, because the verse just before this one made them partners in the danger. The sūrah's pairing of the two communities is not a rhetorical flourish added for variety; it is sustained from the first occurrence of the refrain to the last, and it produces a scene in which the two stand on the same floor with the same question.

**Why the Sūrah Keeps Asking in the Middle of Judgement**

A court that asked the defendant for acknowledgement at every stage of the proceedings would be a strange court, and the sūrah is not conducting a trial. It is teaching a response. The refrain's repetitions in the passages about punishment are there for the sake of the listener in this world, who is reading about a scene he has not yet entered. Each recurrence gives him a place to practise the answer, and the practice is the point of the whole chapter. The Qur'an states repeatedly that its warnings are meant to produce reform before the event, and a warning that ended in silence after the moment of judgement would be useless to the people who need it most. The question is asked in the fire because it is being read on the earth.

**The Favour of Being Told Before the Fire**

Among the favours being counted in this verse is the warning delivered in the one before it. The two multitudes were not told about the fire because God was interested in frightening them; they were told so that they could avoid it, and that is a benefit whether or not it is accepted. The Book's own terminology for punishment includes the idea of a demonstration that leaves no excuse, and the sūrah's structure is arranged so that the warning precedes the event by an entire lifetime. The question about denial therefore reaches a listener who has been given the fire's description well in advance, which is why the correct answer is so easy to give and so costly to refuse.

**What the Question Asks of a Person Who Has Heard It Many Times**

By now the reciter has said these words eleven times, and familiarity is a hazard the sūrah does not ignore. A repeated question can be answered mechanically, and a mechanical answer is a smaller thing than the recognition the verse is after. The placement of the refrain inside a passage about fire is one of the remedies the chapter applies against familiarity: it makes the sound of the question change by changing what surrounds it. The reader who has been answering easily in the passages about harvests and boats now has to answer where the ground is on fire. Nothing in the wording has changed. Everything in the reader is being asked to change, and the verse simply gives him one more opportunity to do it before the next scene begins.

**The Pause Before the Heavens Split**

The verse after this one will describe the sky breaking apart and turning the colour of burnt oil, and the refrain stands at the seam between the warning of fire and the dissolution of the world. The reader who has followed the chapter has been given the whole sequence: favours, account, boundaries, punishment, and now the end of the visible order. The question that closes this verse is the last thing said before that order comes apart, and it is the same question the chapter began counting favours to ask. Whatever answer the two multitudes give, they will give it in the interval that remains.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:37

> ˹How horrible will it be˺ when the heavens will split apart, becoming rose-red like ˹burnt˺ oil!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sky Torn Open**

The sūrah has described provisions, limits, and a fire; it now describes the sky itself coming apart. The event is named in several places in the Book, always as one of the signs of the end. One sūrah opens with it: "When the sky splits open," (82:1), and another does the same: "When the sky bursts open," (84:1). The sūrah of the Criterion places it in company with the descent of the angels: "˹Watch for˺ the Day the heavens will burst with clouds, and the angels will be sent down in successive ranks." (25:25). The rending is therefore not an isolated detail of this chapter. It belongs to a fixed sequence that the Quran refers to whenever it wants to describe the end of the present order, and the audience of al-Raḥmān would have recognised the scene as soon as the first word was spoken.

**Rose-Red Like Burnt Oil**

The colour is the verse's most arresting feature. The sky becomes *wardah*, a rose or ruddy red, and the comparison is to *dihān*, oils or greasy substances that have been heated. Commentators explained the colour in different ways, some taking it as the red of hide that has been dyed, which is how the earlier translation renders the phrase, and others as the red of molten material or of oil set alight. The disagreement concerns the exact shade and not the effect. A sky that has always been blue and clear is being described as turning the colour of something processed by heat, and the image is drawn from a workshop rather than from a landscape. The world's ceiling is not merely opening; it is changing into a substance, and the reader is being shown the end of the most stable thing he has ever seen.

**Why the Heavens Are the Last Thing to Fail**

The order of the sūrah's ending follows the order of human confidence. Water can fail and the harvest can fail, and people have always known that. The sky has never failed. It has been the one feature of existence that no generation has seen disrupted, and the chapter breaks it open in a single line. What the verse destroys is not a physical object but an assumption: that there is a permanent order above the world that will continue regardless of what happens below it. The Book elsewhere describes the heavens as obeying their Lord as they must (84:2), and awe before Him is described as something the heavens nearly burst under (42:5). What holds the sky in place is a command, and the verse is describing the moment the command is completed.

**The Two Multitudes Watching a Collapsing Roof**

The verse is placed between the refrain and the account of the guilty being seized, and the position tells the reader who is watching. The two multitudes who have been addressed throughout the chapter are present in the scene, and the sky they grew up under is coming apart above them. Every favour listed in the sūrah so far has depended on that sky: the reckoning of the sun, the rain, the harvest, the ships, and the balance that was set when the heaven was raised. When the roof fails, the whole inventory is closed at once. The verse therefore functions as the end of the first half of the sūrah's world, and what follows it belongs entirely to the reckoning.

**Authority on the Day the Sky Opens**

The sūrah of the Criterion pairs the splitting of the heavens with a statement about ownership: "True authority on that Day will belong ˹only˺ to the Most Compassionate. And it will be a hard day for the disbelievers." (25:26). That verse and this one describe the same event, and the second names the title that opens this sūrah. The chapter of al-Raḥmān began with the Compassionate and has been listing His gifts for thirty-six verses; the sky now breaks, and the title returns in the form of authority. The mercy that has been counted is not separate from the power that will judge. The same Lord who provided the roof is dismantling it, and the two facts are held together by the name the sūrah chose for its opening word.

**A Description That Refuses to Be Pictured**

The Quran's descriptions of the last day use heat, colour, splitting, and melting, and none of them can be photographed. The verse does not attempt a picture of the scene; it gives the listener a set of comparisons taken from ordinary experience and leaves the rest to the imagination. The method is consistent across the Book's eschatological passages. The reader is told enough to know that the event is total and that nothing in the present order survives it, and he is not told enough to reduce it to something familiar. The colour of burnt oil over a broken sky is deliberately strange. It marks the moment when the world's furniture begins to behave in ways that no one has ever seen.

**What the Refrain Has Prepared the Reader For**

The question asked in the previous verse now stands in front of this scene, and the repetition across the chapter makes the reader carry both at once. He has been asked which favours he denies, and he is now shown the day on which the greatest and most overlooked of those favours, the sky above his head, is taken away. The two verses belong together: the question was about the gifts, and the image is of their withdrawal. What the sūrah is building is a recognition that arrives while the roof is still in place, and this verse is the last description of the world's furniture before the chapter turns to what happens to the people underneath it.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:38

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question at the Sight of a Broken Sky**

This occurrence of the refrain follows the description of the heavens splitting and turning the colour of heated oil. It is the hardest image in the sūrah so far, and the question that accompanies it is the same eleven words that have followed harvests, pearls, and ships. The effect of the placement is to strip the question of any association with comfort. A listener who has been answering it in a mood of pleasant gratitude while reading about fruit and date palms is now answering it with the sky coming apart above him. The wording has not changed, and the reader's understanding of what the favours are has changed completely, which is what the sūrah's arrangement of its refrains is designed to produce.

**Denial When the Roof Has Failed**

Every favour counted in this chapter rested on the stability of the heavens. The sun ran its course in a sky that held; the rain arrived from a sky that held; the ships navigated under stars that were fixed in a sky that held. When the verse before this one describes that sky splitting open, the whole list of provisions is seen at once as a loan that is being called in. A person who has been treating the gifts as permanent has been answered, and the question that follows asks what he says about them now. The answer available in that moment is very different from the answer available in the middle of an ordinary day, and the sūrah's method is to make the reader feel the difference while he still has the ordinary day.

**The One Element That Does Not Change**

Everything in the passage changes: the sky, the colour, the state of the world, and the position of the two multitudes. The refrain does not change. It repeats the same question in the same words at each stage of the dissolution, and its constancy becomes part of the scene's meaning. The gifts of the world are temporary, the world itself is temporary, and the account of how the gifts were received is not. The refrain is the one sentence in the sūrah that survives every context it is placed in, which makes it a fitting companion to the earlier statement that only the Face of the Lord remains. What is being counted does not stop being counted when the sky comes down.

**Two Multitudes Watching the Same Spectacle**

The dual address remains in force, and here the pairing has a particular effect. Neither species watches the sky's destruction alone, and neither can use the other as a shield or an excuse. The scene the verse describes is common property, and the question is put to the joint audience without distinction. In the chapter's earlier passages, the pairing served to widen the list of beneficiaries; by this point in the recitation it has become a shared courtroom. The jinn and the human beings who have been given the same provisions, the same warning, and the same limits now stand under the same collapsing roof and are asked the same thing, and there is no clause anywhere in the sūrah that separates their accounts.

**What an Answer Costs at That Hour**

The verse's position raises a practical question for the reader: what is the use of an acknowledgement offered when the sky is already opening? The Book's answer is that the acknowledgement offered at that hour is a description rather than a remedy, and the time for the remedy is now. The Quran repeatedly contrasts the repentance that is accepted with the confession that arrives too late, and it describes the second as nothing more than an accurate statement of a fact the speaker can no longer avoid. The refrain's function in this passage is therefore to make the living reader ask the question early. The verses about fire and splitting skies are not instructions for the day of judgement; they are the reason to settle the matter before it arrives.

**Repetition as a Discipline for the Forgetful**

A reader who has now heard the refrain thirteen times is being trained, whether or not he notices. The human mind does not hold a truth steadily; it returns to old habits of inattention within hours, and the sūrah's structure takes that weakness into account. The repetition is not a failure of literary economy. It is the deliberate application of the only method that works on a forgetful creature, which is to say the same true thing again in a slightly different setting until it becomes part of the furniture of the mind. The placement of the refrain beside a description of the end of the world is one of the strongest of those settings, and it is meant to be remembered on days when nobody is reading the sūrah.

**The Short Verses That Follow**

From this point the sūrah moves through short verses at a fast pace, and each one will be followed by the refrain. The guilty will be recognised and seized; the fire will be named as the very thing they denied; they will pass between flame and boiling water. The question will come after each of those sentences, and the reader will have to answer it in whatever state he is in while the scenes move past him. This verse begins that run, and it sets the terms: the sky is opened, the question is asked, and the two multitudes are still the ones being addressed. A reader who has followed the sūrah to this point has heard the same sentence in every kind of setting, and the settings will keep changing until the last verse of the chapter.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:39

> On that Day there will be no need for any human or jinn to be asked about their sins.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Court Where the Record Answers for Itself**

The verse states that the two species will not be questioned about their sins, and the reason lies in what the Book says about the record. The Quran describes a written account of every deed, presented on the day of judgement and read by its owner: "And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it. They will cry, 'Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?' They will find whatever they did present ˹before them˺. And your Lord will never wrong anyone." (18:49). The same Book describes the recording as continuous and immediate: "As the two recording-angels— ˹one˺ sitting to the right, and ˹the other to˺ the left—note ˹everything˺, not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺." (50:17–18). A questioning would add nothing to an account kept in that manner.

**The Debate About Whether They Are Questioned at All**

The commentators noticed an apparent tension with another verse, where God swears that the two audiences will be questioned: "So by your Lord! We will certainly question them all about what they used to do." (15:92–93). Several authorities reconciled the two by ordering the events: the people are questioned, and then their mouths are sealed when they attempt to answer, so that their limbs testify instead. Others held that the questioning on that day is not conducted to elicit information, since everything is already known, but as a form of reproach, which leaves the statement in this verse intact. Both reconciliations preserve the plain sense of both verses, and the second has the advantage of explaining why the Book would use the language of interrogation about facts that require no investigation.

**The Mouth Sealed and the Limbs Testifying**

The scene the commentators describe is drawn from verses that are explicit about it. "On this Day We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to commit." (36:65). The same moment is described in another sūrah with an exchange between the accused and their own bodies: "When they reach it, their ears, eyes, and skin will testify against what they used to do. They will ask their skin ˹furiously˺, 'Why have you testified against us?' It will say, 'We have been made to speak by Allah, Who causes all things to speak.'" (41:20–21). A creature whose own members are witnesses has nothing to gain from being asked. The verse in this sūrah states the conclusion of that arrangement before the details are supplied elsewhere.

**Why Both Species Are Named**

The verse specifies human beings and jinn separately, and the specification repeats the sūrah's insistence that the two audiences are accountable. Every refrain in the chapter has been addressed to both, and every provision has been granted to both, so the account belongs to both. A reader might otherwise assume that the reckoning described in the Quran concerns only the species that receives prophets directly, and the phrase rules that assumption out. Earlier in the chapter the two were created from clay and from fire, and here they are named in the same clause as the defendants in the same case. The Book reports the jinn themselves acknowledging the arrangement when they listened to the Quran and carried its warnings to their own people.

**The Language of Not Needing to Ask**

The Arabic construction carries the sense of a question that is unnecessary rather than a question that is forbidden, and the distinction matters for how the scene is read. The verse is not describing a courtroom in which the judge has decided to save time. It is describing a situation in which the evidence is complete and the defendant's own body is a witness, so that interrogation would serve no purpose beyond speech for its own sake. The Quran's descriptions of that day consistently present the account as a display rather than a debate, with the books opened, the limbs speaking, and the faces showing what the person was. The purpose of the display is justice, which is the point the verse quietly establishes before the guilt of the people involved is described.

**The Sufi Reading and Its Limit**

One mystical reading, reported in the commentaries, took the absence of questioning as a statement about the state of the person whose lower self has been effaced, so that no sin remains to be asked about. That reading is an application rather than an interpretation, and it does not fit the passage, since the verse belongs to a passage about the guilty and is followed by their seizure. The plain sense is the one the context supports: the two multitudes will not need to be interrogated because their deeds are already known and recorded, and the judgement will proceed on evidence that no one can dispute. What the sūrah is doing here is closing off the last avenue of concealment before it describes what happens to the people who are found out.

**A Note of Finality in the Middle of the Sūrah**

The verse has the sound of an ending, though it stands in the middle of the chapter, and the sound is deliberate. Everything up to this point has been an invitation: the favours counted, the question repeated, the offer of time to answer. The statement that no questioning will be needed marks the point at which the possibility of an account adjusted by argument disappears. What remains for the reader is the interval in which the account is still being written, and the sūrah has just said how it is written: two angels, one on each side, noting every word. The following verses describe what the guilty look like when they arrive, and the description is possible precisely because the matter has already been settled.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:40

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question After the Courtroom Has Closed**

This refrain follows the statement that no human being or jinn will need to be questioned about their sins. The two verses sit in a sequence that runs from interrogation to documentation: the record is complete, the limbs will testify, and the interrogation is unnecessary. Yet the question is still asked, and the person asking it is the same Lord who has just been described as needing no answers. The apparent oddity is resolved by noticing who the question is for. The two multitudes standing in that scene have reached the end of their opportunity; the question is being put to the reader who is still in the interval where an answer can change something. The refrain is the chapter's way of keeping the door open in the only place where it is still open.

**Two Different Moments in the Same Sentence**

The Arabic dual addresses the two multitudes, and the tense of the question places them in a moment of decision. The reader is addressed as though he were standing in the scene, and the effect of that construction is to collapse the distance between the recitation and the day being described. This is a technique the Quran uses constantly: it narrates the last day in the present or the future and speaks to the audience as participants rather than as spectators. The question in this verse is therefore both a description of what will be asked and an address to the person hearing it now. What the reader does with the sentence in the next minute is the thing the sentence is about.

**Denial That Has Already Been Written Down**

The previous verse established that the account of the two species has been recorded in full, so the denial being asked about here is not an open question from the perspective of the record. It is a question about the state of the person asked. A denial that has been written down is still a denial, and the Book treats the distinction between what God knows and what the creature acknowledges as central to the meaning of human responsibility. The creature is not being asked to inform its Lord about anything. It is being invited to agree with the truth about itself, and the invitation is extended at every stage of a chapter that has been counting favours for forty verses.

**The Favour of an Accurate Record**

The verse that precedes this one can be read as a threat, and it is also a comfort. A world in which deeds are recorded without error and presented without alteration is a world in which the truth about a life cannot be rewritten by the powerful. The Book states the same guarantee in the verse about the record that leaves nothing unlisted, and it ends that verse with the assurance that the Lord will not wrong anyone. For a person who has been wronged and whose case was never heard, the record is the promise that there will be a hearing. The refrain asks about denial in the middle of that promise, which suggests that the favours being counted include the guarantee of a just account.

**Repetition as a Form of Patience**

The question has been asked fourteen times by this point in the recitation, and the repetition is itself a mercy. A warning delivered once can be dismissed; a warning delivered at every turn of the argument cannot be dismissed without a decision. The sūrah has arranged its repetitions so that a listener encounters the question after a gift, then after a threat, then after a description of the end of the world, and now after an account of a court in which no defence is possible. Each setting removes one more excuse, and the repetition gives the reader the time to notice that his excuses are being removed one at a time rather than all at once. The patience of the arrangement is the point.

**The Two Multitudes Named in the Accounting**

The dual form returns, and by now the reader has heard it in every kind of context the chapter contains. It has addressed the two multitudes as beneficiaries, as defendants, as creatures that cannot escape, and as beings whose deeds are recorded by two angels sitting on either side. Its consistency is the sūrah's way of stating that the moral universe is not divided by species or by capability. A jinn who could reach the lower heaven and a human being who built a ship are asked the same question about the same favours by the same Lord. Nothing in the arrangement favours one community over the other, and nothing in it leaves either community out.

**Where the Refrain Leads Next**

The next verses will name the guilty as those who will be recognised and seized, and then a voice will identify the fire as the very thing they used to deny. The refrain sits immediately before that identification, which means the reader is asked about the favours just before he is shown the consequence of refusing them. The order is characteristic of the whole sūrah: the gift is named, the question is asked, and the outcome of the answer is described. Every part of that sequence has now been established, and the sūrah is ready to describe the end of the people who chose against it.

**A Question the Living Can Still Answer**

The scene described in the verses around this refrain has no place for a further answer. The record is closed, the limbs have spoken, and the people being questioned are no longer in a position to change anything. The address nevertheless continues, and the reason is that the recitation is being heard by people who are still within the period in which an answer changes the outcome. The Quran's habit of narrating the last day in the second person turns its warnings into an address to the living, and this sūrah is the clearest example of the technique. Every occurrence of the refrain after a description of punishment is a sentence spoken past the condemned to the person holding the book. The two multitudes in the text cannot reply; the reader can.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:41

> The wicked will be recognized by their appearance, then will be seized by ˹their˺ forelocks and feet.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Identified Before a Word Is Spoken**

The verse follows directly from the statement that no one will need to be questioned about his sins, and it explains why. The wicked are known by their appearance, and the identification is complete before any interrogation could begin. Commentators took the phrase as an adverbial clause connected to the previous verse, supplying the reason: there is no need to ask, because the criminals arrive with their condition visible on them. The Book describes a corresponding visibility on the other side, where the faces of the believers carry the mark of prostration, and it treats the outward sign as the natural consequence of a life rather than as an arbitrary label attached at the end. God knows His creation, and the judgement proceeds on what is already known.

**What the Marks Are**

The interpreters discussed what the signs on the guilty consist of, and they consistently contrasted them with the marks of the righteous. The Quran describes the companions of the Prophet ﷺ in another sūrah as bearing their sign on their faces from the effect of prostration, so that their worship is legible in their appearance. The faces of the guilty in this scene carry the opposite kind of legibility. The Book does not specify the colour or the feature involved, and the commentators left the detail to God while agreeing on the function of the description. What the verse establishes is that the two groups are distinguishable at a glance, and that the distinction is not a matter of accusation but of disclosure. Nothing hidden at that moment stays hidden.

**Seized by Forelock and Feet**

The second half of the verse describes the seizure, and the choice of limbs carries the meaning. The forelock is the front of the head and the feet are the lowest part of the body, so the verse names the two ends of a human being. Commentators offered two readings of the action: that the heads of the guilty will be bent down and bound to their feet, and that the angels will drag them by the hair and the legs. Both readings describe the same total loss of control. A creature that has spent its life deciding where to go is now moved by someone else, and the movement begins with the two parts of the body that least admit resistance.

**The Forelock in the Qur'an's Vocabulary**

The image of the forelock recurs in the Book as a figure for complete ownership. Hūd addresses his people with a sentence that makes the connection explicit: "I have put my trust in Allah—my Lord and your Lord. There is no living creature that is not completely under His control. Surely my Lord's Way is perfect justice." (11:56). The Arabic of that verse says that God holds every creature by its forelock, which is to say that nothing moves except within His grasp. This verse of al-Raḥmān turns the figure into an action. What Hūd stated as a comfort for the trusting is applied here to the ones who would not trust, and the same grip that sustains every creature in life becomes the grip that brings the guilty to their account.

**A Posture That Reverses Pride**

The seized posture is the exact inversion of the stance a guilty person adopts in the world. A neck held high, a face turned away from a warning, a step that takes a person wherever he wants to go: these are the physical forms of a decision that the Quran describes with the same vocabulary. The verse answers the posture with a posture. Commentators understood the bending of the head to the feet as humiliation, and the Book elsewhere states the principle that God will lower those who raise themselves. The verse does not gloat over the reversal. It states it in eight words and moves on, and the restraint is consistent with a sūrah that has spent its length on gifts rather than on penalties.

**A Scene Without Dialogue**

Nothing in this verse is spoken. The guilty are identified, taken, and brought, and no one argues or answers. The Book reserves dialogue for other scenes, and in this one the account has already been settled by the record. The absence of speech is meaningful in a chapter that began with the teaching of speech and has been asking a question ever since. The creature who was given the faculty of language, the ability to name things, and the opportunity to answer a question has reached the point at which none of those capacities is of any use. The one faculty that would have answered the refrain is not requested here and would not be heard.

**From Seizure to Sentence**

The next verses will name the fire that these people are brought to, and the sentence pronounced over them is introduced by a voice that identifies the place as the very thing they denied. This verse is the arrest that precedes the sentence, and it belongs to a sequence of short descriptions that allow the reader to follow a single person from recognition through seizure to the destination. The sūrah has been careful to describe the process without lingering on it. What matters for the reader is that every stage of it is irreversible, and that the point at which irreversibility begins is not this scene but the life that produced the people in it.

**Held by the Same Hand That Sustains**

Hūd's statement that every living creature is held by its forelock was spoken as a ground for trust, and this verse applies the same image to people who spent their lives avoiding the One who held them. The hand that seizes the guilty is not a different hand from the one that provided for them through every year of their lives; the Quran consistently presents the two operations as the work of a single Lord. What changes in this scene is the creature's relation to the grip, not the grip itself. The earth that was spread for him, the rain that reached his fields, and the forelock by which he is now being taken all belong to the same administration. The verse therefore describes what it looks like when provision ends and account begins, and the account is conducted by the One who supplied everything being accounted for.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:42

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question After the Arrest**

This refrain follows the description of the wicked being recognised and dragged by the forelock and the feet, and it is placed before the voice that names the fire. The question therefore stands between the arrest and the sentence, which is the narrowest place in the sūrah. The two multitudes have been shown the promises, the record, the courtroom, and the seizure, and they are asked about the favours one more time before the destination is announced. The arrangement gives the reader a sense of the interval that remains: the sentence has not yet been pronounced in the text, and the question comes exactly at that point. Everything the sūrah has done with its refrains is concentrated in the timing of this one.

**What the Favours Are at This Stage of the Sūrah**

The list of provisions that filled the first third of the chapter is far behind the reader by now. The favours being counted here are of a different order, and they are still favours: the warning delivered before the event, the record kept accurately, the identification that proceeds without an interrogation, and the delay that allows a person to change what the record will contain. The Quran treats each of these as benefits, and it treats their rejection as the root of the destruction described in the surrounding verses. The question in this position is asking whether a person denies the value of being told the truth in advance, which is the last gift available to someone who has already refused the others.

**The Dual Address Before the Sentence Falls**

The refrain keeps its dual form, and its persistence here is significant. The fire about to be named belongs to the people who denied the truth, and the two multitudes are addressed together as the audience for whom that outcome is a possibility and a warning. Nothing in the wording excludes either species from the mercy of the question, and nothing in the surrounding verses excludes either from the penalty that is described. The sūrah has kept the two communities in one sentence from its first refrain to this one, and the pattern will continue through the gardens that come later. A book that divides humanity into the saved and the lost does not divide jinn from humans in the process.

**Denial Measured Against a Warning**

The specific form of denial that this verse confronts is the refusal of a warning, which is a different act from the refusal of a harvest. A person who attributes his food to his own effort has made a claim about the past; a person who dismisses a warning about the future has made a decision about his own safety. The Quran treats the second as the more serious of the two, because the warning is the last provision given before the account closes. The verses immediately before this one have described the arrest of people who dismissed exactly this kind of notice. The question therefore invites the reader to consider what he is doing with the information he has received, and the answer it expects is not a statement about the past but a change in the present.

**The Repetition That Keeps the Reader in the Scene**

A reader following the sūrah in one sitting is now moving through short verses at speed, and the refrain is arriving roughly every other line. The effect is to keep the listener in the scene rather than allowing him to move past it. Long passages of description allow the mind to drift; a question repeated at close intervals does not. The sūrah's designers placed these repetitions deliberately, and the density increases exactly where the subject matter is hardest to hold. The reader who is struggling with the description of seizure and fire is given the familiar sentence again, and the familiarity is what keeps him from turning away from a scene he needs to see.

**The Refrain and the Reader's Own Alibi**

Every repetition of the question also removes the possibility of an alibi. By this point the reader has heard it after gifts he enjoys, after warnings that concern him, and after descriptions of a process that will not spare him. If he has been answering it falsely or not at all, he has now been given a dozen opportunities to notice what he is doing. The sūrah's repetition works like a refrain in a song that a person cannot stop hearing: its words become the background of his own thoughts, and the background eventually produces an answer of some kind. The question is designed to be inescapable, and this verse is one of the places where its inescapability is most visible.

**The Voice That Answers It**

The verse after this one introduces a sentence spoken over the guilty, identifying the fire as the very thing they denied, and the refrain stands immediately before that identification as the last question asked in the sequence. The two verses form a unit: the question is put, and the answer that the guilty gave in life is then read back to them as the name of their punishment. The sūrah does not wait for a reply in the text. It supplies the reply from the record and lets the reader hear how a denial sounds when it returns as a description of where the denier has arrived.

**Before the Last Word Is Spoken**

The refrain at this point functions as a stay of sentence, and the stay is the last one in the passage. The verses before it have described the arrest of the guilty, and the verse after it names the fire and its inhabitants. The question is therefore asked in the interval, and the interval is where the reader is standing while the recitation continues. The sūrah gives no indication that the pause in the text corresponds to a pause in the events; the point is the reader's position, not the defendant's. A person who hears the question between the arrest and the fire is being offered the same interval in his own life, and the answer he gives during it is the answer the sūrah has been requesting since its first refrain.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:43

> ˹They will be told,˺ “This is the Hell which the wicked denied.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence Spoken Over the Guilty**

The verse introduces a voice. The Quran does not say who speaks, and the translation marks the sentence as something said to the people being brought forward. The words are simple and they contain no description of the fire itself; they contain only a demonstration. The guilty are told that what they are looking at is the thing they used to call a lie, and the sentence is designed so that the identification comes from their own denial. A punishment they had dismissed as a myth is presented to them as an address, and the address concerns their own past statements. The Book elsewhere uses the same construction, and the effect is to make the penalty a sentence about truth rather than a description of pain.

**The Denial That Comes Back as a Name**

The verb in this verse is the same one that runs through the refrain. The people being addressed are the ones who called the warnings untrue, and the sentence states that the thing they denied is now in front of them. There is a precise justice in the arrangement. Their denial is not answered with an argument; it is answered with an object. This is why the verse can be so short and still carry its weight. The Quran does not describe the fire in this line at all, because the point is not what the fire is like but what the relation between the fire and the denier now amounts to. What was rejected as a report has become a place, and the two are named with the same word.

**The Parallel in Another Sūrah**

The sūrah of the Prostration states the same scene with more detail: it names the fire as the refuge of the wicked, describes them being returned to it whenever they try to leave it, and records the sentence spoken over them — "Taste the punishment of the Fire that you used to deny." (32:20). The two passages share the verb of denial and the same movement from rejection in life to identification afterwards. The Prostration adds an attempt to leave, which is refused. This sūrah leaves the attempt out and states only the naming, and its restraint makes the identification the whole content of the verse. The reader is shown the moment at which a person's own words become the label of his destination.

**The Word for the Guilty**

The people in this verse are called *mujrimūn*, a word built on a root that carries the sense of cutting off and of earning by one's own action. The term is not a generic name for bad people; it describes those who severed a connection that should have been maintained and who harvested what their own hands produced. The word fits the sūrah's argument better than a general term for sin would. The chapter has been describing a relation of giver and receiver, with favours counted and acknowledged or denied. The person who cuts that relation and then denies the account is precisely the criminal the verse has in view, and the punishment named is what remains when the connection is finally severed on the other side.

**Why the Fire Is Named Before It Is Described**

The order of the next two verses is instructive. This verse identifies the fire, and the verse after it describes what the people in it experience. The identification comes first, so that the reader understands the destruction as the conclusion of a relationship rather than as an accident that befalls a person. The Book's descriptions of the last day are constructed throughout on that logic, and the sūrah in particular has spent its first half establishing what was given and who gave it. Read in that light, the sentence spoken over the guilty is a summary of their whole history: they were provided for by a Lord they then denied, and they have now been brought to the consequence of that denial.

**A Demonstration Rather Than a Definition**

Nothing in the verse defines the fire. The word used has been familiar to the audience from the beginning of the Quran's preaching in Makkah, and the verse simply points. This is the method of the whole passage, which describes the end of the world with a few strokes of colour and heat and then returns to the question. The Quran's refusal to explain the nature of the punishment in detail is consistent with its refusal to make the punishment the centre of the message. What matters to the sūrah is the recognition, and the recognition is achieved by a single sentence spoken to people who cannot avoid looking at what they rejected.

**The Refrain That Follows the Description**

The verse after this one describes the people passing between flame and boiling water, and then the refrain returns. The question about denied favours will be asked again after the punishment has been described, which means the sūrah's structure does not allow the reader to end on the sentence of judgement. Every description of the fire is bracketed by the question, and the effect is to keep the reader answering from wherever he happens to be standing. The sentence in this verse is final for the people it addresses. For the person reciting the chapter, it is one more line in a book that is still open in front of him.

**The Fire the Warnings Described**

The sentence in this verse contains no description of the punishment, only the identification of the place with the report that was rejected. That identification is the substance of the penalty's justice: the Book treats the truth of the warnings as the matter in dispute, and the final scene settles the dispute by showing the thing itself. The Quran returns to this pattern at the end of several sūrahs, where the people of the fire are asked whether the promise of their Lord was true and answer that it was. The acknowledgement arrives too late to be of use, but it establishes the point the sūrah has been making all along: the warnings were accurate, the messengers were truthful, and the denial was the one act that could not be undone once the report became a place.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:44

> They will alternate between its flames and scalding water.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Between Fire and Boiling Water**

The verse describes a movement rather than a condition. Those in the punishment pass to and fro between two things, and the commentators identified them as the fire of Hell and a liquid heated to the extreme. The image is of a creature that cannot settle anywhere. Fire burns, and the water that would answer the burning is itself scalding, so the two halves of the punishment drive the person from one to the other without relief. The Book uses the same pairing elsewhere, and the sūrah of the Believer describes a procession into both: "They will be dragged, with shackles and chains around their necks, into boiling liquid; then they will be set aflame in the Fire." (40:71–72). The Quran's descriptions of the punishment consistently combine solid and liquid, heat and drowning, in a way that leaves no element of the body unaddressed.

**The Two Readings of the Word for Boiling**

The word translated as scalding is *ān*, and the early authorities gave two explanations of it. Some took it as describing water at the highest possible temperature, water that has reached its limit. Others took it as meaning something prepared or held ready. The two readings do not conflict, as Ibn Kathīr noted, since what has been prepared for the people of the fire is precisely what has been heated to the extreme. The disagreement is an example of the kind of interpretive detail that the sūrah's language allows, and neither reading changes the scene. What matters for the reader is that the second element in the punishment is described with a word whose range runs from readiness to maximum heat, and both senses fit the account of a penalty that has been prepared rather than improvised.

**An Alternation That Gives No Rest**

The motion is the distinctive feature of this penalty, and what it implies deserves a moment's thought. A fixed punishment has at least the stability of a known quantity. A punishment that moves the person from one element to another gives him the momentary hope of escape that is immediately cancelled by the next stage, and it does so repeatedly. The Book describes the people of the fire attempting to leave in another passage, and it records no exit. What is being described in this verse is not a physical arrangement so much as an experience of restlessness imposed from outside, in which the sufferer supplies the movement and has no control over the interval. The phrase describing it takes only a few words in Arabic and has occupied preachers for centuries.

**The Two Directions of the Suffering**

Some commentators read the pairing of fire and boiling liquid as a description of punishment experienced from outside and from within: the fire acts on the surface of the body, and the liquid is drunk and acts on the inside. That reading is supported by the many verses that describe the people of the fire being given boiling water to drink, and it accounts for the two elements being named together rather than as alternatives. The Book's descriptions of the punishment repeatedly include what is drunk as well as what is worn, and the effect of the whole set is a complete account of a creature's exposure. Nothing in the verse suggests an interval of comfort between the two states, and no part of the description offers the person in it any part of himself that is untouched.

**Why the Qur'an Describes Punishment in Physical Terms**

A reader may ask why a book concerned with the unseen describes the last day with fire, water, heat, and shade rather than with abstractions about the state of the soul. The answer lies in the audience and in the nature of human knowledge. A person understands the condition of the body before he understands the condition of a self, and the Book uses the language of the body to speak about a reality that exceeds it. The same method is used in the descriptions of the gardens, which are full of water, fruit, fabric, and shade. Both destinies are narrated in terms of the strongest human experiences of pleasure and pain, and the narration is honest about its own limits: the Quran states elsewhere that no eye has seen what has been prepared, in both directions.

**A Suspension of the Refrain**

The refrain does not appear at the end of this verse; it arrives in the next one, after the description of the punishment is complete. The delay is deliberate. The sūrah gives the reader one verse of unbroken description before returning to the question, and the effect is to place the scene in his mind without interruption. When the refrain comes, it asks about the favours at the moment the reader is looking at the alternative to them, and the two verses are then read as a pair: this is what the denial produces, and this is the question that should have prevented it. The sūrah's arrangement of description and question is doing theological work at the level of pacing.

**The End of the First Destination**

This verse completes the account of the punishment that began with the sky splitting open, and everything it describes belongs to the loss of the favours that have been counted since the beginning of the chapter. The alternation between fire and water is the exact inversion of the earlier verses about fresh water and salt, the rain, the two seas, the springs, and the harvest. A book that began with the Most Compassionate teaching the Quran has now shown what happens to the people who called the favours untrue, and the next verse returns to the question in the same words it has used from the start of the account.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:45

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question at the End of the Fire**

This refrain closes the passage that began with the splitting of the heavens and describes the people of the fire passing between flame and boiling water. The question arrives after all of it, and the placement is the strongest example of the technique the sūrah has been using throughout. The reader has been shown the end of the deniers in detail. He is then asked which of his Lord's favours he denies, and the question is not softened by anything that comes between the description and itself. The sūrah does not say that the fire is what the answer produces; it says the fire is what the answer has produced and then asks the question again, leaving the reader to connect the two halves.

**A Turning Point in the Chapter**

The verse stands at the boundary between the two halves of the sūrah's second section. Everything before it has concerned the end of the world and the fate of the guilty; everything after it will concern the gardens prepared for the God-fearing, described in the same detail and with the same refrain after every line. The refrain therefore functions as a hinge, and it does so because it belongs equally to both halves. It has been asked after provisions and after punishments, and it will be asked after rewards. The chapter's structure is not two lists set side by side; it is a single question applied to everything a person can expect from his Lord, in this world and in the next.

**The Question That Belongs to Both Destinies**

Because the refrain follows both the fire and the gardens, it cannot be read as a threat that has been resolved by the verses before it. The question is not over. A reader who has passed through the description of the punishment and moved on to the gardens has still to answer it, and the gardens will be described in greater detail than the fire was. The sūrah gives the rewards more verses than the penalties, and it repeats the question after each feature of the gardens. The question's persistence on the side of reward shows what kind of question it is: it is not a sentence, it is an examination that continues while there is anything left to be grateful for.

**Denial in the Presence of Both Outcomes**

The two multitudes are now in a position to measure denial against the whole account. They have seen what follows from the favours being received with gratitude, and they have seen what follows from the other response. No additional information is available, and none is required. The Book's method in such passages is to lay out the consequences and then return the decision to the listener, and the Quran states explicitly that its messages are delivered so that people will have no argument left on the day of reckoning. The refrain in this position is the last stage of that delivery, presented as a question because a question requires the listener to answer rather than to observe.

**Two Multitudes at the Divide**

The dual address continues at the pivot, and the sūrah does not split its audience at any point. The fire and the gardens are not assigned to different species in the text; both are described for the two multitudes as a whole, and the sorting that takes place on the day of judgement is by deed and by answer, not by kind. The Quran states that among the jinn there are the righteous and those who fall short, exactly as among human beings, and it reports their own testimony to that effect. The refrain at this hinge, addressed to both together, keeps the reader from imagining that a species is saved or condemned by its nature. What separates the two groups in the sūrah is the answer to a single question.

**The Answer That the Verses Have Been Asking For**

Everything in the chapter so far can be read as an attempt to produce one sentence in the reader's mouth. The favours have been counted, the warnings given, the record described, the punishment shown, and the question repeated fifteen times. The jinn, according to the report preserved from the Prophet's recitation, answered that they denied none of their Lord's favours, and the Prophet ﷺ praised their response above the silence of the people with him. That answer is what this refrain is designed to elicit, and the sūrah gives the reader every opportunity to say it before turning to the description of what is prepared for those who do.

**What Comes After the Turn**

The next verses introduce the gardens with a statement about the people who are to receive them, and the description will unfold in pairs of pairs, each section followed by the same refrain. The sūrah's second half is fuller than its first, and the reader who has reached this hinge has completed the darker half of the chapter. What remains is a catalogue of water, foliage, fruit, fabric, and companions, with the question waiting after every line, and the question will sound different when it is asked in the middle of a garden than it did in the middle of a fire. The words will not change. The reader's answer will.

**A Question About Favours in the Middle of a Punishment**

The wording of the refrain is worth watching at this point in the sūrah. It asks about favours, *ālāʾ*, and it stays with that word even when the verses around it describe flame and boiling water. The question is never rephrased to ask which punishment is being denied or which warning has been rejected. The Book keeps its register on the side of mercy even in the passage on judgement, and the reader who notices this has understood something about the sūrah's method. The punishment is not presented as the subject of the chapter; it is presented as the consequence that follows when the favours are refused. The refrain's refusal to change its vocabulary is the sūrah's way of keeping the gift in view at the moment when its loss has become the whole scene.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:46

> And whoever is in awe of standing before their Lord will have two Gardens.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Condition Attached to the Reward**

The sūrah has moved from fire to gardens with a single conjunction, and the verse states the qualification for the reward before it describes the reward. The phrase translated as standing before their Lord is literally the station of his Lord, and the interpreters gave it two readings. It can mean the place where a person will stand on the day of judgement to answer for what he did, which is how the verse in the sūrah of the Cleaving states it: "˹Beware of˺ the Day We will summon every people with their leader." Another reading takes the station as God's own oversight of every soul, which the Book expresses where it describes Him as attending to every soul in accordance with what it has earned. On either reading, the fear in question is not fear of punishment first; it is awe at the fact that one is being seen.

**Mujāhid's Definition of the Fearful Person**

Mujāhid, one of the leading scholars of the generation after the Companions, defined the person described in this verse in practical terms: he is someone who thinks about committing a sin, then remembers God and abandons it out of fear. The definition turns an emotion into an action, and it locates the whole matter at the moment of decision rather than at the moment of judgement. Awe of the station of one's Lord, in that account, is what happens in a person's body and mind at the point where a wrong is within reach. The sūrah's placement of the verse directly after the description of the fire gives the definition its context. The fear that earns the gardens is not a mood produced by reading about punishment; it is a habit practised while the temptation is present.

**Why the Reward Comes in Pairs**

The commentators produced a long list of explanations for the two gardens. Some took them as one reward for faith and another for good deeds; some as one for the fear of God and another for the abandonment of desire; some as one spiritual and one physical; some as one created specially for the believer and one inherited; some as one garden of eternity and one of blessing. A report attributed to the Prophet ﷺ supports the most widely accepted explanation, describing two gardens of gold for the foremost and two of silver for the companions of the right, an arrangement that fits the Quran's distinction between those brought near and those on the right hand. The disagreement concerns which pair is meant rather than whether the pairing matters, and the sūrah's whole structure works in twos.

**The Two Halves of the Reward**

The chapter's habit of pairing has been visible since its early verses, and here the pattern reaches the reward itself. The reader who has followed the sūrah through two east and two west, two seas, and two multitudes is not surprised by two gardens. The pairing also does what the earlier pairs did: it suggests that the reward exceeds what a simple calculation would produce. A single garden would answer the fear; two gardens answer it generously, in the manner of a host who provides more than the guest requires. The sūrah will describe four gardens in total by the time it finishes, arranged in two tiers, and this verse introduces the higher tier before the lower one is named.

**Awe Without Despair**

The fear described in this verse is not the fear of a person who expects to be destroyed. The Book distinguishes between the dread that drives a creature away from God and the awe that keeps it from wrongdoing, and it praises the second throughout. The person described here is not someone who has achieved a record without fault; the definition supplied by Mujāhid makes him a person who was tempted and then stopped. That leaves room for a life that includes stumbles as well as refusals, and it leaves room for hope, which is why the verse can end in gardens rather than in warnings. Awe of God in this sūrah is a protective instinct, and the reward attached to it is described immediately as a place of shade and water.

**The God Who Attends to Every Soul**

The second reading of the phrase — the Lord's own attendance upon every soul — deserves the emphasis it received from the commentators. If a person is in awe because he understands that his Lord is overseeing what he does, the motive is continuous rather than occasional. It does not depend on the thought of a courtroom; it operates in a shop, a bedroom, and a market, and it never turns off. The sūrah has already named the Lord as the One who is occupied with a matter every day, and this verse applies that fact to the individual life. A creature that knows it is being attended to in accordance with what it earns will behave differently from one that thinks the account is kept somewhere else.

**The Reward Stated Before It Is Described**

The verse names the gardens and stops. Nothing is said about their contents, their foliage, or their water; the description begins in the following verses and continues for another thirty lines. The order is the reverse of the punishment passage, which named the fire after describing the arrest, and the effect is a change of tempo. The reader is given the headline first and then taken through the detail, and the refrain that follows this verse will ask its question over each item as it appears. The sūrah's second half is arranged like a tour, and the first thing the visitor is told is who is entitled to enter.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:47

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question at the Door of the Gardens**

This refrain follows the announcement that those in awe of standing before their Lord will receive two gardens, and it is placed before any of the gardens' contents are described. The reader is therefore asked the familiar question at the threshold, with the destination named and the details still to come. The effect of the placement is to treat the gardens themselves as one item on the ledger, and a large one. Everything else in the chapter has been a provision for a life that ends; this is the first item counted that is described as lasting. The question that asks about denial is now being asked about a reward that has been promised rather than about a benefit already received, and belief in the promise becomes part of what the answer involves.

**The Favours of the Hereafter Added to the List**

The chapter's opening third counted gifts that any person can see: the sun, the sea, the harvest, the pearls, the ships. This refrain marks the point at which the list is extended to include things that no listener has seen. The shift requires something of the reader. The earlier favours could be verified by looking out of a window; the gardens cannot, and the acknowledgement of them rests on the truthfulness of the One who promised them. The Quran treats that reliance as a continuation of the same gratitude rather than as a separate virtue, and it consistently joins the two in its descriptions of the faithful: those who thank God for what is present and trust Him for what is absent.

**Two Multitudes at the Same Threshold**

The dual form persists into the reward, and the sūrah does not switch to a single audience when the subject changes from punishment to paradise. Both communities are addressed as the recipients of the promise, and both are asked the question at the door. The Quran reports that among the jinn there are those who believed and those who deviated, exactly as it reports the same division among human beings, and it never assigns the gardens to one species alone. A reader who has heard the refrain addressed to himself and to the jinn through the passage on fire now hears it in the same form at the entrance to the reward, and the continuity of the address is the point.

**Denial as Ingratitude for What Is Promised**

The question in this position covers a form of denial that is subtler than the rejection of a visible gift. It includes the refusal to take the promise seriously, the assumption that the description of the gardens is a figure of speech with no referent, and the practical disbelief of a person who says the right words and plans his life as though nothing of the kind existed. The sūrah has been careful about the difference between verbal acknowledgement and lived acknowledgement from the beginning, and its refrain is designed to reach both. The gardens described in the verses that follow will be a test of the reader's willingness to let a promise shape his conduct, and the question at the threshold asks whether he is prepared to do that.

**The Refrain's Work at a Change of Subject**

A long passage has just ended with the punishment of the guilty, and the sūrah now begins a long passage about reward. The refrain is the seam between the two, and its presence there prevents either from being read as a separate composition. Without it, the chapter would look like two catalogues with different subjects. With it, the chapter is a single argument in which every item, whether gift, penalty, or promise, is material for the same question. The reader who has been answering the refrain through the fire passage must now answer it again in the gardens, and the answer cannot be a different one, since the question and the Lord have not changed.

**A Question That Fixes the Reader's Place**

The refrain keeps the reader from settling into the position of a spectator in either half of the sūrah. He is asked the question after the favours of the world, after the description of the record, after the seizure of the guilty, and now at the entrance to the reward. Each time, the address is to him, in the second person dual, with the assumption that he is one of the two addressed communities. The technique is one the Book uses throughout, and it means that no reader can finish the sūrah as an observer of other people's destinies. The question at the door of the gardens is asked of the person reading about the gardens, and it will be asked again after every feature of them.

**The Description That Begins Next**

The verse after this one begins the description of the gardens with a single feature: green branches or, on another reading, many varieties. The chapters of the reward passages that follow are notable for what they leave out as much as for what they include; they describe shade, water, fruit, fabric, and company, and they never describe a cost. The refrain will follow every item in the list until the description is complete. The reader is therefore given a place to stand while the garden is unfolded in front of him, and the place is the same sentence he has been answering since the sūrah's first refrain.

**A Door Marked by One Sentence**

The refrain between the punishment and the gardens marks a change of subject, and it marks it without changing its own words. That is the sūrah's method at every transition. The question does not become easier when the subject becomes pleasant, and it does not become a threat when the subject is fire; it remains a question about gratitude, and it is asked of a reader who has now seen both outcomes named in the same chapter. The result is a text in which no section can be read as an isolated unit. The gardens that follow are not a reward described for its own sake; they are the second half of an answer to a question that was already put twice in the middle of the punishment, and the refrain at the door signals that the same audit is being applied to the gift.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:48

> ˹Both will be˺ with lush branches.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Feature of the Gardens Is Foliage**

The description begins with branches rather than with a building, a throne, or a light. The word translated as lush branches, *afnān*, denotes the spreading limbs of trees that bear fruit and leaves, providing both shade and food, and the choice locates the gardens in the experience of a people who knew what a single tree could mean in a dry country. The opening image is abundance that grows. Everything else the sūrah will name in the gardens — springs, fruit, brocade, companions — unfolds under the canopy established in this line, and the reader who has spent the first half of the chapter on harvested fields and date palms is being given a continuation of the same landscape rather than a departure from it.

**The Second Reading: Varieties**

The word can also be read as a plural of *fann*, meaning a sort or a variety, in which case the verse describes gardens containing many different kinds of trees and fruits. The two readings are both well supported by Arabic usage, and the commentators reported both without treating them as rivals. Read together, they produce a picture in which the branches and the diversity are two aspects of one abundance: an orchard whose limbs spread wide and whose species are numbered beyond the familiar ones. The sūrah's later verses will describe fruit in pairs and in kinds, and those descriptions fit either sense of this word. The ambiguity enriches the line, and the translators who chose branches chose the reading that fits the image best.

**Shade as a Description of Security**

In a landscape where the sun is the enemy of the traveller, shade is not a decorative feature. The Book repeatedly uses shade in its descriptions of the final reward, and one sūrah describes the people of the garden as seeing neither sun nor bitter cold, with the shade close above them and the clusters hanging low. A canopy of branches answers thirst and heat together, and the sūrah names it first because it is the condition under which everything else in the garden can be enjoyed. The gardens described in this chapter are not described as interiors or as palaces in the ordinary sense; they are described as a planted world in its best season, with the water running and the fruit within reach.

**The Branches Bear Fruit and Leaves Together**

The word chosen for the branches carries the sense of what is productive as well as what is green. A tree in this description is not valued for ornament alone; it is a source of food and of cover at the same time, which is a way of saying that the reward is both useful and beautiful without separating the two. The Book's descriptions of the gardens consistently run pleasure and provision together, and they never suggest that one is a concession to the other. A branch that shades its owner and feeds him is the smallest complete unit of the reward, and the sūrah's description begins with it rather than with the grandest feature listed later in the passage.

**A Garden Described to People Who Knew Gardens**

The audience of the sūrah lived in a region of oases and wadis, and the images used in this passage are drawn from the best things they knew. The Book does not describe the reward in terms that would have been unintelligible to them, and it does not simply reproduce the gardens of their world either. The features it names are the ones they valued most: shade, running water, fruit that arrives without labour, and company. What the description adds to their experience is permanence and certainty, the two things that no earthly garden has ever offered. A person who had watched a wadi dry up would understand the reward better than a modern reader who has never depended on a season.

**The Pairing of the Two Gardens**

The verse is in the dual, and it is describing both gardens together. Each garden has branches, and the parallel construction will be maintained throughout the passage, with each feature named in the dual and each refrain asking the same question. The design of the description is symmetrical because the chapter's whole vision of creation is symmetrical: two of everything, ordered by one Lord. The reader who noticed the two east and two west, the two seas, and the two multitudes is prepared for the two gardens, and the sūrah does not need to explain the pattern by the time it reaches this verse.

**What the Line Does Not Say**

Nothing in the verse mentions gold, jewels, or any material that would have to be measured. The first thing named in paradise is a plant, and this is a decision about what the reward consists of. The Quran's gardens are places where things grow, water flows, and shade falls, and the descriptions consistently treat these as the substance of the reward rather than as illustrations of something else. The reader is being told that the reward is a life rather than a payment, and the sūrah's choice of an orchard as its first image sets the tone for everything that follows, including the fabric and the companions named later in the passage.

**Why the Reward Grows**

The gardens of the Quran are described with plants, water, and shade because those are the things whose loss makes life in this world hard, and the reward is presented as the restoration of what was lost and the multiplication of what was given. A tree that bears fruit and gives shade is the smallest complete picture of the reward in this sūrah, and it is named before the springs, the fruit, and the furnishings. The order may reflect the order of value as well as the order of the landscape. A person who has lived without shade knows what the first line of the description is worth, and the sūrah lets a reader who has never thought about it begin with the plainest of paradise's gifts.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:49

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question After a Single Feature**

This refrain follows the description of the gardens as possessing spreading branches, and it comes after only one item of the tour. The timing shows how the sūrah means to present the reward: not as a single grant to be acknowledged once, but as a series of features each deserving its own response. The reader is asked to answer the question about the branches before he is told about the springs. The method turns the description of paradise into a slow inventory, and the effect is that no element of it is passed over as part of a whole. Each line is treated as a favour in its own right, and each favour is counted.

**What the Question Asks of Someone Who Has Not Seen a Garden**

The reader has no experience of the gardens, and the verse asks him about them anyway. The answer it expects is not a report about something observed but a recognition of something promised, and the Quran treats the two operations as belonging to the same act of faith. The sūrah's audience was being asked to accept a description of a place nobody had visited from a messenger they could question, and the question about denial applies to that acceptance. It also applies to the manner of it. A person can accept a promise without allowing it to matter, and the refrain asks whether such a person intends to call the promise true while living as though it were not.

**The Refrain as a Measure of Attention**

The chapter's structure makes the refrain a gauge. Its occurrences after the favours of the world test whether the reader notices what he has received; its occurrences after the punishments test whether he is willing to face the consequence of refusing it; its occurrences in the gardens test whether he pays attention to a description that will never be verified until too late. The same words perform all three functions, and the reader who has been answering without thinking will discover, at this point in the recitation, that there is nothing to look at while he answers. The garden has been described but not seen, and the only thing in the room is the sentence.

**Two Multitudes Hearing About a Promise**

The address remains dual, and the two communities are both being told about the gardens as a reward for the same conduct. A jinn who has heard the description of the branches is asked the same question as a human being who has heard it, and the promise concerns them both. The Book's answer to those who ask whether the reward is for their kind is to keep addressing them in the same sentence as everyone else. In this sūrah the two are never separated, whether in provision, in warning, in punishment, or in reward, and the refrain at this point in the description maintains the pattern without comment.

**Denial and the Quality of Hope**

There is a form of denial that consists of refusing to hope. A person who has convinced himself that the promises of religion are a manner of speaking will not organise his life around them, and the practical outcome is the same as if he had denied them outright. The sūrah's refrain addresses the mind that has decided in advance what sort of book it is reading. Placed after a verse about branches, it invites the reader to consider what he actually expects to happen after death, and the question presses whether the answer to that has any effect on how he lives. A promise that changes nothing in a person's conduct has been denied by that person, whatever his words.

**The Description Continues**

The next verses will add springs, fruit, furnishings, and companions, and the refrain will follow each of them. The reader who has answered the question once at this point will be asked it again, and the repetition is designed to make the answer a habit rather than an event. The sūrah's method has been consistent from its first refrain, and the gardens section applies it with the same patience the earlier sections received. What the reader does with the question after the branches will determine what he does with it after the springs, the fruit, and the carpets, and the chapter gives him all the time he needs.

**Where the Reader Stands at This Point**

Half of the sūrah remains, and the reader has now passed through the gifts of the world, the record of deeds, the end of the world, the punishment of the deniers, and the beginning of the description of the reward. At every stage he has been asked the same question, and the question has never changed its wording. The consistency is the chapter's message as much as any single verse in it. Whatever the two multitudes encounter, in this world or the next, the accounting is the same and the Lord is the same, and the refrain that has followed them through fire is the same sentence that will follow them through the gardens.

**A Sentence Meant to Be Said Aloud**

The refrain in the Quran's recitation is not only read; it is answered, and the answer is preserved in the report of the jinn who heard the Prophet ﷺ recite this sūrah. They said that they denied none of their Lord's favours, and the Prophet ﷺ praised their response. The practice of responding to these verses belongs to the way the sūrah has been received from its first audience, and it explains why the question is phrased to invite speech rather than reflection alone. A reader who merely notes the question has done less than what the text asks for. The refrain is designed as a prompt, and the prompt's purpose is a spoken acknowledgement. The verse after the branches and before the springs is an occasion for exactly that.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:50

> In each ˹Garden˺ will be two flowing springs.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Water Named Before Anything Else in the Gardens**

The first feature described in the gardens after the foliage is water, and the order matches the order of the sūrah's earthly passage, which moved from the sky and the balance to the two seas and the harvest. A spring that flows is a supply rather than a pool; it arrives and leaves, and its movement is the guarantee that it will not stagnate. The word used describes water that runs freely, and the commentators understood it as the abundance of the supply rather than as a description of speed. The gardens of the Quran are consistently described in terms of what sustains life, and the reader who has been following the chapter since the verse about the two seas is not surprised to find water at the centre of the reward.

**Two Springs in Each Garden**

The dual construction continues into the reward, and the interpreters offered two explanations of the pairing. Some understood the verse as stating that each garden contains one spring that flows, with the dual referring to the two gardens together. Others took it as an allusion to the named springs of paradise mentioned elsewhere in the Book, and a third reading connected the verse to the rivers described in another sūrah, which flow with water, milk, wine, and honey. The readings differ in detail and agree on the substance: the reward includes an abundance of pure water, and the abundance is stated in the sūrah's characteristic pairs. A single spring would have answered the thirst of the people described in the earlier verses; two springs answer it in the manner of a host who does not measure.

**The Named Springs of Paradise**

Two springs are named in the Quran, and the commentators brought them into the discussion of this verse. One is *Tasnīm*, described as a spring from which those brought near to God drink, and the other is *Salsabīl*, the spring from which the righteous are given to drink in the sūrah of Man. Neither passage identifies the springs of this verse with those names, and the connection is a suggestion rather than an interpretation. What the references supply is a sense of the vocabulary the Book uses for the water of the reward. The names are not measurements and not locations; they are words that evoke clarity, abundance, and ease, and the sūrah's audience would have heard them as such rather than as geography.

**The Rivers of This World as a Comparison**

The Quran describes the gardens in one place with a list of rivers: "a river of pure water, a river of milk that never changes taste, a river of wine, pleasing to those who drink it, and a river of pure honey." The imagery is deliberately drawn from the four best things a desert audience could imagine, and the sūrah of al-Raḥmān gives the same picture in a single line about flowing springs. Both descriptions avoid naming a cost or a limit, and neither supplies a map. The water of a garden in the Quran is not a reservoir that can be drained or a well that can fail; the verb used for its movement is the same one used for the rivers of this world that flow to the sea and do not run out in a lifetime.

**Why Flowing Water Is the Right Opening**

A still pool in a hot country is a warning; the water in it is going nowhere and will turn. A spring that runs is a promise of permanence, because clean water arrives continuously from a source that the viewer cannot see. The description in this verse therefore carries more than the fact of water. It states that the supply is alive, which is the difference between a garden kept by human labour and a garden kept by its Lord. The sūrah's earthly passages already connected provision to a source beyond human control, and the reward is described with the same logic. The people in the gardens are not maintaining their springs. They are living beside a supply that runs whether anyone tends it or not.

**The Garden's Grammar**

The verse stands in the middle of a description that names every feature in pairs, and by this point in the recitation the pattern is unmistakable. The reader has been given two gardens, two branches, two springs, and two multitudes, and the descriptions of the four gardens in this sūrah will keep the same shape throughout. The pairing is not incidental to the chapter's theology; it belongs to the way the sūrah presents creation as a system of matched things held by one Lord. The reward increases the pattern rather than breaking it, and the water arrives in pairs like everything else in the scene. What the sūrah is describing is not an exception to the world's order but that order brought to completion.

**The Refrain That Follows the Water**

The next verse will ask the question again, and it will be asked immediately after a line about water. The sūrah's audience lived in a land where water was the difference between life and departure, and the placement of the question after this particular feature is not accidental. Nobody who has carried water from a well needs an explanation of what a flowing spring is worth, and the refrain comes at the point where the reader's own experience supplies the valuation. The question about denial is therefore easiest to answer here for the poorest listener and hardest for the one who has never been thirsty. The sūrah gives both the same sentence and the same spring.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:51

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question After the Springs**

This refrain follows the description of the flowing springs, and the reader is now asked the familiar question about the second feature of the gardens. The description is being unfolded one line at a time, and the question arrives after each item rather than after the whole list. The design is worth stating plainly: the sūrah treats every feature of the reward as a separate favour requiring a separate response. A person who has answered the question about the branches is not thereby excused from answering it about the water. The chapter will go on applying the same standard to the fruit, the furnishings, and the companions, and the standard is applied with the same wording each time.

**What a Spring Means to an Audience That Carried Water**

The audience of this sūrah knew the labour of drawing water. Fetching it, carrying it, and rationing it were part of the daily work of men and children, and a settlement's existence depended on a well or a spring that did not fail. When the verse before this one describes two flowing springs in each garden, it is offering a description of the end of that labour, and the refrain then asks whether the gift is acknowledged. The Book's descriptions of the reward repeatedly name the things whose absence defined hardship in this world: shade in a land without it, water in a land of scarcity, fruit out of season, and rest that is never interrupted. The reader is invited to measure the garden against the worst thing he has had to do.

**The Refrain and the Two Gardens Together**

The dual form of the refrain has been constant throughout the chapter, and it now carries a second reference alongside the first. The sūrah has been pairing the two multitudes since its earliest refrains, and in this passage it has begun pairing the two gardens. Both pairs are addressed in the same grammatical construction, and the reader who has been hearing the dual as an address to human beings and jinn can hear it here as a description of the two gardens as well. The ambiguity is a feature of the Arabic, not a difficulty. In either reading the question concerns a pair, and the answer is required of everything the pair contains.

**Denial Measured Against a Promise in Detail**

The sūrah's approach turns the gardens into a test of attention. A promise stated in general terms can be accepted without much thought; a promise described detail by detail has to be considered at each step. The reader who has followed the description of branches and springs is now in a position to notice how specific the account has become, and the refrain asks whether anything in it is being called untrue. The question covers the whole of the promise and each part of it. A person who accepts that there is a reward while treating the particular descriptions as decorative has not yet answered the verse, and the sūrah keeps asking until the disclosure is complete.

**The Question as an Act of Worship**

In the practice of recitation, the refrain has a liturgical function. The jinn answered it aloud when the Prophet ﷺ recited the sūrah, and their words are preserved: none of Your favours do we deny, our Lord. The response turns a rhetorical question into a spoken act of worship, and the sūrah is built to invite exactly that. A reciter who reaches these verses is not merely reading a text; he is being offered the opportunity to say the same sentence the jinn said. The refrain's repetition is therefore part of the reward rather than an interruption of it. Every occurrence gives the listener another chance to make the acknowledgement with his own voice.

**A Pause Built Into the Description**

The line that follows this verse will describe the fruit of the two gardens, and the refrain gives the reader a moment between the water and the harvest. The pause is a feature of the chapter's composition rather than a by-product of it. Long descriptions in any text become a blur, and the sūrah's questions interrupt the blur at regular intervals, forcing the reader to consider what he has just been told. The result is that a passage of thirty verses about gardens does not read as a list. It reads as a series of offerings, each one accompanied by the same request for a response, and the reader who has been answering keeps pace with the description.

**The Reward and the Question That Watches It**

The sūrah has now placed its refrain inside the description of the gardens twice, and it will do so many more times before the tour is finished. The effect is that no reader can regard the gardens as a settled subject that he has finished considering. The question is asked at the branches, at the springs, and will be asked at the fruit, and each occurrence is a reminder that the reward is being described for a reason. The Book does not display paradise for its own sake. It displays it to persuade the living, and the refrain is the instrument of that persuasion. Every line of the garden is a favour, and every favour is an occasion for the question.

**A Refrain Heard Where the Water Runs**

The gardens passage fills the space between its refrains more tightly than any earlier section, so that the question returns after every single feature. The passage on creation left several verses between occurrences, and the passage on the fire ran to three verses at one point. In the gardens the reader hears the question after the branches, after the springs, and after the fruit, with only one verse of description in between. The closer spacing is a measure of the sūrah's intensity as it approaches its end, and it gives the second half of the chapter a rhythm of its own. The reader is not being asked once about paradise; he is being asked about each thing in it, and the water of this verse is the second occasion.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:52

> In each will be two types of every fruit.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Every Fruit, and Every Fruit in Pairs**

The verse names fruit in general and then doubles it, and the construction allows for two readings of the word used. It can mean two kinds of every fruit, so that the garden supplies both the familiar and the unfamiliar; or it can mean pairs, so that each fruit is present in two forms. The commentators who took the first reading understood the reward as including varieties the visitor to this world has never tasted, since the Quran elsewhere says that the people of the garden will be given what they desire and that no soul knows what has been prepared for them. On either reading, the verse excludes a garden with a limited menu. The abundance described is not only of quantity but of kinds, and the text goes out of its way to say so.

**The Word Zawjān and the Vocabulary of Pairing**

The Arabic term translated as two kinds is *zawjān*, the same word the sūrah has been using for the pairs that structure the created order. It is the term used for the mating pairs of animals, for the two halves of a whole, and for the matching of things that belong together. Its use here places the fruit of the gardens inside the chapter's larger scheme rather than outside it. The sūrah has shown the reader two east and two west, two seas, two creations of clay and fire, and two gardens; now the fruit arrives in the same form. The reward is not an escape from the order of created things. It is that order perfected, and the vocabulary is consistent from the first verses to this one.

**One Kind Familiar, One Kind Unknown**

A specific interpretation of the pair was reported by al-Zajjāj: one of the two kinds is the fruit the person knew in this world, and the other is a fruit he has never seen. That reading explains why the verse mentions both kinds rather than simply saying that the gardens contain every fruit. The familiar produces recognition and the unfamiliar produces wonder, and the reward supplies both in the same garden. The Quran states in another passage that no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has conceived what has been prepared for the righteous, and this verse can be read as a concrete instance of that general statement. The person entering the garden will find what he already loves and what he could never have imagined.

**A Fruit That Grows Without Labour**

The earthly passage of this sūrah described fruit and date palms as favours, and it attached them to the labour of cultivation, the seasons, and the harvest. The garden's fruit is described as near at hand in a later verse of this same passage, hanging within reach of the people reclining beneath it. The change is not incidental. The reward answers the conditions of this world by removing the interval between desire and satisfaction, and it does so without making the garden dull. The descriptions consistently include what people enjoy rather than what people need, and the fruit of the gardens is presented as a pleasure rather than as a ration. The sūrah's promise is not the absence of hunger alone; it is a place where appetite is answered with delight.

**The Doubling as a Statement About Generosity**

The chapter's habit of doubling every good thing reaches its clearest expression in the garden passages, where the reader is given two gardens, two branches, two springs, and two kinds of every fruit. Nothing in the description suggests scarcity or rationing; the world's economy of limited goods is not carried into the reward. The Quran states elsewhere that its provision is without account, and the sūrah's grammar communicates the same thing in a different way. A host who places one cup before a guest has answered the guest's thirst; a host who places two has answered it and then gone further. The reward in this chapter is consistently described in the second manner, and the reader is left to draw the conclusion.

**The Refrain That Follows the Fruit**

The next verse will ask the question again, immediately after this one, and the sequence continues to treat each item of the garden as a separate favour. The description has now accumulated enough detail for the reader to see the method: the sūrah is building a picture and auditing it at every stage. Fruit in this world was the first item named in the earthly list, and the fruit of the garden is offered in the same position here, doubled and without season. The question that follows it will be the same one that followed the harvests and the pearls, and the reader who has answered honestly along the way will find this occurrence the easiest to answer of all of them.

**The Garden's Economy of Abundance**

Nothing in the description of the gardens is measured, and the verse about the fruit states the principle directly: every kind of fruit is present, and each kind appears in two forms. The Book's promises of provision elsewhere use the language of sufficiency and satisfaction, but the reward passages in this sūrah use the language of surplus. The difference is deliberate. A guest who is fed has been answered; a guest before whom the table is doubled has been honoured, and the sūrah's description of paradise is built on the second image from beginning to end. The fruits arrive in pairs, the gardens arrive in pairs, and the springs arrive in pairs, and the reader is being told that the Lord's generosity toward the people of the garden does not operate on the scale of the minimum needed to satisfy them.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:53

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question After the Fruit**

This refrain comes immediately after the description of the fruit of the two gardens, and it is asked in the same words as the occurrences that followed branches and water. The reader has now been through three features of the reward, and each has been followed by the question. The consistency is deliberate. Nothing in the sūrah suggests that the response owed for a garden is different in kind from the response owed for a harvest, and the repetition treats the reward as a continuation of the provision already counted in the chapter's earthly verses. The gardens do not require a second religion; they require the same acknowledgement that the first half of the sūrah has been asking for.

**An Answer at Every Course**

The structure of the passage resembles a meal served in courses, with each item presented and the host waiting for a word before the next is brought. The reader who has been answering at the branches, at the springs, and now at the fruit is in the position of a guest who has been acknowledging the same generosity three times in a single sitting. The repetition has a practical effect on the reader's own sense of the whole passage: the description no longer reads as a catalogue but as a series of individual gifts, each with its own claim. The sūrah's concern is that a person should not accept a whole package of blessings without noticing the parts.

**What the Question Requires of the Reader's Imagination**

The gardens have not been seen. The description has been supplied, and the reader is asked whether he denies what he has been told. The answer to that question depends on something the sūrah cannot supply: the reader's confidence in the One who described the garden. The chapter has spent its first half establishing the trustworthiness of that source through visible gifts, and the second half draws on the trust the first half has built. A person who has acknowledged the sun and the harvest has already accepted the Giver's character, and the gardens are then presented as a promise from the same source. The refrain tests whether the reader is consistent with his own earlier answers.

**The Question Addressed to Both Multitudes Again**

The dual address remains in the gardens, and it produces a picture in which the two communities are invited to the same reward under the same conditions. The Quran describes the residents of the gardens as human beings and jinn without distinguishing their portion, and it reports that among the jinn are those who believed and submitted. The refrain's persistence here prevents a reader from imagining that the promise belongs to his species alone, and it prevents a jinn listener from excluding himself. The two gardens are for whoever feared the station of his Lord, and the phrase has no qualifying clause about the kind of creature involved. The invitation is issued to the pair throughout the chapter and is never narrowed.

**Repetition and the Study of the Text**

The sūrah's repeated question is also an aid to those who study it. A refrain marks a division in the text, and the division helps a reader or reciter keep his place in a long passage. In a chapter recited in one sitting during prayer or in a gathering, the sentence serves as a structural marker as well as a spiritual prompt. The tradition of reciting this sūrah and answering its refrain is preserved in the report of the jinn and in the practice of those who followed, and the answer's repetition mirrors the question's. The design is practical in the same way that the chant of a caravan is practical: it measures the road and keeps the traveller moving.

**The Garden Described as a Response to the Earthly List**

The chapter's earthly list named fruit, date palms, grain, and fragrance. The gardens list branches, springs, every sort of fruit, and then furnishings and company, and the two lists answer each other at several points. What was given as a season in this world is given as a permanent condition in the next, and what was harvested by labour is offered hanging within reach. The sūrah's audience would have heard the comparison without needing to have it explained, because the difference between a good year and a bad one was the difference between a life and a departure. The refrain that follows each item keeps the comparison in view and asks the reader to say what he thinks of the second list.

**The Scene the Reader Has Been Given**

By this point in the recitation the reader has been shown the whole arc of the sūrah: mercy at the opening, provision in the middle, judgement near the end of the first half, and now the reward described in detail. The question has been put to him more than twenty times, and the answer it invites has not changed. He is still in the interval in which the answer can be spoken with consequence, and the sūrah continues to give him the words. What follows will describe how the people of the gardens are seated, what they rest on, and who accompanies them, and the refrain will keep arriving after each new feature with the same question and the same patience.

**Held in the Same Balance as the Harvest**

The refrain follows the fruit of the gardens with the same words it used after the grain and the palms of this world, and the parallel is exact enough to be instructive. The Book does not divide the favours of the world from the favours of the hereafter into two categories with two different responses; the same question covers both, and the same answer is owed for both. A person who has thanked God for his food and treated the promise of paradise as a matter of casual belief has answered one half of the ledger and left the other, and the sūrah's refrain closes that gap by asking about the garden's fruit in the vocabulary of the harvest. The reward is not a separate subject. It is the continuation of a single provision that begins in this life and does not end.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:54

> Those ˹believers˺ will recline on furnishings lined with rich brocade. And the fruit of both Gardens will hang within reach.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Seated Before Anything Is Served**

The description of the gardens turns from what grows in them to how their people are seated, and the posture chosen is reclining. The commentators read it as an image of contentment and security, because a person does not recline in a place where he expects to be disturbed. The posture stands at the opposite end of the sūrah's vocabulary from the seizure described earlier, where the guilty were dragged by the forelock and the feet. The chapter has now shown two bodies in two positions: one taken against its will and one at rest. Between those two images lies the whole distance between the two destinies, and the sūrah states the contrast without needing to comment on it.

**The Fabric and Its Name**

The furnishing described is lined with brocade, and the Arabic word used is *istabraq*, a thick silk woven with gold. Commentators described it as a heavy fabric whose surface is embroidered and whose lining is of the same quality, meaning that the part of the furnishing turned away from the eye is as costly as the part that faces it. The detail is characteristic of the Quran's descriptions of the reward, which repeatedly include clothing, cushions, and coverings alongside food and water. A human being is not only fed in these passages; he is housed and dressed, and the Book attends to the difference between a reward that answers hunger and one that answers a whole life.

**What the Posture Says About Time**

A reclining person is not on his way anywhere. The gardens are described in this sūrah and in others as places where no one grows weary and nothing is interrupted, and the posture in this verse states that condition in a single word. The people of the reward are not waiting for a next stage; the description contains no sequence of events, no departure, and no appointment. The Quran makes the same point in another sūrah where the residents of the garden are described as never seeing scorching heat or bitter cold, a pair that excludes both discomfort and the turning of a season. The reclining of this verse belongs to the same set of images, and together they describe rest as the permanent form of the reward rather than as a pause in it.

**The Wondering of the Commentators**

Several of the early commentators responded to this verse with a remark that has been repeated in every generation since. Looking at the furnishings and their lining, they asked what the interior of such an arrangement must be like if its exterior is of this quality. The question is not a piece of exegesis in the technical sense; it is a reaction, and its preservation in the tafsīr literature shows how the sūrah's descriptions were received. The Quran's pictures of the reward were not constructed to be exhausted by analysis. They were constructed so that the listener would follow a description of a lining to the thought of what it covers, and the thought is left open on purpose.

**Fruit Within Reach**

The second half of the verse describes the fruit of both gardens as hanging near the people who recline beneath it. The Quran uses the same image elsewhere when it describes an elevated garden whose fruit hangs within reach, and another passage states that the fruit will be made easy to reach. The point of the image is that the reward has no labour in it. A person who has worked for his food in this world, waited for a season, and climbed for a piece of fruit is being told that the effort has been removed without the pleasure being diminished. The people of the gardens do not harvest; they reach out, and what they reach for is already there.

**Both Gardens in a Single Line**

The verse speaks of the fruit of both gardens, and the phrase serves as a reminder that the reward being described is a pair. The sūrah has been working in twos since its early verses, and the gardens continue the pattern, with the higher pair described first and a second pair introduced later in the passage. The description of this verse applies to both gardens at once, and the refrains that follow each verse apply to both multitudes. The symmetry is sustained at every level from the opening verse of the chapter to its final line, and the reader is not permitted to imagine the reward as a single undivided place with a single undivided population.

**A Picture of the People, Not the Place**

Everything in this verse is arranged around human beings: they recline, the furnishings are under them, and the fruit is within their reach. The Book's descriptions of the garden are consistently written from the perspective of the person inside it, and the effect is that paradise is presented as a condition of creatures rather than as a location to be surveyed. The trees and the water of the previous verses were named as features; this verse names a posture, a texture, and a gesture of the hand. The reward is being described in terms of what a person will experience, and the sūrah's final verses will continue the same approach with companions, cushions, and carpets rather than with architecture or geography.

**Provision and Company in One Frame**

The verse completes the account of what the people of the gardens are given before the passage turns to who will be with them, and the two halves belong together. A person who is fed and housed but alone has been provided for without being answered. The reward described in this chapter supplies both, and the order in which it does so is worth noticing: the food comes first, and the company comes second, as though the account were being settled from the outside in. What is being described is not a transaction but a household, and the furnishings, the fruit, and the companions are its furniture, its table, and its family.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:55

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Asked at the Table**

This refrain arrives after the description of the furnishings and the fruit that hangs within reach, and it is the third occurrence inside the gardens passage. The reader has now been asked the question over branches, over water, over fruit of every kind, and now over the seating and the food laid before the people of the reward. The sequence has become a meal, and the question follows the course. The sūrah is treating the description of paradise the way a host treats a table: each thing presented is accompanied by a word, and the word expected from the guest is an acknowledgement rather than a compliment. The repetition is the sūrah's way of keeping the guest's attention on who provided the meal.

**Provision Described in the Present Tense**

The verses around this refrain use the language of the present and the future, and the gardens are spoken of as though they were already furnished. The Book's practice in its descriptions of the last day is to narrate what has not yet happened with the certainty of what has, and the grammar carries a claim about the reliability of the report. A reader who accepts the present-tense description has accepted the promise; a reader who treats it as a figure of speech has not yet answered this refrain. The question therefore touches the reader's estimate of the whole book he is holding, and it is asked at a point where the description has become specific enough to be impossible to dismiss as purely decorative.

**Gratitude as the Guest's Portion**

The Arabic word for the favours being asked about covers gifts of every kind, and the Quran uses it for the benefit received rather than for the benefit deserved. The reward described in these verses is not presented as a wage; nothing in the gardens passage mentions a price, an exchange, or a scale of payment. The Book states the principle elsewhere when it promises the people of the garden whatever they desire and adds that the greatest bounty is the Lord's good pleasure. The refrain at the table is consistent with that framing. What is asked of the guest is not repayment, since no repayment is possible, but recognition of the generosity that set the table in the first place.

**The Dual That Never Splits**

The address remains dual at this verse, and the two communities remain in a single sentence. The Quran has been describing the gardens for both of them since the beginning of the passage, and it has never inserted a clause that gives one species a different portion or a different promise. The Book elsewhere reports the testimony of the jinn themselves, who acknowledged that among them were the righteous and those who fell short, and it treats the reward as available to whoever qualifies. The refrain in this place prevents the reader from imagining a paradise reserved for his own kind, and it prevents a jinn listener from excluding himself from the invitation that the verse has just described.

**A Refrain That Slows the Reading**

The gardens passage is the sūrah's longest stretch of sustained description, and without the refrains it would read as a single list. The design serves the reciter as well as the reader. In the oral setting of the Quran's delivery, the refrain functions as a place to breathe and as a signal to the listeners that a section has ended. The chapter was composed for recitation before it was composed for study, and the rhythm of this passage shows the oral design in the written text.

**Denial in a Place Like This**

The question is asked in the middle of a description of comfort, and the juxtaposition sharpens it. A person who denies a favour while standing in the middle of a desert is making a statement about his own memory; a person who denies a favour while reclining on furniture and reaching for fruit is making a statement about his perception. The people of the gardens in this passage are the ones who answered the refrain correctly, and their reward is being described for readers who still have the opportunity to give the same answer. The question in this position is therefore not addressed to the diners. It is addressed to the reader who has just been shown where they are sitting.

**What Follows the Table**

The next verse turns from the furniture to the company, and it introduces the maidens of the gardens in the same measured style. The passage will continue in this order to its end: features of the place, companions, and the question between them. The reader who has answered the refrain this many times has been prepared for what comes next, and the sūrah does not soften the transition. What is being described in these lines is a complete life in the presence of its Lord, and every part of it is offered as a reason to say that none of the favours has been denied.

**Every Gift Carries the Same Question**

The refrain that follows the furnishings uses the words the chapter has used since its opening, and the wording has not been adjusted for the setting. The question is not softened in a garden and not sharpened over a fire; it is the same sentence, and the same Lord stands behind it. A reader who has noticed this consistency has understood the sūrah's purpose better than one who has been reading the refrains as decoration. Every pleasure in the passage is a claim upon the reader's acknowledgement, and the claim is filed in the same terms whether the subject is a tree, a spring, a meal, or a companion.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:56

> In both ˹Gardens˺ will be maidens of modest gaze, who no human or jinn has ever touched before.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Whose Gaze Is Restrained**

The phrase used for the women of the gardens describes them as restricting their gaze, and the commentators explained it as meaning that they look at no one but their own spouses. The detail distinguishes the description from a merely physical one. The Book is describing a disposition as much as an appearance, and the quality named first is a matter of character rather than of form. The Quran uses the same phrase in other passages about the reward, and in each case the point is that the relationship between the spouses is exclusive and undisturbed. The gardens are not described as a crowd of beauties; they are described as a company in which attention is directed inward and no one is placed in competition.

**The Verb and What It Implies**

The verb translated as touched is *ṭamatha*, and the scholars of the language noted that its meaning shifts with its subject. Used of a man with a woman as the object, it refers to intimacy; used with a woman as the subject, it refers to the monthly cycle. Both senses were reported in the classical discussions of this verse, which is why the interpreters read it as excluding two things at once: the women of the gardens are untouched by any husband from the previous world, and they are free of the burdens that attach to women in this life. The Quran does not dwell on the second point, and it states it inside a phrase about chastity, as one more removal of a hardship among the removals that the gardens represent.

**The Force of "Before"**

The phrase no human or jinn has ever touched before does not imply that they will never be touched. The Book is describing a beginning, and the specification of the two species excludes the possibility that the spouse was preceded by someone else, whether from the world of human beings or from the jinn. The Quran has addressed the two communities together throughout the sūrah, and it maintains the pairing even in a detail of this kind. What is being described is a relationship in which the spouses meet without a past, and the Quran elsewhere states the same idea when it describes the purified spouses of the believers in the gardens. The reward is being presented as the absence of everything that damages a marriage in this world.

**Where "Therein" Points**

The verse begins with a word that can refer to the two gardens just described, and the commentators noted a second possibility: it may refer to the furnishings on which the people recline, since the previous verse described them as seated. A third reading takes it as a reference to all the dwelling places of paradise. The differences do not affect the substance of the description, and the scholars reported them without dispute. The classical practice of listing several possible referents for a pronoun is a feature of Quranic commentary rather than a sign of confusion in the text, and it reflects a willingness to let a verse mean more than one thing at once when the language allows it.

**The Same Description in Other Sūrahs**

The Quran describes the spouses of the garden in several places with a set of overlapping images. One sūrah compares them to well-protected pearls and describes them as gazing only at their husbands. Another states that they will be perfectly created and made virgins, loving and of matching age. The sūrah of the Ranks describes them with large, lovely eyes and compares them to hidden pearls. The descriptions avoid names, histories, and genealogies, and they concentrate on the qualities that make a companion agreeable: exclusivity, beauty, and freedom from the flaws that accompany human relationships. This verse belongs to that group, and its wording is the most restrained of them all.

**Companions Described and Not Purchased**

The gardens of the Quran include companions as a part of the reward, and the descriptions consistently treat them as persons rather than as property. Their defining quality in this verse is their own restraint of gaze, which is a description of what they do rather than of how they are owned. The reward passages never suggest a market or a transaction, and the Quran elsewhere insists on the dignity of women as believers and as recipients of the same promise. The classical commentators treated the verses about the spouses of paradise as statements about companionship and mutual exclusivity, and the language of this verse supports the reading. What the text offers the reader is a bond without defect rather than an object without will.

**What the Refrain Will Ask**

The next verse repeats the question, and it will be asked over this description in the same words used for the branches, the water, and the fruit. The sūrah's structure treats the companions of the gardens as another favour on the same list, and the question after the verse is a reminder that the list as a whole belongs to one Provider. Nothing in the passage isolates any item as the reward's centre, and the refrain keeps the reader from treating any single feature as the point of paradise. What the garden contains is a complete life, and each part of it is being counted separately as the chapter moves toward its final line.

**A Verse Read at the Centre of the Passage**

The description of the companions stands near the middle of the passage on the higher gardens, and the interpreters treated it as the point at which the reward's social character becomes explicit. Everything before it was landscape and furniture; everything after it will return to fabric and to a second pair of gardens. The verse therefore supplies the human element that the earlier lines had postponed, and the arrangement keeps the reader's attention moving between what surrounds a life and what completes it. The chapter never suggests that either part alone would be sufficient, and the placement of this verse is one of the ways it says so.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:57

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question After the Maidens**

This refrain follows the description of the companions of the gardens, and it completes the pattern the passage has been establishing. The reader has now been asked the question over the planting, the water, the fruit, the furniture, and the company, and the same words serve each time. The sūrah's refusal to vary the wording is a statement about the uniformity of the standard being applied. A favour is a favour whether it is a tree or a companion, and the response owed for each is the same acknowledgement. The question has been asked so many times by this point in the recitation that a reader who has been answering aloud is answering almost without thinking, and that is what the sūrah's repetition is designed to produce.

**Why the Sūrah Names Companions**

The Quran includes spouses among the rewards of the garden in several sūrahs, and it treats the companionship as one of the things that make the reward complete. The description is not an afterthought to the water and the fruit. Human beings were created as social creatures, and the Book's account of the first human being places him in a garden with a companion before anything else is given. The reward restores that original arrangement at a higher level, and the sūrah describes it with the same restraint used for everything else in the passage. What the reader is offered is not an addition to a life but a fulfilment of the condition in which human beings were first placed.

**A Reward Described Without Coarseness**

The Quran's descriptions of the reward avoid the language of appetite for its own sake. The traditions that describe the women of the gardens use comparison with rubies, coral, and pearls, and one report preserved from the commentators says that the veil over the head of a woman of paradise is better than this world and everything in it. The Book's purpose in these verses is to answer the human longing for a perfect bond, and it answers it in the language a serious book would use rather than the language of an entertainment.

**The Two Multitudes Under One Promise**

The address remains dual, and the promise of companions is stated for the two species in the same sentence. What qualifies a person for the gardens is stated at the beginning of the passage: fear of standing before one's Lord, and nothing in the wording restricts that fear to a species.

By this occurrence, the refrain has become the passage's punctuation, and each of the sūrah's gifts now arrives with the question attached. The structure suggests that a blessing received without acknowledgement is incomplete, and the Book's treatment of gratitude elsewhere supports the same idea. The Quran describes thankfulness as the purpose for which creation was ordered and reports that the favours of God cannot be counted. A chapter that lists the favours and interrupts itself to ask about them is therefore performing the very act it recommends. The reciter who answers the refrain is doing what the sūrah was written to elicit, and the question after the companions is one more occasion for it.

**The Reader Who Has Been Answering All Along**

A reader who has followed the sūrah from its opening verses has been answering the same question for fifty verses, and the passage of the gardens is where the answers accumulate. The repetition has a cumulative effect that a single question could not produce. Each occurrence reminds the reader that he has already agreed, and the reminders turn a moment of assent into a settled position. The sūrah is building the habit of acknowledgement in the same way it built the catalogue of gifts: one item at a time, with the same words used at every stage. The question after the companions is not new information about the answer. It is one more opportunity to give it.

**The Two Stones That Follow**

The next verse describes the companions as resembling rubies and coral, and the refrain stands immediately before that description. The reader is asked about the favours and is then shown what the chapter means by them in one more detail. The pattern the passage has established will continue to the end of the sūrah: a feature is named, the question is asked, and the next feature is introduced. Nothing in the arrangement is accidental, and the reader who has noticed the method by this point will not be surprised by anything that follows. The chapter is doing one thing from beginning to end, and it is doing it patiently.

**Counting the Favours One by One**

The refrains in the gardens passage occur so closely together that the reader is made to acknowledge each item of the description separately, and this occurrence follows the most personal of them. The method has a clear purpose: a person who accepts a general promise has agreed to very little, while a person who has answered after each feature has agreed to the whole account in detail. The sūrah is not content with a single expression of gratitude at the end of the passage. It asks for one at every step, and the question after the companions is the passage's most demanding occurrence because it concerns not a place but a relationship.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:58

> Those ˹maidens˺ will be ˹as elegant˺ as rubies and coral.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Stones Named Together**

The comparison pairs a precious stone with a substance taken from the sea, and the commentators discussed both. The ruby is valued for its colour and its clarity, and it was among the most expensive stones known to the audience of the Quran. The coral stone, *marjān*, appears earlier in this same sūrah among the favours of the two seas, where it is named alongside the pearl. The reward therefore draws on an image the chapter has already used for the provision of this world, and the reader who has been following the sūrah hears the echo. What was brought up from the sea as a gift in the earlier passage reappears as a standard of beauty in the later one.

**The Colour That the Comparison Suggests**

Some commentators read the reference to coral as a description of complexion, since coral has a rosy tint that was associated with a fair and healthy appearance. On that reading the verse describes the women of the gardens as combining the whiteness of the ruby's setting with the colour of the coral, and the effect is a picture of radiance rather than of form. The classical commentators were careful not to describe what the verse does not describe, and they left the physical details to God while explaining the impression the images produce. What the comparison establishes is that the reward's beauty is of a kind that human beings recognise and value at a glance.

**The Earthly Image and the Higher One**

The comparisons in the gardens passage are consistently drawn from things the audience knew and prized: silk, brocade, pearls, coral, and the finest stones. The Book does not describe the reward in a vocabulary no human being could understand, and it does not limit the reward to the things it names either. A comparison in the Quran points toward something rather than capturing it, and another verse of this chapter states that no eye has seen what has been prepared for the righteous. The stones in this verse are the outer limit of what the audience could picture, and the verse uses them to produce a picture whose actual subject is beyond them. The reader is given the best he knows as a scale for what he cannot know.

**Purity as the Common Quality**

Both stones named in the verse share a quality that matters more than their price: light passes through them. A ruby and a piece of coral are both valued for what they do with light rather than for their weight, and the Quran's descriptions of the reward use this property repeatedly with pearls and gems. The Book compares the people of the garden to pearls in several places and describes the reward in terms of radiance and glow. The comparison in this verse belongs to that family, and the quality being named is not ornamentation but transparency. The women of the gardens are described as things through which light passes, which is the furthest the Quranic description goes in the direction of purity.

**The Report About the Women of the Garden**

The commentators preserved a report describing the women of the garden in terms that raise the comparison of this verse to another level. In it, if a woman of the people of the garden were to look upon the earth, she would fill the space between the two with fragrance, and everything between them would become delightful; and the covering over her head is better than this world and everything in it. The report was transmitted in the tafsīr literature and was used to explain the comparison in this verse. Its function is to break the scale of the comparison: the stones of this world are not adequate to describe the reward, and the report says as much by setting the reward above the world's total value.

**What the Verse Leaves Unsaid**

Nothing in the verse describes a face, a height, a voice, or a garment. The Book compares the people of the garden to stones and stops, and the restraint is consistent with the whole passage's method. The Quran's descriptions of paradise are notable for what they refuse to specify, and this verse follows the pattern. The reader is given a quality rather than an appearance, and the choice leaves the reward's actual form in the hands of its Creator. The comparison does what a comparison can do, which is to set a direction; the sūrah's later verses will continue with cushions and carpets and another pair of gardens, and none of them will provide more detail than this one about the particulars of what the reward looks like.

**The Refrain Waiting in the Next Line**

The verse after this one repeats the question, and the reader is once again asked about the favours immediately after being given a comparison rather than an explanation. The placement means that the question must be answered on the strength of an image, and the sūrah has been training the reader for exactly that from its first pages. The favours of this world were answered on the evidence of the senses; the favours of the gardens are answered on the evidence of a description. The chapter does not apologise for requiring that step, and the refrain that follows the rubies is one more request for it.

**Stones That Close the First Gardens**

The comparison ends the description of the higher pair of gardens, and the verses that follow will introduce a second pair below them with its own features and its own refrains. The chapter is therefore closing one room before opening another, and it ends this one with an image of something precious and bright. The reader who has followed the passage from the branches to the rubies has completed a portrait of the reward in its higher form, and the sūrah does not allow him to dwell on it. The question comes immediately, and then the tour resumes with two more gardens described in the same patient detail.

**A Refrain Between Two Pictures**

The question about the favours is therefore asked in the narrow space between an image and a summary, and it functions as the seam. Nothing in the arrangement is random; the refrain marks the point at which description hands over to explanation.

The reader has been given the reward in detail, and the detail has been accumulating since the verse about the branches. ## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:60

> Is there any reward for goodness except goodness?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Expects Agreement**

The verse is phrased as a question in Arabic, and the form is the kind that expects the listener to agree rather than the kind that seeks information. Nobody is being asked to supply a fact; the reader is being invited to confirm something he already knows. The Quran uses this construction when it states a principle that no reasonable person would dispute, and it uses it here to explain the gardens that have just been described. The verse is therefore a summary rather than a new command. It tells the reader why the reward is what it is: the gardens answer the goodness that preceded them, and the answer is of the same kind.

**The Word for Goodness**

The Arabic word used covers both doing good and being beautiful, and the two senses are not far apart in the language. It names the act of treating others well, and it also names the quality of excellence that the Prophet ﷺ described when he was asked what it means to perfect one's religion. His answer, preserved in the well-known report of the angel's questions, was that it means worshipping God as though you see Him, and that if you do not see Him, He nevertheless sees you. The verse therefore stands at the centre of a vocabulary that runs through the Quran and the traditions, and its question concerns the whole of a life rather than a single deed.

**Goodness Answered in This World and the Next**

The Quran states the same principle in other passages, and the commentators collected them alongside this verse. One sūrah promises those who do good the finest reward and an increase beyond it, and another states that whoever performs a good deed will be given more goodness for it. A related verse promises the believers whatever they desire from their Lord and calls that the greatest bounty. The interpreters drew the conclusion that the principle in this verse operates in both directions: goodness performed in this life is answered with goodness in the next, and it is also answered within this life by an increase in the capacity to do good. The reward is presented as a continuation rather than a settlement.

**The Report That Read It as the Garden**

Several commentators recorded an explanation of this verse attributed to the Prophet ﷺ, in which he told his Companions that the reward of a person whom God has blessed with the testimony of His oneness is nothing other than the Garden. The report belongs to the class of statements in the tafsīr literature that tie the verse to the central doctrine of the faith, and its logic is consistent with the verse's wording. If goodness is the whole of a life directed toward God, then the reward for it cannot be something other than the fulfilment of that direction, and the Garden is the name the Book gives to that fulfilment. The question in the verse then becomes a way of stating that the believer's end matches his beginning.

**A Saying About Goodness Before Creation**

A saying attributed to Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq and reported in the commentaries makes the same point from a different angle. It reads the verse as asking whether those to whom God showed His goodness before creation could be answered with anything other than the continuation of that goodness afterwards. The formulation has the flavour of the early pietists, and it locates the reward in the constancy of God's treatment of His servants rather than in a payment for services. The Quran's own language supports the emphasis: the Book describes its provision as a grace from God in many places and never as a wage owed to the creature. The gardens of this sūrah are introduced, after all, as a reward for fear rather than for achievement.

**A Deed That Is Its Own Reward**

One of the readings reported by the commentators takes the verse in a more direct sense: a good deed is its own reward, and the person who performs it is already being paid by the act. The reading rests on the observation that the Arabic word for goodness appears twice in the verse with no qualifying clause, so that the second occurrence can be understood as the same thing as the first rather than as something given later. On this view the gardens described in the surrounding verses are not a wage but the natural flowering of a life lived well. The other readings are richer, and the Quran's habit of layered reference allows them to coexist with this one.

**Why the Verse Stands After the Gardens**

The question in this verse arrives after the sūrah has described the reward in detail, and its position explains its force. The reader has just been shown what goodness is answered with: branches, springs, fruit, furnishings, and company, described in the most generous terms the language can supply. The verse then names the principle behind the whole list in a single line, and the reader who has been following the description has already been given a demonstration before the rule is stated. The sūrah does not ask its readers to accept an abstract proposition about divine generosity; it shows them the gardens first and then asks whether any other outcome would have been fitting.

**A Principle Stated as a Question**

The Quran often states a principle in interrogative form when the answer is not in doubt, and the purpose of the form is to make the listener an active party to the statement rather than a passive recipient of it. The verse in this place does exactly that: the idea that goodness is answered with goodness is presented for the reader's confirmation, and the reader who agrees has committed himself to the principle. The Book's arguments frequently take this shape, and the shape is well suited to a chapter that has been asking a question of its own since the thirteenth verse. The sūrah asks about denial; this verse asks about reward; both ask the reader to say what he thinks, and both expect the same answer.

## Sūrah al-Raḥmān 55:61

> Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

**Expanded Commentary**

The sūrah's description of the gardens ends with a scene of rest, and the details chosen are the furnishings of a seated company.
