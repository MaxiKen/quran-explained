# Sūrah al-Raʿd (Chapter 13) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Skeptic's Demand for Spectacle**

The historical climate of al-Raʿd is marked by the persistent, obstinate cynicism of those who demanded sensational, coercive signs: *“Why has not a sign been sent down to him from his Lord?”* (13:7, 27). The opponents mocked the reality of bodily resurrection (*“When we are dust, will we indeed be in a new creation?”*, 13:5) and challenged the Prophet to hasten the divine punishment upon them rather than warning them of it. Sūrah al-Raʿd meets this intellectual cynicism with an overwhelming display of cosmic evidence. It tells the deniers that the universe itself is an unbroken tapestry of signs for those who reflect: the invisible pillars that suspend the celestial bodies, the sun and moon running their appointed courses, the earth spread out with firm mountains and rivers, and diverse fruit trees growing side by side, watered by the same rain yet differing in taste. A society that cannot perceive God in the daily rising of the sun or the sprouting of a date-palm will never believe even if an angel were to speak to them or the mountains were moved from their places.

**The Shape and Rhythmic Cadence**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Raʿd moves between cosmic contemplation, psychological radiography, and Last-Day certainty. The opening movement (1–4) declares the Book to be the undeniable Truth (*al-ḥaqq*), anchoring revelation in the observable architecture of nature. The second movement (5–16) exposes the absurdity of idolatry: how can men worship partners who have no power to benefit or harm even themselves? It introduces the stunning question of spiritual perception: *“Are the blind and the seeing equal, or are darkness and light equal?”* (13:16). The third movement (17) presents one of the most celebrated parables in the Qur'an: rain falling into valley beds according to their measure, bearing on its surface a swelling foam, contrasted with the dross that rises when metals are smelted for jewelry or tools—the foam and dross vanish as useless scum, while what benefits humanity endures upon the earth. The fourth movement (18–29) contrasts the spiritual mindset of the mindful (*ulū al-albāb*)—who fulfill the covenant of God, join what Allah has commanded to be joined, endure patiently, and repel evil with good—against the covenant-breakers who spread corruption. It is here that the immortal verse 28 appears: *“Those who believe and whose hearts find rest in the remembrance of Allah. Unquestionably, by the remembrance of Allah hearts are assured.”* The fifth movement (30–43) delivers the famous socio-historical law of verse 11: *“Indeed, Allah will not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves,”* and closes with the sovereign testimony of verse 43: when the disbelievers say *“You are not a messenger,”* the Prophet is instructed to reply: *“Sufficient is Allah as witness between me and you, and whoever has knowledge of the Book.”*

## Verse 13:1

> Alif-Lãm-Mĩm-Ra. These are the verses of the Book. What has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ from your Lord is the truth, but most people do not believe

**“Most People Do Not Believe” as a Law, Not a Lament**

The closing clause sounds like a disappointment and functions like a statute. The Qur'an counts its audience often, and the count keeps coming out the same way: “And most people will not believe—no matter how keen you are—” (12:103). The same arithmetic appears near the end of the prophetic career's horizon: “The Hour is certainly coming, there is no doubt about it. But most people do not believe” (40:59). Two things follow from reading these texts together. First, unbelief is not presented as an accident of this audience or that century; it is the Qur'an's own repeated finding about human beings at large, which means the Prophet ﷺ is not being told his mission failed — he is being told in advance what its statistics will look like. Second, the majority is disqualified as a measure of truth. If most people do not believe, then belief cannot be validated by counting heads, and the believer loses nothing by standing where the count says he will be standing: against the grain of his own census. The clause converts what could have been a wound into information about the world.

**Four Letters Put the Book Inside Its Own Language**

The sūrah opens with four detached letters — alif, lām, mīm, rāʾ — spoken by no word and parsed by no grammar of the verse that follows. They stand at the head of the revelation the way a signature stands at the foot of a letter: not part of the argument, but the mark that fixes who is speaking. Commentators have offered readings of these disconnected openings since the earliest generations, and the honest summary is that their full meaning is known to God alone; what can be said with confidence is what they *do*. They announce, before any proposition is made, that the Qur'an is not being translated into Arabic from some higher language but composed in it — delivered in the very medium in which its first hearers thought, traded, recited poetry, and argued. The point matters for everything that follows, because the claims of this sūrah will be checked against eyes and ears: skies overhead (13:2), earth below (13:3–4), lightning and thunder in a storm (13:12–13).

**Which Book? Two Readings That End in the Same Place**

The phrase “these are the verses of the Book” (13:1) received two main interpretations among the commentators. Some took “the Book” to mean this sūrah itself, so that the following verses are the exhibit immediately at hand and the *verses* are its own portions; others read “the Book” as the Qur'an entire, making the opening a franchise statement for revelation as a whole. Each reading has its ground. The narrower reading suits a chapter about to argue so hard from its own inventory of signs, and it fits the way the detached-letter sūrahs speak of the Book as their own warrant. The broader reading matches the word's grander uses across the Qur'an, where the Book names the whole revealed corpus.

## Verse 13:2

> It is Allah Who has raised the heavens without pillars—as you can see—then established Himself on the Throne. He has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term. He conducts the whole affair. He makes the signs clear so that you may be certain of the meeting with your Lord

**“Without Pillars” and the Logic of Sustained Order**

Commentators propose two readings of the phrase “without pillars—as you can see” (13:2), and the difference between them is smaller than it looks. On one view the heavens are held by pillars that simply cannot be seen; on the other, they are held by nothing visible at all. Either way the point lands on the same spot: whatever holds the sky up, the hold is not a made thing that you can point to, inspect, or repair — it is sustained continuously by its Maker. The parallel text makes the claim explicit: “He created the heavens without pillars—as you can see—and placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with you” (31:10). Sky and ground appear together there as the two halves of one engineered shelter, and the human being lives inside both without paying for either. Notice also what the phrase “as you can see” (13:2) does: it certifies the absence as part of the testimony. The audience is not asked to imagine invisible engineering; they are asked to look and confirm that nothing up there needs visible stilts, which makes the sustaining an act, performed at every moment, rather than a structure, built once.

**Enthronement Is a Job Description, Not a Departure**

“Then established Himself on the Throne” (13:2) has been misread, in argument old and new, as God finishing His work and sitting down away from it. The Qur'an's own usage says the opposite. The parallel formula runs: “Surely your Lord is Allah Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne, conducting every affair” (10:3). The enthronement and the conduct of affairs are stated in the same breath, because the throne is the image of active sovereignty — the king at work, not the king on holiday. Creation in six days followed by enthronement means the universe moved, at the moment of its completion, from being God's project to being God's administration. Nothing in it is left to run itself. The verse's next clause says the same thing in administrative language: “He conducts the whole affair” (13:2). A cosmos with no vacancies in its management leaves no room for the rival managers the Makkans proposed, and that is exactly where this sūrah is headed: if the affair is conducted, the idols are unemployed. The full authority claimed in the parallel — “That is Allah—your Lord! All authority belongs to Him” (35:13) — is the same claim this verse makes quietly, by description.

**Orbits With Expiry Dates**

“He has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term” (13:2). The sentence contains an argument for a future event, and it works by a simple logic. The precision of the orbits — “The sun and the moon ˹travel˺ with precision” (55:5) — is offered to the audience as proof that the system is governed, not self-running; and the “appointed term” attached to each orbit announces that the same governed system carries an end clause. The sun and moon are not eternal. They run on schedule toward a scheduled end, and the earlier commentators drew the natural conclusion: a term appointed means the celestial order will one day cease, that the heavens themselves are mortal equipment. The same subjection with the same term appears in a verse that ends by naming the Owner: “He merges the night into the day and the day into the night, and has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term” (35:13). Once subjection is granted, the far smaller claim — that human beings, who already die daily inside this mortal system, will be raised — loses its air of absurdity. The audience that finds resurrection incredible is asked to explain why the sun's death sentence is credible and their own is not.

## Verse 13:3

> And He is the One Who spread out the earth and placed firm mountains and rivers upon it, and created fruits of every kind in pairs. He covers the day with night. Surely in this are signs for those who reflect

**The Same Argument Moves Downstairs**

Verse 2 looked up; verse 3 looks down, and the pronouns tighten as it does. “He is the One Who” (13:3) — the sentence insists on singularity at every clause, spread, placed, created, covers, as though stacking a load-bearing wall of verbs on one Subject. The earth is “spread out” as a dwelling is spread with a carpet: the image is of preparation for habitation, not of a geometric verdict about the planet's shape. The commentators of the classical period were already alive to that question. Ibn Juzayy's resolution was that every stretch of ground is locally level to the people standing on it while the whole remains round — a reading that took the text at its word about what the verse is actually doing, which is describing the human experience of the world as an usable floor. The sūrah is building an inventory of what a resident of the earth receives without asking, and each item is added as evidence in the case opened at 13:1 against unbelief.

**Pairs: Two Readings, One Point**

“Created fruits of every kind in pairs” (13:3) has attracted two main interpretations, and the disagreement is instructive. Some read the pairing botanically: each kind of fruit comes in male and female, the pollination and reproduction of plants being invisible to most observers then and now. Others read it as pairing of qualities — light and dark colors, sweet and sour tastes — so that every kind arrives with its own contrasting partner. Each side has a reason. The botanical reading gains force from the fact of plant sexual reproduction, which the Qur'an's first audience could not have examined. It sits naturally with a doxology that names “their genders”: “Glory be to the One Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs —˹be it˺ what the earth produces, their genders, or what they do not know!” (36:36). The qualitative reading gains force from immediate visibility, since anyone who has eaten two dates from one bunch has met the pairing. On balance the readings are not rivals but scales of one claim: the pairing operates at every level the observer chooses to examine. The parallel text generalizes deliberately — “And We created pairs of all things so perhaps you would be mindful” (51:49) — and the Qur'anic clause “what they do not know” (36:36) quietly extends the pairing beyond what any exegete can enumerate. Whether the pairs are sexes or tastes, the fruit tree is announcing its Author in stereo.

**Night Made to Fall on the Day**

“He covers the day with night” (13:3). The verb is one of enveloping, a garment drawn over, and the image carries two messages at once. The first is mastery: night is not the default state that the sun merely interrupts, but a covering placed deliberately by the same hand that spread the day. The second is mercy. The alternation is described elsewhere in strictly functional terms: “He is the One Who has made the night for you to rest in and the day bright” (10:67). Work and rest are built into the sky as an alternating rhythm no one voted on and no one has ever been able to suspend. The great creation passage binds the whole system to God's command: “He makes the day and night overlap in rapid succession. He created the sun, the moon, and the stars—all subjected by His command” (7:54). To cover is also to limit: the day cannot extend itself by one minute into the night's jurisdiction. For an audience tempted to think the luminaries divine, a light that can be switched off on schedule is a decisive argument — divinities are not extinguished nightly, but equipment is.

The parallel verse names the mountains' job directly: “He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you, as well as rivers, and pathways so you may find your way” (16:15).

## Verse 13:4

> And on the earth there are ˹different˺ neighbouring tracts, gardens of grapevines, ˹various˺ crops, palm trees—some stemming from the same root, others standing alone. They are all irrigated with the same water, yet We make some taste better than others. Surely in this are signs for those who understand

**A Field Experiment With One Held-Constant Variable**

Verse 3 ended with paired fruits; verse 4 widens the lens from the tree to the landscape. “Neighbouring tracts” — adjacent plots of unlike produce: vineyards beside grain fields beside date groves, palm trees joined at the root beside palms standing on their own. The verse then fixes the one variable that matters: “They are all irrigated with the same water” (13:4). Anyone who has farmed knows what that sentence is doing. It is the design of a controlled comparison, stated in the language of a farmer fourteen centuries before the phrase existed. Same rain, same irrigation — different grapes, grains, and dates, differing in size, color, sweetness, and yield. The verse even footnotes its own botany: some palms spring from a shared root, others stand on their own stock, and both reach the same sky and drink the same water. The dead earth itself is part of the demonstration: “There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat” (36:33). The produce is elaborated in the verse that follows: “And We have placed in it gardens of palm trees and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth in it,” (36:34). The question the design forces is the one the verse will not let the audience dodge: if the water is identical, what accounts for the harvest's differences?

**Reflection, Then Understanding**

The verse ends with the formula “signs for those who understand” (13:4), and placed beside the previous verse's “signs for those who reflect” (13:3), the two closings describe a sequence rather than a redundancy. Reflection is the mind at work on the given — watching the same water, noticing the different fruit. Understanding is the mind arriving at what the noticing means: that differentiation is a decision, and decisions require a decider. The sūrah walks its audience up the two steps in order, one verse each, and the walk has a direction: from observation toward ownership, from what the world shows to whom the world belongs. The materials for the conclusion are public, abundant, and ordinary; the evidence is arranged at the level of daily bread precisely so that no class of people is exempt from it.

## Verse 13:5

> ˹Now,˺ if anything should amaze you ˹O Prophet˺, then it is their question: “When we are reduced to dust, will we really be raised as a new creation?” It is they who have disbelieved in their Lord. It is they who will have shackles around their necks. And it is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever

**What the Mocking Question Concedes**

“When we are reduced to dust, will we really be raised as a new creation?” (13:5). The question is a rhetorical staple of the denial, recorded in nearly the same words elsewhere: “And they say ˹mockingly˺, “When we are reduced to bones and ashes, will we really be raised as a new creation?”” (17:49). Read as evidence, the question makes two concessions and one denial. It concedes that death happens and that dissolution into dust is the ordinary course — the objectors are not idealists denying decay. It concedes, implicitly, that resurrection is at least thinkable, since a question needs a concept to mock. What it denies is precedent: no one has come back, therefore no one will. The Qur'an's answer, given at length elsewhere, goes at the denial directly: “And they argue with Us— forgetting they were created—saying, “Who will give life to decayed bones?”” (36:78) — and the reply fixes the reasoning: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time. He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being” (36:79). The first creation is the standing precedent. The objector argues from the absence of a second creation while standing inside the first, and the reasoning of 36:79 is that the first is the harder act — making something from nothing exceeds reassembling what once existed and merely returned to where it came from.

**Shackles as a Portrait of the Questioner's Mind**

“It is they who will have shackles around their necks” (13:5). The image is physical punishment, but it is also the exact counter-image of the question just quoted. A shackle at the neck forces the head into one position; the neck that asked the mocking question was already fixed, unable to look anywhere but along its own line of doubt. Elsewhere the Qur'an draws the same figure for a different crowd: “˹It is as if˺ We have put shackles around their necks up to their chins, so their heads are forced up,” (36:8) — heads forced up so that they cannot even see the road under their feet. The correspondence between the questioners and the chained is not accidental in the Qur'an's way of showing things: the punishment completes the posture the person chose in life. These are the people who asked whether the dust could be raised; the chain is the answer rendered in iron — the creature who was certain that nothing follows dust will keep his certainty, in the one place where certainty can no longer be revised. The verse's sequence is therefore not three unrelated threats but a portrait: the question, the frozen neck, the closed residence. It is also the chapter's own spine made visible — the disbelief declared in 13:1 is here given its final posture and address.

## Verse 13:6

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment rather than grace, though there have ˹already˺ been ˹many˺ torments before them. Surely your Lord is full of forgiveness for people, despite their wrongdoing, and your Lord is truly severe in punishment

**The Dare Hidden in the Request**

“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment rather than grace” (13:6). A request for punishment sounds like a thirst for justice, and it is nothing of the kind. The commentators who read the demand as mockery got it right, and the reason is structural: a person who asks heaven to hurry its punishment is a person who believes the punishment will never arrive. The demand is a dare wearing judicial robes. The same dare recurs through the Qur'an with the same sneer: “They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment” (22:47). The answer given there dismantles the dare's hidden premise — that delay means absence: “And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting” (22:47). The requesters are measuring eternity with their own calendar and concluding that silence in the sky is vacancy in the throne. The Prophet ﷺ is told elsewhere to reply in kind: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Perhaps some of what you seek to hasten is close at hand”” (27:72). The dare, in other words, is answered not with argument but with an appointment — and the answerer remains, as this sūrah will insist at 13:7, a warner rather than a scheduler. A dare, after all, is a challenge issued to a power one does not believe in; the verse replies by naming the power — full of forgiveness, truly severe — and leaves the dare hanging in front of its addressee.

**Forgiveness Already in Circulation**

“Surely your Lord is full of forgiveness for people, despite their wrongdoing” (13:6). The clause is more radical than a promise of pardon; it describes forgiveness as an ongoing state — He is, right now, Possessed of Forgiveness, even while the wrongdoing is in progress. Forgiveness here is not a queue that opens after repentance; it is standing inventory, waiting to be drawn on. The same doctrine in direct address: “O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins” (39:53). Placed against the dare of the verse's first half, the clause exposes what the dare-makers have misread. They take the withheld torment as indifference or weakness. The verse re-frames the delay as forgiveness in motion — every day the sky does not fall is a day the account remains open, and the open account is mercy, not neglect. The people demanding the torment are living inside the answer to a prayer they never made: they are alive, unseized, and invited. The world, on this showing, is an interval of amnesty, and the demand to end the interval is an argument against the demanders — a demand that mercy stop being patient, when patience is precisely what mercy looks like while extended to the unrepentant.

## Verse 13:7

> The disbelievers say, “If only a sign could be sent down to him from his Lord.” You ˹O Prophet˺ are only a warner. And every people had a guide

The disbelievers' request sounds modest: one sign, sent down. But the sūrah has just spent three verses walking them through signs that never needed sending because they never left. The heavens are raised, the sun and moon run their courses, the earth is spread out, and neighbouring plots of land drink the same water. The same request is recorded elsewhere with the answer attached. They ask why no sign has been sent down to him from his Lord, and the answer is that God certainly has the power to send one, though most of them do not know (6:37 gives both parts).

**A Warner, Not a Performer**

The answer to the demand is a job description. You are only a warner (13:7 states it). The word "only" draws the boundary of the job with unusual bluntness. A warner delivers the warning. He does not schedule the disaster, force the audience, or stage a performance to entertain doubters into belief. The Book states the same limit at the end of its recital: keep reminding, because your duty is only to remind (88:21 states it), followed at once by the statement that you are not there to compel them (88:22 states it).

Two things follow. First, the demand for signs misunderstands what a messenger is for. A sign would make belief a piece of forced arithmetic, and the whole moral weight of answering a warning would disappear. Second, the warner's job keeps the Prophet ﷺ free of the anxiety that would otherwise consume him, the illusion that the outcome was his to force. He delivers, and the receiving is someone else's business. The answer also meets the unspoken charge that a true messenger would dazzle on demand. Dazzling was never in the contract.

**"Every People Has a Guide"**

The next words say that every people has a guide. The verb tense matters. The pattern is stated as settled fact: every community, without exception, has received its guide. The Book draws the administrative consequence elsewhere: every community has a messenger, and once the messenger has come, judgment is passed on them fairly, and they are not wronged (10:47 gives it). The guide's arrival is therefore not decoration but jurisdiction. Once the guide has come and spoken, the people's record is complete, and the judgment proceeds with perfect fairness.

For the Makkans the sentence lands with particular weight. Their guide is not a figure from a vanished nation. He is standing among them, reciting. The demand for a sign from his Lord comes from inside the very arrangement, guide sent and warning delivered, that the demanders claim is not enough. The pattern also explains the impatience about punishment in the verse before, where they asked for the evil to be brought on quickly (13:6 records it). The torment follows the guide's completed delivery, on time, and the schedule is not moved by heckling.

## Verse 13:8

> Allah knows what every female bears and what increases and decreases in the wombs. And with Him everything is determined with precision

**The Knowledge No Womb Owns**

“What increases and decreases in the wombs” (13:8) was read by the commentators as the varying length of gestation — the months that lengthen and shorten from pregnancy to pregnancy. Other readings widen the field: whether the child will be male or female, sound or otherwise, and what its provision and term will be. The parallel verse wraps all of it into one claim: “No female ever conceives or delivers without His knowledge. And no one’s life is made long or cut short but is ˹written˺ in a Record” (35:11) — and note how that verse brackets the womb between dust at one end and the recorded term at the other, making conception one station on a line drawn before either parent met. Even the plant world obeys the same disclosure schedule: “No fruit comes out of its husk, nor does a female conceive or deliver without His knowledge” (41:47). The pairing of fruit and fetus in one sentence is telling. The emerging bud and the emerging child belong to the same category of events: beginnings, hidden until they announce themselves, and never hidden from the One who scheduled them. The verse does not merely say God knows such things eventually; the knowledge is present at the moment when no microscope in the world could find the object known.

**Determined With Precision: The Vocabulary of Decree**

The closing clause — “And with Him everything is determined with precision” (13:8) — moves from God's knowledge to the settled measure behind it. The word for precision is the vocabulary of decree: the same root that says “Indeed, We have created everything, perfectly preordained” (54:49), and the same idea as “He has created everything, ordaining it precisely” (25:2). Everything, the text says — not only wombs but the whole inventory this sūrah has been walking through: orbits, mountains, rivers, fruits, the alternation of night and day. Each has its measure, and the measure is not approximate. This is where the verse quietly meets the deepest religious question the Makkans never asked and every generation since has asked: whether the world runs on chance, on fate, or on care. The verse answers with a third option that is neither of the pagan guesses — the world runs on measured decree, known and set by a knowing Lord. Chance is eliminated as an account of reality; the astrologer's fatalism is eliminated as an account of knowledge, because the measure is with Him, not with the stars.

**What the Knowledge Is For**

The verse's comfort and its warning are the same fact. To the anxious, it says nothing about your origin, your children, or your term is unmanaged; the knowledge that outruns yours was never going to misplace you. The famous verse of the five unknowns puts the boundary in place: “Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ has the knowledge of the Hour. He sends down the rain, and knows what is in the wombs. No soul knows what it will earn for tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die” (31:34). The precision clause also sets the terms for the verse that follows: because everything is measured with Him, His knowledge can be described without remainder as knowing the seen and the unseen (13:9).

## Verse 13:9

> ˹He is the˺ Knower of the seen and the unseen—the All-Great, Most Exalted

**Seen and Unseen as One Field**

The pairing covers everything without exception, and the Qur'an delights in showing the two extremes of the range in a single sentence. At the small end: “Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth” (6:59) — and that same verse opens the department completely: “With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him” (6:59). The leaf is the chosen example precisely because nothing could matter less; a knowledge that reaches one falling leaf cannot be pictured as the knowledge of important things only. At the large end stands the Hour itself, the unseen above all unseen. Between leaf and Hour, the verse of this sūrah asserts a single continuous knowledge with no departments left over — no province of the real where the knowledge is partial, inferred, or delegated. The seen is known without eyes; the unseen is known without intermediaries. This completeness that lets the next verse apply the attribute to the most private human acts, and it is this completeness that the idols could not survive.

**Exalted Above What They Associate**

The verse's closing pair — “the All-Great, Most Exalted” (13:9) — carries the argument in its adjective. A parallel text makes the connection explicit: “˹He is the˺ Knower of the seen and unseen. Exalted is He above what they associate ˹with Him˺” (23:92). The grammar of idolatry requires comparison: partners are only thinkable as comparable, near-rivals, department heads alongside a chairman. Total knowledge breaks the comparison at its root. What can be associated with a knowledge that has no outside? The pagan system worked, as far as it worked, by ignorance — gods who did not know what was in hearts could be flattered, bargained with, and cheated. The verse removes the operating condition. Exaltation here is not a mood word; it is the statement of an epistemic gap so total that the word "partner" loses its meaning on contact. This is why the attribute appears in 59:22 as the opening of the divine self-naming and why this sūrah places it immediately before its most uncomfortable verse about human hiding: the attribute is the reason the hiding will not work.

**The Verse as Hinge**

Placed where it is, the verse is the hinge between the sūrah's two halves of its opening movement. Behind it: the creation inventory (13:2–4) and the hidden things (13:8) — evidence that the world is conducted, not abandoned. Ahead of it: the application (13:10) — your whisper and your daylight walk, your midnight lurking and your morning traffic, all equally known — and then the guarded road of 13:11, where angels front and rear escort every soul by the command of the One who misses nothing. The hinge holds because the attribute is stated absolutely. If God knew only the seen, the angels would matter as informants; if He knew only the unseen, the seen would be ungoverned. Knowing both, He needs no spies and governs no exceptions — and the sūrah's audience, on both sides of belief, now knows exactly what kind of account they are keeping their lives in.

The verse's economy also explains its endurance in the daily prayer of believers, where the attribute is recited as confession rather than argument. A community that says the Knower of the seen and the unseen nightly is not proving a theorem; it is keeping the sūrah's arithmetic warm — the whispered act known (13:10), the guarded road escorted (13:11), the record kept (13:33) — all filed under one name, recited as both comfort and audit.

## Verse 13:10

> It is the same ˹to Him˺ whether any of you speaks secretly or openly, whether one hides in the darkness of night or goes about in broad daylight

**A Verse About Hiding Places**

The verse enumerates the complete human taxonomy of concealment: the whisper, the public declaration, the midnight lurker, the daylight walker. Four modes, and the verse rules them all identical — “It is the same ˹to Him˺” (13:10). The equality asserted is not moral but epistemic: the four acts differ in visibility to other people and do not differ at all in visibility to God. The verse is a direct application of the attribute just named. If He is the Knower of the seen and the unseen (13:9), then the secret/open and night/day distinctions — the load-bearing walls of all human privacy — mark no boundary in His knowledge. Notice the craft of the list: it includes not only deeds but speech, and within speech both the hidden and the broadcast, so that no hearer can locate himself outside the verse's scope. Whoever you are, one of the four lines describes your situation at this moment, and the verse says God's knowledge finds you exactly there, unchanged by the darkness or the crowd.

**For Self-Scrutiny, Not Just Surveillance**

The commentators pressed the verse's purpose beyond surveillance, and they were right to. A text that only meant "you are watched" would produce an audience of performers who behave when observed; this verse, read with the ones before it, aims at something inward — vigilance over one's own thoughts, and the self-audit of the heart. The supporting texts put the matter in the heart's own vocabulary: “Whether you speak secretly or openly—He surely knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart” (67:13). Again, “He certainly knows what is secret and what is even more hidden” (20:7) — the even-more-hidden being, as the commentators said, what a person has not yet formulated even to himself. The parallel verse keeps God's knowledge tied to His oneness: “He knows whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal, and knows whatever you do” (6:3). The design of the verse, then, is to make the heart its own witness. A person who has internalized that the whisper is already public in one direction — toward God — stops negotiating with an imaginary audience and starts asking what is actually in him. That shift is the beginning of the fear-and-hope economy that this sūrah will demand in its storm verses.

**The Idols Need Spies; God Does Not**

There is an argument folded into the verse, and it aims at the whole institution of intermediaries. A pantheon runs on information flow: the devotee must inform the god, attract its attention, alert it to needs and offenses — the entire apparatus of offering and incantation exists because the small gods are not assumed to know. The verse ends that economy in one clause. A God for whom whisper and proclamation are identical needs no informing, no alerting, no attracting. This is why the verse's equality — “the same ˹to Him˺” — is a denial of the idols' business model and not merely a statement about God. It also anticipates the sūrah's coming question to the idolaters (13:33): will you inform Him of something He does not know? The verse of the hiding places has already settled the answer. The whisper was never a whisper; the dark was never dark; and the god who needed to be told was never in the business the worshipper thought.

## Verse 13:11

> For each one there are successive angels before and behind, protecting them by Allah’s command. Indeed, Allah would never change a people’s state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺. And if it is Allah’s Will to torment a people, it can never be averted, nor can they find a protector other than Him

**The Guard Detail: A Life Under Escort**

“For each one there are successive angels before and behind, protecting them by Allah’s command” (13:11). The verse opens with the word for each one — no exemptions, no unwatched lives — and with the image of succession: angels taking post before and behind, relieving one another, so that the escort is continuous. The commentators tie the guard to the verse before it: the one who lurks by night and goes forth by day (13:10) is the one being escorted, and the lurker's escort applies precisely because the lurking was never hidden. What the angels do is specified: they protect, by the command of God — and the phrase by His command matters as much as the escort itself. The angels are not independent guardians with their own policies; they execute orders. The picture drawn is of a life conducted under supervision at every step, arrival and departure both covered. Comfort and warning travel in the same image: nothing reaches you unsearched, and nothing leaves you unrecorded.

**The Grammar of Responsibility**

The clause's grammar does the religious work. The change outside waits on a change inside — and the inside is named as the people's own act: “until they change their own state ˹of faith˺” (13:11). The parallel text confirms the reading: “This is because Allah would never discontinue His favour to a people until they discontinue their faith” (8:53). Two practical consequences follow, and they cut in opposite directions. Against fatalism: no people may attribute its condition wholly to heaven, since the verse assigns the first move to the people themselves. Against self-salvation: the change within is only ever a change *awaiting* God's act — the verse does not say people change their state, but that God does not change it until they change what is in themselves, keeping cause and effect on both sides of the clause. The sentence therefore leaves no room for the two easiest postures — despair, which says nothing can move, and arrogance, which says we moved it ourselves. It installs instead a discipline: look inward first, act, and await the alteration as from God.

**The Unrepellable Sentence**

“If it is Allah’s Will to torment a people, it can never be averted, nor can they find a protector other than Him” (13:11). The second half of the verse is the first half's dark twin, and the two halves together state one doctrine: change is possible while the term is running, and impossible once the sentence is served. The verse so answers, in advance, the two great abuses of the teaching about decree. The demander of hastened torment (13:6) is told the sentence when it comes is unrepellable — his heckling moves nothing. And the complacent are told that the unrepellable sentence has a condition they still control before it falls. Note also what the clause does to the protector-economy of paganism: nor can they find a protector other than Him — the idols are shut out of the one transaction that matters. The coming verse will show the same order in the sky: the thunderbolt strikes whom He wills (13:13), and no purchased charm stands in its path.

**Neither Fatalism nor Autonomy**

Read as a single sentence, the verse draws the day's whole itinerary. The soul sets out escorted, arrives at every fork holding the lever, and stands finally before the one sentence that cannot be appealed — three stations, one road, and the walking done at the middle station only. Its placement between the hiding places (13:10) and the storm (13:12) gives it the chapter's hinge position: knowledge finds you, the road offers you your one move, and the weather ahead will preach the rest.

## Verse 13:12

> He is the One Who shows you lightning, inspiring ˹you with˺ hope and fear, and produces heavy clouds

**The Storm Verse Arrives With the Weather**

After four verses on knowledge, hiding, and guarded roads, the sūrah turns to the sky's drama: “He is the One Who shows you lightning” (13:12). The verb is chosen with care — God *shows*, personally and directly, the way a host presents a marvel. The audience is addressed in the second person because every one of them has stood in the storm this verse describes; the evidence needs no reconstruction, only recall. The verse also restores the word *He* to the front of the sentence — He is the One Who — reasserting, at the threshold of the weather, the singularity that the idols' departmental universe denied. In the pagan division of labor, some lesser power owned the storm; the verse hands the entire event — flash, cloud, and what follows in 13:13, the thunder and the bolt — to the same One who raised the heavens in 13:2 and reads the wombs in 13:8. The storm is not a rival jurisdiction. It is the Lord's own exhibition, and its peculiar instrument is a mixed emotion.

**The Twin Engines Readied for What Follows**

The verse is the first of a triple. It shows the lightning; 13:13 will voice the thunder and drop the bolt; and the storm as a whole will end in the prostration of everything that has a shadow (13:15). This first verse does the preparatory work: it installs in the hearer the two dispositions — hope and fear — that the thunder and bolt will exercise. The order is pedagogy. A heart taught by the storm holds both truths without collapsing into either: the sky that threatens is the sky that waters, and the God who strikes is the God who gives. What the pagan bargains away — fear, by flattering the god, or hope, by despising him — the believer is trained to carry whole. The storm, on this reading, is not an interruption of the world's order but one of its clearest lessons: the same flash that could kill the reaper ripens his field. Only a Lord of both life and death could design a sign that way, and only such a Lord is worth the hope and fear He built into you to feel.

The verse's singular grammar deserves the final word, because the storm was precisely where the pagan system claimed a specialist. A god of clouds would be invoked before travel; a god of lightning would be bribed after near-misses; the whole apparatus of weather-divination exists to manage specialists. The storm's management is by this removed from the shrine and returned to the same hand that raises the heavens (13:2), reads the wombs (13:8), and escorts every walker (13:11).

The parallel text states the double signal exactly: “And one of His signs is that He shows you lightning, inspiring ˹you with˺ hope and fear. And He sends down rain from the sky, reviving the earth after its death” (30:24).

## Verse 13:13

> The thunder glorifies His praises, as do the angels in awe of Him. He sends thunderbolts, striking with them whoever He wills. Yet they dispute about Allah. And He is tremendous in might

**The Storm's Hidden Liturgy**

The verse hands the storm a different soundtrack than the one human ears assume. What the audience heard as menace — the rolling boom over the hills of Makkah — the verse identifies as praise: “The thunder glorifies His praises” (13:13). A tradition cited by the commentators goes further and identifies thunder as the name of the angel charged with the clouds, so that the sound itself is a voice at worship, the hymn of a servant mid-task. Either way the teaching is the same: the loudest object in the sky is not an omen but a worshipper, and the storm's menace is one layer over a liturgy. The Qur'an generalizes the claim beyond the storm: “The seven heavens, the earth, and all those in them glorify Him. There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot understand their glorification” (17:44). The limitation is stated honestly — you cannot understand it — and the verse of the birds makes the same claim audible in principle: “Do you not see that Allah is glorified by all those in the heavens and the earth, even the birds as they soar?” (24:41). The thunder verse is this doctrine's loudest exhibit, chosen from the one phenomenon every hearer had heard and feared.

**Fear and Hope Resolved Into Awe**

The previous verse installed two dispositions in the storm — hope and fear (13:12) — and this verse names the third station toward which both tend: awe, the angels' state, “in awe of Him” (13:13). The sequence is precise. Lightning excites hope of rain and fear of the strike; the thunder then discloses what the fear was for — not cowering before a blind force, but the reverence of those who know what the force is: a commanded instrument of the One being praised. The angels stand in the verse as the model audience of the storm. Where humans hear noise, they hear the voice of a fellow servant magnifying the Lord; their awe is the correct translation of the same event. This is why the sūrah's storm verses must be read together: the flash teaches the mixed heart (hope and fear), and the thunder teaches the resolved heart (awe) — the two dispositions of 13:12 finding their single object.

**Might in the Lexicon, and the Prophet's ﷺ Response to Thunder**

“And He is tremendous in might” (13:13). The word translated as might here opened a small interpretive door: it derives from the root of plotting and stratagem, so the phrase was read as “intense in planning” — the same root this sūrah will turn against the plotters at 13:42. The linguistic note changes the devotional response: the might behind the storm is not temper but plan, which means the correct prayer in a storm is not appeasement but seeking shelter in the Planner. That is exactly how the Prophet ﷺ responded. It is recorded that upon hearing thunder he would say: “O Allah, do not kill us with Your anger, and do not destroy us with Your punishment, and grant us well-being before that” (recorded by Aḥmad in his Musnad). His cousin's son ʿAbdullāh ibn al-Zubayr would stop speaking at the thunder's sound and supplicate: “Glory be to Him whom the thunder glorifies with His praise, and so do the angels out of awe of Him,” collected by Mālik in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and by al-Bukhārī in al-Adab al-Mufrad. Both responses are the verse internalized: one reads the storm as warning and asks for well-being; the other joins the liturgy it has just been told is happening overhead. The verse, in short, converts the most feared sound in the sky into the loudest reminder of the One whose plan the sūrah's skeptics are so casually challenging.

The Qur'an diagnoses such dispute elsewhere in one triple: “˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, guidance, or an enlightening scripture” (22:8).

## Verse 13:14

> Calling upon Him ˹alone˺ is the truth. But those ˹idols˺ the pagans invoke besides Him ˹can˺ never respond to them in any way. ˹It is˺ just like someone who stretches out their hands to water, ˹asking it˺ to reach their mouths, but it can never do so. The calls of the disbelievers are only in vain

**The Deities That Cannot Hear**

The negation is total: those invoked besides Him “˹can˺ never respond to them in any way” (13:14). The parallel text does the demolition in three steps: “If you call upon them, they cannot hear your calls. And if they were to hear, they could not respond to you. On the Day of Judgment they will disown your worship ˹of them˺” (35:14). Cannot hear; could not answer; will disown — the three steps remove the idols from every role the worshipper assigned them: recipient, agent, and even patron. And their poverty is a matter of inventory: “But those ˹idols˺ you ˹pagans˺ invoke besides Him do not possess even the skin of a date stone” (35:13). The skin of a date stone is the Qur'an's deliberately tiny unit of property — the shaving a child peels and discards — and the gods of the pantheon do not have one. The irony has a historical bite, since the Makkans' own rites centered on offerings, including dates, laid before idols; the verse audits the gods and finds their estate smaller than the offerings. A religious system whose deities cannot hear, cannot act, and will deny their worshippers at the audit is not a rival theology. It is an empty office with a queue outside.

**Why the Test of Prayer Is Decisive**

The verse quietly installs supplication as the practical test of godhood. A lord who cannot respond is not a lesser lord but a category error, and prayer is the experiment that reveals the error. This is why the verse follows the storm verses so naturally: the pagan, watching the lightning of 13:12–13, is the man stretching hands to water — he reads the storm as the mood of some local power and begins bargaining with a force that has never once answered anyone. The believer reads the same storm as the work of the One who said “Call upon Me, I will respond to you” (40:60) and asks — and the ask is not wasted, because the address is real. The verse so prepares the courtroom of the next two verses: the prostration of everything that has being (13:15), and the interrogation that ends with the Makkans' own confession (13:16). The order of the sūrah's argument is now complete in outline: conduct the test, watch everything fall before the One, and then make the idolater answer the only question his own tongue can answer correctly.

The pagans did not pray to nothingness outright; they prayed to things — stones, names, ancestral customs — and the verse's water is chosen because it, too, is something: real, near, even exalted in its origin as rain from the same sky the thunder praises (13:13).

## Verse 13:15

> To Allah ˹alone˺ bow down ˹in submission˺ all those in the heavens and the earth—willingly or unwillingly—as do their shadows, morning and evening

**Shadows: The Anatomy of Compelled Worship**

“As do their shadows, morning and evening” (13:15). The shadow is the verse's masterstroke, because a shadow is the most obedient thing in creation: it never votes, never negotiates, never breaks rank. Where the light is, it lies; where the light moves, it moves; and it performs this liturgy twice daily on schedule — morning and evening — exactly as the verse says. The parallel text turns the shadow into an argument addressed to the observing: “Have they not considered how the shadows of everything Allah has created incline to the right and the left ˹as the sun moves˺, totally submitting to Allah in all humility?” (16:48). Another verse slows the film: “Have you not seen how your Lord extends the shade—He could have simply made it ˹remain˺ still if He so willed—then We make the sun its guide” (25:45), with the retreat described as gradual (25:46). The shadow is so a worship whose choreography is God's and whose mechanism is the sun's appointed course — the same subjection announced at 13:2. Every wall at mid-afternoon is displaying a prostration that never skips, and the verse asks its audience to notice what they have been walking through all their lives.

**Willingly and Unwillingly: The Two Kneelings**

“Willingly or unwillingly” (13:15) divides the universe's worship into the verse's two classes, and the Qur'an supplies membership lists for both. The willing include the angels — “And to Allah ˹alone˺ bows down ˹in submission˺ whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth of living creatures, as do the angels—who are not too proud ˹to do so˺” (16:49) — whose submission is conscious, chosen, and prompt. The unwilling include the shadows, the storms, the atoms — and, uncomfortably, “many humans, while many are deserving of punishment” (22:18). That last clause from the prostration verse of Sūrah al-Ḥajj is the hinge: human beings are the one creature who can relocate between the classes, and the sentence of the punished is not that they refused to bow but that they insisted on bowing in the wrong mode until the mode hardened. The general law is stated with terrible simplicity: “There is none in the heavens or the earth who will not return to the Most Compassionate in full submission” (19:93). Return, in full submission, is not on the table as an option; only its timing and manner are. The verse of the shadows makes every sunset a rehearsal of that law — the light withdraws, the shadow yields, and the creature who watched it happen is asked which class he is enrolled in.

**The Question the Verse Ends On**

The translation closes the verse with a dare compressed into two alternatives: “Almighty Allah or Powerless Gods?” (13:15). On the face of the recited text, the prostration of everything has just been witnessed; the question then invites the audience to name the power that could compete with what they have seen. It is the same tactic the next verse will formalize as interrogation — ask them. Let their own answer convict them — and the same tactic the Qur'an aims at the sun-worshipper and the idolater alike through the day-night thought experiment: if the night were made perpetual, “which god other than Allah could bring you sunlight? Will you not then listen?” (28:71), and if the day were perpetual, “which god other than Allah could bring you night to rest in?” (28:72). The rhetorical design is consistent throughout: the rival's name is never refuted at length because the rival's name is never supplied.

The prostration in question is not the ritual posture alone but existential submission, the creature's inability to exit its dependence. The Qur'an states the same doctrine as an appeal: “Do they desire a way other than Allah’s—knowing that all those in the heavens and the earth submit to His Will, willingly or unwillingly, and to Him they will ˹all˺ be returned?” (3:83).

## Verse 13:16

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth?” Say, “Allah!” Ask ˹them˺, “Why ˹then˺ have you taken besides Him lords who cannot even benefit or protect themselves?” Say, “Can the blind and the sighted be equal? Or can darkness and light be equal?” Or have they associated with Allah partners who ˹supposedly˺ produced a creation like His, leaving them confused between the two creations? Say, “Allah is the Creator of all things, and He is the One, the Supreme.”

**The Answer They Already Carry**

“Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth?” Say, “Allah!” (13:16). The commentators observed the striking fact that the answer is supplied by the verse itself: the questioners cannot give any other, because a number of them already ranked Allāh atop their pantheon as the high god. The Qur'an exploits that latent confession repeatedly: “If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon ˹for your benefit˺, they will certainly say, “Allah!”” (29:61). “And if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will definitely say, “Allah!”” (31:25); “If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created them, they will certainly say, “Allah!”” (43:87). Three parallel verses, three certain confessions. The argument's force comes from what the confession concedes: creation, lordship over the heavens, the subjection of the sun and moon — the entire portfolio this sūrah has been cataloguing since 13:2. The pagans are so not atheists with bad theology; they are witnesses with testimony, and the verse simply administers the oath. What remains is to confront them with the consequences of their own answer: if Allah is the Lord, the others are unemployed — and the second question fires exactly there.

**The Confusion Between Two Creations**

“Or have they associated with Allah partners who ˹supposedly˺ produced a creation like His, leaving them confused between the two creations?” (13:16). The fourth question finally names the pagans' actual claim — that their partners share in making — and diagnoses it as confusion: two creations set side by side in the mind until the mind cannot tell which is which. The refutation is the teaching about origination: “˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and the earth! When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, “Be!” And it is!” (2:117). Nothing in the record resembles a second “Be.” The rival creators have no portfolio: no orbit appointed, no womb read, no mountain set, no rain sent — the whole inventory of 13:2–4 is single-signed. The pagans' confusion, on the verse's showing, is not an intellectual disability but a maintenance problem: they keep two ledgers, one for the Creator they confess and one for the lords they court, and the verse demands they reconcile the accounts. Reconciliation has only one honest outcome, which is why the verse supplies it: “Allah is the Creator of all things, and He is the One, the Supreme” (13:16). Creator of all — the idols included, it might be said, made of the very wood their worshippers cut — One, and Supreme: the final answer that folds all four questions shut.

The verse's closing titles — the One, the Supreme — also hand the sūrah its headline for what remains: everything after this verse sorts by whether the titles were accepted. The thunder accepts them (13:13), the water sorts by them (13:17), the witnessing Books confirm them (13:43), and the two galleries of 13:19–25 are simply the biography of the two answers.

The Qur'an can render the darkness of disbelief with terrible physicality: “Or ˹their deeds are˺ like the darkness in a deep sea, covered by waves upon waves, topped by ˹dark˺ clouds. Darkness upon darkness! If one stretches out their hand, they can hardly see it” (24:40) — a darkness so total the outstretched hand disappears, and the verse of hiding places (13:10) has already told us what such a hand hopes to do.

## Verse 13:17

> He sends down rain from the sky, causing the valleys to flow, each according to its capacity. The currents then carry along rising foam, similar to the slag produced from metal that people melt in the fire for ornaments or tools. This is how Allah compares truth to falsehood. The ˹worthless˺ residue is then cast away, but what benefits people remains on the earth. This is how Allah sets forth parables

**Rain That Sorts a Landscape**

“He sends down rain from the sky, causing the valleys to flow, each according to its capacity” (13:17). The parable begins with an act the previous verses have prepared us to recognize: sending down, the signature verb of the Sustainer who has been managing sky and soil since 13:2. The rain is one; the valleys are many, and each receives by its capacity — the broad wadi runs brown and full, the narrow channel gurgles and is done. With the flow comes sorting: the current churns the ground and lifts a froth of dirt and impurity, which rides the foam until the water settles and the scum dries on the banks. The commentators read the verse as delivering two similitudes, and the second is announced in its middle: the froth of the flood is “similar to the slag produced from metal that people melt in the fire for ornaments or tools” (13:17). In the forge, exactly as in the wadi, the heating raises impurities to the surface, where they are skimmed and discarded, while the clean metal underneath becomes the ornament and the tool. One sorting happens by water, one by fire; both separate the same way — foam up, value down.

**The Map Drawn Over Both Similitudes**

“This is how Allah compares truth to falsehood” (13:17). The application is explicit, which is rare and welcome: revelation is the descending water, human hearts and societies are the valleys of differing capacity, and the commotion that truth's arrival produces — the froth of objection, slander, fashion, and noise — is slag, not substance. The verse's economics are unforgiving in one direction only: “The ˹worthless˺ residue is then cast away, but what benefits people remains on the earth” (13:17). Falsehood's fate is not punishment imposed from outside but uselessness discovered: no one keeps scum. Truth's persistence, likewise, is not stubbornness but benefit — it remains because it is the water itself, the drink and the irrigation and the life. The same verdict is pronounced in the chapter of the Night Journey as a proclamation: “The truth has come and falsehood has vanished. Indeed, falsehood is bound to vanish” (17:81). And the Qur'an's other great parable of persistence makes the same point vegetatively: “Do you not see how Allah compares a good word to a good tree? Its root is firm and its branches reach the sky” (14:24) — root and sky, the two directions of what stays.

**The Prophet's ﷺ Rain: One Water, Three Receptions**

The parable's structure — one rain, different capacities — was used by the Prophet ﷺ for revelation itself. He said: “The example of guidance and knowledge with which Allah has sent me is like abundant rain falling on the earth” — and he described fertile ground that sprouted, hard ground that held the water so people and cattle could drink. Barren ground that neither held water nor grew (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 79). The hadith is this verse in narrative form, with one decisive addition: the capacities are moral. The fertile soil is the person who understands the religion and teaches it; the hard soil is the one who at least preserves the knowledge for others; the barren soil is the one who turns away from it completely. Same sky, same water, three receptions — and the difference, in both the verse and the hadith, is located in the ground, not the rain. That addition is also the verse's consolation: the froth that forms when revelation descends on a generation is no evidence against the revelation. The water was water; the wadi was shallow.

## Verse 13:18
> Those who respond to ˹the call of˺ their Lord will have the finest reward. As for those who do not respond to Him, even if they were to possess everything in the world twice over, they would certainly offer it to ransom themselves. They will face strict judgment, and Hell will be their home. What an evil place to rest

**Two Answers, Two Outcomes**

The verse sets two groups side by side. Those who respond to their Lord have the finest reward waiting for them. Those who do not respond are described by what they would give if they could: everything on the earth, and twice as much again, offered as ransom to buy themselves out (13:18 states both).

The rest of the Book prices the same bid and reaches the same refusal. It says that if a person who rejects the faith owned gold enough to fill the whole earth, it would never be accepted from him (3:91 states it). It says that even if the wrongdoers owned everything on earth twice over and offered it to ransom themselves from the punishment of the day of judgment, it would not be accepted (5:36 states it). And it says they will wish they could offer such a ransom when they see what God has prepared, because they will see from God what they never expected at all (39:47 states it).

So the doubled earth in this verse is the most expensive useless object in the Book. It is the price that cannot buy the thing being sold.

**A Strict Reckoning and a Bad Home**

The verse then names two things about what follows for them: a strict reckoning, and a home in Hell, which it calls an evil place to rest.

The reckoning is strict in the sense of being exact, with every entry settled. And the chapter has already explained why nothing will be missing from the account: the road is guarded, and escorts record the comings and goings of every person (13:11 describes the guards and the record).

The second word is the more cutting one. The verse calls Hell an evil resting place, and that word is the language of an inn or a place where a traveller stops for the night. It takes the one thing every human being looks for at the end of a day and tells the reader that they will find it there forever and never have it. The word used for their home is the same word the chapter already used for the people of the fire earlier in the sūrah (13:5 describes them).

And the chapter has already closed the door of help: the people in this position will find no protector other than God (13:11 states it). The patronage they trusted in has no branch in that place, and the idols they called on were answered earlier in the sūrah (13:14 names the prayer that has one address), and the confession that they can neither harm nor benefit was drawn out of them (13:16 gives it).

**Where the Verse Stands in the Sūrah**

This verse is the hinge of the sūrah. Everything before it was argument, and everything after it is description of two kinds of lives.

The first seventeen verses built the case. Revelation is the truth (13:1 states it). The denial is absurdity (13:5 shows it). The demand for punishment to come quickly is a dare rather than a question. The knowledge of God is total (13:11 states it before doing so in detail). Everything in the heavens and the earth submits to Him (13:15 states it). And truth separates itself from worthless froth the way a torrent carries scum on its surface while the water that matters runs clear (13:17 gives the image).

Then this verse makes the case personal. Respond, and the finest reward is yours. Refuse, and the doubled earth is offered for a soul that cannot be bought back.

And the verses that follow are simply the long description of what each answer looks like when it becomes a life: the one who knows and the one who is blind (13:19 draws the contrast), the people who keep their agreements, the people who are patient and answer evil with good (13:22 describes the second), and the gardens entered from every gate (13:23 begins that scene). The people who break their agreements are the biography of the other answer. So the sūrah's remaining work is to show what each of the two words produces, and this verse is where it turns.

The reward of the first group is described with deliberate richness. The Book says that those who do good will have the finest reward and even more than that, and that neither gloom nor disgrace will cover their faces (10:26 states both).

## Verse 13:19

> Can the one who knows that your Lord’s revelation to you ˹O Prophet˺ is the truth be like the one who is blind? None will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason

**The Question Divides the Audience in Two**

After seventeen verses of evidence, the sūrah stops presenting and starts asking: “Can the one who knows that your Lord’s revelation to you ˹O Prophet˺ is the truth be like the one who is blind?” (13:19). The question is rhetorical, and its answer is the sorting the whole chapter has been building toward. The parable of the rain (13:17) left the audience watching water separate from foam; this verse names the two containers the sorting was running through. On one side stands a person who *knows* — not suspects, not hopes, but knows — that what came down to the Prophet ﷺ is truth. On the other stands a person the Qur'an elsewhere measures in exactly these terms: “Those blind ˹to the truth˺ and those who can see are not equal,” (35:19). The comparison is not an insult but a diagnosis. Blindness here, as the commentators were careful to say, is blindness of the heart — the failure to “see” what stands in plain sight — and the sixteen verses of signs behind this verse are precisely the things standing in plain sight. The question so closes the case that 13:1 opened: the truth is known; the only remaining variable is the eye.

**The Third Audience Label: People of Reason**

“None will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason” (13:19). The sūrah has been signing its inventory verses with audience labels: “signs for those who reflect” (13:3), “signs for those who understand” (13:4), and now “people of reason” — the word is the Qur'an's for insight, the eye of the mind rather than of the face. The same formula closes the battle-sign verse: “Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight” (3:13). The ladder of labels is itself a teaching: reflection (13:3) is thought working on evidence; understanding (13:4) is the grasp of what the evidence means; reason (13:19) is the faculty that carries the verdict into conduct. The verse's closing formula is therefore not a compliment to the intelligent but a definition of intelligence — and a warning to cleverness without it, since a man can be brilliant with his face-eyes and blind with the other pair. The Qur'an consistently treats that second blindness as the greater disability, because it is chosen: the mirage-watcher chose his thirst; the heart-blind chose his refusal.

**The Verse Heads a Portrait Gallery**

Placement finishes the verse's meaning. “People of reason” is not left as a category; the next three verses paint them — those who honour the covenant (13:20), join what God commanded joined (13:21), endure, pray, spend, and answer evil with good (13:22) — until the portrait walks into Gardens entered from every gate (13:23–24). The structure is the sūrah's answer to an old misconception: that reason's work ends at the verdict. Here the verdict produces a biography. The one who knows the revelation is truth is described not by his opinions but by his kept promises, his joined ties, his secret donations, and his answered insults — which is to say that in the Qur'an's accounting, seeing is a way of living. The blind man, correspondingly, will be described at 13:25 by his broken covenant and severed ties: the same biography, inverted. One verse of comparison (13:19) so opens the gallery, and the gallery is the comparison.

The Prophet ﷺ described his own position in the debate with the sign-demanders in exactly these terms: “I only follow what is revealed to me” (6:50), and he pressed the same comparison on his audience there: “Are those blind ˹to the truth˺ equal to those who can see?” (6:50).

The Qur'an supplies a portrait of the blind man's sight, and it is one of its bleakest images: “As for the disbelievers, their deeds are like a mirage in a desert, which the thirsty perceive as water, but when they approach it, they find it to be nothing” (24:39).

## Verse 13:20

> ˹They are˺ those who honour Allah’s covenant, never breaking the pledge

**The Portrait Gallery Opens With a Doorkeeper's Word**

“˹They are˺ those who honour Allah’s covenant, never breaking the pledge” (13:20). The relative pronoun arrives like a door opening onto the people of reason whom 13:19 promised — and the first thing visible inside is not a doctrine but a practice: keeping an agreement. The sūrah, having spent eighteen verses on evidence, now describes the knower in the vocabulary of obligation. The choice is deliberate and defines the Qur'anic believer from the inside out: before prayer is listed, before charity, before patience, the believer is a creature that keeps its word to God. The clause also carries a quiet universality. A covenant is not a sect's password; it is the structure of the Creator-creature relationship as such — terms stated, acceptance given, faithfulness owed. Honouring it is the elementary act of piety, and the verse places it first because everything else in the portrait (the joined ties of 13:21, the patience of 13:22) is a branch growing from that one kept trunk.

**Never Breaking: The Standard Hidden in the Word**

The negation is absolute, and it is the verse's hardest word. Not "rarely breaking," not "breaking but repairing" — the portrait's standard is a promise kept whole. The Qur'an knows what happens to standards softened into percentages: they produce a piety of exceptions, a ledger kept by the debtor. The standard here is the mirror of the covenant's own Giver, whose promise-keeping is without breach — the sun's appointment (13:2) has never lapsed, the wombs' ledger (13:8) has never misplaced an entry. To honor such a covenant is to imitate, at creaturely scale, the fidelity of its Author. And the absoluteness has a pastoral edge: the believer who has broken the pledge is not told the portrait excludes him forever; he is told what the portrait looks like, so that repentance — the covenant's own repair clause — is exercised before the repair is needed at the audit. The verse describes the destination, not the excuse.

**A Covenant the Cosmos Already Keeps**

The verse's demand reads differently once the sūrah's own testimony is remembered: the covenant-keeper joins a creation that keeps its covenants. The heavens hold their appointment (13:2); the night covers the day on schedule (13:3); the thunder keeps its post and its praise (13:13); the shadow keeps its prostration (13:15). Nothing in the inventory of these verses has broken terms with its Maker. The human being is the one creature holding a covenant he is able to violate — and that capacity is not a flaw in the arrangement but its honor: obedience that cannot be withheld is not kept, only enforced. The shadow's fidelity, in other words, is worth less than the believer's, precisely because the shadow has no alternative.

**A Covenant With Terms the Body Already Recites**

What the covenant is made of deserves a look, because the verse's plainness hides its width. The pledge binds acknowledgment — “Yes, You are! We testify” (7:172) — but a testimony is lived, not merely voiced, and the portrait that follows will show the pledge's fine print: joining the ties God joined (13:21), standing in awe (13:21), patience and prayer and secret spending (13:22). The covenant, in other words, is not one ceremony but a standing state — the interior posture of 13:11 ("what is in themselves") kept in repair. This is also why the verse needs no list of stations: the whole sūrah since 13:1 has been the stations, delivered as signs. The audience that watched the thunder praise its Lord (13:13), the shadows prostrate (13:15), and the water sort truth from froth (13:17) has already read the covenant's first chapter. The verse now asks one question about the reader: he has read it — will he keep it?

## Verse 13:21

> and those who maintain whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, stand in awe of their Lord, and fear strict judgment

**Kinship as Theology: The Womb That Carries Mercy**

The Arabic word for kinship ties descends from the same root as the word for mercy — the womb is, in the language itself, a mercy — and the Qur'an treats the connection as doctrine rather than accident. Severing the ties is listed in the Qur'an among the signatures of the corrupter: “Now if you ˹hypocrites˺ turn away, perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ˹ties of˺ kinship!” (47:22). Corruption and severed kinship appear there as the two hands of one work — which is why the covenant-breakers of this sūrah, at 13:25, will be described in exactly that pair: spreading corruption in the land, breaking the ties God ordered joined. The linguistic note and the structural echo say the same thing: the family bond is not a social courtesy appended to religion; it is mercy localized in flesh, and its maintenance is worship. The believer who keeps his kin is performing, in miniature, the attribute of the Lord who keeps His covenant (13:20) — the joiner imitating the Joiner.

**Awe and the Dread of a Strict Reckoning**

“Stand in awe of their Lord, and fear strict judgment” (13:21). The two fears close the verse like a bookend, and their placement after the command to join is precise. Kindness to kin is the easiest virtue to corrupt — into duty performed for reputation, gifts given for leverage, generosity practised as investment. The verse installs the audit inside the kindness: the tie-keeper stands in awe of his Lord, and dreads the strict reckoning — the same exactness of account the sūrah named at 13:18 (“They will face strict judgment”), where nothing unrecorded survives. The fear is not the fear of kin but of the Judge who watches how the kin are treated; it keeps the joining honest when no relative is looking. This is also why the fear of judgment and the love of ties are not competing motives in the Qur'an's portrait. They are the same motive at two temperatures: the dread of severing what God joined, and the awe of the One who joined it — the emotional grammar, once more, of hope and fear holding a single object.

**Why Ties Belong in a Godward Portrait**

The deepest claim of the verse is structural: maintaining ties appears *inside* the description of the people who know the revelation is truth (13:19). In the Qur'an's architecture, the vertical covenant and the horizontal bonds are one fabric — tear the horizontal and the vertical frays with it. This is why the portrait will not pass from the covenant (13:20) to prayer (13:22) without stopping at the relatives in between: the stop itself is the doctrine. A faith that keeps God's covenant while cutting God's creatures has, in the verse's logic, already misunderstood the covenant — since the covenant's terms include them. The verse so prepares the believer for the portrait's next panels, where patience, prayer, and spending secretly and openly (13:22) will fill out the interior life: the household was the training ground where all three were first exercised. The person who has learned to hold the nearest ropes without profit and without applause has already learned the two disciplines the next verse will command — giving without being seen, and enduring without being thanked.

Humanity was created through connection — “through both He spread countless men and women” (4:1) — and the same verse commands the honouring of family ties because spread and binding are one act of God.

## Verse 13:22

> And ˹they are˺ those who endure patiently, seeking their Lord’s pleasure, establish prayer, donate from what We have provided for them—secretly and openly—and respond to evil with good. It is they who will have the ultimate abode

**Patience Aimed at a Face**

“Endure patiently, seeking their Lord’s pleasure” (13:22). Patience is the first habit, and the pairing with the motive clause transforms it. Patience as grim endurance is available to anyone with no alternative; patience aimed at a Face is worship — the deliberate holding of a position because its Holder is watching. The Qur'an repeatedly yokes patience to prayer as the believer's two crutches, and the yoking appears here in the verse's own order: endurance flows directly into “establish prayer” (13:22), the daily appointment where the patience is renewed. For the sūrah's first audience, the patience had a specific face: the mockery of 13:5, the sign-demands of 13:7, the hastening-dares of 13:6 — a decade of heckling before the promise was vindicated. The verse does not tell the mockers' victims to answer in kind or to withdraw. It files their endurance under the same heading as their prayer: acts done for the pleasure of a Lord who, as this sūrah has established verse by verse, misses nothing that is done secretly and nothing that is done openly.

**The Gift That Is Safe From Both Extremes**

“Donate from what We have provided for them—secretly and openly” (13:22). First, the provision is acknowledged in the verb: what We have provided — the giver is a steward distributing another's goods, which is the verse-level consequence of 13:4's teaching that the very differences in harvest were authored by God. Second, the giving is prescribed in both modes — secretly and openly — because each mode has its own disease. Public giving can rot into display; secret giving can rot into its own quiet vanity. The scope of the provision is the whole sūrah's testimony: rain, rivers, fruits, paired harvests (13:3–4) — the believer gives from an inventory he watched God assemble.

**Responding to Evil With Good: The Hardest Habit**

“And respond to evil with good” (13:22). The clause is the portrait's summit, and the Qur'an elsewhere measures its altitude: “Good and evil cannot be equal. Respond ˹to evil˺ with what is best, then the one you are in a feud with will be like a close friend” (41:34) — followed immediately by the fine print: “But this cannot be attained except by those who are patient and who are truly fortunate” (41:35). The patience of this verse is so the price of that alchemy: only an endured insult can be answered with gift. The classical reading of the command was practical — repel the evil by doing good, a strategy rather than a mood — and its promised effect is enmity's disappearance into friendship (41:34). Placed in this sūrah, the habit has a religious root: the believer who answers evil with good is imitating the order of the verse of 13:6, where forgiveness is listed before severity, mercy running ahead of the account. The responding believer extends to men the priority God extends to servants.

**It Is They Who Will Have the Ultimate Abode**

“It is they who will have the ultimate abode” (13:22). The verse ends mid-promise, and the next verse arrives to complete it — the Gardens of Eternity, entered with the righteous of one's own family, greeted by angels at every gate (13:23–24). The incomplete sentence is itself eloquent: the reward is named here as an abode — the vocabulary of arrival — and its contents are held over, because the portrait's climax deserves its own verse. The next verse will open the Gardens' gates and show the abode's first minutes — and the opening word of those minutes, as the translation of 13:24 records, is peace.

The Qur'an elsewhere weighs the two registers and pronounces on both: “To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you of your sins. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do” (2:271).

## Verse 13:23

> the Gardens of Eternity, which they will enter along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants. And the angels will enter upon them from every gate, ˹saying,˺

**The Sentence Completes: Abode Into Gardens**

“The Gardens of Eternity” (13:23) — the verse finishes the sentence the previous one left hanging, converting the promised abode into an address. The name carries both its gifts: gardens, the word for the walled, cultivated, watered enclosure that the Qur'an uses for Paradise throughout. And eternity, the term that answers the sūrah's earlier residency language — the residents of the Fire (13:5) here meet the residents of the Gardens, two addresses, both permanent, both chosen on earth. The commentators noted that the Gardens are the content of the “ultimate abode” of 13:22 — the reward the portrait earned. What deserves attention first is the entry's social shape: “which they will enter along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants” (13:23). The believer does not arrive alone. The verse names the three circles of intimacy — the generation above, the partner beside, the generation below — and admits the righteous of each. The reward is engineered for togetherness.

**Families Rejoined: The Justice of Togetherness**

The promise has a deliberate theology: it answers the joining commanded at 13:21. Another verse sets the promise's fine detail: “As for those who believe and whose descendants follow them in faith, We will elevate their descendants to their rank, never discounting anything ˹of the reward˺ of their deeds” (52:21) — a parent's station extended downward to a child without the child's own record being diminished, a mechanism of pure mercy. And the angels are heard praying for exactly this outcome: “Our Lord! Admit them into the Gardens of Eternity which You have promised them, along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants” (40:8). Togetherness, in the Qur'an's Paradise, is not the reward's garnish; it is half the reward, prepared for the people who treated ties as trusts (13:21) while the ties could still be cut.

**Every Gate, One Word**

The verse ends on the hinge word — “˹saying,˺” (13:23) — and the next verse supplies the speech: “Peace be upon you for your perseverance” (13:24). The entire apparatus of Gardens, families, gates, and angels converges on one word. The greeting that awaits is the greeting the believer practised: the salām offered in this world's meetings is revealed as rehearsal for the only speech Paradise opens with. The parallel promise states the pattern plainly: “Those who believe and do good will be admitted into Gardens, under which rivers flow—to stay there forever by the Will of their Lord—where they will be greeted with “Peace!”” (14:23). Greeted — the verb of a household receiving a guest, the same word the Book uses for how the people of Paradise meet one another. The sūrah's portrait so closes where its storm verses began: the thunder was already praising (13:13), the shadows already bowing (13:15); the righteous, it turns out, were already rehearsing the language those worshippers speak. At every gate, the rehearsal ends and the liturgy begins.

## Verse 13:24

> “Peace be upon you for your perseverance. How excellent is the ultimate abode!”

**The Wages of Perseverance, Spoken by Angels**

“Peace be upon you for your perseverance” (13:24). The verse is the speech held over from 13:23 — the angels at every gate open their mouths, and the first thing said in the Gardens is a greeting and a wage-statement at once. The grammar is worth watching: peace *because you were patient*. The angelic welcome does not say "for your wealth," "for your eloquence," or "for your numbers". The one quality named is the one habit the portrait of 13:20–22 listed first and the one the Book's arrival scene repeats: “When they arrive at its ˹already˺ open gates, its keepers will say, “Peace be upon you! You have done well, so come in, to stay forever”” (39:73). Patience is the common denominator of the whole portrait — endurance under mockery (13:5–7), constancy in prayer, the long discipline of answering evil with good (13:22) — and it is therefore the one quality the greeters can cite as the reason for the address. The greeting is also the only salary the verse mentions: the entrants are paid in a word, and the word is the residency itself.

**The Greeting That Answers a Lifetime of Heckling**

The verse is also the sūrah's own argument brought to rest. It opened with the announcement that most people do not believe (13:1), continued through the mockery of resurrection (13:5), the demand for hastened torment (13:6), and the demand for signs (13:7) — a catalogue of heckling that no single verse answered point by point. The greeting at the gates is the answer, delivered at the only venue where the answer matters. The hecklers questioned the destination; the entrants are standing in it. The hecklers demanded immediacy; the entrants are holding permanence. And the greeting's content — peace — is precisely what the heckling never offered anyone. A generation of believers heard insults daily and replied with the salām; the verse records the exchange rate at which that patience was bought: one word of peace, spoken by the angels, at every gate, forever. No rebuttal composed in Makkah could have answered the mockery so completely, because the mockery was an argument about a future, and the future is where this verse collects.

**A Greeting With a Long Liturgical History**

The greeting did not begin at the gates. The Book records it as the ambient speech of Paradise throughout: the companions of the Garden are “greeted with “Peace!”” (14:23), and the greeting circulates among the residents themselves as the ordinary currency of speech. The pattern reaches back into the sūrah's own materials: the whole creation was already speaking praise — the thunder's hymn (13:13), the shadows' prostration (13:15) — and the believer's practiced salām in this world was his participation in that liturgy before he ever saw it from inside. The verse of the gates so closes the portrait gallery (13:19–24) with a sound rather than a sight: the first thing the homeowner hears is not the gardens or the rivers but the word he spent a lifetime learning to say. This is why the greeting is delivered by angels — the same beings who entered from every gate (13:23) — as though the whole created order were handing the arrival its language. The next verse will turn to those who broke what these entrants kept, and its verdict will be built from the same vocabulary of covenant, ties, and abode — the two galleries sharing one grammar, at opposite addresses.

## Verse 13:25

> And those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land—it is they who will be condemned and will have the worst abode

**The Mirror Portrait: Same Materials, Opposite Uses**

“And those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land” (13:25). Set this beside 13:20–21 and the design of the sūrah's gallery becomes explicit: the two portraits are built from the same three materials — the covenant, the ties, the earth — used in opposite ways. The keeper honors the covenant, maintains the ties, and lives on the land; the violator breaks the covenant, severs the ties, and corrupts the land. The Qur'an repeats the triplet almost verbatim in another chapter: “those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed. Sever what Allah has ordered to be joined, and spread corruption in the land—it is they who are the losers” (2:27) — and that repetition confirms the pairing is deliberate doctrine, not local rhetoric. The mirror construction preempts a comfortable misunderstanding. No reader can imagine the two portraits as different sorts of people with different sorts of religion; they are the same sort of creature, holding the same pledges, whose difference is completely a matter of what they did with what was entrusted to them.

**Condemned and Housed: The Two Sentences**

“It is they who will be condemned and will have the worst abode” (13:25). The sentence falls in two registers, and the commentators read both with care. The condemnation was understood as distance from God's mercy — the curse as exile rather than tantrum, the creature placed outside the reach of the mercy it refused; another reading makes the distance spatial: distance from God's presence, sorrow standing behind a closed door of one's own locking. Both readings preserve the point that matters: the curse is not a mood of heaven but a separation, and the separation was chosen from inside. The evil abode, on the other reading of the commentators, may describe an evil outcome within this world — the corrupted land consuming its corrupters — or the Fire in the next. The ambiguity is itself instructive, since the two readings were never rivals in the Qur'an's economy: the evil abode has branches in both worlds, and the covenant-breaker pays in the currency of both. Against the portrait's opening — those who have the finest reward and the ultimate abode (13:18, 13:22–23) — the closing panel's address is the same word turned inside out: the worst abode, the residence that matches the refusal.

**The Gallery Closes the Case Opened at 13:19**

With this verse the sūrah's double portrait is complete, and the case that 13:19 filed is decided. There, the question was whether the knower could be like the blind; here, the blind man's biography is finished — covenant violated, ties severed, land corrupted, address assigned. The structure of the whole passage (13:19–25) now reads as one sustained act of sorting: a question about sight, four verses of the seeing man's habits, and a closing verse of the blind man's habits, with the addresses announced at each end. Nothing in the sūrah's remaining verses will withdraw from this sorting; the following passages will refine the portraits' theology — provision (13:26), guidance (13:27–29), the mission's patience (13:30–32) — but the division is fixed. The mirror construction leaves the reader where it leaves everyone: between the panels, holding a covenant that has now been affirmed to him twice over, once in the primordial testimony and once in the reading. The verse's final function is therefore diagnostic — it tells the reader not who the violators were, but which portrait is currently being drawn in him.

## Verse 13:26

> Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills. And the disbelievers become prideful of ˹the pleasures of˺ this worldly life. But the life of this world, compared to the Hereafter, is nothing but a fleeting enjoyment

**The Verse Names the Distributor Before the Distribution**

“Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills” (13:26). The verse opens the economics of this world by naming the economist: provision is not generated by luck, inherited by right, or earned by cleverness — it is *given*, in measures that vary by will. The Qur'an states the doctrine repeatedly and precisely: “Surely your Lord gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills. He is certainly All-Aware, All-Seeing of His servants” (17:30). The distribution is therefore administered — the All-Aware of that verse is the same Knower of wombs and seen-and-unseen that this sūrah installed at 13:8–9 — and what looks from the ground like luck is, from the office, a decision. The Book even stages the confession: when Qārūn's wealth swallowed him and the earth then swallowed him, the very people who had envied him the day before said, “It is certainly Allah Who gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills of His servants” (28:82). Envy and pride, the two errors wealth generates, are corrected by the same sentence: the Distributor gives, withholds, and explains to no committee. The verse of this sūrah is the calm statement of that office before either error is addressed.

**Pride in the Cheque: The Disbelievers' Economics**

“And the disbelievers become prideful of ˹the pleasures of˺ this worldly life” (13:26). The verse's second clause diagnoses what the disbeliever does with the distribution: he reads his own balance as his own verdict. The Book names the mechanics of that illusion with unusual harshness: “Whoever desires this fleeting world ˹alone˺, We hasten in it whatever We please to whoever We will; then We destine them for Hell” (17:18). Note the terrible verb — *hasten*: the very abundance the prideful man boasts of is described as an accelerated payment, the wage handed over early to the worker who demanded it in the wrong currency. The prideful disbeliever of 13:26 is so not merely foolish; he is cashing the instrument that will ruin him, and signing for it gratefully.

**A Fleeting Enjoyment: The Arithmetic of Comparison**

“But the life of this world, compared to the Hereafter, is nothing but a fleeting enjoyment” (13:26). The corrective is not denial but division. The verse does not say the world's pleasures are nothing; it says they are nothing *compared to the Hereafter* — the arithmetic of a specific ratio, not a renunciation. The Qur'an applies the same arithmetic elsewhere with vivid materials: “This worldly life is no more than play and amusement. Far better is the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter for those mindful ˹of Allah˺” (6:32); and the great rain-simile prices the whole show: “This is like rain that causes plants to grow, to the delight of the planters. But later the plants dry up and you see them wither, then they are reduced to chaff” (57:20). The rain-simile is this sūrah's own water-theme (13:17) turned economic: the same rain that sorts truth from foam in the wadi sorts fortunes in the field, and the crop's green week is the world's whole dividend. For the investor, the Book offers the alternative portfolio in one verse: “Whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter, We will increase their harvest” (42:20) — the Hereafter's yield is not only equal but multiplied, and the world's yield is not only small but spent. A Companion of the Prophet ﷺ, Abū'l-Dardāʾ, is reported to have compressed the arithmetic into a sentence: when a man comes to loathe this world, he comes to love the next, and he abstains from this one to the measure of his love for the other. The saying belongs to the verse's school of thought: the world is not escaped by poverty or ascetic display but by a corrected exchange rate — and the verse is the correction, issued in advance of the audit.

## Verse 13:27

> The disbelievers say, “If only a sign could be sent down to him from his Lord.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Indeed, Allah leaves to stray whoever He wills, and guides to Himself whoever turns to Him—

**The Turn Is the Sign**

The second half of the reply installs the real venue of the miracle: the turn. The Qur'an elsewhere locates all the choice in exactly this hinge: “We already showed them the Way, whether they ˹choose to˺ be grateful or ungrateful” (76:3); the Way is shown, and the whole drama is the direction of the walking. Another verse states the offer without varnish: “to whoever of you wills to take the Straight Way” (81:28) — the Way is open to willing takers, which means the sign-demanders were asking heaven to do the one thing heaven has structured the world to refuse: believe for them. This is the deep answer to the repeated demand of 13:7 and 13:27. A sign sent down on demand would not produce the guided; it would produce the coerced — and the coerced are neither of the two categories the verse divides. The turn is the sign's replacement because the turn is the thing being tested: whoever turns finds guidance waiting, which means the guidance was never the missing element. The will was.

**The Sūrah's Third Refusal, and the Reply That Becomes a Portrait**

This is the sūrah's third recorded demand, and the sequence of answers shows a patience with its own escalation. The demand to hasten torment (13:6) was answered with the archive and the two attributes; the demand for a sign (13:7) was answered with the warner's office and the universal guide; the repeated demand here is answered with the theology of guidance itself. Then the sūrah does something no disputation does: it stops arguing and paints. The clause “whoever turns to Him” (13:27) is left grammatically open, and the next verse walks through the opening — “those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah” (13:28) — followed by the summation of bliss and beautiful return (13:29). The refusers asked for a spectacle; the reply they receive is a portrait of the people who turned without one: their inner weather described, their destination named. The argument's force is quiet and total. The sign they demanded was meant to reassure them; the portrait shows what reassurance actually looks like — and it is available on terms the demanders have so far refused to meet: a turn. The reply is also left grammatically open, the sentence running past its comma into the next verse, because its completion is not a doctrine but a population: the guided of 13:28, described in two clauses. The demand for a sign receives, in place of a decree, an unfinished invitation — and the invitation's completion is the reader's. The openness is itself the answer: the mockers wanted heaven to close the question; heaven handed the question back, alive.

One last turn of the verse's grammar: the straying and the guiding are both attributed to God, and the turning is attributed to the servant. The distribution of verbs is the verse's whole counsel — God's acts are not argued with, and the servant's act is not performed by anyone else.

The Prophet ﷺ was instructed to state the freedom aloud: “Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve” (18:29).

## Verse 13:28

> those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort

**The Restless Heart and Its Only Home**

The verse says it twice — statement, then the absolute affirmation: “Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort” (13:28) — and the doubling teaches that the doctrine is stated without exception. The Book elsewhere shows remembrance operating on the same hearts from the other side: “The ˹true˺ believers are only those whose hearts tremble at the remembrance of Allah” (8:2) — the trembling of the guilty conscience, the shake that wakes — and again as an alarm against numbness: “Has the time not yet come for believers’ hearts to be humbled at the remembrance of Allah” (57:16). Trembling, humbling, settling: one name, three effects, each matched to what the heart needs. The comfort promised here is therefore not comfort as the world distributes it — the anesthetic of distraction — but the settledness of a thing returned to its element. The commentators read this verse as the founding charter of the discipline of invocation: the tongue moist with God's name, the heart following the tongue, until the tossed thing finds the anchor it was made for. The world's comforts wear out because they are foreign to the heart; the remembrance does not wear out because the heart, by its creation, belongs to the One remembered.

**Remembrance as an Exchange Already Underway**

“Remember Me; I will remember you. And thank Me, and never be ungrateful” (2:152). The verse of this sūrah stands inside a relationship the Qur'an describes as mutual: the servant's remembrance answered by the Lord's — an exchange whose greater half is stated first in the divine grammar of that verse, *I will remember you*, and whose creaturely half is the practice 13:28 prices. The sūrah's own architecture makes the comfort credible. It has spent verses 8 through 10 establishing that God's knowledge already wraps every whisper, every hidden thing, every womb; the heart that remembers Him is not attracting an absent attention but returning an ongoing one. That is the ground of the comfort's security: the remembrance is not a summons that may go unanswered — the answer preceded the summons. And the exchange has an argumentative tail aimed at the idolaters of this sūrah: their gods had to be informed, attract, reminded. The exchange also explains the verse's verb of finding: hearts *find* comfort — discovery, not manufacture.

**The Sūrah's Quiet Center**

Placement makes this verse the fulcrum of the chapter. Behind it: the sign-refused of 13:27, the taunts and demands of 13:5–7, the denials of 13:1 — the noise of the unturned. Ahead of it: “bliss and an honourable destination” (13:29) — the verdict on the settled. The verse of the resting hearts sits between them as both the destination's first installment and the noise's only cure. The commentators noted that the guided of 13:27 are precisely these: those who turned, believed, and found the heart's repose in the remembrance. The sequence is worth keeping intact, because it is the verse's practical instruction. The turn comes first (13:27); belief follows; the remembrance is the practice; the comfort is the fruit — and the fruit arrives in this world, before the bliss of the next. For a sūrah that has catalogued storms (13:12–13), thunderbolts (13:13), shackles (13:5). Corrupted lands (13:25), the quiet at the center is itself an argument: the same Lord who shakes the sky with signs has installed a sign in the chest, and its name is a heart at rest that was not at rest before. The verse is short enough to memorize, which is part of its design: the cure it prescribes is the verse's own first dose.

## Verse 13:29

> Those who believe and do good, for them will be bliss and an honourable destination.”

**The Good Life, Priced and Verified**

The first reading of *ṭūbā* — a good life in this world — is not a commentator's fancy. The Qur'an states it elsewhere as a promise with its own verse: “Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, We will surely bless them with a good life, and We will certainly reward them according to the best of their deeds” (16:97). Two features of that promise matter for understanding 13:29. First, the good life is given to *belief joined with deed* — the exact pair of this verse — which means the world's bliss is not a wealth bracket but a state: the heart settled by remembrance (13:28), the conscience clean, the ties maintained (13:21), the insults answered with good (13:22). The believer's bliss is so already on deposit before the grave. Second, the reward is calculated “according to the best” — the audit pays on the maximum, which is the same generosity the arrival scenes show: the gates already open, the greeting already warm (13:23–24). A worldly reading of *ṭūbā* also corrects a modern misreading of this sūrah: the comforts promised are not comforts purchased, which is why the verse can promise them to people the world is currently mocking (13:5–7). The good life is also portable in a way prosperity is not: the mocked believer carries it through the mockery, since 13:28's settled heart is its residence, while the prosperous mocker cannot buy it back at any price — the doubled earth of 13:18 was refused as a ransom for far less. Bliss, in the verse's economy, is the first property that survives every relocation. One grammatical thread ties the verse into the sūrah's fabric: the word for return shares its root with the turning of 13:30 — to Him I turn, to Him is my return. The practice and the destination are one motion, begun in time and completed outside it. The believer who spends a life turning arrives by the only road that exists: the road he is already on.

**An Honourable Destination: The Grammar of Arrival**

The second gift is described with the word for return, and the Qur'an uses that word for death deliberately — the end of life is a going back, not a going away. The same paired words — beautiful return — recur through the Book as the reward's signature, and the return is attended along the whole route. At the moment of death, the Book shows the steadfast receiving their travelers' news in advance: “the angels descend upon them, ˹saying,˺ “Do not fear, nor grieve. Rather, rejoice in the good news of Paradise, which you have been promised”” (41:30). The descent answers the two great fears of the crossing — fear of what comes, grief for what is left — and its cargo is the destination's own name. At the arrival, the protocol of 13:23–24 resumes: gates open, angels visiting, the greeting of peace. The honor of the destination, in other words, is not only the place's quality; it is the treatment received *en route and at the door* — the measure of how the Returning One receives. The destination that other addresses in this sūrah call the worst abode (13:25) is here the return made honorable — the same journey, two receptions.

## Verse 13:30

> And so We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ to a community, like ˹We did with˺ earlier communities, so that you may recite to them what We have revealed to you. Yet they deny the Most Compassionate. Say, “He is my Lord! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. In Him I put my trust, and to Him I turn ˹in repentance˺.”

**A Prophet Sent the Way Prophets Are Sent**

“And so We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ to a community, like ˹We did with˺ earlier communities” (13:30). The verse places the Prophet's ﷺ mission inside the standing series: the last of the communities of revelation receiving the latest of the messengers, dispatched in the same manner and for the same task as those before. The Qur'an insists on the pattern's scale: “˹Imagine˺ how many prophets We sent to those ˹destroyed˺ before!” (43:6) — and on its outcome, which the next verse records flatly: “But no prophet ever came to them without being mocked” (43:7). The pattern also had its method: “Indeed, We have sent messengers before you ˹O Prophet˺ to other people who We put through suffering and adversity ˹for their denial˺, so perhaps they would be humbled” (6:42). For the Prophet ﷺ, the placement is both comfort and calibration. Comfort: his rejection is not a novelty but the series' ordinary weather, already survived by every messenger before him. Calibration: his task is the series' task, defined in the verse's next clause without embellishment — “so that you may recite to them what We have revealed to you” (13:30) — recitation, the delivery of the text, not the manufacture of the signs the town kept demanding at 13:7 and 13:27.

**The Denial Named With Unusual Precision: the Most Compassionate**

“Yet they deny the Most Compassionate” (13:30). The sūrah has recorded denials of resurrection, of messengerhood, of signs; this one names its object with a specific divine name — *al-Raḥmān*, the Most Compassionate — and the reports preserved about the Makkans' reaction explain why. The Book itself confirms the mockery as a pattern: “When the disbelievers see you ˹O Prophet˺, they only make fun of you, ˹saying,˺ “Is this the one who speaks ˹ill˺ of your gods?” while they disbelieve at the mention of the Most Compassionate” (21:36). The denial of the name is the denial of mercy as a doctrine — the claim that the Lord of the thunder (13:13) and the wombs (13:8) is characterized first by compassion. That is why the name matters doctrinally: to deny al-Raḥmān is not one superstition among many; it is the rejection of the attribute that makes every other divine address bearable, from the standing forgiveness of 13:6 to the greeting of peace at the gates (13:24).

**Reciting in a Town That Denies the Name**

The verse's assignment — recite to them what We have revealed (13:30) — deserves the final word, because it defines the mission's whole method in one verb. The Prophet ﷺ is not told to answer the town's philosophy, match its poets, or satisfy its sign-demands; he is told to recite. The method is the argument: the Book's own voice, delivered as received, is the operative sign — the claim this sūrah has been making since its first verse, that what came down is “the truth” (13:1). A community denying the Most Compassionate is being handed the Compassionate's own speech, which is either the worst possible gift or the best, depending on the ear. The verse's placement also turns the sūrah toward its close: the remaining thirteen verses will deal with the mission's open questions — the Book's power (13:31), the mocked messengers' end (13:32), the plotting (13:33, 13:42), the shrinking borders (13:41), and the final witness (13:43). All of it is underwritten by the stance the verse has just installed: trust and return, recitation and patience, in a town that denies the name of the One who sent the text.

An earlier prophet is recorded using nearly the same closing formula in his own defense: “My success comes only through Allah. In Him I trust and to Him I turn” (11:88).

## Verse 13:31

> If there were a recitation that could cause mountains to move, or the earth to split, or the dead to speak, ˹it would have been this Quran˺. But all matters are by Allah’s Will. Have the believers not yet realized that had Allah willed, He could have guided all of humanity? And disasters will continue to afflict the disbelievers or strike close to their homes for their misdeeds, until Allah’s promise comes to pass. Surely Allah never fails in His promise

**The Mockers' Wishlist, Granted Only in the Negative**

“If there were a recitation that could cause mountains to move, or the earth to split, or the dead to speak, ˹it would have been this Quran˺” (13:31). The verse opens on the mockers' own wishlist — mountains walked, ground torn open, ancestors raised to chat — and the commentary tradition recorded the demand as literal: the disbelievers of Makkah told the Prophet ﷺ to ask his Lord for exactly these productions. The verse's answer is a backhanded concession: such a recitation exists, and it is this one — the Qur'an's power outruns the requested theatrics — but the power is not on hire. The sign-demand of 13:7 and 13:27 here meets its final, religious refusal: not "the miracles are unavailable" but "the miracles are not the missing ingredient.".

**Why the Refusal Holds: The Will Comes First**

“But all matters are by Allah’s Will” (13:31). The clause is the verse's hinge, and the doctrine it states is the one this sūrah has been assembling since its first verses: the conducted cosmos (13:2), the measured wombs (13:8), the knowledge without outside (13:9) — and now the will that governs guidance itself. The believers are then addressed directly: “Have the believers not yet realized that had Allah willed, He could have guided all of humanity?” (13:31). The question is aimed at the faithful's frustration, not the deniers' doubt. A companion of the Prophet ﷺ could be forgiven for measuring the mission's success by its conversion count and finding the count insultingly low; the verse re-ranks the metrics: the mission's success was never the town's surrender, because the town's surrender was never available for purchase by spectacle. Had Allah willed, every soul on earth would be a believer — the parallel text states it with its own rebuke: “Had your Lord so willed ˹O Prophet˺, all ˹people˺ on earth would have certainly believed, every single one of them! Would you then force people to become believers?” (10:99). Guidance belongs to the will of God and the turn of the servant (13:27); it is not extracted by wonders.

**Never Fails the Promise: The Chapter's Track Record Applied**

“Surely Allah never fails in His promise” (13:31). The closing clause is a formula the sūrah has already earned the right to use. Its second verse established the track record: heavens conducted, orbits appointed, the sun never once late to its entrance (13:2). Its sixth verse answered the demand to hasten torment with the reminder that His promise will not be defaulted (13:6, in the parallel text's own words: “And Allah will never fail in His promise,” 22:47). Now the same reliability is transferred to the promise of the verse's middle — the promise that will come to pass — and by extension to every promise the Book has made to either party of the gallery: the Gardens of 13:23 as surely as the sentence of 13:25. The clause also quietly closes the chapter's longest running dispute. The mockers' wager, from 13:5 onward, was that nothing follows the denials — no raising, no seizure, no settling. The verse's last words price that wager: the promise-keeping God who has never missed an orbital appointment will not begin missing them now. What remains for the audience is the only variable the verse leaves open — the turn, before the clock runs out. The clause's position as the verse's last words gives the promise the final say over the disasters: afflictions are penultimate; the promise is ultimate. A chapter that opened by calling the revelation truth (13:1) closes its longest verse by pricing the word — truth-telling, for God, is measured in missed appointments, and the count is zero.

## Verse 13:32

> Other messengers had already been ridiculed before you, but I delayed the disbelievers ˹for a while˺ then seized them. And how ˹horrible˺ was My punishment

**The Mocking Precedent: A Standing Series**

“Other messengers had already been ridiculed before you” (13:32). The verse hands the Prophet ﷺ the file on his predecessors, and the file is thick. The Qur'an states the pattern as a rule without exceptions: “but no messenger ever came to them without being mocked” (15:11); and again, in nearly the same words: “But no prophet ever came to them without being mocked” (43:7). The endurance of the mocked is recorded with its reward attached: “Indeed, messengers before you were rejected but patiently endured rejection and persecution until Our help came to them. And Allah’s promise ˹to help˺ is never broken” (6:34). For the Prophet ﷺ, standing in a town that ridicules him — the sūrah has documented the mockery at 13:5, the derision at the Compassionate's name at 13:30 — the precedent performs two services at once. It normalizes his experience: the mockery is the office's standard equipment, not a malfunction of his mission. And it guarantees the sequence's other half: the same series that records universal mocking records universal patience, universal help, and — as this verse adds — universal seizure of the mockers. The Prophet ﷺ is not living through an anomaly. He is living inside a pattern with a fixed middle and a fixed end.

**The Divine First Person: “I Delayed, I Seized”**

“But I delayed the disbelievers ˹for a while˺ then seized them” (13:32). The verse shifts into God's own voice — I delayed, I seized — and the first person is doing doctrinal work. This is the respite doctrine that 13:6 already planted: the demand to hasten the torment was answered with the reminder that forgiveness is in circulation and severity is real; here the same two hands are named in the first person, delay and seizure, as consecutive movements of one will. The pattern-verse of Sūrah al-Ḥajj states the identical sequence as settled history: “But I granted the disbelievers a respite, then I seized them” — and the parallel is exact because the doctrine is exact. The respite's purpose is legible from the sūrah's own teaching: the interval is mercy's open account (13:6), the turn's available window (13:27–28).

**Respite as the Believer's Reading Assignment**

For the mocked, the verse is an instruction manual disguised as a report. The same verse that threatens the mockers authorizes the patient: the pattern's end is fixed, the help of 6:34 is never defaulted, and the middle of the pattern — the decade of ridicule — is the zone the believer is assigned to work. That is why the command that brackets the whole doctrine is patience: “So endure patiently, as did the Messengers of Firm Resolve. And do not ˹seek to˺ hasten ˹the torment˺ for the deniers” (46:35) — patience and *no hastening*, the exact opposite of the Makkans' demand at 13:6, and the exact opposite of the believer's own temptation. The verse of this sūrah so completes the mission's operating system. 13:30 installed the four clauses — my Lord, no god but He, trust, return; 13:31 explained the will behind the unconverted town; 13:32 explains the calendar: delay is designed, seizure is certain, and the interval between them is where the recitation (13:30) and the patience (13:22) do their work. The mission's armor, in other words, now includes a watch: the Prophet ﷺ is being told not when the seizure falls — the schedule is God's — but what the waiting is for. The delay's visibility deserves one more word. Every generation of mockers in the recorded pattern died comfortably — the respite did not fail them on schedule; it failed their heirs' confidence instead, which is precisely its design: each cohort watches the previous cohort's comfortable old age and concludes the seizure is legend. The verse is written to break that inference before it compounds: the same sentence that admits the delay removes its alibi.

## Verse 13:33

> Is ˹there any equal to˺ the Vigilant One Who knows what every soul commits? Yet the pagans have associated others with Allah ˹in worship˺. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Name them! Or do you ˹mean to˺ inform Him of something He does not know on the earth? Or are these ˹gods˺ just empty words?” In fact, the disbelievers’ falsehood has been made so appealing to them that they have been turned away from the Path. And whoever Allah leaves to stray will be left with no guide

**“Name Them”: The Interrogation's Sharpest Line**

“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Name them!”” (13:33). After the suspended comparison, the command is almost comic in its confidence: produce the rivals. The Qur'an has already audited what the naming would yield: “These ˹idols˺ are mere names that you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized” (53:23). The inventory of the pantheon is a list of words — names with no referent, titles with no office — and the command to name them is a command to read the list aloud, where its emptiness becomes audible. The verse then offers the namers a choice of explanations for their own practice, each more damaging than the last. Perhaps they intend “to inform Him of something He does not know on the earth” (13:33) — the absurdity of briefing omniscience, the same overturned economy that 13:14 exposed in the idolater's prayers, where informing was the entire service. Or — “are these ˹gods˺ just empty words?” (13:33) — the vocabulary of the commentators' own note: words with no inward meaning, built on a false opinion. The interrogation leaves the pagan no exit: if the partners are real, name them; if real, God would know them without briefing; if unknown to God, they are nothing; if nothing, the worship was a transaction in empty words.

**Fairness Made Diabolical: Why They Cannot See the Emptiness**

“In fact, the disbelievers’ falsehood has been made so appealing to them that they have been turned away from the Path” (13:33). The verse then answers the question the interrogation begs: how do grown men worship words? Because the falsehood has been *made appealing* — the same mechanism the Book documents as a settled law: “Are those whose evil-doing is made so appealing to them that they deem it good ˹like those who are rightly guided˺?” (35:8). The appeal is not innocence; it is consequence — the deed loved long enough until the loving feels like light. The commentators connected this made-fairness to the sūrah's own closing doctrine at 13:42, where plotting belongs to God altogether: the plotters' plot is permitted to glitter right up to the moment it closes on them. And the turning away has a terminus: “And whoever Allah leaves to stray will be left with no guide” (13:33) — the locked door of 13:27, restated at the interrogation's end. The verse so explains the pagans' condition without excusing it: the appeal of the falsehood is the harvest of their own planting, the wandering is in their defiance (7:186), and the Guide's departure is the ratification of a walking-away that began on their own feet.

**Why the Verse Ends on the Locked Door**

The verse's architecture — the suspended comparison, the command to name, the offered absurdities, the made-appealing falsehood, the guideless end — traces a diagnosis from symptom to cause, and its final clause is both the sternest sentence in the sūrah and, read carefully, its last offer. “Left with no guide” (13:33) is the precise inverse of 13:27's “guides to Himself whoever turns to Him”: the two verses bracket the chapter's doctrine of guidance, one describing the turning that is met, the other the defiance that is ratified. What the verse forecloses is not repentance but autonomy: no third party can walk the wanderer home, no purchased guidance, no inherited merit, no idol's intercession — the whole intermediary economy that 13:14–16 bankrupted stays bankrupt here. The interrogation of the idols (13:33) sits immediately before the two verses of outcome (13:34's double punishment, 13:35's two ends), so that the reader passes from the naming of the empty words to the pricing of them: words today, wandering tomorrow, address after that.

## Verse 13:34

> For them is punishment in this worldly life, but the punishment of the Hereafter is truly far worse. And none can shield them from Allah

**The World's Ruins Are a Standing Lecture**

The worldly half of the punishment is not hidden; it is published, and the Qur'an orders field trips. “Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them? They were far superior in might and ˹richer in˺ monuments throughout the land. But Allah seized them for their sins, and they had no protector from Allah” (40:21). The ruined civilizations were mightier and better built than the audiences being addressed — the lesson is calibrated to deflate every material excuse: wealth did not shield, monuments did not shield, and “they had no protector from Allah” (40:21). The lecture is repeated with an inventory: “Many are the societies We have destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, leaving them in total ruin. ˹Many are˺ also the abandoned wells and lofty palaces!” (22:45). The verse of this sūrah asks its audience to read the same landscape the sūrah's own geography offered: the empty tracts of 13:4 are the survivors' side of the ledger, and the ruins are the other. The sūrah's audience needed no museum: the busy shrines of Makkah housed gods whose devotees had already lost arguments to drought, market shifts, and the desert's ordinary verdicts — the world punishes misplaced trust without waiting for the Hereafter to open the file.

**None to Shield: The Bankruptcy of the Intermediary Economy**

“And none can shield them from Allah” (13:34). The closing clause ends the shield business, and it ends it against every vendor. The idols, already exposed as unable to hear (13:14) and unable to create (13:16), are here exposed as unable to insure: the one service the whole institution of intermediaries existed to sell — protection from the Lord — is a service no one can provide. The jinn of another chapter state the same bankruptcy from inside: “No one can protect me from Allah ˹if I were to disobey Him˺, nor can I find any refuge other than Him” (72:22) — even spirits who might be imagined to have reach confess the shield's nonexistence. The verse's negative is therefore comprehensive: none — not wealth, not tribe, not the partners just challenged to name themselves (13:33), not the monuments of the ruined nations. The sūrah has been closing these offices one by one: the gods who cannot respond (13:14), the partners who cannot create (13:16), the protectors who cannot be found (13:11), and now the shields that cannot shield (13:34). What remains standing in the cleared field is the single unmediated relationship — creature and Creator — which is the sūrah's whole theology: the verse leaves its readers, like everyone else, without a representative and with an account.

## Verse 13:35

> The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that under it rivers flow; eternal is its fruit as well as its shade. That is the ˹ultimate˺ outcome for the righteous. But the outcome for the disbelievers is the Fire

**A Description Built From This World's Best, Subtracting Its Flaws**

“The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that under it rivers flow; eternal is its fruit as well as its shade” (13:35). Rivers here do not flood, dry, or carry foam (contrast 13:17); fruit does not rot, end with the season, or run out; shade does not withdraw with the sun's afternoon descent. The fuller description in the Book confirms the method — this world's materials, upgraded beyond their expiries: “rivers of fresh water, rivers of milk that never changes in taste, rivers of wine delicious to drink. Rivers of pure honey” (47:15) — four rivers, each a familiar drink with its familiar failure removed: water's stagnation, milk's souring, and so on. The Garden, on this showing, is not an escape from the created order but its completion — the same water, fruit, and shade that God gives here (13:3–4), given there without the withdrawal clause.

**The Ultimate Outcome: One Word, Two Addresses**

“That is the ˹ultimate˺ outcome for the righteous. But the outcome for the disbelievers is the Fire!” (13:35). The verse's pivot rests on a single word — outcome, the end-of-account term the Qur'an uses when the ledger closes — and the sentence hands each party its closing balance. The design mirrors the sūrah's earlier verdicts and completes them. At 13:18 the responders received the finest reward and the refusers an evil resting place. At 13:23–24 the keepers entered Gardens with greetings; at 13:25 the violators received the worst abode; at 13:29 the believers received bliss and a honorable destination; and here the whole system is restated in one verse of pure contrast — outcome against outcome, Garden against Fire. The harvest vocabulary of the verse is deliberate: the sūrah has been an agricultural document from its fourth verse — one water, different tastes (13:4); the wadi sorting truth from froth (13:17) — and the outcome is the harvest festival of that imagery. The Book's economic promise states the harvest's two fields: “Whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter, We will increase their harvest” (42:20) — the Garden as the increased yield, the Fire as the field left unsown. The verse asks its audience the planter's question: which field is receiving your water? The word's own weight does the persuading: an outcome is what remains when comparing is over. The verse lets the two addresses carry their arguments — rivers, fruit, shade against a single clause — because by the time comparison ends, the parties will no longer be comparing but residing.

**Why the Description Stops Where It Does**

The verse's restraint is worth a final word. It promises the Garden in one sentence and prices the Fire in one clause — no inventory of torments, no elaborate architecture — because the description's job is orientation, not tourism. The righteous are told enough to re-price their present: the fruit they deny themselves, the shade of obedience they stand in (13:22's patience), the water of remembrance they drink (13:28) are all installments of an address whose full form is rivers, fruit, shade without end. The disbelievers are told enough to re-price their present as well: the prosperity they flaunt (13:26) is chaff by the arithmetic of 57:20, and its outcome — the word does the work — is announced in a single clause with an exclamation. The Book, elsewhere, unrolls the fuller description (47:15) for those who want the detail; this verse's economy is a mercy to the mocked Prophet ﷺ and his congregation: the argument of thirteen chapters' worth of signs (13:1–34) is now compact enough to carry — a description, an outcome, a contrast. What remains for the hearer is the sorting verb the sūrah has used since 13:18: respond.

Fruit in this world is seasonal — a few weeks of ripeness, then gone or forbidden — and the Book's fuller portrait removes both defects at once: “abundant fruit—never out of season nor forbidden—” (56:32–33).

## Verse 13:36

> ˹The believers among˺ those who were given the Scripture rejoice at what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺, while some ˹disbelieving˺ factions deny a portion of it. Say, “I have only been commanded to worship Allah, associating none with Him ˹in worship˺. To Him I invite ˹all˺, and to Him is my return.”

**Two Readings of One Revelation**

“˹The believers among˺ those who were given the Scripture rejoice at what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺, while some ˹disbelieving˺ factions deny a portion of it” (13:36). The verse sets two ways of receiving revelation side by side, and both are documented among the People of the Book. The rejoicing recipients are real: the faithful of the earlier Scriptures who recognized the new revelation as their own books' continuation — “˹As for˺ those ˹faithful˺ to whom We had given the Scripture before this ˹Quran˺, they do believe in it. When it is recited to them, they declare, “We believe in it. This is definitely the truth from our Lord.”” (28:52–53). The factions, by contrast, are described with a revealing precision: they deny *a portion of it* — not the whole.

**The Prophet's ﷺ Three-Part Program**

“Say, “I have only been commanded to worship Allah, associating none with Him ˹in worship˺. To Him I invite ˹all˺, and to Him is my return.”” (13:36). The dictated answer gives the Prophet's ﷺ entire program in three clauses, each pointed at a different audience. *Worship Allah, associating none with Him* — the creed without factions: no portions, no parties, no partners; the command is stated as the whole of his commission, which quietly declares the selection-business beside the point. *To Him I invite* — the invitation extended to all, the factional gatekeeping answered by a call with no door except the one it stands at. *To Him is my return* — the mission's horizon: the One who sent him receives him back, which is the standing answer to every faction's verdict on him. The Book has the Prophet ﷺ state the same program as a settled rule: “Be steadfast as you are commanded, and do not follow their desires. And say, “I believe in every Scripture Allah has revealed.”” (42:15) — belief in *every* Scripture being the exact opposite of the portion-deniers' method, and the desires being the committee that the selectors served. The program's coherence is its argument: a messenger who believes every Book cannot be recruiting for a faction, and a caller whose return is to God cannot be bribed by any of theirs.

**Rejoicing or Selecting: The Heart's Two Ways With a Revelation**

The verse's deeper teaching is about what a revelation does to the interior that receives it. The rejoicers of 13:36 — the believers among the People of the Book and the Prophet's ﷺ own companions — receive the whole and are enlarged; the selecting factions receive the portion and are diminished, turned by their own edits (13:33's made-appealing falsehood is the same mechanism in the pagan register). The sūrah has already supplied the image for both operations: the water of 13:17, sorting as it lands. The whole received is water into a wadi — it flows, it settles, it remains; the portion received, with the rest denied, is the froth on the same flood — an impurity riding the revelation it edited. The verse's closing clause — to Him is my return (13:36) — then prices both styles at the only counter where portions do not trade. The selectors' portions will not be presentable there; the whole received will be. For the reader, the verse functions as a mirror held at the canon: not what do you believe of the Book, but *how much* — and the answer's honest form is not a percentage. Either the truth is received the way the weeping receivers received it, in full and with the eyes, or it is being cut to fit — and the verse has already told us which of the two styles can rejoice.

The Book records the physiognomy of the rejoicing reception, and it is one of its most tender scenes: “you see their eyes overflowing with tears for recognizing the truth. They say, “Our Lord! We believe, so count us among the witnesses.”” (5:83).

## Verse 13:37

> And so We have revealed it as an authority in Arabic. And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or shield you from Allah

**A Ruling in the Language of Its First Audience**

“And so We have revealed it as an authority in Arabic” (13:37). The verse names the Qur'an a ruling — a criterion that judges — and locates the judging in a language: the Arabs' own. The choice of tongue is doctrine, not convenience, and the Qur'an states the rule behind it in the general law of missions: “We have not sent a messenger except in the language of his people to clarify ˹the message˺ for them” (14:4). Revelation addresses each people in the language they think in, because clarification — the stated purpose — is impossible in an imported tongue. The Book insists on its own Arabic repeatedly: “Indeed, We have sent it down as an Arabic Quran so that you may understand” (12:2), and again: “Indeed, We have made this ˹Quran˺ easy in your own language ˹O Prophet˺ so perhaps they will be mindful” (44:58). Two things follow. The revelation is not diminished by being Arabic; it is *deployed* as Arabic — a ruling needs a language the judged party cannot claim to have misheard. And the mission's scope is not bounded by the tongue: the law of 14:4 explains the Arabic; it does not confine the message, which another verse sends to mankind entire. The language is the delivery system; the authority is the cargo.

**The Warning Sized to the Recipient**

“And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or shield you from Allah” (13:37). The sentence is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ and, as the commentators observed, through him to the whole community — the verse's form is a warning to the most unlikely candidate for desire-following, which is how the Qur'an sizes its warnings for everyone else. The Qur'an keeps this warning in circulation for the Prophet's ﷺ followers in exactly this form: steadfastness as commanded, no following of desires, belief in every Scripture revealed (42:15). The warning also completes the sūrah's accounting of protection: 13:11 announced that apart from Him no protector exists; 13:34 announced that none can shield the disbelievers; 13:37 applies the same arithmetic to the believer's hypothetical treason — protection is not lost by being attacked; it is lost by being traded.

**An Authority, Therefore Not a Proposal**

The verse's first word — authority, ruling — deserves the closing word, because it settles the standing of the text in one noun. A ruling does not negotiate with desire; it outlaws it. The Makkans' demand throughout this sūrah has been, in one form or another, that the revelation accommodate the town: hasten or soften the threats (13:6), display the requested signs (13:7, 13:27), meet the factions at their portions (13:36). The verse's answer is structural: what came down is a *ḥukm* — a judgment — delivered in the language of those being judged, and a judgment cannot be edited by the defendant. For the community reading after him, the verse is a constitution: the knowledge that has come — all of it, 13:1's truth, 13:2–4's signs, 13:17's sorting water — is the standing law of the heart. The verse's only open question is the one it leaves with the reader: what the desires will be permitted to do with their remaining days. The sūrah that sorted one water into many tastes (13:4) ends its argument by sorting its readers the same way: one revelation, many desires, and the ruling in Arabic to judge between them.

The Qur'an elsewhere paints the full portrait of the desire-follower, and it is not a fool: “Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god? ˹And so˺ Allah left them to stray knowingly, sealed their hearing and hearts, and placed a cover on their sight” (45:23).

## Verse 13:38

> We have certainly sent messengers before you ˹O Prophet˺ and blessed them with wives and offspring. It was not for any messenger to bring a sign without Allah’s permission. Every destined matter has a ˹set˺ time

**No Sign on Private Authority**

“It was not for any messenger to bring a sign without Allah’s permission” (13:38). The clause is repeated verbatim in another chapter, which marks it as a settled constitutional rule of the missions: the sign is never the messenger's property. The full parallel text supplies the rule's setting: “It was not for any messenger to bring a sign without Allah’s permission. But when Allah’s decree comes, judgment will be passed with fairness” (40:78) — the messenger's inability to self-authorize miracles is bracketed by the decree that will arrive anyway, with fairness attached. The rule answers the sign-demands of 13:7, 13:27, and 13:31 at their root: the Prophet ﷺ cannot produce the requested spectacle because production was never in his brief; he is the conveyor of what is given, and the giving is scheduled elsewhere. The rule also protects the office from its counterfeiters. A messenger who could signal on demand would be a freelancer; the permission clause makes every true sign an act of God's own initiative, which is why the false prophet's career is structurally impossible to sustain — he has no account to draw on. The verse so positions the whole dispute exactly where the sūrah has kept it since 13:27: between God's will and the servant's turn, with the messenger as the appointed witness in between — not the treasurer of wonders.

**Every Destined Matter Has Its Written Term**

“Every destined matter has a ˹set˺ time” (13:38). The closing clause widens from missions to everything: each decreed matter carries its term — a fixed, written tenure. The readings the commentators gave the clause agree on the essentials: God holds a fixed term for every matter He has determined, and the clause also echoes the Qur'an's larger doctrine of revelation's universality — every community's term included a Book. The doctrine has been the sūrah's spine from its second verse: “each orbiting for an appointed term” (13:2) — the heavens themselves run on tenures. The wombs' arithmetic (13:8), the guarded roads (13:11), the respite's delay (13:32), the promise's arrival (13:31): all of it is tenure-language, and this clause states the general law in one line. The creation verse of the starred chapter confirms the vocabulary: “Indeed, We have created everything, perfectly preordained” (54:49) — measured, timed, written. The clause's placement after the permission rule is deliberate: signs are held for their appointed hour (40:78's decree), and so are seizures, rescues, victories, and deaths. Nothing in this sūrah's world is unscheduled; the audience's complaint, from beginning to end, has been about scheduling — and the verse's answer is that the schedule exists, is written, and is not available for revision at the town's request.

**The Verse as the Mission's Job Description Completed**

Read together, the three clauses form the completed job description the sūrah has been assembling for its messenger. Human, like the series before him (13:38's wives and offspring) — therefore approachable, answerable, imitable in his humanity. Unauthorized, like the series before him (the permission rule) — therefore incapable of the favoritism the sign-demanders were really seeking: a private wonder for a private audience. And patient, because the term-language of the closing clause commits him to working inside schedules he cannot see. The verse also consoles the believers who watched the town's taunts (13:5–7): the Prophet's ﷺ humanity and the signs' absence were never defects awaiting repair but features built into the series from its first members. What remains scheduled — the decree of 40:78, the promise of 13:31, the seizure of 13:32 — will arrive on its written term, and the verse's last line is the sūrah's whole the teaching about the Last Day in seven words: the universe keeps its appointments, and so will its Keeper.

The verse answers an objection the Makkans had made a standing theme: that a true messenger would be otherworldly — the request recorded in another chapter, “What ails this Messenger, who eats food and walks in the markets?” (25:7), asked as though eating disqualified.

## Verse 13:39

> Allah eliminates and confirms what He wills, and with Him is the Master Record

**The Verse of Divine Editing**

“Allah eliminates and confirms what He wills, and with Him is the Master Record” (13:39). After a chapter of fixed terms and kept appointments (13:2, 13:31, 13:38), the verse announces that the ledger's entries are still editable — by its Author. The classical reading of the clause is comprehensive: He brings into existence and removes; gives life and causes death; grants wealth and strips it — elimination and confirmation run through the whole created order, not merely through texts. The verse so answers a question the sūrah's audience had not thought to ask but every reader of decree eventually asks: if everything is timed and written (13:38), is the writing itself subject to revision? The answer is yes — at one level — and the revision happens on one desk only. Creation is not a machine left running on its first settings; it is a living document under continuous authorship. What the verse refuses, with equal clarity, is the pagan conclusion: a changing record does not mean a changing author, and the clause's second half — “and with Him is the Master Record” (13:39) — anchors the edits to an unedited original, of which the next section will say more.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Administration Cipher**

Placed between the missions' terms (13:38) and the mission's deadline (13:40), the verse is the administration cipher of the whole chapter: it explains how a world of fixed terms can also be a world of continuous divine action. The orbits run on appointments (13:2), yet provision is spread and straitened by will (13:26); every matter has its written term (13:38), yet plots are undone and promises accelerated; the woman's womb is measured (13:8), yet its schedule includes eliminations no physician predicted. Flux and fixity, on the verse's showing, are not rival descriptions of reality but its two registers — the timed enactments below, the stable code above. For the Prophet ﷺ, the verse lands as both caution and comfort. The verse's short length, one sentence, is its own argument: administration this total needs no elaboration — everything beneath it, from the thunder's schedule (13:13) to the reader's remaining days, is a line item on a desk the verse has just located. The verse also supplies the sūrah's answer to the planner's question hiding in 13:38: if every matter has a written term, what exactly changed when a famine lifted or a treaty held?

The Qur'an names the tablet in its own closing chapters: “In fact, this is a glorious Quran, ˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet” (85:21–22); and names the Mother again for itself: “And indeed, it is—in the Master Record with Us—highly esteemed, rich in wisdom” (43:4).

The revealed text itself underwent ordered transmission under divine supervision: another chapter describes revelation arriving under interference and being answered — Satan's suggestions eliminated, God's verses firmly established, in that order (22:52).

The Qur'an's own promise-keeping is included in the stable side of the ledger: “There is no change in the promise of Allah” (10:64).

## Verse 13:40

> Whether We show you ˹O Prophet˺ some of what We threaten them with, or cause you to die ˹before that˺, your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. Judgment is for Us

**The Mission Stated in Two Outcomes**

The verse writes the Prophet's ﷺ contract in two clauses and refuses to let him, or his audience, confuse the outcome with the assignment. Whether We show you some of what We promise them, or cause you to die before that, your duty is only to deliver the message (13:40 gives it). Either he sees the promised punishment begin in his lifetime, or he dies before it falls. In both cases his duty is the same: delivery.

He might also fear that dying before the promise lands means the mission lapsed, and the verse rules that out too. A death inside the recorded term is part of the plan rather than a failure of it (13:38 mentions the written term). This verse completes what the earlier one began, where he was told that he is only a warner, by defining the job so completely that no outcome can prove it false.

**Delivery Without Waiting for the Audience's Verdict**

The Qur'an states the prophetic duty in this exact form several times, which marks it as teaching rather than reassurance. The Messenger's duty is only to deliver the message, and God fully knows what you reveal and what you conceal (5:99 gives both parts). Note the second half of that verse. The delivery is owed alongside an audit of what is hidden, which means the messenger's honesty, not the audience's response, is what is measured. And again: if you persist in denial, other communities before you did the same, and the Messenger's duty is only to deliver the message clearly (29:18 gives it). Denial is a pattern the delivery has to survive, not a failure of the delivery.

Defining the duty this way also closes the door the sign-demanders kept knocking on (13:7, 13:27 and 13:31 record those demands). If delivery were measured by conversions, demanding miracles would be reasonable. Since delivery is measured by clarity, the demand sits outside the contract. The Prophet's ﷺ accountability runs to the One who sent the message in the language it was sent in (13:37 mentions the language), and to no committee of hearers. The audience listening to this receives a protected man, a messenger whose standing cannot be voted down.

**Judgment Belongs to God**

The final clause assigns the reckoning to its owner and separates two offices that every failing society merges: the office of proclamation and the office of judgment. The Prophet ﷺ holds the first. The second was never his to apply. The Qur'an states the separation as the norm of the missions: the warner warns, and the reckoning is God's (88:21 and 88:22 pair the two).

The separation is what makes the mission moral at all. If the messenger judged, belief would be compliance. If God's judgment were handed to deputies, the vigilance described earlier, over every soul and every guarded road, would need watchers of its own (13:8 through 13:11 describe it). The clause is also the sūrah's last word in its long argument with the impatient. The demand in the earlier verse was, at bottom, a request to move into the judgment office and schedule the torment now. The answer closes the window: judgment belongs to God, the schedule has one keeper (13:38 gives it), and the Prophet's ﷺ signature appears nowhere on the warrant. What the mockers read as weakness, a messenger who does not strike back, is the constitution working exactly as written.

## Verse 13:41

> Do they not see that We gradually reduce their land from its borders? Allah decides—none can reverse His decision. And He is swift in reckoning

**The Edges Go First: The Mechanics of Unnoticed Judgment**

The verse's geography carries its lesson. Borders go first because borders are where attention never looks: the periphery is conceded in silence while the center keeps its festivals. The Book's ruin-inventory shows the end state of the same process: “˹Imagine˺ how many gardens and springs the tyrants left behind, as well as ˹various˺ crops and splendid residences” (44:25–26) — the centers of vanished powers, abandoned whole. Between the shrinking border and the abandoned garden stands the interval the verse is describing: a people watching their map redraw itself and calling it weather. The gradualness is not mercy misplaced but method: it gives the perceptive time to read the trend and the obstinate time to finish their refusal — the same two-audience design the respite doctrine carries (13:32). And the field trip command of 40:21 — travel and look at the ends of the seized — is this verse's retrospective: every ruined civilization in the region was once a center whose edges went first. The verse asks its audience to read the process in real time, which is the hardest reading of all, because the process is designed to be legible only to those who admit it is a process — that someone is behind it, operating at the speed of politics.

**Allah Decides: The Irreversible Jurisdiction**

“Allah decides—none can reverse His decision” (13:41). The clause moves from observation to jurisdiction: the reduction is not a trend but a ruling, and rulings of this Judge do not get appealed. The sūrah has been assembling this exclusivity all along: no protector besides Him (13:11), no shield against Him (13:34), no guide apart from Him (13:27, 13:33) — and now no reversal of Him. The negative is comprehensive because the office is single: reversals of decree are sought, in every age, from the same vendors — the idols of Makkah, the intermediaries of every later market — and the verse empties the market with one sentence. The irreversibility is also the comfort's underside, and the sūrah states both faces without embarrassment: the promise that never fails (13:31) and the judgment none can repeal (13:41) are one attribute — reliability at the scale of verdicts. For the mocked believers watching Makkah's borders quietly move, the clause is a vow: what has begun will not be un-begun. For the complacent inside the shrinking circle, it is the opposite: the process they are calling churn is a signed decision, and signed decisions in this sūrah's world have execution dates (13:38).

**Swift in Reckoning: The Speed of the Accountant**

The Book confirms the method as its standard operating procedure: “In fact, We have allowed enjoyment for these ˹Meccans˺ and their forefathers for such a long time ˹that they took it for granted˺. Do they not see that We gradually reduce ˹their˺ land from its borders?” (21:44).

## Verse 13:42

> Those ˹disbelievers˺ before them ˹secretly˺ planned, but Allah has the ultimate plan. He knows what every soul commits. And the disbelievers will soon know who will have the ultimate outcome

**The Contest of Plans, Already Decided**

The verse opens by placing the Makkans in a series of earlier nations who plotted, and it closes the contest before it starts. They plot, and God has the final plan (13:42 states it). The Book's fullest statement of the theme uses the strongest word available: they plotted, and God also planned, and God is the best of planners (8:30 states it). That sentence was spoken over the actual conspiracy to capture, kill or expel the Prophet ﷺ, the plot that dissolved into the migration that founded the community.

Another verse states the contest from heaven's own seat, where God says that they are devising their plans and He is planning too (86:15–16 say it). The first person appears there again, the same two hands of delay and seizure described earlier in this sūrah, now working in the register of strategy (13:32 mentions them). The classic instance of the pattern is told with the plotters' ignorance inside it: they made their plan, and We made Our plan, while they were unaware (27:50 states it). Unawareness is the plotters' permanent condition, because the counter-plan works invisibly. A plot that closes on its authors feels, from the inside, like success right up to the moment it shuts. This verse gives the series its verdict in advance. God has the final plan, and the outcome is not contested ground.

**He Knows What Every Soul Does**

The verse puts the audit clause into the discussion of plotting on purpose. Plots are built on a superstition: that knowledge can be fenced in, that what happens in a closed room stays there. The clause removes the superstition with the equipment this sūrah has already set up. The Vigilant One stands over every soul (13:33 names Him). A whisper is overheard (13:10 records it). What is in the wombs is known (13:8 states it), and angels write down what people do (86:4 mentions them).

Every deed is entered in a record the plotter can neither summon nor destroy, and the record includes the plot itself: its meetings, its motives and its drafts. The clause also explains how the final plan can be final without improvisation. Sovereignty over outcomes does not require interrupting them when every input is already known. A plan resting on complete knowledge looks, from inside history, like patience. This verse ties the two halves of the picture together. The plotter measures the room, and God measures the room and everyone in it. The verses around it carry the same argument about the nations that plotted before (13:41 and 13:42 work as a pair), and the Book states in another sūrah that God's way does not change, however carefully a human plan is drawn (35:43 states it).

## Verse 13:43

> The disbelievers say, “You ˹Muḥammad˺ are no messenger.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah is sufficient as a Witness between me and you, as is whoever has knowledge of the Scripture.”

**The Final Objection Is the Bare Denial**

“The disbelievers say, “You ˹Muḥammad˺ are no messenger.”” (13:43). The sūrah's last recorded objection strips every earlier argument down to its floor: no more demands for moved mountains (13:31), no more mockery of resurrection (13:5), no more requests to hasten the torment (13:6) — just the bare denial, said without equipment. The progression is itself the sūrah's argument coming to rest: a position that began with challenges ends with a flat no, because every intermediate position has been dismantled across forty verses. What remains is the personal refusal: not "this message is false" but "you are no messenger" — the objection relocated from the content to the man, which is where objections land when the content has stopped being contestable.

**The Witness Who Needs No Introducing**

“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah is sufficient as a Witness between me and you”” (13:43). The answer begins with the Witness above every court: God Himself. The Book has prepared this answer all chapter — the God who knows the whisper and the daylight walk (13:10), who reads every womb (13:8), who attends every soul's record (13:33) — is the one witness whose testimony no cross-examination can touch, because His knowledge is not derived. The Qur'an deploys the same answer against the same denial elsewhere: “Sufficient is He as a Witness between you and me” (46:8) — and that verse adds the assymetrical stakes: if the denial were true, the deniers could do nothing to save the fabricator from God; the witness is also the judge. Another verse widens the witness list to heaven's population: “Allah bears witness to what He has sent down to you—He has sent it with His knowledge. The angels too bear witness. And Allah ˹alone˺ is enough as a Witness” (4:166) — angels added, then immediately redundant before the sufficiency of the first. The answer to "you are no messenger" is therefore not a defense but an elevation: the office being denied is attested by the Being whose attestation the deniers' entire system assumes — they argue about Allah (13:13), they confess Allah's lordship when asked (13:16). Now Allah's testimony is entered on the disputed fact.

**A Chapter That Ends in a Courtroom**

It is fitting that the sūrah closes in a courtroom, because it began as one. Its first verse declared the revelation truth and predicted the disbelief of most people (13:1). Its middle sections entered the evidence — the conducted heavens (13:2), the sorted harvests (13:4), the worshipping thunder (13:13), the bankrupt idols (13:14–16), the sorted water (13:17) — and its gallery sorted the audience into two portraits with two addresses (13:19–25). Now, at the end, the last objection is met with the last exhibit: witnesses. The structure hands the reader the case file complete — evidence, verdicts, and testimony — with the verdict's execution scheduled (13:31, 13:38, 13:41) and the appeal window governed by the turn (13:27–28). The Book's own confirmation of the verse's logic is stated in its great witness verse: “Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and so are the angels and people of knowledge” (3:18) — the same witness list, testifying to the same confession this sūrah has been extracting since 13:16. The chapter's final word so leaves its audience standing where a jury stands: the evidence is in, the witnesses have spoken. The only remaining motion — belief or denial — is the reader's own, filed where all the sūrah's verses have said it must be filed: in the interior that God has been reading all along.

The Qur'an confirms the phenomenon as doctrine: the believers of the earlier revelation stand on “clear proof from their Lord, backed by ˹the Quran as˺ a witness from Him, and preceded by the Book of Moses” (11:17) — the Books witnessing to one another like servers of one chain of title.
