# Sūrah al-Raʿd (Chapter 13) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:1

> Alif-Lãm-Mĩm-Ra. These are the verses of the Book. What has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ from your Lord is the truth, but most people do not believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Letters Put the Book Inside Its Own Language**

The sūrah opens with four detached letters — alif, lām, mīm, rāʾ — spoken by no word and parsed by no grammar of the verse that follows. They stand at the head of the revelation the way a signature stands at the foot of a letter: not part of the argument, but the mark that fixes who is speaking. Commentators have offered readings of these disconnected openings since the earliest generations, and the honest summary is that their full meaning is known to God alone; what can be said with confidence is what they *do*. They announce, before any proposition is made, that the Qur'an is not being translated into Arabic from some higher language but composed in it — delivered in the very medium in which its first hearers thought, traded, recited poetry, and argued. A text that opens by gesturing at its own letters is a text calling attention to its own linguistic body, letter by letter, at the level where speech begins. The point matters for everything that follows, because the claims of this sūrah will be checked against eyes and ears: skies overhead (13:2), earth below (13:3–4), lightning and thunder in a storm (13:12–13). The letters tell the audience that the same precision they will be asked to observe in the visible world is present in the audible one — and that the Book therefore makes a claim on the same attention the world already demands.

**The Truth Is Asserted Before It Is Demonstrated**

The verse calls what was revealed “the truth,” and it does so in the indicative mood, with no proof attached. That is a deliberate rhetorical order. The sūrah declares the epistemic status of the revelation first, then spends its opening verses assembling the demonstration: the raised heavens, the orbiting sun and moon, the spread earth, the paired fruits. A reader who expects the declaration to be *earned* by the demonstrations has the order backwards; the demonstrations are illustrations of a status already fixed. The word used is *ḥaqq* — the real, the binding, that which stands when everything provisional is stripped away. The clause also locates the source precisely: what has come is “from your Lord,” the same Lord who owns the thunder and the harvest that the sūrah will soon put on display. The claim is never detached from its Owner, which is why rejecting the text will later be treated as refusing Him rather than misgrading a document.

**Which Book? Two Readings That End in the Same Place**

The phrase “these are the verses of the Book” (13:1) received two main interpretations among the commentators. Some took “the Book” to mean this sūrah itself, so that the following verses are the exhibit immediately at hand and the *verses* are its own portions; others read “the Book” as the Qur'an entire, making the opening a franchise statement for revelation as a whole. Each reading has its ground. The narrower reading suits a chapter about to argue so hard from its own inventory of signs, and it fits the way the detached-letter sūrahs speak of the Book as their own warrant. The broader reading matches the word's grander uses across the Qur'an, where the Book names the whole revealed corpus. What is striking is how little turns on the choice: on either reading, the sentence claims divine authorship for what follows, and on either reading the denial predicted at the end of the verse is the same denial. The disagreement between the exegetes is real but local; the argument they are both annotating is single and intact.

**“Most People Do Not Believe” as a Law, Not a Lament**

The closing clause sounds like a disappointment and functions like a statute. The Qur'an counts its audience often, and the count keeps coming out the same way: “And most people will not believe—no matter how keen you are—” (12:103). The same arithmetic appears near the end of the prophetic career's horizon: “The Hour is certainly coming, there is no doubt about it. But most people do not believe” (40:59). Two things follow from reading these texts together. First, unbelief is not presented as an accident of this audience or that century; it is the Qur'an's own repeated finding about human beings at large, which means the Prophet ﷺ is not being told his mission failed — he is being told in advance what its statistics will look like. Second, the majority is thereby disqualified as a measure of truth. If most people do not believe, then belief cannot be validated by counting heads, and the believer loses nothing by standing where the count says he will be standing: against the grain of his own census. The clause converts what could have been a wound into information about the world.

**Āyāt: One Word for Verses and for Signs**

The verse says “these are the verses of the Book” (13:1), and the Arabic word is *āyāt* — the same word the sūrah will use three verses later when the heavens, the earth, the fruits, and the alternation of night and day are called “signs.” One term covers both the sentences of revelation and the phenomena of creation, and the choice is not decoration. It asserts that the two sets belong to the same category: both are communications, both are meant to be read, both carry the signature of the same author. The Book's verses end in “but most people do not believe” (13:1); the world's signs will end in “for those who reflect” and “for those who understand” (13:3–4) — audience labels, and filters. The pairing quietly sets up the sūrah's whole method: the audience is not being asked to accept revelation against evidence but to read two books by one author and notice that neither contradicts the other. A people that can read the sky and the harvest can learn the grammar; the question the sūrah keeps asking is why they decline to apply it to the third book, the one recited among them.

**A Denial the Sūrah Anticipates Rather Than Fears**

Most people do not believe — and the sūrah does not end there, in resigned observation. The clause functions as an inoculation. When the disbelievers' objections arrive later in the chapter — the demand for signs (13:7), the mockery of resurrection (13:5), the denial of the messengerhood itself (13:43) — the reader has already been told that rejection is the expected case, not a surprise that requires explanation. This shapes the tone of the whole chapter. The Prophet ﷺ is never depicted in it as arguing from anxiety, and the believers are never depicted as bewildered by hostility; the opposition is a known quantity, priced in from the first verse. What remains open is not whether most people will believe but which side of the line any individual will stand on — and that is precisely the choice the sūrah will spend forty-two more verses laying out, with the signs of the cosmos on one side and the shackles of the Fire on the other.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:2

> It is Allah Who has raised the heavens without pillars—as you can see— then established Himself on the Throne. He has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term. He conducts the whole affair. He makes the signs clear so that you may be certain of the meeting with your Lord.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Answer to Denial Is Overhead**

Having declared in 13:1 that most people do not believe, the sūrah begins its case where every member of its audience stood every night of their lives: under an open sky. The argument does not open with abstract theology but with a spectacle available to shepherd and merchant alike. “It is Allah Who has raised the heavens” (13:2) — the verb is one of construction and elevation, and the subject is God personally, not nature acting on its own. The commentators also noticed whom the verse is arguing with: it answers, specifically, the deniers counted in the previous verse, and it argues from the perfect order of the natural world on the understanding that honest reflection on that order ends in belief in its Sustainer. That framing matters. The sky is not offered as decoration or as a hymn; it is offered as evidence, the first exhibit in a case that the audience is competent to judge, because the exhibit has been in front of them all their lives and costs nothing to re-examine.

**“Without Pillars” and the Logic of Sustained Order**

Commentators propose two readings of the phrase “without pillars—as you can see” (13:2), and the difference between them is smaller than it looks. On one view the heavens are held by pillars that simply cannot be seen; on the other, they are held by nothing visible at all. Either way the point lands on the same spot: whatever holds the sky up, the hold is not a made thing that you can point to, inspect, or repair — it is sustained continuously by its Maker. The parallel text makes the claim explicit: “He created the heavens without pillars—as you can see—and placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with you” (31:10). Sky and ground appear together there as the two halves of one engineered shelter, and the human being lives inside both without paying for either. Notice also what the phrase “as you can see” (13:2) does: it certifies the absence as part of the testimony. The audience is not asked to imagine invisible engineering; they are asked to look and confirm that nothing up there needs visible stilts, which makes the sustaining an act, performed at every moment, rather than a structure, built once.

**Enthronement Is a Job Description, Not a Departure**

“Then established Himself on the Throne” (13:2) has been misread, in polemics old and new, as God finishing His work and sitting down away from it. The Qur'an's own usage says the opposite. The parallel formula runs: “Surely your Lord is Allah Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne, conducting every affair” (10:3). The enthronement and the conduct of affairs are stated in the same breath, because the throne is the image of active sovereignty — the king at work, not the king on holiday. Creation in six days followed by enthronement means the universe moved, at the moment of its completion, from being God's project to being God's administration. Nothing in it is left to run itself. The verse's next clause says the same thing in administrative language: “He conducts the whole affair” (13:2). A cosmos with no vacancies in its management leaves no room for the rival managers the Makkans proposed, and that is exactly where this sūrah is headed: if the affair is conducted, the idols are unemployed. The full authority claimed in the parallel — “That is Allah—your Lord! All authority belongs to Him” (35:13) — is the same claim this verse makes quietly, by description.

**Orbits With Expiry Dates**

“He has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term” (13:2). The sentence contains an argument for a future event, and it works by a simple logic. The precision of the orbits — “The sun and the moon ˹travel˺ with precision” (55:5) — is offered to the audience as proof that the system is governed, not self-running; and the “appointed term” attached to each orbit announces that the same governed system carries a termination clause. The sun and moon are not eternal. They run on schedule toward a scheduled end, and the earlier commentators drew the natural conclusion: a term appointed means the celestial order will one day cease, that the heavens themselves are mortal equipment. The same subjection with the same term appears in a verse that ends by naming the Owner: “He merges the night into the day and the day into the night, and has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term” (35:13). Once subjection is granted, the far smaller claim — that human beings, who already die daily inside this mortal system, will be raised — loses its air of absurdity. The audience that finds resurrection incredible is asked to explain why the sun's death sentence is credible and their own is not.

**From Reliable Orbits to a Reliable Appointment**

The verse ends by stating what all this astronomy is for: “He makes the signs clear so that you may be certain of the meeting with your Lord” (13:2). The structure of the argument deserves attention, because it runs in the direction skeptics least expect. One might think celestial regularity proves only that God made a fine clock. The verse treats it as evidence for a personal appointment: the God who has never once missed the sun's morning entrance can be relied upon for the meeting He has promised. Certainty — the text says they will be certain, not merely hopeful — is grounded in a track record. This is why the signs are described as made clear rather than made impressive: their function is evidentiary, the establishing of a pattern of perfect reliability, and the pattern's only remaining undelivered clause is the meeting. A reader who accepts the sun as God's punctual servant has already conceded everything the doctrine of the meeting needs. The sūrah's opening pair of verses thus sets its terms: revelation is true (13:1), and the reason to believe its promises is standing above your head in plain sight (13:2).

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:3

> And He is the One Who spread out the earth and placed firm mountains and rivers upon it, and created fruits of every kind in pairs. He covers the day with night. Surely in this are signs for those who reflect.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Argument Moves Downstairs**

Verse 2 looked up; verse 3 looks down, and the pronouns tighten as it does. “He is the One Who” (13:3) — the sentence insists on singularity at every clause, spread, placed, created, covers, as though stacking a load-bearing wall of verbs on one Subject. The demonstrative opening also ties this verse to the last: the One being pointed at is the enthroned conductor of the heavens, now seen doing ground work. The earth is “spread out” as a dwelling is spread with a carpet: the image is of preparation for habitation, not of a geometric verdict about the planet's shape. The commentators of the classical period were already alive to that question; Ibn Juzayy's resolution was that every stretch of ground is locally level to the people standing on it while the whole remains round — a reading that took the text at its word about what the verse is actually doing, which is describing the human experience of the world as a usable floor. Mountains and rivers come next, and they come together because they belong together in the design: the mountains anchor, the rivers deliver. The sūrah is building an inventory of what a resident of the earth receives without asking, and each item is added as evidence in the case opened at 13:1 against unbelief.

**Pegs That Hold, Rivers That Serve**

“He placed firm mountains and rivers upon it” (13:3). The pairing is functional. The parallel verse names the mountains' job directly: “He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you, as well as rivers, and pathways so you may find your way” (16:15). The mountains are described there with a purpose clause — so that it does not shake with you — and the phrasing makes the mountain range a service to human beings rather than scenery. Classical exegesis, reading such texts, understood the mountains as stabilizers driven into the crust like pegs into sand. Rivers are their complement: where the peg fixes, the water flows, and agriculture, trade, and settlement follow the rivers' courses throughout history. The verse offers the two together because together they answer the two oldest human anxieties about land — that it will move, and that it will starve. The design answers both, permanently, and without any invoice presented to the beneficiaries. That absence of an invoice is part of the argument: a provider who charges nothing for the service is not a merchant but a Lord, and the sūrah's coming project of unmasking the idols as unemployed intermediaries depends on exactly that distinction.

**Pairs: Two Readings, One Point**

“Created fruits of every kind in pairs” (13:3) has attracted two main interpretations, and the disagreement is instructive. Some read the pairing botanically: each kind of fruit comes in male and female, the pollination and reproduction of plants being invisible to most observers then and now. Others read it as pairing of qualities — light and dark colors, sweet and sour tastes — so that every kind arrives with its own contrasting partner. Each side has a reason. The botanical reading gains force from the fact of plant sexual reproduction, which the Qur'an's first audience could not have examined, and it sits naturally with a doxology that names “their genders”: “Glory be to the One Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs —˹be it˺ what the earth produces, their genders, or what they do not know!” (36:36). The qualitative reading gains force from immediate visibility, since anyone who has eaten two dates from one bunch has met the pairing. On balance the readings are not rivals but scales of one claim: the pairing operates at every level the observer chooses to examine. The parallel text generalizes deliberately — “And We created pairs of all things so perhaps you would be mindful” (51:49) — and the Qur'anic clause “what they do not know” (36:36) quietly extends the pairing beyond what any exegete can enumerate. Whether the pairs are sexes or tastes, the fruit tree is announcing its Author in stereo.

**Night Made to Fall on the Day**

“He covers the day with night” (13:3). The verb is one of enveloping, a garment drawn over, and the image carries two messages at once. The first is mastery: night is not the default state that the sun merely interrupts, but a covering placed deliberately by the same hand that spread the day. The second is mercy. The alternation is described elsewhere in strictly functional terms: “He is the One Who has made the night for you to rest in and the day bright” (10:67). Work and rest are built into the sky as an alternating rhythm no one voted on and no one has ever been able to suspend. The great creation passage binds the whole system to God's command: “He makes the day and night overlap in rapid succession. He created the sun, the moon, and the stars—all subjected by His command” (7:54). To cover is also to limit: the day cannot extend itself by one minute into the night's jurisdiction. For an audience tempted to think the luminaries divine, a light that can be switched off on schedule is a decisive argument — divinities are not extinguished nightly, but equipment is.

**The Closing Formula Is a Filter**

“Surely in this are signs for those who reflect” (13:3). The verse ends not with a fact but with a description of the audience the facts are addressed to. The formula will recur at 13:4 with a different faculty — “for those who understand” — and the two terms form a ladder: reflection is the exercise of thought on what is observed, understanding the grasp of what the reflection is *for*. The phrasing quietly divides the audience in two. The signs are not hidden; what is in question is the activity of the observer. A person can stand in the middle of this triple inventory — mountains, rivers, paired fruits, covered days — and register none of it, the way a man can live in a house without ever looking at its foundations. The Qur'an treats that refusal as a moral act, not a limitation of intellect, which is why the formula functions as both invitation and diagnosis. It tells the thoughtful that their thinking has an object worth the effort, and it tells the indifferent exactly what they are declining. It also consoles the herald: the failure, when it comes, is a failure of attention in the audience, not a defect in the evidence.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:4

> And on the earth there are ˹different˺ neighbouring tracts, gardens of grapevines, ˹various˺ crops, palm trees—some stemming from the same root, others standing alone. They are all irrigated with the same water, yet We make some taste better than others. Surely in this are signs for those who understand.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Field Experiment With One Held-Constant Variable**

Verse 3 ended with paired fruits; verse 4 widens the lens from the tree to the landscape. “Neighbouring tracts” — adjacent plots of unlike produce: vineyards beside grain fields beside date groves, palm trees joined at the root beside palms standing on their own. The verse then fixes the one variable that matters: “They are all irrigated with the same water” (13:4). Anyone who has farmed knows what that sentence is doing. It is the design of a controlled comparison, stated in the language of a farmer fourteen centuries before the phrase existed. Same rain, same irrigation — different grapes, grains, and dates, differing in size, color, sweetness, and yield. The verse even footnotes its own botany: some palms spring from a shared root, others stand on their own stock, and both reach the same sky and drink the same water. The dead earth itself is part of the demonstration: “There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat” (36:33), and the produce is elaborated in the verse that follows: “And We have placed in it gardens of palm trees and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth in it,” (36:34). The question the design forces is the one the verse will not let the audience dodge: if the water is identical, what accounts for the harvest's differences?

**The Difference the Water Cannot Explain**

The naturalist's answer is ready to hand, and the Qur'an does not deny its first half: the seeds differ, the soils differ, the rootstocks differ. Of course the outputs differ — the inputs were never the same. But that answer has a floor under it, and the commentators pressed down until they reached it. Where did the differentiating seeds come from? No soil manufactures its own seed; no seed arranges its own genetic furniture. If the difference in produce is traced back through soil to seed and back through seed to its origin, the chain of differentiation begins at a source that is neither the water nor the earth, and that source is the one the verse names. The argument, developed carefully, is not that soil science is false but that it is *derivative* — it explains how each harvest unfolds from its seed, and it falls silent at the question of why the seeds differ at all. A chain of natural causes is still a chain, and every chain in this world hangs from a hook nobody in the chain manufactured. That is what the same water is for: it removes the trivial answer so the real question stands exposed in the middle of the field.

**Preference as a Statement of Ownership**

The grammar of the central clause carries the theology. The verse does not say “some taste better” and leave the differentiator anonymous; it says “yet We make some taste better than others” (13:4), with the divine “We” inside the sentence, claiming the differentiation as an act. Taste is the most ordinary of human experiences, and the verse puts God's agency inside it. Two neighbors eat dates from trees drinking the same water, and one date is honey and the other is chaff — the verse says that gap is authored. The claim has a polemical edge aimed at the pagan division of labor. A pantheon assigns minor gods to minor departments — a deity of the vine, a deity of the palm — and the verse collapses the departments: the one “We” runs every plot at once. This has a moral tail as well. To attribute the superiority of one's own harvest to one's own cleverness is, on the verse's showing, a misreading of a signature: the farmer tended, but God distinguished. For an audience whose wealth rested on trade in exactly such produce, the lesson is pointed — the goods you sell are differences God made. The verse thus functions simultaneously as cosmology and as an audit of pride, and in the Qur'an's economy the second function is not incidental to the first: noticing who made the difference is the root of worship, and misattributing it is the root of idolatry.

**Reflection, Then Understanding**

The verse ends with the formula “signs for those who understand” (13:4), and placed beside the previous verse's “signs for those who reflect” (13:3), the two closings describe a sequence rather than a redundancy. Reflection is the mind at work on the given — watching the same water, noticing the different fruit. Understanding is the mind arriving at what the noticing means: that differentiation is a decision, and decisions require a decider. The sūrah walks its audience up the two steps in order, one verse each, and the walk has a direction: from observation toward ownership, from what the world shows to whom the world belongs. The materials for the conclusion are public, abundant, and ordinary; the evidence is arranged at the level of daily bread precisely so that no class of people is exempt from it. Kings and farmers eat from the same kinds of trees. The audience filter of these closing formulas is therefore not education or rank but attention — and attention, unlike intellect, is something every hearer controls. The next verse will show what happens to those who look at all this and still ask their mocking question about the dust.

The field's lesson, in the end, is small enough to carry and large enough to live by: the same water is everybody's; the difference is nobody's but God's.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:5

> ˹Now,˺ if anything should amaze you ˹O Prophet˺, then it is their question: “When we are reduced to dust, will we really be raised as a new creation?” It is they who have disbelieved in their Lord. It is they who will have shackles around their necks. And it is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Amazement Reassigned to Its Proper Object**

The verse performs a transfer of wonder. The Prophet ﷺ might reasonably be astonished by the denial itself — a messenger known to be truthful, rejected by people who concede his truthfulness in other settings. The commentary tradition put the sense precisely: if anything should astonish you, it is not that they reject you but that they deny the resurrection — that a people who watch the sky run on schedule and the dead earth revive each spring should find the return of the human soul incredible. The verse redirects the amazement onto the questioners' own words: “if anything should amaze you ˹O Prophet˺, then it is their question” (13:5). Note what makes the question amazing rather than merely mistaken. It is asked with confidence — the sneer is built into the grammar — as though the denial were the sober position and belief in resurrection the superstition. The Qur'an throughout treats that confidence inversion as the true scandal. The mockers are not doubting something unclear; they are certain about something they could not possibly know, and uncertain about the God whose works surround them on every side. Wonder, in this verse, is an instrument that has been pointed the wrong way by its owner.

**What the Mocking Question Concedes**

“When we are reduced to dust, will we really be raised as a new creation?” (13:5). The question is a rhetorical staple of the denial, recorded in nearly the same words elsewhere: “And they say ˹mockingly˺, “When we are reduced to bones and ashes, will we really be raised as a new creation?”” (17:49). Read as evidence, the question makes two concessions and one denial. It concedes that death happens and that dissolution into dust is the ordinary course — the objectors are not idealists denying decay. It concedes, implicitly, that resurrection is at least thinkable, since a question needs a concept to mock. What it denies is precedent: no one has come back, therefore no one will. The Qur'an's answer, given at length elsewhere, goes at the denial directly: “And they argue with Us— forgetting they were created—saying, “Who will give life to decayed bones?”” (36:78) — and the reply fixes the reasoning: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being” (36:79). The first creation is the standing precedent. The objector argues from the absence of a second creation while standing inside the first, and the reasoning of 36:79 is that the first is the harder act — making something from nothing exceeds reassembling what once existed and merely returned to where it came from.

**Shackles as a Portrait of the Questioner's Mind**

“It is they who will have shackles around their necks” (13:5). The image is physical punishment, but it is also the exact counter-image of the question just quoted. A shackle at the neck forces the head into one position; the neck that asked the mocking question was already fixed, unable to look anywhere but along its own line of doubt. Elsewhere the Qur'an draws the same figure for a different crowd: “˹It is as if˺ We have put shackles around their necks up to their chins, so their heads are forced up,” (36:8) — heads forced up so that they cannot even see the road under their feet. The correspondence between the questioners and the chained is not accidental in the Qur'an's way of showing things: the punishment completes the posture the person chose in life. These are the people who asked whether the dust could be raised; the chain is the answer rendered in iron — the creature who was certain that nothing follows dust will keep his certainty, in the one place where certainty can no longer be revised. The verse's sequence is therefore not three unrelated threats but a portrait: the question, the frozen neck, the closed residence. It is also the chapter's own spine made visible — the disbelief declared in 13:1 is here given its final posture and address.

**Why “Forever” Is Not an Overcharge**

“They will be there forever” (13:5). The eternity of the Fire is the hardest of the doctrine's edges, on the theory that a finite life cannot earn an infinite penalty. The verse's logic runs the other way. The sin defined throughout this passage is not an act of the moment but a settled verdict about reality — disbelief in one's Lord, the refusal of the Source of one's existence — and a refusal of that kind does not terminate when the refuser does, because the One refused does not terminate. The punishment matches the shape of the offence: a denial without expiry date meets an abode without expiry date. Three considerations sharpen the point. First, the guilty are described as residents, not as passing convicts — residence is the vocabulary of home, and it implies the state has become native to them. Second, the alternative offered by the Qur'an for the saved is described in the same vocabulary of permanence, which means eternity here is not a separate punishment policy but the ordinary weight of a settled outcome. Third, the forever answers the certainty: the questioners were sure “really” — and certainty about the wrong thing is precisely what a forever preserves. The verse gives their own words the last emphasis before the sentence falls.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:6

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment rather than grace, though there have ˹already˺ been ˹many˺ torments before them. Surely your Lord is full of forgiveness for people, despite their wrongdoing, and your Lord is truly severe in punishment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Dare Hidden in the Request**

“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment rather than grace” (13:6). A request for punishment sounds like a thirst for justice, and it is nothing of the kind. The commentators who read the demand as mockery got it right, and the reason is structural: a person who asks heaven to hurry its punishment is a person who believes the punishment will never arrive. The demand is a dare wearing judicial robes. The same dare recurs through the Qur'an with the same sneer: “They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment” (22:47). The answer given there dismantles the dare's hidden premise — that delay means absence: “And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting” (22:47). The requesters are measuring eternity with their own calendar and concluding that silence in the sky is vacancy in the throne. The Prophet ﷺ is told elsewhere to reply in kind: “Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Perhaps some of what you seek to hasten is close at hand”” (27:72). The dare, in other words, is answered not with argument but with an appointment — and the answerer remains, as this sūrah will insist at 13:7, a warner rather than a scheduler. A dare, after all, is a challenge issued to a power one does not believe in; the verse replies by naming the power — full of forgiveness, truly severe — and leaves the dare hanging in front of its addressee.

**Torments Already on the Historical Record**

“Though there have ˹already˺ been ˹many˺ torments before them” (13:6). The verse meets the dare with the archive. The nations that preceded the Makkans — and every listener knew the stories, and could stand in the ruins that the caravans stood in — had heard their own messengers and made their own demands, and the torment came on schedule. The record is the answer to the implied claim that this generation will be the exception. Nothing in the verse suggests the Makkans doubted the old stories; the point of citing them is that precedent destroys the dare's confidence without needing a new miracle. The old cases also share a shape worth stating: a messenger arrives, the people demand the impossible or mock the inevitable, a term runs out, and the seizure comes — not late, and never early. This is a consistent method in the Qur'an: the past is treated as a public document whose lesson no one can claim was delivered privately. A generation that asks to hasten the torment has persuaded itself that it is unlike the people who were seized. The verse will not spend its words refuting that self-image directly; it simply sets the archive beside the request and lets the reader draw the conclusion.

**Forgiveness Already in Circulation**

“Surely your Lord is full of forgiveness for people, despite their wrongdoing” (13:6). The clause is more radical than a promise of pardon; it describes forgiveness as an ongoing state — He is, right now, Possessed of Forgiveness, even while the wrongdoing is in progress. Forgiveness here is not a queue that opens after repentance; it is standing inventory, waiting to be drawn on. The same doctrine in direct address: “O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins” (39:53). Placed against the dare of the verse's first half, the clause exposes what the dare-makers have misread. They take the withheld torment as indifference or weakness. The verse re-frames the delay as forgiveness in motion — every day the sky does not fall is a day the account remains open, and the open account is mercy, not neglect. The people demanding the torment are living inside the answer to a prayer they never made: they are alive, unseized, and invited. The world, on this showing, is an interval of amnesty, and the demand to end the interval is an argument against the demanders — a demand that mercy stop being patient, when patience is precisely what mercy looks like while extended to the unrepentant.

**Severity as the Second Half of One Justice**

“And your Lord is truly severe in punishment” (13:6). The verse holds its two attributes in one breath and refuses to dissolve either. Forgiveness is described with richness — “full of” — severity with certainty — “truly.” The commentators' reading preserves the logic: the forgiveness operates on repentance, and the severity on refusal; pardon for those who turn, severity for those who take the standing mercy as a verdict in their favor. The pairing is not a contradiction to be managed but a single justice viewed from its two faces. A lord who forgave everything would make wrongdoing cost nothing, and a lord who punished everything would make repentance pointless; the verse's audience is being told, with equal clarity, that the door is open and that the door will close. Notice, finally, the order of the clauses: forgiveness is stated first, severity second. The ordering itself is a message about the Speaker's disposition — mercy is the default on display, severity the edge kept sheathed. The severity clause also answers the dare one last time. The Makkans ask to hasten the torment as though torment were a bluff. The verse's answer: your Lord's forgiveness is full and His severity is real, and the only variable left in the equation is which of them you will meet.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:7

> The disbelievers say, “If only a sign could be sent down to him from his Lord.” You ˹O Prophet˺ are only a warner. And every people had a guide.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Demand for Proof That Ignores the Proof Arriving Daily**

The disbelievers' request sounds modest — one sign, sent down — but the sūrah has just spent three verses walking them through signs that require no sending because they never left: the raised heavens, the orbited sun and moon, the spread earth, the neighboring tracts drinking one water. The demand is for a bespoke miracle, engineered on the spot for this audience. The same request is documented at 6:37 with the reply attached: “They ask, “Why has no ˹other˺ sign been sent down to him from his Lord?” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah certainly has the power to send down a sign”—though most of them do not know” (6:37). Read the two clauses of that answer together: the power is conceded — Allah certainly has it — and the refusal is explained — most of them do not know. The sign is withheld not because heaven cannot produce it but because the requesters have misdiagnosed their own problem. A people who decline the signs already standing in the sky and the harvest would decline a sent-down sign too; the missing ingredient is not evidence but willingness, and willingness is the one thing a miracle cannot manufacture.

**Warner, Not Performer**

The answer to the demand is a job description: “You ˹O Prophet˺ are only a warner” (13:7). The word “only” draws the office's boundary with unusual bluntness. The warner delivers the warning; he does not schedule the disaster, coerce the audience, or stage productions to entertain the skeptics into belief. The Qur'an states the same limits at the end of its own recital: “So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind” (88:21), followed at once by “You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺” (88:22). Two consequences follow. First, the demand for signs misunderstands what the messenger is for: a sign would make belief a piece of involuntary arithmetic, and the whole moral weight of responding to warning would evaporate. Second, the warner's office keeps the Prophet ﷺ free of the one anxiety that would otherwise consume him — the illusion that outcomes were his to force. He transmits; the receiving is another matter. The verse thereby also answers the disbelievers' implied charge that a true messenger would dazzle on demand: dazzling was never in the contract.

**“Every People Had a Guide”: The Universal Pattern**

“And every people had a guide” (13:7). The verb tense matters: had. The pattern is stated as completed fact — every community, without exception, has received its guide, and the Qur'an draws the administrative consequence: “And for every community there is a messenger. After their messenger has come, judgment is passed on them in all fairness, and they are not wronged” (10:47). The guide's arrival is therefore not decoration but jurisdiction: once the guide has come and spoken, the people's record is complete and judgment proceeds with perfect fairness — the verse is emphatic that they are not wronged. For the Makkans, the sentence lands with a specific weight. Their guide is not a story from a vanished nation; he is standing among them, reciting. The demand for a sign from his Lord comes from inside the very arrangement — guide sent, warning delivered — that the sign-demanders claim is insufficient. The pattern also quietly explains the previous verse's anxiety about hastened torment (13:6): the torment follows the guide's completed delivery, on schedule, and the schedule is not moved by heckling.

**The Warner and the Guide in One Verse**

The verse gives the Prophet ﷺ two titles in two clauses — warner and (by implication of the pattern) part of the guide-tradition — and the pairing has its own history. Ibn ʿAjībah records that when this verse came down, the Prophet ﷺ said to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, “I am the warner and you, O ʿAlī, are the guide.” [UNVERIFIED: this report as cited by Ibn ʿAjībah — wording and chain not independently checked]. Whatever one makes of the report, the verse itself requires no assistance to make its structure clear. The warner warns; the guide shows the road; and the same divine economy that sent a guide to every people sends the warning ahead of the judgment. The Makkans are being told that the questions they keep asking — hurry the torment (13:6), produce a sign (13:7) — are questions addressed to the wrong office. The warner does not move dates, and the guide does not perform tricks. Both offices have one function: to place the truth before the people so that whatever follows is recognizably their own doing.

**What the Demand Would Have Destroyed**

It is worth pressing the logic one step further, because the verse's economy of roles defends something larger than the Prophet's ﷺ comfort. Suppose the sign were sent down as requested. The audience would then believe in the way witnesses of a demolition believe in explosives — involuntarily, and with no credit to themselves. The Qur'an's whole anthropology rests on the opposite arrangement: signs sufficient for attention, warnings sufficient for decision, and then a verdict that measures the decision. That is why the pattern of “every people had a guide” ends in fairness rather than force. The verse thus closes the door on the skeptics' bargain — we will believe what we cannot refuse — and keeps open the only door under which belief is still belief: the door the warner holds, with the guide's road behind it and the appointment of 13:6 somewhere ahead on it.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:8

> Allah knows what every female bears and what increases and decreases in the wombs. And with Him everything is determined with precision.

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Sky's Orbits to the Womb's Arithmetic**

The sūrah pivots without warning from the largest visible system to the smallest hidden one. Verse 2 managed the heavens and their orbits; verse 8 reads what every female carries before she knows it herself. The juxtaposition is the argument. A Lord whose administration covers the skies does not lose track of the matters nearest to a person — indeed the very scale of the first makes the completeness of the second credible. An audience might suspect that a God of galaxies attends to galaxies only; the verse closes that gap in a single sentence. The same administrative hand that set the sun and moon on appointed terms (13:2) holds the ledger of every gestation on earth. And the choice of womb as the example is deliberate: it is the place where human ignorance is most absolute and human stakes are highest. What a mother most wants to know, she cannot know; what the verse asserts is that the ignorance has an opposite number — a knowledge, complete and current, held elsewhere.

**The Knowledge No Womb Owns**

“What increases and decreases in the wombs” (13:8) was read by the commentators as the varying length of gestation — the months that lengthen and shorten from pregnancy to pregnancy. Other readings widen the field: whether the child will be male or female, sound or otherwise, and what its provision and term will be. The parallel verse wraps all of it into one claim: “No female ever conceives or delivers without His knowledge. And no one’s life is made long or cut short but is ˹written˺ in a Record” (35:11) — and note how that verse brackets the womb between dust at one end and the recorded term at the other, making conception one station on a line drawn before either parent met. Even the plant world obeys the same disclosure schedule: “No fruit comes out of its husk, nor does a female conceive or deliver without His knowledge” (41:47). The pairing of fruit and fetus in one sentence is telling. The emerging bud and the emerging child belong to the same category of events: beginnings, hidden until they announce themselves, and never hidden from the One who scheduled them. The verse does not merely say God knows such things eventually; the knowledge is present at the moment when no microscope in the world could find the object known.

**Determined With Precision: The Vocabulary of Decree**

The closing clause — “And with Him everything is determined with precision” (13:8) — moves from God's knowledge to the settled measure behind it. The word for precision is the vocabulary of decree: the same root that says “Indeed, We have created everything, perfectly preordained” (54:49), and the same idea as “He has created everything, ordaining it precisely” (25:2). Everything, the text says — not only wombs but the whole inventory this sūrah has been walking through: orbits, mountains, rivers, fruits, the alternation of night and day. Each has its measure, and the measure is not approximate. This is where the verse quietly meets the deepest theological question the Makkans never asked and every generation since has asked: whether the world runs on chance, on fate, or on care. The verse answers with a third option that is neither of the pagan guesses — the world runs on measured decree, known and set by a knowing Lord. Chance is eliminated as an account of reality; the astrologer's fatalism is eliminated as an account of knowledge, because the measure is with Him, not with the stars.

**What the Knowledge Is For**

The verse's comfort and its warning are the same fact. To the anxious, it says nothing about your origin, your children, or your term is unmanaged; the knowledge that outruns yours was never going to misplace you. The famous verse of the five unknowns puts the boundary in place: “Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ has the knowledge of the Hour. He sends down the rain, and knows what is in the wombs. No soul knows what it will earn for tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die” (31:34). Every item in that list is a thing human beings scheme to learn by other means — the astrologer, the diviner, the omen-reader — and the verse assigns the whole department to God alone. The precision clause also sets the terms for the verse that follows: because everything is measured with Him, His knowledge can be described without remainder as knowing the seen and the unseen (13:9). The womb was the proof case; the attribute is the general law.

**The Hidden Womb Refutes the Hidden Gods**

There is a polemical edge here that should not be smoothed away. The idols of the Makkans claimed jurisdictions — this one for fertility, that one for fortune — and the verse walks into the one department no rival was ever able to plausibly claim and takes it without argument: knowledge of what is unborn, unformed, and increasing or decreasing in secret. A god who could be cheated by a hidden pregnancy is not in the same business as the One who authored it. The sequence of this sūrah now becomes legible as an argument in three steps: the affairs are conducted (13:2), the conduct includes the wombs (13:8), and the conductor is the Knower of the seen and the unseen (13:9). Each step borrows the authority of the last, and the idols drop out of the accounting at every stage — not refuted by argument so much as found to be already absent from the books.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:9

> ˹He is the˺ Knower of the seen and the unseen—the All-Great, Most Exalted.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shortest Verse, the Largest Claim**

This is the sūrah's shortest verse, and it functions as a name. Verse 8 stated what God knows; verse 9 states what God *is* — the Knower of the seen and the unseen — as an attribute that identifies Him. The construction matters theologically. The verse does not say God knows everything; it says knowledge of everything is who He is, in the same breath by which other verses make the confession that has no partners in it: “He is Allah—there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him: Knower of the seen and unseen” (59:22). The attribute is welded to the shahāda. That welding is an argument: a rival god could only be a rival if it knew something the one God does not, and the verse has just emptied that possibility of all content. The verse also closes with greatness and exaltation, as though to seal the attribute against diminishment — the knowledge in question is not surveillance but the total grasp of the Great and Exalted, before whom seen and unseen are one undivided field.

**Seen and Unseen as One Field**

The pairing covers everything without exception, and the Qur'an delights in showing the two extremes of the range in a single sentence. At the small end: “Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth” (6:59) — and that same verse opens the department completely: “With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him” (6:59). The leaf is the chosen example precisely because nothing could matter less; a knowledge that reaches one falling leaf cannot be pictured as the knowledge of important things only. At the large end stands the Hour itself, the unseen above all unseen. Between leaf and Hour, the verse of this sūrah asserts a single continuous knowledge with no departments left over — no province of the real where the knowledge is partial, inferred, or delegated. The seen is known without eyes; the unseen is known without intermediaries. It is this completeness that lets the next verse apply the attribute to the most private human acts, and it is this completeness that the idols could not survive.

**Exalted Above What They Associate**

The verse's closing pair — “the All-Great, Most Exalted” (13:9) — carries the polemic in its adjective. A parallel text makes the connection explicit: “˹He is the˺ Knower of the seen and unseen. Exalted is He above what they associate ˹with Him˺” (23:92). The grammar of idolatry requires comparison: partners are only thinkable as comparable, near-rivals, department heads alongside a chairman. Total knowledge breaks the comparison at its root. What can be associated with a knowledge that has no outside? The pagan system worked, insofar as it worked, by ignorance — gods who did not know what was in hearts could be flattered, bargained with, and cheated. The verse removes the operating condition. Exaltation here is not a mood word; it is the statement of an epistemic gap so total that the word "partner" loses its meaning on contact. This is why the attribute appears in 59:22 as the opening of the divine self-naming and why this sūrah places it immediately before its most uncomfortable verse about human hiding: the attribute is the reason the hiding will not work.

**Why Total Knowledge Reads as Mercy and Not Only as Threat**

A verse about being fully known can be read as menace, and the sūrah guards against that reading by its placement and its vocabulary. The attribute arrives directly after the verse about wombs (13:8), where the knowledge was protective — the measured child, the recorded term, the matters no parent can manage being managed. The same verse that frightened the cheating pagan console the believer: the Lord who knows the unseen knows the unspoken prayer, the hidden intention behind an ambiguous act, the grief that never found words. Nothing in the believer's life is misfiled. And because the knowledge is precise — the previous verse said “with precision” — it does not confuse the secret righteousness with the public show, which is the specific comfort of those whose best works are the ones nobody saw. The Great and Exalted, in the verse's own order, is first the Knower; greatness without that knowledge would be power without perception, and the verse refuses that picture of God as firmly as it refuses the idols who needed informants.

**The Verse as Hinge**

Placed where it is, the verse is the hinge between the sūrah's two halves of its opening movement. Behind it: the creation inventory (13:2–4) and the hidden things (13:8) — evidence that the world is conducted, not abandoned. Ahead of it: the application (13:10) — your whisper and your daylight walk, your midnight lurking and your morning traffic, all equally known — and then the guarded road of 13:11, where angels front and rear escort every soul by the command of the One who misses nothing. The hinge holds because the attribute is stated absolutely. If God knew only the seen, the angels would matter as informants; if He knew only the unseen, the seen would be ungoverned. Knowing both, He needs no spies and governs no exceptions — and the sūrah's audience, on both sides of belief, now knows exactly what kind of account they are keeping their lives in.

The verse's economy also explains its endurance in the daily prayer of believers, where the attribute is recited as confession rather than argument. A community that says the Knower of the seen and the unseen nightly is not proving a theorem; it is keeping the sūrah's arithmetic warm — the whispered act known (13:10), the guarded road escorted (13:11), the record kept (13:33) — all filed under one name, recited as both comfort and audit.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:10

> It is the same ˹to Him˺ whether any of you speaks secretly or openly, whether one hides in the darkness of night or goes about in broad daylight.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse About Hiding Places**

The verse enumerates the complete human taxonomy of concealment: the whisper, the public declaration, the midnight lurker, the daylight walker. Four modes, and the verse rules them all identical — “It is the same ˹to Him˺” (13:10). The equality asserted is not moral but epistemic: the four acts differ in visibility to other people and do not differ at all in visibility to God. The verse is a direct application of the attribute just named. If He is the Knower of the seen and the unseen (13:9), then the secret/open and night/day distinctions — the load-bearing walls of all human privacy — mark no boundary in His knowledge. Notice the craft of the list: it includes not only deeds but speech, and within speech both the hidden and the broadcast, so that no hearer can locate himself outside the verse's scope. Whoever you are, one of the four lines describes your situation at this moment, and the verse says God's knowledge finds you exactly there, unchanged by the darkness or the crowd.

**The Verse Descends From the Attribute**

The sequence of these three verses is itself an argument, and it is worth tracing. Verse 8: God knows the womb's contents — knowledge of the hidden future. Verse 9: He is the Knower of the seen and the unseen — the general attribute. Verse 10: therefore your whisper and your daylight walk are one to Him — the personal consequence. The sūrah moves from the far case to the general law to the near case, and the near case is the one that pricks. A hearer might nod at divine knowledge of unborn children and divine knowledge of falling leaves, and feel untouched by either. The tenth verse removes that distance: the attribute means *you*, tonight, and the thing you said only to yourself this afternoon. The Qur'an's method here is consistent — establish the doctrine in its largest frame, then walk it into the hearer's room. The verse does not accuse; it equalizes. Before God, the loudest hypocrite and the quietest believer stand in the same light, and the difference between them will be what is actually in them, not what anyone was able to see.

**For Self-Scrutiny, Not Just Surveillance**

The commentators pressed the verse's purpose beyond surveillance, and they were right to. A text that only meant "you are watched" would produce a audience of performers who behave when observed; this verse, read with the ones before it, aims at something inward — vigilance over one's own thoughts, and the self-audit of the heart. The supporting texts put the matter in the heart's own vocabulary: “Whether you speak secretly or openly—He surely knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart” (67:13), and again, “He certainly knows what is secret and what is even more hidden” (20:7) — the even-more-hidden being, as the commentators said, what a person has not yet formulated even to himself. The parallel verse keeps God's knowledge tied to His oneness: “He knows whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal, and knows whatever you do” (6:3). The design of the verse, then, is to make the heart its own witness. A person who has internalized that the whisper is already public in one direction — toward God — stops negotiating with an imaginary audience and starts asking what is actually in him. That shift is the beginning of the fear-and-hope economy that this sūrah will demand in its storm verses.

**The Idols Need Spies; God Does Not**

There is a polemic folded into the verse, and it aims at the whole institution of intermediaries. A pantheon runs on information flow: the devotee must inform the god, attract its attention, alert it to needs and offenses — the entire apparatus of offering and incantation exists because the small gods are not assumed to know. The verse ends that economy in one clause. A God for whom whisper and proclamation are identical needs no informing, no alerting, no attracting. This is why the verse's equality — “the same ˹to Him˺” — is a denial of the idols' business model and not merely a statement about God. It also anticipates the sūrah's coming question to the idolaters (13:33): will you inform Him of something He does not know? The verse of the hiding places has already settled the answer. The whisper was never a whisper; the dark was never dark; and the god who needed to be told was never in the business the worshipper thought.

**What the Equality Demands Next**

The verse closes the hiding option, and the next verse opens the guarded road — angels before and behind every soul (13:11). The two verses belong together as a sentence and its subject. The person who cannot hide is the same person being escorted; the knowledge that found the midnight lurker is the knowledge whose command station the escort. Between the two stands a lesson about what to do with a life that cannot be concealed: live it as if it is being read, because it is — and the reading, as the sūrah will insist, reaches all the way to what is in the soul, which is exactly the place where the coming verse will say that change must begin.

The verse's list also reads as an inventory of the era's hiding technologies: the whisper for speech, the night for movement, the crowd for the rest. Each technology is real — they hide from neighbors effectively — and each is declared irrelevant to the one Audience who matters. The verse thus leaves privacy intact between people and abolishes it only in one direction, which is the exact shape of a life lived before God.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:11

> For each one there are successive angels before and behind, protecting them by Allah’s command. Indeed, Allah would never change a people’s state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺. And if it is Allah’s Will to torment a people, it can never be averted, nor can they find a protector other than Him.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Guard Detail: A Life Under Escort**

“For each one there are successive angels before and behind, protecting them by Allah’s command” (13:11). The verse opens with the word for each one — no exemptions, no unwatched lives — and with the image of succession: angels taking post before and behind, relieving one another, so that the escort is continuous. The commentators tie the guard to the verse before it: the one who lurks by night and goes forth by day (13:10) is the one being escorted, and the lurker's escort applies precisely because the lurking was never hidden. What the angels do is specified: they protect, by the command of God — and the phrase by His command matters as much as the escort itself. The angels are not independent guardians with their own policies; they execute orders. The picture drawn is of a life conducted under supervision at every step, arrival and departure both covered. Comfort and warning travel in the same image: nothing reaches you unsearched, and nothing leaves you unrecorded.

**The Change Verse: The Hinge Is Inside**

Then comes the clause that has carried more weight in human affairs than any other sentence of this sūrah: “Indeed, Allah would never change a people’s state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺” (13:11). The commentators took the clause in two directions, and both are anchored in the wording. On one reading, the change is God's alteration of a people's blessings — health, wealth, security — and the condition is their misuse of them: He does not withdraw the favor until the favor itself has been turned into the currency of disobedience. On the other, broader reading, the clause states a general law: God does not alter a people's circumstances at all, for better or worse, until they alter what is in themselves — their belief, their resolve, their interior state. What unites the readings is the location of the hinge. In both, the decisive variable is not the enemy at the border, the economy, the stars, or the idols, but what is in the people — an interior noun, deliberately chosen, and deliberately placed before any talk of exterior consequence.

**The Grammar of Responsibility**

The clause's grammar does the theological work. The change outside waits on a change inside — and the inside is named as the people's own act: “until they change their own state ˹of faith˺” (13:11). The parallel text confirms the reading: “This is because Allah would never discontinue His favour to a people until they discontinue their faith” (8:53). Two practical consequences follow, and they cut in opposite directions. Against fatalism: no people may attribute its condition wholly to heaven, since the verse assigns the first move to the people themselves. Against self-salvation: the change within is only ever a change *awaiting* God's act — the verse does not say people change their state, but that God does not change it until they change what is in themselves, keeping cause and effect on both sides of the clause. The sentence therefore leaves no room for the two easiest postures — despair, which says nothing can move, and arrogance, which says we moved it ourselves. It installs instead a discipline: look inward first, act, and await the alteration as from God.

**The Unrepellable Sentence**

“If it is Allah’s Will to torment a people, it can never be averted, nor can they find a protector other than Him” (13:11). The second half of the verse is the first half's dark twin, and the two halves together state one doctrine: change is possible while the term is running, and impossible once the sentence is served. The verse thus answers, in advance, the two great abuses of the doctrine of decree. The demander of hastened torment (13:6) is told the sentence when it comes is unrepellable — his heckling moves nothing. And the complacent are told that the unrepellable sentence has a condition they still control before it falls. Note also what the clause does to the protector-economy of paganism: nor can they find a protector other than Him — the idols are shut out of the one transaction that matters. The coming verse will show the same order in the sky: the thunderbolt strikes whom He wills (13:13), and no purchased charm stands in its path.

**Neither Fatalism nor Autonomy**

The verse's two halves must be held together, because each is a cure for the other's disease. Taken alone, the escort of angels could be read as a world so managed that human choice is theater; taken alone, the change clause could be read as a world so open that heaven merely reacts. Read together, they describe a road: guarded at every step by God's command, and leading, at every step, toward the consequence of what is in the walker. The angels do not steer; they escort. The change does not descend uninvited; it waits on the interior move. This is the sūrah's whole anthropology in one verse — a creature neither abandoned nor coerced, watched from before and behind, and holding, in the one place no angel occupies and no idol can enter, the lever of his own alteration.

Read as a single sentence, the verse draws the day's whole itinerary. The soul sets out escorted, arrives at every fork holding the lever, and stands finally before the one sentence that cannot be appealed — three stations, one road, and the walking done at the middle station only. The order is also the comfort's order: the escort means nothing reaches you unsearched; the lever means nothing about you is final before you die; the unrepellable sentence means nothing about you is provisional after it. Believers have carried this verse into collapses, exiles, and reform movements precisely because it assigns each party its work and each outcome its Owner: the interior to the people, the alteration to God, and the sentence to a schedule no protest moves. A generation that reads it upside down — all lever, no Owner — burns out in pride; one that reads it downward — all sentence, no lever — rots in despair. Its placement between the hiding places (13:10) and the storm (13:12) gives it the chapter's hinge position: knowledge finds you, the road offers you your one move, and the weather ahead will preach the rest.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:12

> He is the One Who shows you lightning, inspiring ˹you with˺ hope and fear, and produces heavy clouds.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Storm Verse Arrives With the Weather**

After four verses on knowledge, hiding, and guarded roads, the sūrah turns to the sky's drama: “He is the One Who shows you lightning” (13:12). The verb is chosen with care — God *shows*, personally and directly, the way a host presents a marvel. The audience is addressed in the second person because every one of them has stood in the storm this verse describes; the evidence needs no reconstruction, only recall. The verse also restores the word *He* to the front of the sentence — He is the One Who — reasserting, at the threshold of the weather, the singularity that the idols' departmental universe denied. In the pagan division of labor, some lesser power owned the storm; the verse hands the entire event — flash, cloud, and what follows in 13:13, the thunder and the bolt — to the same One who raised the heavens in 13:2 and reads the wombs in 13:8. The storm is not a rival jurisdiction. It is the Lord's own exhibition, and its peculiar instrument is a mixed emotion.

**Hope and Fear in One Flash**

“Inspiring ˹you with˺ hope and fear” (13:12) — and here the verse names the psychological mechanism as deliberately as it names the meteorology. Lightning carries two messages at once: the flash promises rain for a thirsty land, and it threatens the strike. The parallel text states the double signal exactly: “And one of His signs is that He shows you lightning, inspiring ˹you with˺ hope and fear. And He sends down rain from the sky, reviving the earth after its death” (30:24). Hope and fear are not casualties of the storm; they are its design. A heart that felt only fear would hide from the sky, and a heart that felt only comfort would build on the floodplain; the storm trains both dispositions together, which is to say it trains the exact posture the Qur'an demands of a believer toward God — hope in His mercy, fear of His severity, neither permitted to expel the other. The pagan, by contrast, manages the sky by managing the god, buying protection and divining moods. The verse offers the truer reading: the mixture in your chest during a storm is the sermon.

**Heavy Clouds: Weight as a Sign**

“And produces heavy clouds” (13:12). The closing clause sounds like small talk until its physics is heard. Clouds are water, and water is weight — the verse says heavy, and says it about the thing floating overhead. The design being displayed is layered. The cloud arrives visibly, builds gradually, darkens by degrees: the mercy of rain comes with a warning light, so that the hope announced in the flash has time to ripen and the fear announced with it has time to send the traveler home. Warning and provision travel in the same vehicle. This is the signature of the God this sūrah has been describing since 13:2 — the conductor of affairs whose every severity is fused to a mercy, and whose every mercy carries a threat's address. The same pairing governs the verse of the attributes at 13:6, forgiveness running ahead of severity; the storm is that verse made weather. And the commentary tradition heard in the heavy clouds the image of armies and afflictions too — clouds as the loaded heavens — but the primary sense stands on the surface: the weight overhead is the water of life, and only its Author could hang the reservoir in the sky and keep it there.

**The Twin Engines Readied for What Follows**

The verse is the first of a triple. It shows the lightning; 13:13 will voice the thunder and drop the bolt; and the storm as a whole will end in the prostration of everything that has a shadow (13:15). This first verse does the preparatory work: it installs in the hearer the two dispositions — hope and fear — that the thunder and bolt will exercise. The order is pedagogy. A heart taught by the storm holds both truths without collapsing into either: the sky that threatens is the sky that waters, and the God who strikes is the God who gives. What the pagan bargains away — fear, by flattering the god, or hope, by despising him — the believer is trained to carry whole. The storm, on this reading, is not an interruption of the world's order but one of its clearest lessons: the same flash that could kill the reaper ripens his field. Only a Lord of both life and death could design a sign that way, and only such a Lord is worth the hope and fear He built into you to feel.

The verse's singular grammar deserves the final word, because the storm was precisely where the pagan system claimed a specialist. A god of clouds would be invoked before travel; a god of lightning would be bribed after near-misses; the whole apparatus of weather-divination exists to manage specialists. The verse answers the entire consultancy with one pronoun repeated at every clause: He shows, He produces — and the next verse will add He sends the bolts. The storm's management is thereby removed from the shrine and returned to the same hand that raises the heavens (13:2), reads the wombs (13:8), and escorts every walker (13:11). A believer caught in the open under a building sky is thus not in a specialist's jurisdiction but in his Lord's own exhibition — frightened, hopeful, and — the verse's quiet promise — exactly where the lesson is best taught.

And the verse's audience label — you — keeps the lesson personal: the storm on the horizon is not a topic; it is an appointment being kept with the reader.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:13

> The thunder glorifies His praises, as do the angels in awe of Him. He sends thunderbolts, striking with them whoever He wills. Yet they dispute about Allah. And He is tremendous in might.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Storm's Hidden Liturgy**

The verse hands the storm a different soundtrack than the one human ears assume. What the audience heard as menace — the rolling boom over the hills of Makkah — the verse identifies as praise: “The thunder glorifies His praises” (13:13). A tradition cited by the commentators goes further and identifies thunder as the name of the angel charged with the clouds, so that the sound itself is a voice at worship, the hymn of a servant mid-task. Either way the teaching is the same: the loudest object in the sky is not an omen but a worshipper, and the storm's menace is one layer over a liturgy. The Qur'an generalizes the claim beyond the storm: “The seven heavens, the earth, and all those in them glorify Him. There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification” (17:44). The limitation is stated honestly — you cannot comprehend it — and the verse of the birds makes the same claim audible in principle: “Do you not see that Allah is glorified by all those in the heavens and the earth, even the birds as they soar?” (24:41). The thunder verse is this doctrine's loudest exhibit, chosen from the one phenomenon every hearer had heard and feared.

**Fear and Hope Resolved Into Awe**

The previous verse installed two dispositions in the storm — hope and fear (13:12) — and this verse names the third station toward which both tend: awe, the angels' state, “in awe of Him” (13:13). The sequence is precise. Lightning excites hope of rain and fear of the strike; the thunder then discloses what the fear was for — not cowering before a blind force, but the reverence of those who know what the force is: a commanded instrument of the One being praised. The angels stand in the verse as the model audience of the storm. Where humans hear noise, they hear the voice of a fellow servant magnifying the Lord; their awe is the correct translation of the same event. This is why the sūrah's storm verses must be read together: the flash teaches the mixed heart (hope and fear), and the thunder teaches the resolved heart (awe) — the two dispositions of 13:12 finding their single object.

**The Bolt Is Addressed, Not Random**

“He sends thunderbolts, striking with them whoever He wills” (13:13). The clause refuses the pagan reading of the bolt — a god's tantrum, a luckless accident — and installs aim: whoever He wills. The commentators cited a case they considered the verse's own: Arbad ibn Rabīʿah, who had plotted with ʿĀmir ibn al-Ṭufayl against the Prophet ﷺ and was struck dead by a thunderbolt; others answered that the verse's wording is too general to be pinned to one man. Both positions can stand without quarrel, and weighing them is instructive. The general wording certainly holds — the bolt is an instrument of address, not a lottery — and the specific story, whatever its chain, illustrates the general rule rather than exhausting it. What matters doctrinally is the refusals the clause performs: the bolt is not random (it is sent), not autonomous (He sends it), and not indiscriminate (whoever He wills). A sky like that is a courtroom with weather for a bailiff, and the Makkans are about to be told what their arguing sounds like in it.

**Disputing Under the Thunder**

“Yet they dispute about Allah” (13:13). The sentence lands with the bolt still in the air. A people are arguing about the Owner of the sky while the sky is mid-sermon — the thunder praising, the angels in awe, the bolt on call — and their argument has no instrument but speculation. The Qur'an diagnoses such dispute elsewhere in one triple: “˹Still˺ there are some who dispute about Allah without knowledge, guidance, or an enlightening scripture” (22:8). No knowledge: the facts are missing. No guidance: the disposition to be taught is missing. No scripture: the reference book is missing. The Makkans' dispute about Allah is thus exposed as a performance conducted in a theater where every prop testifies against the performers. The placement of the clause is also deliberate: it follows the bolt as it followed the lightning's hope-and-fear — the audience is being shown that the same weather which trains the believer in awe leaves the disputer exactly where he chose to stand, mid-sentence, under the sky he was arguing about.

**Might in the Lexicon, and the Prophet's ﷺ Response to Thunder**

“And He is tremendous in might” (13:13). The word translated as might here opened a small exegetical door: it derives from the root of plotting and stratagem, so the phrase was read as “intense in planning” — the same root this sūrah will turn against the plotters at 13:42. The linguistic note changes the devotional response: the might behind the storm is not temper but plan, which means the correct prayer in a storm is not appeasement but seeking shelter in the Planner. That is exactly how the Prophet ﷺ responded. It is recorded that upon hearing thunder he would say: “O Allah, do not kill us with Your anger, and do not destroy us with Your punishment, and grant us well-being before that” (recorded by Aḥmad in his Musnad). His cousin's son ʿAbdullāh ibn al-Zubayr would stop speaking at the thunder's sound and supplicate: “Glory be to Him whom the thunder glorifies with His praise, and so do the angels out of awe of Him,” collected by Mālik in al-Muwaṭṭaʾ and by al-Bukhārī in al-Adab al-Mufrad. Both responses are the verse internalized: one reads the storm as warning and asks for well-being; the other joins the liturgy it has just been told is happening overhead. The verse, in short, converts the most feared sound in the sky into the loudest reminder of the One whose plan the sūrah's skeptics are so casually challenging.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:14

> Calling upon Him ˹alone˺ is the truth. But those ˹idols˺ the pagans invoke besides Him ˹can˺ never respond to them in any way. ˹It is˺ just like someone who stretches out their hands to water, ˹asking it˺ to reach their mouths, but it can never do so. The calls of the disbelievers are only in vain.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Prayer Is Answered; the Rest Are Eavesdropping**

“Calling upon Him ˹alone˺ is the truth” (13:14). The verse pronounces on the whole human industry of prayer with a single criterion: address. Prayer directed to Him belongs to reality; prayer directed elsewhere is noise. The commentators heard the shahāda inside the clause — the call to Him alone *is* “there is no god but He” performed rather than recited — and the connection is exact, because supplication is worship in its most exposed form: the moment a person names, in act, whom he takes to be able to help. The standard of response is set by God's own proclamation: “Your Lord has proclaimed, “Call upon Me, I will respond to you”” (40:60). A standing invitation with a standing guarantee — that is the truth the verse affirms. Everything prayed to besides Him has, by contrast, issued no invitation, made no promise, and possesses no treasury to pay from. The verse's first sentence is thus a map of the whole religious world divided by a single line: the one prayer that ascends to a Hearer, and the many that merely leave the mouth.

**The Deities That Cannot Hear**

The negation is total: those invoked besides Him “˹can˺ never respond to them in any way” (13:14). The parallel text does the demolition in three steps: “If you call upon them, they cannot hear your calls. And if they were to hear, they could not respond to you. On the Day of Judgment they will disown your worship ˹of them˺” (35:14). Cannot hear; could not answer; will disown — the three steps remove the idols from every role the worshipper assigned them: recipient, agent, and even patron. And their poverty is a matter of inventory: “But those ˹idols˺ you ˹pagans˺ invoke besides Him do not possess even the skin of a date stone” (35:13). The skin of a date stone is the Qur'an's deliberately tiny unit of property — the shaving a child peels and discards — and the gods of the pantheon do not possess one. The irony has a historical bite, since the Makkans' own rites centered on offerings, including dates, laid before idols; the verse audits the gods and finds their estate smaller than the offerings. A religious system whose deities cannot hear, cannot act, and will deny their worshippers at the audit is not a rival theology. It is an empty office with a queue outside.

**The Man Watering His Own Hands**

Then comes one of the Qur'an's most quietly cruel images: “˹It is˺ just like someone who stretches out their hands to water, ˹asking it˺ to reach their mouths, but it can never do so” (13:14). The genius of the picture is its fairness. The water is real; the thirst is real; the hands are stretched toward something that genuinely sustains life — the disbeliever's wants are not foolish wants. What fails is the direction of the request: water can be carried, drunk, channeled, and valued, but it cannot be asked for anything, because asking requires a hearer and water has none. The parable therefore explains the failure of pagan religion without mocking its appetites. The idolater wanted rain, children, victory, provision — the same goods the believer asks God for. The failure is not in the wanting but in the addressing: a real need directed at an unreachable recipient, with hands doing the work that only response can do. The verse's verdict follows in its last clause — “The calls of the disbelievers are only in vain” (13:14) — and the word for vain carries the sense of wandering and being lost: the calls do not merely fail to arrive; they have no road to travel on.

**Why the Test of Prayer Is Decisive**

The verse quietly installs supplication as the practical test of godhood. A lord who cannot respond is not a lesser lord but a category error, and prayer is the experiment that reveals the error. This is why the verse follows the storm verses so naturally: the pagan, watching the lightning of 13:12–13, is the man stretching hands to water — he reads the storm as the mood of some local power and begins bargaining with a force that has never once answered anyone. The believer reads the same storm as the work of the One who said “Call upon Me, I will respond to you” (40:60) and asks — and the ask is not wasted, because the address is real. The verse thus prepares the courtroom of the next two verses: the prostration of everything that has being (13:15), and the interrogation that ends with the Makkans' own confession (13:16). The order of the sūrah's argument is now complete in outline: conduct the test, watch everything fall before the One, and then make the idolater answer the only question his own tongue can answer correctly.

The image's placement also completes the sūrah's argument about addresses. The pagans did not pray to nothingness outright; they prayed to things — stones, names, ancestral customs — and the verse's water is chosen because it, too, is something: real, near, even exalted in its origin as rain from the same sky the thunder praises (13:13). The error the verse isolates is therefore subtler than stupidity: it is the category mistake of asking a creature to do what only its Lord does. Every generation commits the mistake in updated materials — wealth, systems, patrons, the self — and the parable's test transfers unchanged: address your need to it, and watch whether anything answers. The calls of the disbelievers are in vain not because their needs were vain but because the needs were addressed past the only Hearer — and the hands stretched toward water stay dry precisely in the shape of their gesture.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:15

> To Allah ˹alone˺ bow down ˹in submission˺ all those in the heavens and the earth—willingly or unwillingly—as do their shadows, morning and evening. Almighty Allah or Powerless Gods?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Prostration Already in Progress**

“To Allah ˹alone˺ bow down ˹in submission˺ all those in the heavens and the earth” (13:15). The verb's tense is the verse's surprise: not will bow, but bow — present, continuous, already underway. The prostration in question is not the ritual posture alone but existential submission, the creature's inability to exit its dependence, and the Qur'an states the same doctrine as an appeal: “Do they desire a way other than Allah’s—knowing that all those in the heavens and the earth submit to His Will, willingly or unwillingly, and to Him they will ˹all˺ be returned?” (3:83). Every being is prostrate in one of two modes: the mode that knows it is kneeling, or the mode that has not admitted it. Nothing is standing upright in the absolute sense; the rebel's rebellion is itself performed on his knees, since the heart beating in the rebel, the air in his lungs, and the ground under his demonstration are all in enforced obedience. The verse therefore does not threaten that submission will come. It reports that it has — and asks only which of the two kinds the reader is practicing.

**Shadows: The Anatomy of Compelled Worship**

“As do their shadows, morning and evening” (13:15). The shadow is the verse's masterstroke, because a shadow is the most obedient thing in creation: it never votes, never negotiates, never breaks rank. Where the light is, it lies; where the light moves, it moves; and it performs this liturgy twice daily on schedule — morning and evening — exactly as the verse says. The parallel text turns the shadow into an argument addressed to the observing: “Have they not considered how the shadows of everything Allah has created incline to the right and the left ˹as the sun moves˺, totally submitting to Allah in all humility?” (16:48). Another verse slows the film: “Have you not seen how your Lord extends the shade—He could have simply made it ˹remain˺ still if He so willed—then We make the sun its guide” (25:45), with the retreat described as gradual (25:46). The shadow is thus a worship whose choreography is God's and whose mechanism is the sun's appointed course — the same subjection announced at 13:2. Every wall at mid-afternoon is displaying a prostration that never skips, and the verse asks its audience to notice what they have been walking through all their lives.

**Willingly and Unwillingly: The Two Kneelings**

“Willingly or unwillingly” (13:15) divides the universe's worship into the verse's two classes, and the Qur'an supplies membership lists for both. The willing include the angels — “And to Allah ˹alone˺ bows down ˹in submission˺ whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth of living creatures, as do the angels—who are not too proud ˹to do so˺” (16:49) — whose submission is conscious, chosen, and prompt. The unwilling include the shadows, the storms, the atoms — and, uncomfortably, “many humans, while many are deserving of punishment” (22:18). That last clause from the prostration verse of Sūrah al-Ḥajj is the hinge: human beings are the one creature who can relocate between the classes, and the sentence of the punished is not that they refused to bow but that they insisted on bowing in the wrong mode until the mode hardened. The general law is stated with terrible simplicity: “There is none in the heavens or the earth who will not return to the Most Compassionate in full submission” (19:93). Return, in full submission, is not on the table as an option; only its timing and manner are. The verse of the shadows makes every sunset a rehearsal of that law — the light withdraws, the shadow yields, and the creature who watched it happen is asked which class he is enrolled in.

**The Question the Verse Ends On**

The translation closes the verse with a dare compressed into two alternatives: “Almighty Allah or Powerless Gods?” (13:15). On the face of the recited text, the prostration of everything has just been witnessed; the question then invites the audience to name the power that could compete with what they have seen. It is the same tactic the next verse will formalize as interrogation — ask them, and let their own answer convict them — and the same tactic the Qur'an aims at the sun-worshipper and the idolater alike through the day-night thought experiment: if the night were made perpetual, “which god other than Allah could bring you sunlight? Will you not then listen?” (28:71), and if the day were perpetual, “which god other than Allah could bring you night to rest in?” (28:72). The rhetorical design is consistent throughout: the rival's name is never refuted at length because the rival's name is never supplied. The audience is left holding the two nouns — the Mighty and the powerless — and the verse has already shown, in the shadows on their own walls, which of them everything else has been bowing to.

The verse's double witness — creatures and shadows — also explains why prostration is the Qur'an's chosen word rather than obedience or service. Prostration is the body's admission of prior claim: the knee bends because the ground under it was never one's own. The phrase willingly or unwillingly then sorts the cosmos into two schools of the same posture: the learning school, whose prostration is conscious and grows into love, and the material school, whose prostration is the physics of dependence — matter holding its shape, light obeying its speed, shadows keeping their morning and evening appointments. The human being is the only creature who can transfer between the schools, and the transfer is the whole business of religion. The shadows' testimony also humbles the proud with special precision: the tyrant who forbids prostration to God walks home under a shadow prostrating the entire way, his own silhouette arguing against him at every step — creation's silent comment on the creature who mistook his refusal for an option.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:16

> Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth?” Say, “Allah!” Ask ˹them˺, “Why ˹then˺ have you taken besides Him lords who cannot even benefit or protect themselves?” Say, “Can the blind and the sighted be equal? Or can darkness and light be equal?” Or have they associated with Allah partners who ˹supposedly˺ produced a creation like His, leaving them confused between the two creations? Say, “Allah is the Creator of all things, and He is the One, the Supreme.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse Built as Cross-Examination**

The verse is an interrogation, and its architecture should be seen before its content. Four questions follow in ascending order of penetration: Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth? Why take lords besides Him who cannot help themselves? Are the blind and the seeing equal, darkness and light? Did your partners produce a creation like His? Each question tightens the screw of the last, and the answer to the first is supplied before the accused can speak — “Say, “Allah!”” (13:16) — because, as the next sections show, the accused already knows it. The method is the Qur'an's recurring courtroom move: not a lecture but a deposition, in which the witness's own knowledge is entered into evidence. The Prophet ﷺ is the examining counsel throughout — Ask… Say, Ask… Say — and the accused is never allowed to change the subject to the demand for signs that the sūrah has twice recorded (13:6–7). The subject stays where the facts are.

**The Answer They Already Carry**

“Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth?” Say, “Allah!” (13:16). The commentators observed the striking fact that the answer is supplied by the verse itself: the questioners cannot give any other, because a number of them already ranked Allāh atop their pantheon as the high god. The Qur'an exploits that latent confession repeatedly: “If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon ˹for your benefit˺, they will certainly say, “Allah!”” (29:61); “And if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will definitely say, “Allah!”” (31:25); “If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created them, they will certainly say, “Allah!”” (43:87). Three parallel verses, three certain confessions. The argument's force comes from what the confession concedes: creation, lordship over the heavens, the subjection of the sun and moon — the entire portfolio this sūrah has been cataloguing since 13:2. The pagans are thus not atheists with bad theology; they are witnesses with testimony, and the verse simply administers the oath. What remains is to confront them with the consequences of their own answer: if Allah is the Lord, the others are unemployed — and the second question fires exactly there.

**Blind and Seeing, Darkness and Light**

“Say, “Can the blind and the sighted be equal? Or can darkness and light be equal?”” (13:16). The paired contrasts return the doctrine of pairs from 13:3–4 in moral key: creation is built on meaningful differences, and the verse now applies the principle to the two conditions of the human interior — belief and disbelief, knowledge and ignorance. The Qur'an can render the darkness of disbelief with terrible physicality: “Or ˹their deeds are˺ like the darkness in a deep sea, covered by waves upon waves, topped by ˹dark˺ clouds. Darkness upon darkness! If one stretches out their hand, they can hardly see it” (24:40) — a darkness so total the outstretched hand disappears, and the verse of hiding places (13:10) has already told us what such a hand hopes to do. The sighted and the blind cannot be equal because one knows where he is going; the light and the dark cannot be equal because one of them contains the road. The questions are rhetorical, but their premise is empirical: nobody in the audience has ever seen a blind man and a sighted man interchangeable, and nobody has ever watched darkness guide anyone home. The moral taxonomy is therefore not imposed from outside; it is read off the world the audience already lives in.

**The Confusion Between Two Creations**

“Or have they associated with Allah partners who ˹supposedly˺ produced a creation like His, leaving them confused between the two creations?” (13:16). The fourth question finally names the pagans' actual claim — that their partners share in making — and diagnoses it as confusion: two creations set side by side in the mind until the mind cannot tell which is which. The refutation is the doctrine of origination: “˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and the earth! When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, “Be!” And it is!” (2:117). Nothing in the record resembles a second “Be.” The rival creators have no portfolio: no orbit appointed, no womb read, no mountain set, no rain sent — the whole inventory of 13:2–4 is single-signed. The pagans' confusion, on the verse's showing, is not an intellectual disability but a maintenance problem: they keep two ledgers, one for the Creator they confess and one for the lords they court, and the verse demands they reconcile the accounts. Reconciliation has only one honest outcome, which is why the verse supplies it: “Allah is the Creator of all things, and He is the One, the Supreme” (13:16). Creator of all — the idols included, it might be said, made of the very wood their worshippers cut — One, and Supreme: the final answer that folds all four questions shut.

The verse's closing titles — the One, the Supreme — also hand the sūrah its headline for what remains: everything after this verse sorts by whether the titles were accepted. The thunder accepts them (13:13), the water sorts by them (13:17), the witnessing Books confirm them (13:43), and the two galleries of 13:19–25 are simply the biography of the two answers.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:17

> He sends down rain from the sky, causing the valleys to flow, each according to its capacity. The currents then carry along rising foam, similar to the slag produced from metal that people melt in the fire for ornaments or tools. This is how Allah compares truth to falsehood. The ˹worthless˺ residue is then cast away, but what benefits people remains on the earth. This is how Allah sets forth parables.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Rain That Sorts a Landscape**

“He sends down rain from the sky, causing the valleys to flow, each according to its capacity” (13:17). The parable begins with an act the previous verses have prepared us to recognize: sending down, the signature verb of the Sustainer who has been managing sky and soil since 13:2. The rain is one; the valleys are many, and each receives by its capacity — the broad wadi runs brown and full, the narrow channel gurgles and is done. With the flow comes sorting: the current churns the ground and lifts a froth of dirt and impurity, which rides the foam until the water settles and the scum dries on the banks. The commentators read the verse as delivering two similitudes, and the second is announced in its middle: the froth of the flood is “similar to the slag produced from metal that people melt in the fire for ornaments or tools” (13:17). In the forge, exactly as in the wadi, the heating raises impurities to the surface, where they are skimmed and discarded, while the clean metal underneath becomes the ornament and the tool. One sorting happens by water, one by fire; both separate the same way — foam up, value down.

**The Map Drawn Over Both Similitudes**

“This is how Allah compares truth to falsehood” (13:17). The application is explicit, which is rare and welcome: revelation is the descending water, human hearts and societies are the valleys of differing capacity, and the commotion that truth's arrival produces — the froth of objection, slander, fashion, and noise — is slag, not substance. The verse's economics are unforgiving in one direction only: “The ˹worthless˺ residue is then cast away, but what benefits people remains on the earth” (13:17). Falsehood's fate is not punishment imposed from outside but uselessness discovered: no one keeps scum. Truth's persistence, likewise, is not stubbornness but benefit — it remains because it is the water itself, the drink and the irrigation and the life. The same verdict is pronounced in the chapter of the Night Journey as a proclamation: “The truth has come and falsehood has vanished. Indeed, falsehood is bound to vanish” (17:81). And the Qur'an's other great parable of persistence makes the same point vegetatively: “Do you not see how Allah compares a good word to a good tree? Its root is firm and its branches reach the sky” (14:24) — root and sky, the two directions of what stays.

**The Parable's Efficiency Is Its Cruelty**

It is worth seeing how much the parable refuses to say. It never names the froth's content, never tells the foam that it is foam, and — the sharpest cut — never says the froth is not wet. The scum rides the same water as the flood; the slag sits in the same fire as the gold. Falsehood, in this verse, is not an alien substance but an impurity carried by the very truth it derides: objections ride on the revelation they mock, noise rides on the silence it interrupts, and the pharaohs of history quote the prophets they flog. The separation therefore cannot be made by appearance — it is made by endurance and use. Time is the wadi's subsiding; need is the assayer's cupel. When the water settles, the foam is a stain on the bank and the water is in the well; when the fire cools, the slag is swept and the metal is worn at the wedding. The parable thus tells the truth-seeker how to wait and tells the truth-hater what he is: not an opponent with a counter-argument but a residue with a deadline.

**The Prophet's ﷺ Rain: One Water, Three Receptions**

The parable's structure — one rain, different capacities — was used by the Prophet ﷺ for revelation itself. He said: “The example of guidance and knowledge with which Allah has sent me is like abundant rain falling on the earth” — and he described fertile ground that sprouted, hard ground that held the water so people and cattle could drink, and barren ground that neither held water nor grew (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 79). The hadith is this verse in narrative form, with one decisive addition: the capacities are moral. The fertile soil is the person who comprehends the religion and teaches it; the hard soil is the one who at least preserves the knowledge for others; the barren soil is the one who turns away from it entirely. Same sky, same water, three receptions — and the difference, in both the verse and the hadith, is located in the ground, not the rain. That addition is also the verse's consolation: the froth that forms when revelation descends on a generation is no evidence against the revelation. The water was water; the wadi was shallow.

**Each Valley by Its Capacity**

The verse's opening clause deserves the last word, because its preposition carries the sūrah's whole ethic: each according to its capacity. The flood measures the valley; the revelation measures the heart; and neither accepts an alibi. A people cannot blame the rain for what their channels were — this is the verse-length elaboration of 13:11's law that God does not change a people's state until they change what is in themselves. Nor can the valley inflate its capacity by boasting: the wadi's job is not to be the widest but to be true to its depth when the water comes. The parable ends where the sūrah's next verse begins — with response: those who answer their Lord (13:18) are simply the valleys that carried water, and the residue is simply everything that would not. Between the two stands the whole difference this sūrah has been drawing since its first verse: between the Book recited and the Book denied, between the thunder heard as praise and heard as noise, between what remains and what is cast away.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:18

> Those who respond to ˹the call of˺ their Lord will have the finest reward. As for those who do not respond to Him, even if they were to possess everything in the world twice over, they would certainly offer it to ransom themselves. They will face strict judgment, and Hell will be their home. What an evil place to rest!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Two Responses and Their Two Destinations**

“Those who respond to ˹the call of˺ their Lord will have the finest reward” (13:18). After seventeen verses of signs, storms, parables, and cross-examination, the sūrah finally sorts its audience by a single verb: respond. The call in question is the one the whole chapter has been making — the call of 13:14 that is answered by Him alone, the road of 13:15 that everything already bows along, the confession of 13:16 that every tongue already knows. Response is the entire moral event, and its reward is described with deliberate richness: the finest — and the parallel text adds the famous surplus: “Those who do good will have the finest reward and ˹even˺ more. Neither gloom nor disgrace will cover their faces” (10:26). The finest reward is Paradise, and the “more” has been understood as the vision of God's face — the reward above the rewards. The response, meanwhile, is defined against its opposite by the verse's own grammar: those who respond, and those who do not respond to Him — the refusal is not of a command here but of a call, which makes the refusal personal in a way disobedience to an abstract rule is not. One has declined an invitation from a Speaker.

**The Ransom the Universe Could Not Buy**

“As for those who do not respond to Him, even if they were to possess everything in the world twice over, they would certainly offer it to ransom themselves” (13:18). The clause performs a calculation the Day of Judgment will force on every refuser: the whole earth's wealth, doubled, tabled as a bid for one soul. The Qur'an prices the same bid elsewhere and reaches the same refusal: “Indeed, if each of those who disbelieve then die as disbelievers were to offer a ransom of enough gold to fill the whole world, it would never be accepted from them” (3:91); “even if they were to possess everything in the world twice over ˹and offer it all˺ to ransom themselves from the punishment of the Day of Judgment, it would never be accepted from them” (5:36). The wrongdoers' own foresight agrees: “they would certainly offer it to ransom themselves from the horrible punishment on Judgment Day, for they will see from Allah what they had never expected” (39:47). Why is the offer refused? Because the debt is denominated in the one currency the debtor owns and cannot mint: himself. Wealth was never the stake; the stake was the response, and response cannot be retroactively purchased, only given — and the day for giving it was the day of the call. The doubled earth's gold thus sits on the scales of 13:18 as the most expensive useless object in the Qur'an.

**Strict Judgment Without Patronage**

“They will face strict judgment, and Hell will be their home. What an evil place to rest!” (13:18). Three features of the sentence deserve attention. The reckoning is strict — the Qur'an's word implies exactness, the settling of accounts to the last entry, and the verse of the guarded road (13:11) has already explained why no entry will be missing: escorts recorded the comings and goings. The refuge is Hell — the word is the vocabulary of a dump or enclosure, a place thrown into rather than built for, and the Qur'an names it as their home, the vocabulary of residence that 13:5 already used for the people of the Fire. And the closing exclamation — “What an evil place to rest!” (13:18) — chooses the language of lodging and repose with bitter irony: Hell is described in the terms of a traveler's inn, the thing every human being seeks at day's end, so that the verse's horror lands on the one appetite — rest — that will there be eternally frustrated. The patronage economy of the idols, already bankrupted at 13:14–16, has no branch office here: “nor can they find a protector other than Him” (13:11). The audit is strict because the record is complete; the home is evil because the choice of address was made freely, on earth, one response at a time.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Turnstile**

Structurally, 13:18 is where the sūrah stops proving and starts dividing. Verses 1–17 built the case: revelation is truth (13:1), the creation is conducted (13:2–4), the denial is absurd (13:5), the demand for haste is a dare (13:6–7), the knowledge is total (13:8–10), the road is guarded and the law of change interior (13:11), the storm is liturgy (13:12–13), prayer has one address (13:14), everything prostrates (13:15), the confession is extracted (13:16), and truth sorts itself from froth like water in a wadi (13:17). Verse 18 cashes the case: respond and receive the finest reward; refuse and offer the doubled earth for a soul that cannot be bought back. The turnstile metaphor is exact, because the verses that follow — the knowing and the blind (13:19), the covenant-keepers (13:20–21), the patient who repel evil with good (13:22), the Gardens entered from every gate (13:23–24) — are simply the long biography of the word respond, while the covenant-breakers of 13:25 are the biography of the other word. The sūrah's remaining business, in other words, is to show what each verb looks like when it becomes a life.

The verse's arithmetic also quietly explains the sūrah's earlier portraits: the ransom clause shows why the wealthy mocker of 13:5–7 is the harder case — the doubled earth is a real temptation only to those who hold half of it.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:19

> Can the one who knows that your Lord’s revelation to you ˹O Prophet˺ is the truth be like the one who is blind? None will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Divides the Audience in Two**

After seventeen verses of evidence, the sūrah stops presenting and starts asking: “Can the one who knows that your Lord’s revelation to you ˹O Prophet˺ is the truth be like the one who is blind?” (13:19). The question is rhetorical, and its answer is the sorting the whole chapter has been building toward. The parable of the rain (13:17) left the audience watching water separate from foam; this verse names the two containers the sorting was running through. On one side stands a person who *knows* — not suspects, not hopes, but knows — that what came down to the Prophet ﷺ is truth. On the other stands a person the Qur'an elsewhere measures in exactly these terms: “Those blind ˹to the truth˺ and those who can see are not equal,” (35:19). The comparison is not an insult but a diagnosis. Blindness here, as the commentators were careful to say, is blindness of the heart — the failure to “see” what stands in plain sight — and the sixteen verses of signs behind this verse are precisely the things standing in plain sight. The question thus closes the case that 13:1 opened: the truth is known; the only remaining variable is the eye.

**Knowledge Here Is Not Information but Recognition**

The verb matters. The verse does not describe one who has heard the revelation, or read it, or memorized its stories; it describes one who knows that it is the truth — a knowledge that includes a verdict. The Qur'an's psychology allows no neutral shelf for that verdict. To know the revelation as truth is to be obligated by it, the way knowing the road home obligates the walker to walk. This is why the one who knows and the one who is blind cannot be compared: the difference between them is not two quantities of information but two postures toward the same information — acceptance and refusal. The Prophet ﷺ described his own position in the debate with the sign-demanders in exactly these terms: “I only follow what is revealed to me” (6:50), and he pressed the same comparison on his audience there: “Are those blind ˹to the truth˺ equal to those who can see?” (6:50). The verse of this sūrah inherits that whole argument. Knowledge that carries a verdict is what makes response possible, and response is the hinge on which 13:18 just turned.

**The Mirage: What the Blind Man Calls Seeing**

The Qur'an supplies a portrait of the blind man's sight, and it is one of its bleakest images: “As for the disbelievers, their deeds are like a mirage in a desert, which the thirsty perceive as water, but when they approach it, they find it to be nothing” (24:39). The mirage is sight without knowledge — the eye reporting, the mind inventing, the thirst supplying the wish. That is the verse's point about the objectors of this sūrah: their denial is not clear-sightedness refusing an illusion; it is thirst projecting one. The blind man of 13:19 is not someone who has examined the revelation and found it wanting; he is the thirsty man at the edge of the desert, certain the water is a trick. The mirage portrait also explains why the two men cannot be equal: the knower's sight is anchored to a real thing — the Book, the signs, the thunder's praise — while the blind man's vision terminates in nothing, and then, the Qur'an adds with terrible economy, “Instead, they find Allah there ˹in the Hereafter, ready˺ to settle their account” (24:39). The mirage evaporates; the Accountant does not.

**The Third Audience Label: People of Reason**

“None will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason” (13:19). The sūrah has been signing its inventory verses with audience labels: “signs for those who reflect” (13:3), “signs for those who understand” (13:4), and now “people of reason” — the word is the Qur'an's for insight, the eye of the mind rather than of the face. The same formula closes the battle-sign verse: “Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight” (3:13). The ladder of labels is itself a teaching: reflection (13:3) is thought working on evidence; understanding (13:4) is the grasp of what the evidence means; reason (13:19) is the faculty that carries the verdict into conduct. The verse's closing formula is therefore not a compliment to the intelligent but a definition of intelligence — and a warning to cleverness without it, since a man can be brilliant with his face-eyes and blind with the other pair. The Qur'an consistently treats that second blindness as the greater disability, because it is chosen: the mirage-watcher chose his thirst; the heart-blind chose his refusal.

**The Verse Heads a Portrait Gallery**

Placement finishes the verse's meaning. “People of reason” is not left as a category; the next three verses paint them — those who honour the covenant (13:20), join what God commanded joined (13:21), endure, pray, spend, and answer evil with good (13:22) — until the portrait walks into Gardens entered from every gate (13:23–24). The structure is the sūrah's answer to an old misconception: that reason's work ends at the verdict. Here the verdict produces a biography. The one who knows the revelation is truth is described not by his opinions but by his kept promises, his joined ties, his secret donations, and his answered insults — which is to say that in the Qur'an's accounting, seeing is a way of living. The blind man, correspondingly, will be described at 13:25 by his broken covenant and severed ties: the same biography, inverted. One verse of comparison (13:19) thus opens the gallery, and the gallery is the comparison.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:20

> ˹They are˺ those who honour Allah’s covenant, never breaking the pledge;

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Portrait Gallery Opens With a Doorkeeper's Word**

“˹They are˺ those who honour Allah’s covenant, never breaking the pledge” (13:20). The relative pronoun arrives like a door opening onto the people of reason whom 13:19 promised — and the first thing visible inside is not a doctrine but a practice: keeping an agreement. The sūrah, having spent eighteen verses on evidence, now describes the knower in the vocabulary of obligation. The choice is deliberate and defines the Qur'anic believer from the inside out: before prayer is listed, before charity, before patience, the believer is a creature that keeps its word to God. The clause also carries a quiet universality. A covenant is not a sect's password; it is the structure of the Creator-creature relationship as such — terms stated, acceptance given, faithfulness owed. Honouring it is the elementary act of piety, and the verse places it first because everything else in the portrait (the joined ties of 13:21, the patience of 13:22) is a branch growing from that one kept trunk.

**The Covenant Before Time: “Am I Not Your Lord?”**

What is the covenant, and when was it made? The commentators pointed to the Qur'an's own record of the primordial pledge: “And ˹remember˺ when your Lord brought forth from the loins of the children of Adam their descendants and had them testify regarding themselves. ˹Allah asked,˺ “Am I not your Lord?” They replied, “Yes, You are! We testify.” ˹He cautioned,˺ “Now you have no right to say on Judgment Day, ‘We were not aware of this.’” (7:172). Some of the exegetes took the two words of our verse — the covenant and the pledge — as naming this pretemporal testimony specifically, with the general covenant covering everything built on it: belief in God and the obligations between servants. Others read the two terms as the charge and its particulars: the covenant the moral responsibility God laid on human beings, the pledge the details of obedience accepted freely within it. The two readings disagree about the referent but not about the shape of the thing: in both, a term was stated and a yes was given, and the life of faith is the keeping of that yes. The primordial reading has this much in its favor: it explains why keeping the covenant is treated as remembering rather than inventing — the believer is not signing a new contract but honoring the oldest one on file.

**Never Breaking: The Standard Hidden in the Word**

“Never breaking the pledge” (13:20). The negation is absolute, and it is the verse's hardest word. Not "rarely breaking," not "breaking but repairing" — the portrait's standard is a promise kept whole. The Qur'an knows what happens to standards softened into percentages: they produce a piety of exceptions, a ledger kept by the debtor. The standard here is the mirror of the covenant's own Giver, whose promise-keeping is without breach — the sun's appointment (13:2) has never lapsed, the wombs' ledger (13:8) has never misplaced an entry. To honor such a covenant is to imitate, at creaturely scale, the fidelity of its Author. And the absoluteness has a pastoral edge: the believer who has broken the pledge is not told the portrait excludes him forever; he is told what the portrait looks like, so that repentance — the covenant's own repair clause — is exercised before the repair is needed at the audit. The verse describes the destination, not the excuse.

**A Covenant the Cosmos Already Keeps**

The verse's demand reads differently once the sūrah's own testimony is remembered: the covenant-keeper joins a creation that keeps its covenants. The heavens hold their appointment (13:2); the night covers the day on schedule (13:3); the thunder keeps its post and its praise (13:13); the shadow keeps its prostration (13:15). Nothing in the inventory of these verses has broken terms with its Maker. The human being is the one creature holding a covenant he is able to violate — and that capacity is not a flaw in the arrangement but its honor: obedience that cannot be withheld is not kept, only enforced. The shadow's fidelity, in other words, is worth less than the believer's, precisely because the shadow has no alternative. When the verse crowns the people who honour the covenant, it is placing them above the faithful planets and the punctual orbits — creatures whose keeping is a triumph rather than a physics. And the placement warns as it honors: a person who reads these verses and still breaks the pledge stands below the storm in fidelity, an anomaly in a cosmos of keepers.

**A Covenant With Terms the Body Already Recites**

What the covenant is made of deserves a look, because the verse's plainness hides its width. The pledge binds acknowledgment — “Yes, You are! We testify” (7:172) — but a testimony is lived, not merely voiced, and the portrait that follows will show the pledge's fine print: joining the ties God joined (13:21), standing in awe (13:21), patience and prayer and secret spending (13:22). The covenant, in other words, is not one ceremony but a standing state — the interior posture of 13:11 ("what is in themselves") kept in repair. This is also why the verse needs no list of stipulations: the whole sūrah since 13:1 has been the stipulations, delivered as signs. The audience that watched the thunder praise its Lord (13:13), the shadows prostrate (13:15), and the water sort truth from froth (13:17) has already read the covenant's first chapter. The verse now asks one question about the reader: he has read it — will he keep it?

**The Gallery Grows Two Lists**

The verse's place in the sūrah's architecture is as the first stone of a double gallery. Verses 20–22 will build the portrait of the covenant-keeper, walking him through the gates of Gardens (13:23–24); verse 25 will build the opposite portrait — the covenant violators — and assign them the condemned abode. The two lists are intentionally symmetric: both are described by their handling of the same covenant, the same ties, the same earth. The symmetry is the sermon. Between honouring and violating stands no third term, because the covenant does not permit neutral custody: a pledge is being kept or broken at every moment, including the moment of reading this sentence. The sūrah's audience, standing as it does between the two portraits, is being invited to walk into one of them — and the verse has just handed them the walk's first step.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:21

> and those who maintain whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, stand in awe of their Lord, and fear strict judgment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Join What God Joined**

The portrait of the covenant-keeper moves from heaven's contract to earth's relationships: “and those who maintain whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained” (13:21). The verb is the verse's center of gravity — join, connect, hold together — and the verse assigns the list of joinable things to God, not to society. The primary referent, as the commentators read it, is kinship: keeping faith with blood relatives, doing well by them, and standing by the poor and the needy — the social bonds that God tied and human hands loosen. A second reading widens the referent to all acts of obedience, as though every duty were a rope God attached and the faithful are those who leave the ropes attached. The two readings are less rivals than scopes: the kinship bond is the test case of joining (because its benefits are unearned and its irritations are unavoidable), and obedience in general is the category that contains it. Humanity was created through connection — “through both He spread countless men and women” (4:1) — and the same verse commands the honouring of family ties because spread and binding are one act of God.

**Kinship as Theology: The Womb That Carries Mercy**

The Arabic word for kinship ties descends from the same root as the word for mercy — the womb is, in the language itself, a mercy — and the Qur'an treats the connection as doctrine rather than accident. Severing the ties is listed in the Qur'an among the signatures of the corrupter: “Now if you ˹hypocrites˺ turn away, perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ˹ties of˺ kinship!” (47:22). Corruption and severed kinship appear there as the two hands of one work — which is why the covenant-breakers of this sūrah, at 13:25, will be described in exactly that pair: spreading corruption in the land, breaking the ties God ordered joined. The linguistic note and the structural echo say the same thing: the family bond is not a social courtesy appended to religion; it is mercy localized in flesh, and its maintenance is worship. The believer who keeps his kin is performing, in miniature, the attribute of the Lord who keeps His covenant (13:20) — the joiner imitating the Joiner.

**Awe and the Dread of a Strict Reckoning**

“Stand in awe of their Lord, and fear strict judgment” (13:21). The two fears close the verse like a bookend, and their placement after the command to join is precise. Kindness to kin is the easiest virtue to corrupt — into duty performed for reputation, gifts given for leverage, generosity practised as investment. The verse installs the audit inside the kindness: the tie-keeper stands in awe of his Lord, and dreads the strict reckoning — the same exactness of account the sūrah named at 13:18 (“They will face strict judgment”), where nothing unrecorded survives. The fear is not the fear of kin but of the Judge who watches how the kin are treated; it keeps the joining honest when no relative is looking. This is also why the fear of judgment and the love of ties are not competing motives in the Qur'an's portrait. They are the same motive at two temperatures: the dread of severing what God joined, and the awe of the One who joined it — the emotional grammar, once more, of hope and fear holding a single object.

**The Tie That Tests Without Applause**

Kinship is the hardest of the verse's assignments precisely because it is the least supervised. The poor relative brings no credit; the difficult parent supplies no inspiration; the claim of the needy arrives without regard for timing or mood. Public worship has an audience and a calendar; the tie is tested at random hours, over decades, with no record kept except in heaven. That is why the Qur'an keeps it inside the portrait of the covenant-keeper rather than beside it as social advice: the bond exercises, in miniature, the two disciplines the next verse will command — giving that cannot be repaid and patience that cannot be publicly justified. The commentators' widening of the verse to the poor and the needy extends the same logic: God has attached claims to a person's wealth and household that arrive with the person's kin, and the faithful hand does not select among the attachments. The dread of strict judgment completes the design, because kindness left unwatched drifts. A tie maintained for twenty years and severed in one moment of temper needs a longer memory than temper provides — and the verse supplies the memory, in the shape of an audit where the severed rope will be entered in evidence.

**Why Ties Belong in a Godward Portrait**

The deepest claim of the verse is structural: maintaining ties appears *inside* the description of the people who know the revelation is truth (13:19). In the Qur'an's architecture, the vertical covenant and the horizontal bonds are one fabric — tear the horizontal and the vertical frays with it. This is why the portrait will not pass from the covenant (13:20) to prayer (13:22) without stopping at the relatives in between: the stop itself is the doctrine. A faith that keeps God's covenant while cutting God's creatures has, in the verse's logic, already misunderstood the covenant — since the covenant's terms include them. The verse thus prepares the believer for the portrait's next panels, where patience, prayer, and spending secretly and openly (13:22) will fill out the interior life: the household was the training ground where all three were first exercised. The person who has learned to hold the nearest ropes without profit and without applause has already learned the two disciplines the next verse will command — giving without being seen, and enduring without being thanked.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:22

> And ˹they are˺ those who endure patiently, seeking their Lord’s pleasure, establish prayer, donate from what We have provided for them—secretly and openly—and respond to evil with good. It is they who will have the ultimate abode:

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Habits and One Motive**

The portrait's final panel lists the covenant-keeper's working habits: patience, prayer, giving, and the answering of evil with good — and before any of them, the verse names the motive that makes the list one act rather than four: “seeking their Lord’s pleasure” (13:22). The commentators were emphatic about that phrase. Everything the verse commands — the endurance, the prostrations, the secret donations — is done for no selfish reason, without ostentation, solely for God's contentment. The motive clause is what separates the portrait from mere respectability. A person can be patient out of pride, prayerful out of habit, generous out of image-management; the verse's subject is none of these. The list therefore reads less like a legal code than a description of one disposition expressed four ways: a heart aimed at God's pleasure will endure without despairing, pray without drifting, give without performing, and absorb insults without repaying them — because each response is selected by the same question: which of these pleases Him?

**Patience Aimed at a Face**

“Endure patiently, seeking their Lord’s pleasure” (13:22). Patience is the first habit, and the pairing with the motive clause transforms it. Patience as grim endurance is available to anyone with no alternative; patience aimed at a Face is worship — the deliberate holding of a position because its Holder is watching. The Qur'an repeatedly yokes patience to prayer as the believer's two crutches, and the yoking appears here in the verse's own order: endurance flows directly into “establish prayer” (13:22), the daily appointment where the patience is renewed. For the sūrah's first audience, the patience had a specific face: the mockery of 13:5, the sign-demands of 13:7, the hastening-dares of 13:6 — a decade of heckling before the promise was vindicated. The verse does not tell the mockers' victims to answer in kind or to withdraw; it files their endurance under the same heading as their prayer: acts done for the pleasure of a Lord who, as this sūrah has established verse by verse, misses nothing that is done secretly and nothing that is done openly.

**The Gift That Is Safe From Both Extremes**

“Donate from what We have provided for them—secretly and openly” (13:22). Two features of the clause reward a slow reading. First, the provision is acknowledged in the verb: what We have provided — the giver is a steward distributing another's goods, which is the verse-level consequence of 13:4's teaching that the very differences in harvest were authored by God. Second, the giving is prescribed in both modes — secretly and openly — because each mode has its own disease. Public giving can rot into display; secret giving can rot into its own quiet vanity. The Qur'an elsewhere rates the concealed gift above the broadcast one, while still commanding the giving be done — the cure is neither mode but motive, which is why the verse has already stated the motive first. The scope of the provision is the whole sūrah's testimony: rain, rivers, fruits, paired harvests (13:3–4) — the believer gives from an inventory he watched God assemble.

**Responding to Evil With Good: The Hardest Habit**

“And respond to evil with good” (13:22). The clause is the portrait's summit, and the Qur'an elsewhere measures its altitude: “Good and evil cannot be equal. Respond ˹to evil˺ with what is best, then the one you are in a feud with will be like a close friend” (41:34) — followed immediately by the fine print: “But this cannot be attained except by those who are patient and who are truly fortunate” (41:35). The patience of this verse is thus the price of that alchemy: only an endured insult can be answered with gift. The classical reading of the command was practical — repel the evil by doing good, a strategy rather than a mood — and its promised effect is enmity's disappearance into friendship (41:34). Placed in this sūrah, the habit has a theological root: the believer who answers evil with good is imitating the order of the verse of 13:6, where forgiveness is listed before severity, mercy running ahead of the account. The responding believer extends to men the priority God extends to servants.

**Secretly and Openly: The Two Registers of Giving**

The command's two adverbs are a complete charter of charity. The Qur'an elsewhere weighs the two registers and pronounces on both: “To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you of your sins. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do” (2:271). Public giving is good — it models the duty, normalizes generosity, and meets the community's visible needs; private giving is better — it starves the ego and protects the recipient's dignity. The believer is not asked to choose between the registers but to keep both in service of the motive the verse named first: God's pleasure. The design also closes the two escape routes. The publicity-seeker is told the private gift outranks his platform; the hoarder is told that secrecy is a mode of giving, not an exemption from it. And the whole clause stands under the eye of 13:10, where secret and open were already declared identical before God — so that giving in either register is, for the audit, given in both.

**It Is They Who Will Have the Ultimate Abode**

“It is they who will have the ultimate abode” (13:22). The verse ends mid-promise, and the next verse arrives to complete it — the Gardens of Eternity, entered with the righteous of one's own family, greeted by angels at every gate (13:23–24). The incomplete sentence is itself eloquent: the reward is named here as an abode — the vocabulary of arrival — and its contents are held over, because the portrait's climax deserves its own verse. Note the proportion of the exchange, since it is the sūrah's quiet arithmetic: patience under insults, prayer maintained, wealth given in two modes, evil answered with good — priced against an eternal home. The believer is being told, in effect, that the ledger runs in his favor, which is the meaning of reward: not wages earned but generosity accepted. The next verse will open the Gardens' gates and show the abode's first minutes — and the opening word of those minutes, as the translation of 13:24 records, is peace.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:23

> the Gardens of Eternity, which they will enter along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants. And the angels will enter upon them from every gate, ˹saying,˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Completes: Abode Into Gardens**

“The Gardens of Eternity” (13:23) — the verse finishes the sentence the previous one left hanging, converting the promised abode into an address. The name carries both its gifts: gardens, the word for the walled, cultivated, watered enclosure that the Qur'an uses for Paradise throughout; and eternity, the term that answers the sūrah's earlier residency language — the residents of the Fire (13:5) here meet the residents of the Gardens, two addresses, both permanent, both chosen on earth. The commentators noted that the Gardens are the content of the “ultimate abode” of 13:22 — the reward the portrait earned. What deserves attention first is the entry's social shape: “which they will enter along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants” (13:23). The believer does not arrive alone. The verse names the three circles of intimacy — the generation above, the partner beside, the generation below — and admits the righteous of each. The reward is engineered for togetherness.

**Families Rejoined: The Justice of Togetherness**

The promise has a deliberate theology: it answers the joining commanded at 13:21. There, the believer maintained the ties God ordered joined — the parents honored, the spouse cherished, the children raised; here, the ties are revealed as permanent property, rejoined at a destination where nothing severs again. Another verse sets the promise's fine detail: “As for those who believe and whose descendants follow them in faith, We will elevate their descendants to their rank, never discounting anything ˹of the reward˺ of their deeds” (52:21) — a parent's station extended downward to a child without the child's own record being diminished, a mechanism of pure mercy. And the angels are heard praying for exactly this outcome: “Our Lord! Admit them into the Gardens of Eternity which You have promised them, along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants” (40:8). The angelic prayer uses the same family vocabulary as this verse — proof that the reunion is not an accommodation but the design. Togetherness, in the Qur'an's Paradise, is not the reward's garnish; it is half the reward, prepared for the people who treated ties as trusts (13:21) while the ties could still be cut.

**Angels From Every Gate: The Arrival Protocol**

“And the angels will enter upon them from every gate” (13:23). The commentators, reading the scene, described the angels arriving from every direction with gifts and presentations in honor of the entrants — a protocol of welcome, not a security check. The number of gates is the number of honors: each gate its own greeting, so that the arrival is not one welcome but a circumference of them. The Book's fullest arrival scene shows the gates themselves participating: “When they arrive at its ˹already˺ open gates, its keepers will say, “Peace be upon you! You have done well, so come in, to stay forever”” (39:73). Three features of that scene mirror this verse: the gates stand already open — no queue, no interrogation; the keepers address the entrants with a completed record — you have done well; and the tenancy is declared at the door — to stay forever. The many gates also measure the many roads of the faithful life. The portrait of 13:20–22 listed patience, prayer, secret charity, answered insults; a Garden whose entry is honored from every direction is a fitting address for lives that gave from every direction.

**Hosts Who Arrive as Guests**

One reversal in the scene deserves its own notice: the angels “will enter upon them” (13:23). In the world's etiquette, the incumbent receives and the visitor presents himself; in the Garden the residents are settled and the angels come to them, gate after gate, in the posture of guests and gift-bearers. The commentators read the visits as presentations of honor — an arrival protocol mounted for a household being shown to the universe's nobility. The reversal is the reward's rhetoric: the believer who spent his life as a stranger — mocked at 13:5, demanded signs at 13:7, outwaited decade by decade — is received on arrival as the person to whom visitors come. The Qur'an's other arrival scenes confirm the protocol: the angels descend on the steadfast with the news that removes fear (41:30), and the keepers of the gates themselves address the entrants (39:73). The direction of motion, in every case, runs from the honored powers toward the former strangers of the earth. Heaven, in these verses, is where the record of being underestimated is formally corrected — and the corrections are performed in person, from every direction at once.

**Every Gate, One Word**

The verse ends on the hinge word — “˹saying,˺” (13:23) — and the next verse supplies the speech: “Peace be upon you for your perseverance” (13:24). The entire apparatus of Gardens, families, gates, and angels converges on one word. The greeting that awaits is the greeting the believer practised: the salām offered in this world's meetings is revealed as rehearsal for the only speech Paradise opens with. The parallel promise states the pattern plainly: “Those who believe and do good will be admitted into Gardens, under which rivers flow—to stay there forever by the Will of their Lord—where they will be greeted with “Peace!”” (14:23). Greeted — the verb of a household receiving a guest, the same word the Book uses for how the people of Paradise meet one another. The sūrah's portrait thus closes where its storm verses began: the thunder was already praising (13:13), the shadows already bowing (13:15); the righteous, it turns out, were already rehearsing the language those worshippers speak. At every gate, the rehearsal ends and the liturgy begins.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:24

> “Peace be upon you for your perseverance. How excellent is the ultimate abode!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wages of Perseverance, Spoken by Angels**

“Peace be upon you for your perseverance” (13:24). The verse is the speech held over from 13:23 — the angels at every gate open their mouths, and the first thing said in the Gardens is a greeting and a wage-statement at once. The grammar is worth watching: peace *because you were patient*. The angelic welcome does not say "for your wealth," "for your eloquence," or "for your numbers"; the one quality named is the one habit the portrait of 13:20–22 listed first and the one the Book's arrival scene repeats: “When they arrive at its ˹already˺ open gates, its keepers will say, “Peace be upon you! You have done well, so come in, to stay forever”” (39:73). Patience is the common denominator of the whole portrait — endurance under mockery (13:5–7), constancy in prayer, the long discipline of answering evil with good (13:22) — and it is therefore the one quality the greeters can cite as the reason for the address. The greeting is also the only salary the verse mentions: the entrants are paid in a word, and the word is the residency itself.

**Why Patience Gets the Greeting**

The verse's accounting deserves a second look, because it reverses worldly arithmetic. A life's visible achievements — the buildings, the campaigns, the published works — go unmentioned at the gate; the invisible endurance of insults, loss, and delay goes first. The Book's logic is consistent: patience was never merely waiting; it was worship aimed at a Face (13:22), the deliberate holding of a position because its Holder was watching. What is done for God's pleasure is what survives to be greeted, because it is what was actually addressed to Him. The parallel scene of the mindful being led in groups shows the same greeting operating as the threshold's liturgy: the keepers' words at 39:73, the angels' here — the speech of arrival is fixed, and its subject is the endurance no one else saw. For the sūrah's first audience, this verse lands as vindication of a decade: the hecklers of Makkah got their answer (13:6–7) not by being persuaded but by being outlasted, and the outlasters are here met at the gates with their decade named in full.

**“How Excellent Is the Ultimate Abode!” — the Exclamation as Verdict**

“How excellent is the ultimate abode!” (13:24). The second sentence is an exclamation, and its structure quietly repeats the sūrah's earlier verdicts in mirror image. At 13:18 the Fire's address was closed with a similar burst — “What an evil place to rest!” — and the two exclamations are built to be heard together: the same vocabulary of resting and dwelling, passed over two residences, with the only difference being the word the verse inserts — excellent versus evil. The symmetry is the doctrine: there are two abodes, both permanent, both described in the language of home, and the difference between them is not size but sign. The angels' exclamation is therefore more than decoration; it is the audit's summary. The final home — the phrase's grammar implies a superlative, the abode that outranks all others — receives its certificate from the only witnesses qualified to issue one: the beings who have seen everything God made and still stop to admire this address.

**The Greeting That Answers a Lifetime of Heckling**

The verse is also the sūrah's own argument brought to rest. It opened with the announcement that most people do not believe (13:1), continued through the mockery of resurrection (13:5), the demand for hastened torment (13:6), and the demand for signs (13:7) — a catalogue of heckling that no single verse answered point by point. The greeting at the gates is the answer, delivered at the only venue where the answer matters. The hecklers questioned the destination; the entrants are standing in it. The hecklers demanded immediacy; the entrants are holding permanence. And the greeting's content — peace — is precisely what the heckling never offered anyone. A generation of believers heard insults daily and replied with the salām; the verse records the exchange rate at which that patience was bought: one word of peace, spoken by the angels, at every gate, forever. No rebuttal composed in Makkah could have answered the mockery so completely, because the mockery was an argument about a future, and the future is where this verse collects.

**A Greeting With a Long Liturgical History**

The greeting did not begin at the gates. The Book records it as the ambient speech of Paradise throughout: the companions of the Garden are “greeted with “Peace!”” (14:23), and the greeting circulates among the residents themselves as the ordinary currency of speech. The pattern reaches back into the sūrah's own materials: the whole creation was already speaking praise — the thunder's hymn (13:13), the shadows' prostration (13:15) — and the believer's practiced salām in this world was his participation in that liturgy before he ever saw it from inside. The verse of the gates thus closes the portrait gallery (13:19–24) with a sound rather than a sight: the first thing the homeowner hears is not the gardens or the rivers but the word he spent a lifetime learning to say. This is why the greeting is delivered by angels — the same beings who entered from every gate (13:23) — as though the whole created order were handing the arrival its language. The next verse will turn to those who broke what these entrants kept, and its verdict will be built from the same vocabulary of covenant, ties, and abode — the two galleries sharing one grammar, at opposite addresses.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:25

> And those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land—it is they who will be condemned and will have the worst abode.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mirror Portrait: Same Materials, Opposite Uses**

“And those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land” (13:25). Set this beside 13:20–21 and the design of the sūrah's gallery becomes explicit: the two portraits are built from the same three materials — the covenant, the ties, the earth — used in opposite ways. The keeper honors the covenant, maintains the ties, and lives on the land; the violator breaks the covenant, severs the ties, and corrupts the land. The Qur'an repeats the triplet almost verbatim in another chapter: “those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, and sever what Allah has ordered to be joined, and spread corruption in the land—it is they who are the losers” (2:27) — and that repetition confirms the pairing is deliberate doctrine, not local rhetoric. The mirror construction preempts a comfortable misunderstanding. No reader can imagine the two portraits as different sorts of people with different sorts of religion; they are the same sort of creature, holding the same pledges, whose difference is entirely a matter of what they did with what was entrusted to them.

**After It Has Been Affirmed: The Timing of the Treachery**

The verse's most damaging phrase is temporal: “after it has been affirmed” (13:25). The violation described is not ignorance of terms never seen; it is desertion after the terms were accepted, confirmed, and acted on. This is what separates the covenant-breaker from the merely unaware — and the sūrah will not allow the Makkans that unawareness, having spent twenty-four verses delivering the evidence. The commentators tied the affirmed covenant to the primordial testimony, where every soul answered “Yes, You are! We testify” (7:172) and was warned against the plea of never having known. A later, explicit affirmation — acceptance of the revelation, entry into the faith — re-states the same pledge in waking life. Treason after affirmation is therefore a different offense from error before instruction: the deserter knows the flag he abandons. This aggravation also explains the verse's position as the gallery's closing panel. It follows the portrait of the keepers not merely as a contrast but as a verdict on the readers of both portraits — whoever has read this far, in any generation, has had the covenant affirmed to him, and now knows exactly which side of the mirror he is standing on.

**Corruption as the Covenant-Breaker's Signature**

“And spread corruption in the land” (13:25). In the Qur'an, working corruption on the earth is not primarily an environmental offense; it is the social and moral signature of unkept covenants. The commentary tradition connected the corruption of this phrase with violence and bloodshed in other verses, and with moral and commercial corruption in others — cheating measures, crushing the weak, tearing the social fabric. The common thread is intelligible: corruption is what becomes of a world whose bonds are cut. The land, in the Qur'an's teaching, holds together as a fabric of trusts — the covenant with God above, the ties of kin and neighbor below, the honest weight in the market. Sever the ties (as this verse's second clause records) and the fabric goes with them: the corrupter is the covenant-breaker seen from outside, the man whose private treachery has become public weather. This is why the verse needs no separate doctrine of social ethics. The order of its clauses *is* the doctrine: broken covenant first, severed ties second, corrupted land third — the vertical betrayal propagating downward until the very ground testifies against the breaker.

**Condemned and Housed: The Two Sentences**

“It is they who will be condemned and will have the worst abode” (13:25). The sentence falls in two registers, and the commentators read both with care. The condemnation was understood as distance from God's mercy — the curse as exile rather than tantrum, the creature placed outside the reach of the mercy it refused; another reading makes the distance spatial: distance from God's presence, sorrow standing behind a closed door of one's own locking. Both readings preserve the point that matters: the curse is not a mood of heaven but a separation, and the separation was chosen from inside. The evil abode, on the other reading of the exegetes, may describe an evil outcome within this world — the corrupted land consuming its corrupters — or the Fire in the next; the ambiguity is itself instructive, since the two readings were never rivals in the Qur'an's economy: the evil abode has branches in both worlds, and the covenant-breaker pays in the currency of both. Against the portrait's opening — those who have the finest reward and the ultimate abode (13:18, 13:22–23) — the closing panel's address is the same word turned inside out: the worst abode, the residence that matches the refusal.

**The Gallery Closes the Case Opened at 13:19**

With this verse the sūrah's double portrait is complete, and the case that 13:19 filed is decided. There, the question was whether the knower could be like the blind; here, the blind man's biography is finished — covenant violated, ties severed, land corrupted, address assigned. The structure of the whole passage (13:19–25) now reads as one sustained act of sorting: a question about sight, four verses of the seeing man's habits, and a closing verse of the blind man's habits, with the addresses announced at each end. Nothing in the sūrah's remaining verses will withdraw from this sorting; the following passages will refine the portraits' theology — provision (13:26), guidance (13:27–29), the mission's patience (13:30–32) — but the division is fixed. The mirror construction leaves the reader where it leaves everyone: between the panels, holding a covenant that has now been affirmed to him twice over, once in the primordial testimony and once in the reading. The verse's final function is therefore diagnostic — it tells the reader not who the violators were, but which portrait is currently being drawn in him.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:26

> Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills. And the disbelievers become prideful of ˹the pleasures of˺ this worldly life. But the life of this world, compared to the Hereafter, is nothing but a fleeting enjoyment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse Names the Distributor Before the Distribution**

“Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills” (13:26). The verse opens the economics of this world by naming the economist: provision is not generated by luck, inherited by right, or earned by cleverness — it is *given*, in measures that vary by will. The Qur'an states the doctrine repeatedly and precisely: “Surely your Lord gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills. He is certainly All-Aware, All-Seeing of His servants” (17:30). The distribution is therefore administered — the All-Aware of that verse is the same Knower of wombs and seen-and-unseen that this sūrah installed at 13:8–9 — and what looks from the ground like luck is, from the office, a decision. The Book even stages the confession: when Qārūn's wealth swallowed him and the earth then swallowed him, the very people who had envied him the day before said, “It is certainly Allah Who gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills of His servants” (28:82). Envy and pride, the two errors wealth generates, are corrected by the same sentence: the Distributor gives, withholds, and explains to no committee. The verse of this sūrah is the calm statement of that office before either error is addressed.

**Pride in the Cheque: The Disbelievers' Economics**

“And the disbelievers become prideful of ˹the pleasures of˺ this worldly life” (13:26). The verse's second clause diagnoses what the disbeliever does with the distribution: he reads his own balance as his own verdict. The pride is not merely enjoying the provision — the Qur'an nowhere forbids enjoyment — but *inferring from it*: concluding that the size of the account certifies the standing of the account-holder. The Book names the mechanics of that illusion with unusual harshness: “Whoever desires this fleeting world ˹alone˺, We hasten in it whatever We please to whoever We will; then We destine them for Hell” (17:18). Note the terrible verb — *hasten*: the very abundance the prideful man boasts of is described as an accelerated payment, the wage handed over early to the worker who demanded it in the wrong currency. Prosperity, on this teaching, can be a bill settling rather than a favor continuing — the doctrine the commentators called drawing a person along by stages, so that he mistakes the slackening of the rope for its lengthening. The prideful disbeliever of 13:26 is thus not merely foolish; he is cashing the instrument that will ruin him, and signing for it gratefully.

**A Fleeting Enjoyment: The Arithmetic of Comparison**

“But the life of this world, compared to the Hereafter, is nothing but a fleeting enjoyment” (13:26). The corrective is not denial but division. The verse does not say the world's pleasures are nothing; it says they are nothing *compared to the Hereafter* — the arithmetic of a specific ratio, not a renunciation. The Qur'an applies the same arithmetic elsewhere with vivid materials: “This worldly life is no more than play and amusement, but far better is the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter for those mindful ˹of Allah˺” (6:32); and the great rain-simile prices the whole show: “This is like rain that causes plants to grow, to the delight of the planters. But later the plants dry up and you see them wither, then they are reduced to chaff” (57:20). The rain-simile is this sūrah's own water-theme (13:17) turned economic: the same rain that sorts truth from foam in the wadi sorts fortunes in the field, and the crop's green week is the world's whole dividend. For the investor, the Book offers the alternative portfolio in one verse: “Whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter, We will increase their harvest” (42:20) — the Hereafter's yield is not merely equal but multiplied, and the world's yield is not merely small but spent. A Companion of the Prophet ﷺ, Abū'l-Dardāʾ, is reported to have compressed the arithmetic into a sentence: when a man comes to loathe this world, he comes to love the next, and he abstains from this one to the measure of his love for the other. The saying belongs to the verse's school of thought: the world is not escaped by poverty or ascetic display but by a corrected exchange rate — and the verse is the correction, issued in advance of the audit.

**Why the Warning Stands Beside the Distribution**

The verse's architecture — distribution in the first clause, pride in the second, re-pricing in the third — is a mercy aimed at two audiences at once. To the poor believer it says: the abundance you watch others hold is dispensed by the All-Aware, not hoarded by the favored; your limited portion is a decision of the same office that decided theirs, and 13:22's portrait of the giver who donates from *what We have provided* is written for your hands, not just for the wealthy's. To the rich believer it says: the balance is a trust with a re-pricing attached; spend it secretly and openly (13:22) before the exchange rate of 57:20 — green to chaff — is applied to you. The disbelievers' pride, in short, is treated in this verse as a reading error, and the verse is the reading lesson. It is also the sūrah's connective tissue: the provision that God distributes here is the same provision whose differentials taught at 13:4 that one water yields different harvests by God's authorship. The verse of the crops and the verse of the checkbooks teach one doctrine: the differences are the Distributor's, and the price of them is paid in the Hereafter, not the ledger.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:27

> The disbelievers say, “If only a sign could be sent down to him from his Lord.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Indeed, Allah leaves to stray whoever He wills, and guides to Himself whoever turns to Him—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Demand Repeated, the Answer Turned Inward**

The disbelievers' request returns in almost the same words they used at 13:7 — a sign sent down upon the Prophet ﷺ from his Lord — and the sūrah's reply this time is stranger and deeper. At 13:7 the answer clarified the Prophet's office: he is a warner, every people had a guide. Here the answer turns from his office to their condition: “Indeed, Allah leaves to stray whoever He wills, and guides to Himself whoever turns to Him” (13:27). The reply declines the request's premise. The sign-demanders assume their problem is evidential — that something has not yet been shown — and the answer locates their problem elsewhere: nothing is missing from the evidence; the missing thing is the turn. Guidance, on this showing, is not a spectacle that descends on spectators but a direction taken by the willing, and what God withholds or grants is dispensed in exactly that currency. The demand for one more sign is therefore answered the way a physician answers a patient demanding a second opinion while refusing the prescription: the issue was never the opinion.

**Leaving Astray as Justice, Not Caprice**

The first half of the reply requires care, because it has been misread for centuries as divine caprice — God straying people at whim. The Qur'an's own usage corrects the misreading. The parallel statement binds the straying to the strayer's content: “˹It is˺ certainly Allah ˹Who˺ leaves to stray whoever He wills, and guides whoever He wills” (35:8) — and that verse's context is the man whose evil deeds were made so appealing to him that he *deemed them good*: the leaving astray operates on appetites the person has already fed. The same law is stated as a locked door: “Whoever Allah allows to stray, none can guide, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance” (7:186) — the straying described is *in their defiance*, a wandering the wanderer chose and heaven then ratified. Leaving astray, in other words, is what desertion looks like from the deserted side: the Light is withdrawn from eyes that shut themselves, and the withdrawal is permanent. The doctrine humbles the reader without slandering God: He owes no one a sign; He has never withheld one from a turning heart; and the sentence He passes on the persistent is simply the permanence of their own choice.

**The Turn Is the Sign**

“Guides to Himself whoever turns to Him” (13:27). The second half of the reply installs the real venue of the miracle: the turn. The Qur'an elsewhere locates all the choice in exactly this hinge: “We already showed them the Way, whether they ˹choose to˺ be grateful or ungrateful” (76:3); the Way is shown, and the whole drama is the direction of the walking. Another verse states the offer without varnish: “to whoever of you wills to take the Straight Way” (81:28) — the Way is open to willing takers, which means the sign-demanders were asking heaven to do the one thing heaven has structured the world to refuse: believe for them. The Prophet ﷺ was instructed to state the freedom aloud: “Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve” (18:29). This is the deep answer to the repeated demand of 13:7 and 13:27. A sign sent down on demand would not produce the guided; it would produce the coerced — and the coerced are neither of the two categories the verse divides. The turn is the sign's replacement because the turn is the thing being tested: whoever turns finds guidance waiting, which means the guidance was never the missing element. The will was.

**The Sūrah's Third Refusal, and the Reply That Becomes a Portrait**

This is the sūrah's third recorded demand, and the sequence of answers shows a patience with its own escalation. The demand to hasten torment (13:6) was answered with the archive and the two attributes; the demand for a sign (13:7) was answered with the warner's office and the universal guide; the repeated demand here is answered with the theology of guidance itself. Then the sūrah does something no disputation does: it stops arguing and paints. The clause “whoever turns to Him” (13:27) is left grammatically open, and the next verse walks through the opening — “those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah” (13:28) — followed by the summation of bliss and beautiful return (13:29). The refusers asked for a spectacle; the reply they receive is a portrait of the people who turned without one: their inner weather described, their destination named. The argument's force is quiet and total. The sign they demanded was meant to reassure them; the portrait shows what reassurance actually looks like — and it is available on terms the demanders have so far refused to meet: a turn. The reply is also left grammatically open, the sentence running past its comma into the next verse, because its completion is not a doctrine but a population: the guided of 13:28, described in two clauses. The demand for a sign receives, in place of a decree, an unfinished invitation — and the invitation's completion is the reader's. The openness is itself the answer: the mockers wanted heaven to close the question; heaven handed the question back, alive.

One last turn of the verse's grammar: the straying and the guiding are both attributed to God, and the turning is attributed to the servant. The distribution of verbs is the verse's whole counsel — God's acts are not argued with, and the servant's act is not performed by anyone else.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:28

> those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to the Sign Demand Is a Heart Condition**

The verse completes the clause that 13:27 left open — “guides to Himself whoever turns to Him—those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah” (13:28) — and in doing so it answers the sign-demanders with a state of the soul rather than a spectacle in the sky. The guided, on the verse's showing, are recognizable by their interior weather: hearts that have settled. The restlessness the verse presumes is universal — a heart that tosses between fears, appetites, ambitions, and griefs, seeking a surface that holds — and the verse names the one surface that bears the weight: remembrance. This is why the answer to “show us a sign” is a description of composure. A sign could impress the audience; only settledness could change them, and the settledness is available without the sign. The disbelievers were asking heaven to rearrange the sky; the verse quietly rearranges the terms instead, telling them that the thing they wanted the sign to produce — certainty, rest, the end of striving — is produced by a practice they have not tried.

**The Restless Heart and Its Only Home**

The verse says it twice — statement, then the absolute affirmation: “Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort” (13:28) — and the doubling teaches that the doctrine is stated without exception. The Book elsewhere shows remembrance operating on the same hearts from the other side: “The ˹true˺ believers are only those whose hearts tremble at the remembrance of Allah” (8:2) — the trembling of the guilty conscience, the shake that wakes — and again as an alarm against numbness: “Has the time not yet come for believers’ hearts to be humbled at the remembrance of Allah” (57:16). Trembling, humbling, settling: one name, three effects, each matched to what the heart needs. The comfort promised here is therefore not comfort as the world distributes it — the anesthetic of distraction — but the settledness of a thing returned to its element. The commentators read this verse as the founding charter of the discipline of invocation: the tongue moist with God's name, the heart following the tongue, until the tossed thing finds the anchor it was made for. The world's comforts wear out because they are foreign to the heart; the remembrance does not wear out because the heart, by its creation, belongs to the One remembered.

**Remembrance as an Exchange Already Underway**

“Remember Me; I will remember you. And thank Me, and never be ungrateful” (2:152). The verse of this sūrah stands inside a relationship the Qur'an describes as mutual: the servant's remembrance answered by the Lord's — an exchange whose greater half is stated first in the divine grammar of that verse, *I will remember you*, and whose creaturely half is the practice 13:28 prices. The sūrah's own architecture makes the comfort credible. It has spent verses 8 through 10 establishing that God's knowledge already wraps every whisper, every hidden thing, every womb; the heart that remembers Him is not attracting an absent attention but returning an ongoing one. That is the ground of the comfort's security: the remembrance is not a summons that may go unanswered — the answer preceded the summons. And the exchange has a polemical tail aimed at the idolaters of this sūrah: their gods had to be informed, attract, reminded. The verse of the resting hearts describes the opposite economy — a God who remembers first, needs no informing, and whose remembrance of a servant is the greatest fact in that servant's biography. The exchange also explains the verse's verb of finding: hearts *find* comfort — discovery, not manufacture. The rest was never created by the remembering; it was located. The heart had been laboring at cross-purposes, seeking in what perishes the stability that lives only in the Eternal — and the remembrance is the corrected address, a permanence the finding does not wear out.

**The Sūrah's Quiet Center**

Placement makes this verse the fulcrum of the chapter. Behind it: the sign-refused of 13:27, the taunts and demands of 13:5–7, the denials of 13:1 — the noise of the unturned. Ahead of it: “bliss and an honourable destination” (13:29) — the verdict on the settled. The verse of the resting hearts sits between them as both the destination's first installment and the noise's only cure. The commentators noted that the guided of 13:27 are precisely these: those who turned, believed, and found the heart's repose in the remembrance. The sequence is worth keeping intact, because it is the verse's practical instruction. The turn comes first (13:27); belief follows; the remembrance is the practice; the comfort is the fruit — and the fruit arrives in this world, before the bliss of the next. For a sūrah that has catalogued storms (13:12–13), thunderbolts (13:13), shackles (13:5), and corrupted lands (13:25), the quiet at the center is itself an argument: the same Lord who shakes the sky with signs has installed a sign in the chest, and its name is a heart at rest that was not at rest before. The verse is short enough to memorize, which is part of its design: the cure it prescribes is the verse's own first dose.

The verse's promise also travels. Hearts at rest are the only evidence the sign-demanders were offered that guidance works, and the evidence is portable: it walks into markets, families, and sieges without equipment. A community of such hearts is itself the sign the mockers kept requesting — a people made calm by a Name — and the request, granted in this form, was standing in their streets the whole time they were asking the sky for something else.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:29

> Those who believe and do good, for them will be bliss and an honourable destination.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Bliss and Return: The Portrait's Closing Certificate**

“Those who believe and do good, for them will be bliss and an honourable destination” (13:29). The verse closes the bracket that 13:27–28 opened, and it ends — note the quotation mark — as part of the Prophet's ﷺ dictated reply to the sign-demanders. The argument of that reply is now complete: they asked for a sign; he was given a description of the guided to recite instead. The verse's two gifts are ordered with care. Bliss — the Qur'anic word is *ṭūbā*, a term so rich that the readers of the text divided over it: some took it as the good life granted in this world, some as Paradise itself, some as a tree in the Garden, some simply as the joyous state of its people. The disagreement is a measure of the word's breadth rather than a defect in it, and the readings are less rivals than installments: the bliss begins as a quality of life before death and continues as the address after it. The second gift, the honourable destination, fixes the direction: *return* — the vocabulary of a journey home rather than a sentence into exile.

**The Good Life, Priced and Verified**

The first reading of *ṭūbā* — a good life in this world — is not a commentator's fancy; the Qur'an states it elsewhere as a promise with its own verse: “Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, We will surely bless them with a good life, and We will certainly reward them according to the best of their deeds” (16:97). Two features of that promise matter for understanding 13:29. First, the good life is given to *belief joined with deed* — the exact pair of this verse — which means the world's bliss is not a wealth bracket but a state: the heart settled by remembrance (13:28), the conscience clean, the ties maintained (13:21), the insults answered with good (13:22). The believer's bliss is thus already on deposit before the grave. Second, the reward is calculated “according to the best” — the audit pays on the maximum, which is the same generosity the arrival scenes show: the gates already open, the greeting already warm (13:23–24). A worldly reading of *ṭūbā* also corrects a modern misreading of this sūrah: the comforts promised are not comforts purchased, which is why the verse can promise them to people the world is currently mocking (13:5–7). The good life is also portable in a way prosperity is not: the mocked believer carries it through the mockery, since 13:28's settled heart is its residence, while the prosperous mocker cannot buy it back at any price — the doubled earth of 13:18 was refused as a ransom for far less. Bliss, in the verse's economy, is the first property that survives every relocation. One grammatical thread ties the verse into the sūrah's fabric: the word for return shares its root with the turning of 13:30 — to Him I turn, to Him is my return. The practice and the destination are one motion, begun in time and completed outside it. The believer who spends a life turning arrives by the only road that exists: the road he is already on.

**An Honourable Destination: The Grammar of Arrival**

The second gift is described with the word for return, and the Qur'an uses that word for death deliberately — the end of life is a going back, not a going away. The same paired words — beautiful return — recur through the Book as the reward's signature, and the return is attended along the whole route. At the moment of death, the Book shows the steadfast receiving their travelers' news in advance: “the angels descend upon them, ˹saying,˺ “Do not fear, nor grieve. Rather, rejoice in the good news of Paradise, which you have been promised”” (41:30). The descent answers the two great fears of the crossing — fear of what comes, grief for what is left — and its cargo is the destination's own name. At the arrival, the protocol of 13:23–24 resumes: gates open, angels visiting, the greeting of peace. The honor of the destination, in other words, is not merely the place's quality; it is the treatment received *en route and at the door* — the measure of how the Returning One receives. The destination that other addresses in this sūrah call the worst abode (13:25) is here the return made honorable — the same journey, two receptions.

**The Sūrah's Two Verdicts, Now Symmetrical**

With this verse the sūrah's verdict structure closes into symmetry. At 13:18, the first verdict: those who respond receive the finest reward; those who refuse would offer the doubled earth and be refused, their home Hell and their rest evil. At 13:29, the last verdict of the gallery: those who believe and do good receive bliss and an honorable destination. Between the two verdicts, the sūrah delivered its evidence — signs, storms, parables, portraits — and the symmetry is itself the final exhibit: the same court, the same two parties, the same two outcomes, restated as the chapter's frame. The verse's close also pivots the sūrah toward its remaining business. The next verse returns to the Prophet ﷺ in the field: sent to a community that denies the Most Compassionate, instructed in the four-clause answer that carries him through — my Lord, no god but He, in Him I trust, to Him I turn. The portrait gallery is shut; the mission's patience is the subject now. And the verse that shut the gallery has already told the mission its arithmetic: bliss in the hand, honor at the end of the road, whatever the town says in between.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:30

> And so We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ to a community, like ˹We did with˺ earlier communities, so that you may recite to them what We have revealed to you. Yet they deny the Most Compassionate. Say, “He is my Lord! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. In Him I put my trust, and to Him I turn ˹in repentance˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Prophet Sent the Way Prophets Are Sent**

“And so We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ to a community, like ˹We did with˺ earlier communities” (13:30). The verse places the Prophet's ﷺ mission inside the standing series: the last of the communities of revelation receiving the latest of the messengers, dispatched in the same manner and for the same task as those before. The Qur'an insists on the pattern's scale: “˹Imagine˺ how many prophets We sent to those ˹destroyed˺ before!” (43:6) — and on its outcome, which the next verse records flatly: “But no prophet ever came to them without being mocked” (43:7). The pattern also had its method: “Indeed, We have sent messengers before you ˹O Prophet˺ to other people who We put through suffering and adversity ˹for their denial˺, so perhaps they would be humbled” (6:42). For the Prophet ﷺ, the placement is both comfort and calibration. Comfort: his rejection is not a novelty but the series' ordinary weather, already survived by every messenger before him. Calibration: his task is the series' task, defined in the verse's next clause without embellishment — “so that you may recite to them what We have revealed to you” (13:30) — recitation, the delivery of the text, not the manufacture of the signs the town kept demanding at 13:7 and 13:27.

**The Denial Named With Unusual Precision: the Most Compassionate**

“Yet they deny the Most Compassionate” (13:30). The sūrah has recorded denials of resurrection, of messengerhood, of signs; this one names its object with a specific divine name — *al-Raḥmān*, the Most Compassionate — and the reports preserved about the Makkans' reaction explain why. The name was foreign to their religious vocabulary: when told to prostrate to the Compassionate, they derisively asked who the Compassionate might be; and at the treaty of Ḥudaybiyah, when the scribe wrote the basmala's opening — In the name of God, the Compassionate, the Merciful — a Makkan objected that they did not recognize the Compassionate, and the words were changed. The Book itself confirms the mockery as a pattern: “When the disbelievers see you ˹O Prophet˺, they only make fun of you, ˹saying,˺ “Is this the one who speaks ˹ill˺ of your gods?” while they disbelieve at the mention of the Most Compassionate” (21:36). The denial of the name is the denial of mercy as a doctrine — the claim that the Lord of the thunder (13:13) and the wombs (13:8) is characterized first by compassion. That is why the name matters doctrinally: to deny al-Raḥmān is not one superstition among many; it is the rejection of the attribute that makes every other divine address bearable, from the standing forgiveness of 13:6 to the greeting of peace at the gates (13:24).

**The Prophet's ﷺ Four-Clauses Answer**

The instruction follows immediately: “Say, “He is my Lord! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. In Him I put my trust, and to Him I turn ˹in repentance˺.”” (13:30). Four clauses, each doing one job. *He is my Lord* — a personal confession of the disputed One, the pronoun carrying the whole theology: the One they deny is *mine*, known and held. *There is no god except Him* — the shahāda itself, preached without concession to the pantheon the town defended. *In Him I put my trust* — the operative stance of a man without tribe's full protection, without the signs demanded, without the power to compel; his security is outsourced entirely to the One in question. *To Him I turn* — repentance as the Prophet's own ongoing posture, the acknowledgment that return is not merely the message but the messenger's practice. An earlier prophet is recorded using nearly the same closing formula in his own defense: “My success comes only through Allah. In Him I trust and to Him I turn” (11:88). The refrain across the missions is itself doctrine: the prophetic office is carried by trust and return, not by vindication — the two clauses are the armor, and they are also, quietly, the answer to every hostile town: the Prophet does not need the town's verdict, because his Lord is the subject of both his trust and his turning.

**Reciting in a Town That Denies the Name**

The verse's assignment — recite to them what We have revealed (13:30) — deserves the final word, because it defines the mission's whole method in one verb. The Prophet ﷺ is not told to answer the town's philosophy, match its poets, or satisfy its sign-demands; he is told to recite. The method is the argument: the Book's own voice, delivered as received, is the operative sign — the claim this sūrah has been making since its first verse, that what came down is “the truth” (13:1). A community denying the Most Compassionate is being handed the Compassionate's own speech, which is either the worst possible gift or the best, depending on the ear. The verse's placement also turns the sūrah toward its close: the remaining thirteen verses will deal with the mission's open questions — the Book's power (13:31), the mocked messengers' end (13:32), the plotting (13:33, 13:42), the shrinking borders (13:41), and the final witness (13:43). All of it is underwritten by the stance the verse has just installed: trust and return, recitation and patience, in a town that denies the name of the One who sent the text.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:31

> If there were a recitation that could cause mountains to move, or the earth to split, or the dead to speak, ˹it would have been this Quran˺. But all matters are by Allah’s Will. Have the believers not yet realized that had Allah willed, He could have guided all of humanity? And disasters will continue to afflict the disbelievers or strike close to their homes for their misdeeds, until Allah’s promise comes to pass. Surely Allah never fails in His promise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mockers' Wishlist, Granted Only in the Negative**

“If there were a recitation that could cause mountains to move, or the earth to split, or the dead to speak, ˹it would have been this Quran˺” (13:31). The verse opens on the mockers' own wishlist — mountains walked, ground torn open, ancestors raised to chat — and the commentary tradition recorded the demand as literal: the disbelievers of Makkah told the Prophet ﷺ to ask his Lord for exactly these productions. The verse's answer is a backhanded concession: such a recitation exists, and it is this one — the Qur'an's power outruns the requested theatrics — but the power is not on hire. A parallel text closes the same door with the same key: even angels descending, the dead speaking, everything gathered before their eyes would not make them believe unless God wills. The demand assumed that faith is a reflex produced by spectacle; the verse denies the assumption at its root. The Qur'an could split the earth, the verse says in effect, and the split would change nothing that matters — because what is broken is not in the ground. The sign-demand of 13:7 and 13:27 here meets its final, theological refusal: not "the miracles are unavailable" but "the miracles are not the missing ingredient."

**Why the Refusal Holds: The Will Comes First**

“But all matters are by Allah’s Will” (13:31). The clause is the verse's hinge, and the doctrine it states is the one this sūrah has been assembling since its first verses: the conducted cosmos (13:2), the measured wombs (13:8), the knowledge without outside (13:9) — and now the will that governs guidance itself. The believers are then addressed directly: “Have the believers not yet realized that had Allah willed, He could have guided all of humanity?” (13:31). The question is aimed at the faithful's frustration, not the deniers' doubt. A companion of the Prophet ﷺ could be forgiven for measuring the mission's success by its conversion count and finding the count insultingly low; the verse re-ranks the metrics: the mission's success was never the town's surrender, because the town's surrender was never available for purchase by spectacle. Had Allah willed, every soul on earth would be a believer — the parallel text states it with its own rebuke: “Had your Lord so willed ˹O Prophet˺, all ˹people˺ on earth would have certainly believed, every single one of them! Would you then force people to become believers?” (10:99). Guidance belongs to the will of God and the turn of the servant (13:27); it is not extracted by wonders.

**The Siege Clock: Calamities Until the Promise**

“And disasters will continue to afflict the disbelievers or strike close to their homes for their misdeeds, until Allah’s promise comes to pass” (13:31). The verse now describes what the refusers will get instead of miracles: pressure. The commentators read the afflictions broadly — trials touching selves, children, and property — or drawing near their homes, siege tightening around the quarter. The design of the pressure is itself a mercy with a deadline: the disasters are not random weather but consequences, explicitly tied “for their misdeeds,” and they run on a clock — “until Allah’s promise comes to pass” (13:31). Many of the exegetes read that promise as the conquest of Makkah, the historical event toward which the years of affliction were counting down. The lesson embedded in the reading is structural: between the demand for signs and the arrival of judgment stands a period whose function is not entertainment but warning — pressure applied where it might still produce a turn (13:27–28), because once the promise lands, the era of turning is over. The verse thus gives the mockers a map of their own future: disasters now, promise later, and the whole interval on a fuse.

**Never Fails the Promise: The Chapter's Track Record Applied**

“Surely Allah never fails in His promise” (13:31). The closing clause is a formula the sūrah has already earned the right to use. Its second verse established the track record: heavens conducted, orbits appointed, the sun never once late to its entrance (13:2). Its sixth verse answered the demand to hasten torment with the reminder that His promise will not be defaulted (13:6, in the parallel text's own words: “And Allah will never fail in His promise,” 22:47). Now the same reliability is transferred to the promise of the verse's middle — the promise that will come to pass — and by extension to every promise the Book has made to either party of the gallery: the Gardens of 13:23 as surely as the sentence of 13:25. The clause also quietly closes the chapter's longest running dispute. The mockers' wager, from 13:5 onward, was that nothing follows the denials — no raising, no seizure, no settling. The verse's last words price that wager: the promise-keeping God who has never missed an orbital appointment will not begin missing them now. What remains for the audience is the only variable the verse leaves open — the turn, before the clock runs out. The clause's position as the verse's last words gives the promise the final say over the disasters: afflictions are penultimate; the promise is ultimate. A chapter that opened by calling the revelation truth (13:1) closes its longest verse by pricing the word — truth-telling, for God, is measured in missed appointments, and the count is zero.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:32

> Other messengers had already been ridiculed before you, but I delayed the disbelievers ˹for a while˺ then seized them. And how ˹horrible˺ was My punishment!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mocking Precedent: A Standing Series**

“Other messengers had already been ridiculed before you” (13:32). The verse hands the Prophet ﷺ the file on his predecessors, and the file is thick. The Qur'an states the pattern as a rule without exceptions: “but no messenger ever came to them without being mocked” (15:11); and again, in nearly the same words: “But no prophet ever came to them without being mocked” (43:7). The endurance of the mocked is recorded with its reward attached: “Indeed, messengers before you were rejected but patiently endured rejection and persecution until Our help came to them. And Allah’s promise ˹to help˺ is never broken” (6:34). For the Prophet ﷺ, standing in a town that ridicules him — the sūrah has documented the mockery at 13:5, the derision at the Compassionate's name at 13:30 — the precedent performs two services at once. It normalizes his experience: the mockery is the office's standard equipment, not a malfunction of his mission. And it guarantees the sequence's other half: the same series that records universal mocking records universal patience, universal help, and — as this verse adds — universal seizure of the mockers. The Prophet ﷺ is not living through an anomaly. He is living inside a pattern with a fixed middle and a fixed end.

**The Divine First Person: “I Delayed, I Seized”**

“But I delayed the disbelievers ˹for a while˺ then seized them” (13:32). The verse shifts into God's own voice — I delayed, I seized — and the first person is doing doctrinal work. The delay is not the world's inertia, not the gods' slumber, not oversight; it is an act, willed and timed, by the same Actor who performs the seizure. This is the respite doctrine that 13:6 already planted: the demand to hasten the torment was answered with the reminder that forgiveness is in circulation and severity is real; here the same two hands are named in the first person, delay and seizure, as consecutive movements of one will. The pattern-verse of Sūrah al-Ḥajj states the identical sequence as settled history: “But I granted the disbelievers a respite, then I seized them” — and the parallel is exact because the doctrine is exact. The respite's purpose is legible from the sūrah's own teaching: the interval is mercy's open account (13:6), the turn's available window (13:27–28). The seizure is what the window's closing is called. Both are acts; neither is weather. A generation reading its own prosperity as endorsement is warned by the grammar itself: the sentence's subject never changed, and it is not the disbelievers.

**The Question That Answers Itself: “How Horrible Was My Punishment!”**

“And how ˹horrible˺ was My punishment!” (13:32). The exclamation is a question — *how* was My retribution? — and the classical readers explained its function: it invites the audience to survey the answered ruins of the mocking nations and supply the adjective themselves. The address is pointed. According to the commentators, the sense conveyed to the Prophet's ﷺ opponents is: consider what was done with those who mocked before, and understand that the same treatment waits for the mockers among you. History, on this reading, is not a museum but a warning label, and the verse makes the audience read it aloud. The Book elsewhere shrinks the respite to expose its true size: the Messengers of Firm Resolve are told, “On the Day they see what they have been threatened with, it will be as if they had only stayed ˹in this world˺ for an hour of a day” (46:35) — the delay that felt like permanence from inside the respite registers as an hour at its end. The exclamation's force depends on that asymmetry: the mocked endured a decade; the mockers' punishment has no decade in it at all. The question is rhetorical because the answer has already been standing in the ruins for centuries, and the audience has walked past it (13:41 will make them look at it).

**Respite as the Believer's Reading Assignment**

For the mocked, the verse is an instruction manual disguised as a report. The same verse that threatens the mockers authorizes the patient: the pattern's end is fixed, the help of 6:34 is never defaulted, and the middle of the pattern — the decade of ridicule — is the zone the believer is assigned to work. That is why the command that brackets the whole doctrine is patience: “So endure patiently, as did the Messengers of Firm Resolve. And do not ˹seek to˺ hasten ˹the torment˺ for the deniers” (46:35) — patience and *no hastening*, the exact opposite of the Makkans' demand at 13:6, and the exact opposite of the believer's own temptation. The verse of this sūrah thus completes the mission's operating system. 13:30 installed the four clauses — my Lord, no god but He, trust, return; 13:31 explained the will behind the unconverted town; 13:32 explains the calendar: delay is designed, seizure is certain, and the interval between them is where the recitation (13:30) and the patience (13:22) do their work. The mission's armor, in other words, now includes a watch: the Prophet ﷺ is being told not when the seizure falls — the schedule is God's — but what the waiting is for. The delay's visibility deserves one more word. Every generation of mockers in the recorded pattern died comfortably — the respite did not fail them on schedule; it failed their heirs' confidence instead, which is precisely its design: each cohort watches the previous cohort's comfortable old age and concludes the seizure is legend. The verse is written to break that inference before it compounds: the same sentence that admits the delay removes its alibi.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:33

> Is ˹there any equal to˺ the Vigilant One Who knows what every soul commits? Yet the pagans have associated others with Allah ˹in worship˺. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Name them! Or do you ˹mean to˺ inform Him of something He does not know on the earth? Or are these ˹gods˺ just empty words?” In fact, the disbelievers’ falsehood has been made so appealing to them that they have been turned away from the Path. And whoever Allah leaves to stray will be left with no guide.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Without an Equal**

“Is ˹there any equal to˺ the Vigilant One Who knows what every soul commits?” (13:33). The comparison is grammatically suspended — the translators' brackets supply its missing half — and the suspension is the rhetoric: the verse sets the Vigilant One on one side of a scale and waits for the audience to place the idols on the other. What makes the One incomparable is specified: surveillance of every soul's deeds, the accounting this sūrah has built since 13:8 — the wombs read, the seen and unseen known, the whisper overheard (13:10) — and the infrastructure is itself Qur'anic: “There is no soul without a vigilant angel ˹recording everything˺” (86:4). Every deed is committed twice — once by the doer, once into the record — and the Vigilant One holds the originals. The idols, by contrast, hold nothing: they did not see the deeds, do not know the doers, and cannot weigh anything at the audit. The verse's opening question is therefore not decorative. It is the sūrah's entire knowledge-argument (13:8–11) recast as a single comparison that the pagan pantheon loses by default: no rival can share a ledger it cannot read.

**“Name Them”: The Interrogation's Sharpest Line**

“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Name them!”” (13:33). After the suspended comparison, the command is almost comic in its confidence: produce the rivals. The Qur'an has already audited what the naming would yield: “These ˹idols˺ are mere names that you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized” (53:23). The inventory of the pantheon is a list of words — names with no referent, titles with no office — and the command to name them is a command to read the list aloud, where its emptiness becomes audible. The verse then offers the namers a choice of explanations for their own practice, each more damaging than the last. Perhaps they intend “to inform Him of something He does not know on the earth” (13:33) — the absurdity of briefing omniscience, the same overturned economy that 13:14 exposed in the idolater's prayers, where informing was the entire service. Or — “are these ˹gods˺ just empty words?” (13:33) — the vocabulary of the commentators' own note: words with no inward meaning, built on a false opinion. The interrogation leaves the pagan no exit: if the partners are real, name them; if real, God would know them without briefing; if unknown to God, they are nothing; if nothing, the worship was a transaction in empty words.

**Fairness Made Diabolical: Why They Cannot See the Emptiness**

“In fact, the disbelievers’ falsehood has been made so appealing to them that they have been turned away from the Path” (13:33). The verse then answers the question the interrogation begs: how do grown men worship words? Because the falsehood has been *made appealing* — the same mechanism the Book documents as a settled law: “Are those whose evil-doing is made so appealing to them that they deem it good ˹like those who are rightly guided˺?” (35:8). The appeal is not innocence; it is consequence — the deed loved long enough until the loving feels like light. The commentators connected this made-fairness to the sūrah's own closing doctrine at 13:42, where plotting belongs to God altogether: the plotters' plot is permitted to glitter right up to the moment it closes on them. And the turning away has a terminus: “And whoever Allah leaves to stray will be left with no guide” (13:33) — the locked door of 13:27, restated at the interrogation's end. The verse thus explains the pagans' condition without excusing it: the appeal of the falsehood is the harvest of their own planting, the wandering is in their defiance (7:186), and the Guide's departure is the ratification of a walking-away that began on their own feet.

**Why the Verse Ends on the Locked Door**

The verse's architecture — the suspended comparison, the command to name, the offered absurdities, the made-appealing falsehood, the guideless end — traces a diagnosis from symptom to cause, and its final clause is both the sternest sentence in the sūrah and, read carefully, its last offer. “Left with no guide” (13:33) is the precise inverse of 13:27's “guides to Himself whoever turns to Him”: the two verses bracket the chapter's doctrine of guidance, one describing the turning that is met, the other the defiance that is ratified. The lock on the door, in the Qur'an's economy, always has its key on the inside — the turn. What the verse forecloses is not repentance but autonomy: no third party can walk the wanderer home, no purchased guidance, no inherited merit, no idol's intercession — the whole intermediary economy that 13:14–16 bankrupted stays bankrupt here. For the sūrah's audience the placement is deliberate. The interrogation of the idols (13:33) sits immediately before the two verses of outcome (13:34's double punishment, 13:35's two ends), so that the reader passes from the naming of the empty words to the pricing of them: words today, wandering tomorrow, address after that. The verse that began by asking who could be compared to the Vigilant One ends by showing what a soul looks like when it has compared itself to no one.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:34

> For them is punishment in this worldly life, but the punishment of the Hereafter is truly far worse. And none can shield them from Allah.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Punishments, One Sequence**

“For them is punishment in this worldly life, but the punishment of the Hereafter is truly far worse” (13:34). The verse prices the disbelievers' account across two ledgers, and the commentators itemized the first: the worldly punishment understood as the whole spectrum of trials — defeat and death, subjugation and slavery, illness and affliction — the ordinary catastrophes of a life lived in refusal. The verse's arithmetic is the point: the Hereafter's punishment is not merely equal or pending but *far worse*, and the Qur'an uses the comparative deliberately across its pages — elsewhere describing the final punishment as more severe and more lasting, as greater, as more disgraceful — so that the doctrine emerges as a consistent ratio: whatever the world inflicts, the next life's version exceeds it in kind and duration. The ratio converts the worldly punishment into an advance notice. Every affliction that lands in this life is simultaneously a consequence and a forecast — the storm front of the weather the refused warnings were about. The sūrah has already installed the clock: disasters will keep falling on the mockers “until Allah’s promise comes to pass” (13:31), and this verse names the two terrains — the world's installment and the Hereafter's remainder — of one unbroken sequence.

**The World's Ruins Are a Standing Lecture**

The worldly half of the punishment is not hidden; it is published, and the Qur'an orders field trips. “Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them? They were far superior in might and ˹richer in˺ monuments throughout the land. But Allah seized them for their sins, and they had no protector from Allah” (40:21). The ruined civilizations were mightier and better built than the audiences being addressed — the lesson is calibrated to deflate every material excuse: wealth did not shield, monuments did not shield, and “they had no protector from Allah” (40:21). The lecture is repeated with an inventory: “Many are the societies We have destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, leaving them in total ruin. ˹Many are˺ also the abandoned wells and lofty palaces!” (22:45). Wells and palaces — the two fixtures of settled comfort — stand abandoned over the people who dug and built them. The verse of this sūrah asks its audience to read the same landscape the sūrah's own geography offered: the empty tracts of 13:4 are the survivors' side of the ledger, and the ruins are the other. The world's punishment, in short, is not a private misfortune but a public text — and the verse of the travelling feet asks why the text goes unread. The lecture is also current. The sūrah's audience needed no museum: the busy shrines of Makkah housed gods whose devotees had already lost arguments to drought, market shifts, and the desert's ordinary verdicts — the world punishes misplaced trust without waiting for the Hereafter to open the file. The verse's two-ledger pricing thus reads the present twice: once as sentence, once as syllabus — every affliction teaching what the refused warnings were worth, at rates the next ledger will only repeat at scale.

**None to Shield: The Bankruptcy of the Intermediary Economy**

“And none can shield them from Allah” (13:34). The closing clause ends the shield business, and it ends it against every vendor. The idols, already exposed as unable to hear (13:14) and unable to create (13:16), are here exposed as unable to insure: the one service the whole institution of intermediaries existed to sell — protection from the Lord — is a service no one can provide. The jinn of another chapter state the same bankruptcy from inside: “No one can protect me from Allah ˹if I were to disobey Him˺, nor can I find any refuge other than Him” (72:22) — even spirits who might be imagined to possess reach confess the shield's nonexistence. The verse's negative is therefore comprehensive: none — not wealth, not tribe, not the partners just challenged to name themselves (13:33), not the monuments of the ruined nations. The sūrah has been closing these offices one by one: the gods who cannot respond (13:14), the partners who cannot create (13:16), the protectors who cannot be found (13:11), and now the shields that cannot shield (13:34). What remains standing in the cleared field is the single unmediated relationship — creature and Creator — which is the sūrah's whole theology: the verse leaves its readers, like everyone else, without a representative and with an account.

**Why the Verse Follows the Interrogation**

Placement gives the verse its bite. It follows 13:33's courtroom scene — the challenge to name the partners, the offered absurdities, the made-appealing falsehood — and it functions as that scene's sentence: the mockers who could not name their gods, who proposed to brief the Omniscient, are told the two-ledger price of the performance. The sequence also completes the sūrah's answer to its oldest demand. The disbelievers wanted to hasten the torment (13:6); the answer then was that forgiveness runs ahead of severity; the answer now shows the severity's schedule — an installment in this world, a remainder in the next, the second “truly far worse” (13:34). The hasteners are being handed exactly what they asked for, itemized. And the verse's last clause returns the reader, once more, to the unmediated field: since none can shield, the only movements that matter are the ones made directly — the turn of 13:27, the trust of 13:30 — before the second ledger opens. For all its severity, the verse's function is informational: the account's structure is published in advance, with the comparative already attached, so that no one can claim the pricing was hidden.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:35

> The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that under it rivers flow; eternal is its fruit as well as its shade. That is the ˹ultimate˺ outcome for the righteous. But the outcome for the disbelievers is the Fire!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Description Built From This World's Best, Subtracting Its Flaws**

“The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that under it rivers flow; eternal is its fruit as well as its shade” (13:35). The description is short, and its brevity is the rhetoric: the Garden is sketched with this world's three oldest pleasures — running water, fruit, shade — each mentioned only with its earthly defect deleted. Rivers here do not flood, dry, or carry foam (contrast 13:17); fruit does not rot, end with the season, or run out; shade does not withdraw with the sun's afternoon descent. The fuller description in the Book confirms the method — this world's materials, upgraded beyond their expiries: “rivers of fresh water, rivers of milk that never changes in taste, rivers of wine delicious to drink, and rivers of pure honey” (47:15) — four rivers, each a familiar drink with its familiar failure removed: water's stagnation, milk's souring, and so on. The Garden, on this showing, is not an escape from the created order but its completion — the same water, fruit, and shade that God gives here (13:3–4), given there without the withdrawal clause. The description is calibrated to human imagination precisely because its audience lives among the drafts: everyone hearing the verse has stood in shade, eaten fruit, and drunk from a stream, and the verse promises those goods with their terminations removed.

**Eternal Fruit, Eternal Shade: The Two Expiries Removed**

“Eternal is its fruit as well as its shade” (13:35). The two eternities answer the two great complaints of earthly harvest. Fruit in this world is seasonal — a few weeks of ripeness, then gone or forbidden — and the Book's fuller portrait removes both defects at once: “abundant fruit—never out of season nor forbidden—” (56:32–33). No calendar, no prohibition: the fruit of the Garden is always ripe and always lawful. Shade in this world is a tenancy: it belongs to the moving sun, and the classical commentators observed the contrast with their usual precision — this world's shade withdraws in accord with the sun's positions, while the Garden's shade is everlasting, holding without a sun to move it. The contrast gives the verse of the shadows (13:15) its completion. There, the shadows' glory was their obedience — moving, bowing, prostrating on schedule; here, the faithful are promised the one shade that has finished moving, the shadow of a throne-room rather than a field. The two images teach one doctrine in two directions: the moving shadow of this world is worship under command; the standing shadow of the next is rest under favor. The righteous, in other words, spend their earthly afternoons under shadows that testify (13:15) so that they may spend their eternities under one that shelters.

**The Ultimate Outcome: One Word, Two Addresses**

“That is the ˹ultimate˺ outcome for the righteous. But the outcome for the disbelievers is the Fire!” (13:35). The verse's pivot rests on a single word — outcome, the end-of-account term the Qur'an uses when the ledger closes — and the sentence hands each party its closing balance. The design mirrors the sūrah's earlier verdicts and completes them. At 13:18 the responders received the finest reward and the refusers an evil resting place; at 13:23–24 the keepers entered Gardens with greetings; at 13:25 the violators received the worst abode; at 13:29 the believers received bliss and an honorable destination; and here the whole system is restated in one verse of pure contrast — outcome against outcome, Garden against Fire. The harvest vocabulary of the verse is deliberate: the sūrah has been an agricultural document from its fourth verse — one water, different tastes (13:4); the wadi sorting truth from froth (13:17) — and the outcome is the harvest festival of that imagery. The Book's economic promise states the harvest's two fields: “Whoever desires the harvest of the Hereafter, We will increase their harvest” (42:20) — the Garden as the increased yield, the Fire as the field left unsown. The verse asks its audience the planter's question: which field is receiving your water? The word's own weight does the persuading: an outcome is what remains when comparing is over. The verse lets the two addresses carry their arguments — rivers, fruit, shade against a single clause — because by the time comparison ends, the parties will no longer be comparing but residing.

**Why the Description Stops Where It Does**

The verse's restraint is worth a final word. It promises the Garden in one sentence and prices the Fire in one clause — no inventory of torments, no elaborate architecture — because the description's job is orientation, not tourism. The righteous are told enough to re-price their present: the fruit they deny themselves, the shade of obedience they stand in (13:22's patience), the water of remembrance they drink (13:28) are all installments of an address whose full form is rivers, fruit, shade without end. The disbelievers are told enough to re-price their present as well: the prosperity they flaunt (13:26) is chaff by the arithmetic of 57:20, and its outcome — the word does the work — is announced in a single clause with an exclamation. The Book, elsewhere, unrolls the fuller description (47:15) for those who want the detail; this verse's economy is a mercy to the mocked Prophet ﷺ and his congregation: the argument of thirteen chapters' worth of signs (13:1–34) is now compact enough to carry — a description, an outcome, a contrast. What remains for the hearer is the sorting verb the sūrah has used since 13:18: respond.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:36

> ˹The believers among˺ those who were given the Scripture rejoice at what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺, while some ˹disbelieving˺ factions deny a portion of it. Say, “I have only been commanded to worship Allah, associating none with Him ˹in worship˺. To Him I invite ˹all˺, and to Him is my return.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Readings of One Revelation**

“˹The believers among˺ those who were given the Scripture rejoice at what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺, while some ˹disbelieving˺ factions deny a portion of it” (13:36). The verse sets two ways of receiving revelation side by side, and both are documented among the People of the Book. The rejoicing recipients are real: the faithful of the earlier Scriptures who recognized the new revelation as their own books' continuation — “˹As for˺ those ˹faithful˺ to whom We had given the Scripture before this ˹Quran˺, they do believe in it. When it is recited to them, they declare, “We believe in it. This is definitely the truth from our Lord.”” (28:52–53). Recognition, for them, is the appropriate response to confirmation: they were holding the promise, and the promise arrived. The factions, by contrast, are described with a revealing precision: they deny *a portion of it* — not the whole. The commentators explained the selection: they accepted what matched their inherited law and sacred history, and rejected what was specific to the new message, the prophethood of Muhammad ﷺ above all. Their religion is thus conducted by committee — the canon edited down to the comfortable — and the verse's word for them, factions, names the method: revelation divided into party portions, each faction holding the slice that flatters it.

**The Tears Test: How Recognition Actually Looks**

The Book records the physiognomy of the rejoicing reception, and it is one of its most tender scenes: “you see their eyes overflowing with tears for recognizing the truth. They say, “Our Lord! We believe, so count us among the witnesses.”” (5:83). Note what the recognition does: it does not negotiate, does not condition, does not consult the committee of inherited comfort — it weeps. The tears are the body's confession that the truth arrived was already owed: the monk who finds in the recitation what his own Gospel promised him is meeting a document he has been waiting for, and waiting shows. The selector's face, by contrast, performs no such surrender; selection is a dry-eyed operation, conducted at the desk with a red pen. The verse of this sūrah gives the two operations their summary verbs — rejoice, deny — and the pairing asks its readers to locate their own receiving style. Every hearer of revelation stands somewhere on this axis: the whole received with the eyes' surrender, or the portion received with the pen's corrections. The factions' error, in the verse's showing, is not intellectual but structural: they made the inheritance the judge of the revelation, when the revelation had come as the judge of everything, inheritance included.

**The Prophet's ﷺ Three-Part Program**

“Say, “I have only been commanded to worship Allah, associating none with Him ˹in worship˺. To Him I invite ˹all˺, and to Him is my return.”” (13:36). The dictated answer gives the Prophet's ﷺ entire program in three clauses, each pointed at a different audience. *Worship Allah, associating none with Him* — the creed without factions: no portions, no parties, no partners; the command is stated as the whole of his commission, which quietly declares the selection-business beside the point. *To Him I invite* — the invitation extended to all, the factional gatekeeping answered by a call with no door except the one it stands at. *To Him is my return* — the mission's horizon: the One who sent him receives him back, which is the standing answer to every faction's verdict on him. The Book has the Prophet ﷺ state the same program as a settled rule: “Be steadfast as you are commanded, and do not follow their desires. And say, “I believe in every Scripture Allah has revealed.”” (42:15) — belief in *every* Scripture being the exact opposite of the portion-deniers' method, and the desires being the committee that the selectors served. The program's coherence is its polemic: a messenger who believes every Book cannot be recruiting for a faction, and a caller whose return is to God cannot be bribed by any of theirs.

**Rejoicing or Selecting: The Heart's Two Ways With a Revelation**

The verse's deeper teaching is about what a revelation does to the interior that receives it. The rejoicers of 13:36 — the believers among the People of the Book and the Prophet's ﷺ own companions — receive the whole and are enlarged; the selecting factions receive the portion and are diminished, turned by their own edits (13:33's made-appealing falsehood is the same mechanism in the pagan register). The sūrah has already supplied the image for both operations: the water of 13:17, sorting as it lands. The whole received is water into a wadi — it flows, it settles, it remains; the portion received, with the rest denied, is the froth on the same flood — an impurity riding the revelation it edited. The verse's closing clause — to Him is my return (13:36) — then prices both styles at the only counter where portions do not trade. The selectors' portions will not be presentable there; the whole received will be. For the reader, the verse functions as a mirror held at the canon: not what do you believe of the Book, but *how much* — and the answer's honest form is not a percentage. Either the truth is received the way the weeping receivers received it, in full and with the eyes, or it is being cut to fit — and the verse has already told us which of the two styles can rejoice.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:37

> And so We have revealed it as an authority in Arabic. And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or shield you from Allah.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Ruling in the Language of Its First Audience**

“And so We have revealed it as an authority in Arabic” (13:37). The verse names the Qur'an a ruling — a criterion that judges — and locates the judging in a language: the Arabs' own. The choice of tongue is doctrine, not convenience, and the Qur'an states the rule behind it in the general law of missions: “We have not sent a messenger except in the language of his people to clarify ˹the message˺ for them” (14:4). Revelation addresses each people in the language they think in, because clarification — the stated purpose — is impossible in an imported tongue. The Book insists on its own Arabic repeatedly: “Indeed, We have sent it down as an Arabic Quran so that you may understand” (12:2), and again: “Indeed, We have made this ˹Quran˺ easy in your own language ˹O Prophet˺ so perhaps they will be mindful” (44:58). Two things follow. The revelation is not diminished by being Arabic; it is *deployed* as Arabic — a ruling needs a language the judged party cannot claim to have misheard. And the mission's scope is not bounded by the tongue: the law of 14:4 explains the Arabic; it does not confine the message, which another verse sends to mankind entire. The language is the delivery system; the authority is the cargo.

**The Warning Sized to the Recipient**

“And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or shield you from Allah” (13:37). The sentence is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ and, as the commentators observed, through him to the whole community — the verse's form is a warning to the most unlikely candidate for desire-following, which is how the Qur'an sizes its warnings for everyone else. The logic is a fortiori: if the man who received the knowledge directly, who stands inside the revelation's delivery, would forfeit every protector by trading it for the town's preferences — then the hearer two steps removed stands at even greater distance from excuse. The Qur'an keeps this warning in circulation for the Prophet's ﷺ followers in exactly this form: steadfastness as commanded, no following of desires, belief in every Scripture revealed (42:15). The warning also completes the sūrah's accounting of protection: 13:11 announced that apart from Him no protector exists; 13:34 announced that none can shield the disbelievers; 13:37 applies the same arithmetic to the believer's hypothetical treason — protection is not lost by being attacked; it is lost by being traded.

**Desires After Knowledge: The Exact Shape of the Fall**

The verse's phrase “after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you” (13:37) dates the danger precisely: the fall it warns against is not ignorance's error but knowledge's desertion. The Qur'an elsewhere paints the full portrait of the desire-follower, and it is not a fool: “Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god? ˹And so˺ Allah left them to stray knowingly, sealed their hearing and hearts, and placed a cover on their sight” (45:23). Desire enthroned is idolatry of the interior — the person who would never bow to a stone bows five times a day to his own preferences — and the penalty tracks the crime: left astray *knowingly*, the senses sealed from the side of correction. The verse of this sūrah names the moment the enthronement happens: after knowledge. Knowledge makes desire's rule more dangerous, not less, because it supplies the criteria desire will now manage — the scholar's temptation is not stupidity but placement: he knows exactly which verse his appetite must get around. That is why the warning is delivered with the harshest clause available: none to protect, none to shield — the sūrah's established formula (13:11, 13:34) for the end of the intermediary economy. Knowledge unaccompanied by obedience does not merely fail to protect; it removes the protector.

**An Authority, Therefore Not a Proposal**

The verse's first word — authority, ruling — deserves the closing word, because it settles the standing of the text in one noun. A ruling does not negotiate with desire; it outlaws it. The Makkans' demand throughout this sūrah has been, in one form or another, that the revelation accommodate the town: hasten or soften the threats (13:6), display the requested signs (13:7, 13:27), meet the factions at their portions (13:36). The verse's answer is structural: what came down is a *ḥukm* — a judgment — delivered in the language of those being judged, and a judgment cannot be edited by the defendant. The warning that follows the noun is therefore not a threat appended to a proposal; it is the jurisdiction clause of a verdict already in force. For the Prophet ﷺ, the verse is armor: the town's desires have no court in which to file. For the community reading after him, the verse is a constitution: the knowledge that has come — all of it, 13:1's truth, 13:2–4's signs, 13:17's sorting water — is the standing law of the heart, and the verse's only open question is the one it leaves with the reader: what the desires will be permitted to do with their remaining days. The verse's pronouns keep the courtroom intimate: the knowledge came to you, the desires are theirs, and the choice between them — announced in the second person — is delivered to each reader by name. The sūrah that sorted one water into many tastes (13:4) ends its argument by sorting its readers the same way: one revelation, many desires, and the ruling in Arabic to judge between them.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:38

> We have certainly sent messengers before you ˹O Prophet˺ and blessed them with wives and offspring. It was not for any messenger to bring a sign without Allah’s permission. Every destined matter has a ˹set˺ time.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Humanity Objection, Answered From the Series**

“We have certainly sent messengers before you ˹O Prophet˺ and blessed them with wives and offspring” (13:38). The verse answers an objection the Makkans had made a standing theme: that a true messenger would be otherworldly — the request recorded in another chapter, “What ails this Messenger, who eats food and walks in the markets?” (25:7), asked as though eating disqualified. The answer is the series again: the messengers before him were married, raised children, lived the ordinary appetites of men — blessed, the verse says, with the very things the objectors counted against him. The objection's theology is exposed rather than argued: it assumes divinity is proved by the absence of humanity, a pagan instinct that divides the world into gods who float and men who eat. The Qur'an's counter-doctrine is that mediation requires a man — a messenger must be one of the messaged — and the series proves the rule: hunger, marriage, and offspring appear in the biography of every prophet the Book honors. The verse thereby also defends the Prophet's ﷺ household: his marriages and children, the very facts his opponents sneered at, are listed among the *blessings* conferred on the prophetic line — the sneer misread a mercy as a weakness.

**No Sign on Private Authority**

“It was not for any messenger to bring a sign without Allah’s permission” (13:38). The clause is repeated verbatim in another chapter, which marks it as a settled constitutional rule of the missions: the sign is never the messenger's property. The full parallel text supplies the rule's setting: “It was not for any messenger to bring a sign without Allah’s permission. But when Allah’s decree comes, judgment will be passed with fairness” (40:78) — the messenger's inability to self-authorize miracles is bracketed by the decree that will arrive anyway, with fairness attached. The rule answers the sign-demands of 13:7, 13:27, and 13:31 at their root: the Prophet ﷺ cannot produce the requested spectacle because production was never in his brief; he is the conveyor of what is given, and the giving is scheduled elsewhere. The rule also protects the office from its counterfeiters. A messenger who could signal on demand would be a freelancer; the permission clause makes every true sign an act of God's own initiative, which is why the false prophet's career is structurally impossible to sustain — he possesses no account to draw on. The verse thus positions the whole dispute exactly where the sūrah has kept it since 13:27: between God's will and the servant's turn, with the messenger as the appointed witness in between — not the treasurer of wonders.

**Every Destined Matter Has Its Written Term**

“Every destined matter has a ˹set˺ time” (13:38). The closing clause widens from missions to everything: each decreed matter carries its term — a fixed, written tenure. The readings the commentators gave the clause agree on the essentials: God holds a fixed term for every matter He has determined, and the clause also echoes the Qur'an's larger doctrine of revelation's universality — every community's term included a Book. The doctrine has been the sūrah's spine from its second verse: “each orbiting for an appointed term” (13:2) — the heavens themselves run on tenures. The wombs' arithmetic (13:8), the guarded roads (13:11), the respite's delay (13:32), the promise's arrival (13:31): all of it is tenure-language, and this clause states the general law in one line. The creation verse of the starred chapter confirms the vocabulary: “Indeed, We have created everything, perfectly preordained” (54:49) — measured, timed, written. The clause's placement after the permission rule is deliberate: signs are held for their appointed hour (40:78's decree), and so are seizures, rescues, victories, and deaths. Nothing in this sūrah's world is unscheduled; the audience's complaint, from beginning to end, has been about scheduling — and the verse's answer is that the schedule exists, is written, and is not available for revision at the town's request.

**The Verse as the Mission's Job Description Completed**

Read together, the three clauses form the completed job description the sūrah has been assembling for its messenger. Human, like the series before him (13:38's wives and offspring) — therefore approachable, answerable, imitable in his humanity. Unauthorized, like the series before him (the permission rule) — therefore incapable of the favoritism the sign-demanders were really seeking: a private wonder for a private audience. And patient, because the term-language of the closing clause commits him to working inside schedules he cannot see. The mockers' two objections — his ordinary humanity and his absent miracles — turn out to be the two proofs of his office: a messenger indistinguishable from an angel could not be followed, and a messenger armed at will could not be tested. The verse also consoles the believers who watched the town's taunts (13:5–7): the Prophet's ﷺ humanity and the signs' absence were never defects awaiting repair but features built into the series from its first members. What remains scheduled — the decree of 40:78, the promise of 13:31, the seizure of 13:32 — will arrive on its written term, and the verse's last line is the sūrah's whole eschatology in seven words: the universe keeps its appointments, and so will its Keeper. The verse's three clauses also sketch the believer's proper envy: not of the signs the messenger could not produce, but of the nearness the series confirms — every prophet's household, the verse says, was a blessing, and the blessings ran through ordinary doors.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:39

> Allah eliminates and confirms what He wills, and with Him is the Master Record.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse of Divine Editing**

“Allah eliminates and confirms what He wills, and with Him is the Master Record” (13:39). After a chapter of fixed terms and kept appointments (13:2, 13:31, 13:38), the verse announces that the ledger's entries are nonetheless editable — by its Author. The classical reading of the clause is comprehensive: He brings into existence and removes; gives life and causes death; grants wealth and strips it — elimination and confirmation run through the whole created order, not merely through texts. The verse thus answers a question the sūrah's audience had not thought to ask but every reader of decree eventually asks: if everything is timed and written (13:38), is the writing itself subject to revision? The answer is yes — at one level — and the revision happens on one desk only. Creation is not a machine left running on its first settings; it is a living document under continuous authorship. What the verse refuses, with equal clarity, is the pagan conclusion: a changing record does not mean a changing author, and the clause's second half — “and with Him is the Master Record” (13:39) — anchors the edits to an unedited original, of which the next section will say more.

**The Mother of the Book: The Unedited Original**

“With Him is the Master Record” (13:39). The phrase — in Arabic, the Mother of the Book — names the archetypal text from which, on the classical readings, revelation is transcribed: the fundamental source of divine instruction, identified by other commentators with the Preserved Tablet and by others with God's own knowledge. The Qur'an names the tablet in its own closing chapters: “In fact, this is a glorious Quran, ˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet” (85:21–22); and names the Mother again for itself: “And indeed, it is—in the Master Record with Us—highly esteemed, rich in wisdom” (43:4). The doctrine assembled from these verses is precise: the world's flux — the eliminations and confirmations of 13:39's first clause — plays out beneath a text that does not flux. What is abolished and established are the enactments; the code is stable. This is why divine editing is not divine improv: the ruling that is confirmed or effaced in time was always simultaneously present in the archetype, and the editing is the timed unfolding of what the original already held. The commentators' third reading — that the Mother is God's knowledge — makes the same point at greater depth: the edits are visible; the knowledge editing them has no edits.

**What Elimination Means for a Community Holding a Revelation**

The verse's first clause has a specific application to the people holding this Book, and the Qur'an demonstrates the mechanism in operation. The revealed text itself underwent ordered transmission under divine supervision: another chapter describes revelation arriving under interference and being answered — Satan's suggestions eliminated, God's verses firmly established, in that order (22:52). The elimination-and-confirmation of the verse of this sūrah is the same hand at work on the same material: what stands in the recitation stands by confirmation, not by luck; what was removed was removed by authority, not by accident. For the community, the doctrine removes two errors at once. Against the anxious: no verse is in the Book by oversight, and no corruption survives in it unedited — the Author's hand is still on the text. Against the casual: the confirmed text is confirmed — not provisional, not awaiting the community's revisions. The Qur'an's own promise-keeping is included in the stable side of the ledger: “There is no change in the promise of Allah” (10:64). Elimination and confirmation describe the process; the Master Record guarantees the product; and the reader holding the Book is holding the confirmed edition of a preserved original.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Administration Cipher**

Placed between the missions' terms (13:38) and the mission's deadline (13:40), the verse is the administration cipher of the whole chapter: it explains how a world of fixed terms can also be a world of continuous divine action. The orbits run on appointments (13:2), yet provision is spread and straitened by will (13:26); every matter has its written term (13:38), yet plots are undone and promises accelerated; the woman's womb is measured (13:8), yet its schedule includes eliminations no physician predicted. Flux and fixity, on the verse's showing, are not rival descriptions of reality but its two registers — the timed enactments below, the stable code above. For the Prophet ﷺ, the verse lands as both caution and comfort. Caution: the mission's circumstances will be edited — alliances, persecutions, directions of prayer — and the edits are not evidence against the mission. Comfort: the editing hand is the same hand that confirmed the Book in his lap, and its edits have a Mother to answer to. The verse's short length, one sentence, is its own argument: administration this total needs no elaboration — everything beneath it, from the thunder's schedule (13:13) to the reader's remaining days, is a line item on a desk the verse has just located. The verse also supplies the sūrah's answer to the planner's question hiding in 13:38: if every matter has a written term, what exactly changed when a famine lifted or a treaty held? Nothing in the code moved — the term was always going to run that way. Elimination and confirmation are what the unfolding of a fixed text looks like from inside time, where each development arrives as news. The Mother of the Book is the guarantee that the news was never actually new: the enactment surprises, the code does not, and the believer who grasps the two registers reads history the way the verse reads it — edited in front of us, finished behind Him.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:40

> Whether We show you ˹O Prophet˺ some of what We threaten them with, or cause you to die ˹before that˺, your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. Judgment is for Us.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mission's Contract, Stated in Two Outcomes**

“Whether We show you ˹O Prophet˺ some of what We threaten them with, or cause you to die ˹before that˺, your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺” (13:40). The verse writes the Prophet's ﷺ contract with two clauses and refuses to let him — or his audience — confuse the outcome with the assignment. Either he witnesses the promised punishment begin in his lifetime, or he dies before it falls; in both cases his duty is identical: delivery. The clause removes the last possible leak between the mission and its results. A messenger might reason that vindication in his own lifetime proves the message and its messenger — and the verse forbids the reasoning: the punishment's timing is not a grade on the delivery. He might also despair that dying before the promise lands means the mission lapsed — and the verse forbids that too: death inside the term (13:38's written time) is part of the schedule, not a failure of it. The parallel verse carries the same two-branch structure into the same calm: the promise will be shown or the Prophet taken, and his task remains proclamation either way. The verse thereby completes what 13:7 began — “You ˹O Prophet˺ are only a warner” — by defining the office so completely that no outcome can falsify it.

**Delivery Without an Audience's Verdict**

“Your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺” (13:40). The Qur'an defines the prophetic duty in this exact form repeatedly, which marks it as doctrine rather than reassurance: “The Messenger’s duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah ˹fully˺ knows what you reveal and what you conceal” (5:99) — and note the second clause of that verse: the delivery is owed against an audit of what is concealed, meaning the messenger's honesty, not the audience's response, is the measured variable. And again: “If you persist in denial, so did ˹many˺ communities before you. The Messenger’s duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly” (29:18) — denial itself is a pattern the delivery must survive, not a failure of it. The duty's definition also closes the door the sign-demanders kept knocking on (13:7, 13:27, 13:31): if delivery were measured by conversions, the demand for compulsory miracles would be legitimate; since delivery is measured by clarity, the demand is simply beside the contract. The Prophet's ﷺ accountability runs to the One who sent the text, in the language the text was sent in (13:37) — and to no committee of hearers. The sūrah's audience, hearing this, receives a protected man: a messenger whose standing cannot be voted down.

**Judgment Is for Us: The Office Separated**

“Judgment is for Us” (13:40). The final clause assigns the reckoning to its Owner and, in doing so, separates the two offices that every failed society merges: the office of proclamation and the office of judgment. The Prophet ﷺ holds the first; the second was never open for application. The Qur'an states the separation as the norm of the missions — the warner warns, the reckoning is God's (88:21–22's pair: the duty is only to remind, compulsion is not his) — and the separation is what makes the mission moral at all. If the messenger judged, belief would be compliance; if God's judgment were delegated, the record of 13:8–11 — the vigilance over every soul, the guarded roads — would need deputies, and the deputies would need watching. The clause is also the sūrah's last word in its long dispute with the hasteners: the demand of 13:6 was, at bottom, an application to move into the judgment office — to schedule the torment now. The verse's answer closes the applications window: Judgment is for Us — the office has one occupant, the schedule has one Keeper (13:38), and the Prophet's ﷺ signature appears nowhere on the warrant. What the mockers read as weakness — the messenger who does not strike — is the constitution working exactly as written.

**Peace in a Two-Clause Verse**

For the Prophet ﷺ, the verse is permission to stop carrying what was never his. The man who walks through a hostile town reciting (13:30) carries, by nature, two invisible burdens: the longing to see the truth vindicated and the dread of dying before it is. The verse lifts both in one breath: vindication is authorized — some of the threat may be shown in his presence — and death before vindication is neutralized — the mission's meaning does not depend on his witnessing it. All that transfers across either branch is the delivery, which is his in full and God's to grade. The verse's structure is thus a mirror of 13:31's: there, the believers were told the town's conversion was never the metric; here, the messenger is told his death is not the metric either. What remains is a man with a text, a language, an appointment-keeping Lord, and one verb — deliver — whose success is determined offstage, by the One to whom the judgment belongs. The sūrah could hardly hand its central figure a cleaner commission: the mission is unconditionally survivable, because its success was never parked in the visible world to begin with. One detail of the contract deserves notice: the verse says *some* of what is threatened. Even the vindication branch is rationed — the Prophet ﷺ is promised a sighting of the promise, not its administration. The sight is encouragement; the execution remains where the clause files it, with Us. The verse gives the messenger a window seat on the judgment and no seat on the bench — which is exactly the balance a warner needs and a judge must not have.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:41

> Do they not see that We gradually reduce their land from its borders? Allah decides—none can reverse His decision. And He is swift in reckoning.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Aimed at the Comfortable**

“Do they not see that We gradually reduce their land from its borders?” (13:41). The question is addressed to the Makkans, and it describes a process rather than an event: reduction at the edges, gradual, ongoing — the circumference drawing in while the center feels nothing yet. The commentators read the clause historically: the land whose borders are being reduced is theirs, and the reduction is God's gift of territory to the Prophet ﷺ — the treaty, the allegiance, the expanding sphere around Makkah years before the city fell. The Book confirms the method as its standard operating procedure: “In fact, We have allowed enjoyment for these ˹Meccans˺ and their forefathers for such a long time ˹that they took it for granted˺. Do they not see that We gradually reduce ˹their˺ land from its borders?” (21:44). The long enjoyment is named as part of the mechanism — taking it for granted is what the gradualness produces — and the question is repeated there in almost the same words, which marks the shrinking border as doctrine: judgment in the Qur'an rarely arrives as a surprise attack; it arrives as a perimeter. What the comfortable read as the ordinary churn of politics is, on the verse's showing, a documented operation with an Owner.

**The Edges Go First: The Mechanics of Unnoticed Judgment**

The verse's geography carries its lesson. Borders go first because borders are where attention never looks: the periphery is conceded in silence while the center keeps its festivals. The Book's ruin-inventory shows the end state of the same process: “˹Imagine˺ how many gardens and springs the tyrants left behind, as well as ˹various˺ crops and splendid residences” (44:25–26) — the centers of vanished powers, abandoned whole. Between the shrinking border and the abandoned garden stands the interval the verse is describing: a people watching their map redraw itself and calling it weather. The gradualness is not mercy misplaced but method: it gives the perceptive time to read the trend and the obstinate time to finish their refusal — the same two-audience design the respite doctrine carries (13:32). And the field trip command of 40:21 — travel and look at the ends of the seized — is this verse's retrospective: every ruined civilization in the region was once a center whose edges went first. The verse asks its audience to read the process in real time, which is the hardest reading of all, because the process is designed to be legible only to those who admit it is a process — that someone is behind it, operating at the speed of politics.

**Allah Decides: The Irreversible Jurisdiction**

“Allah decides—none can reverse His decision” (13:41). The clause moves from observation to jurisdiction: the reduction is not a trend but a ruling, and rulings of this Judge do not get appealed. The sūrah has been assembling this exclusivity all along: no protector besides Him (13:11), no shield against Him (13:34), no guide apart from Him (13:27, 13:33) — and now no reversal of Him. The negative is comprehensive because the office is single: reversals of decree are sought, in every age, from the same vendors — the idols of Makkah, the intermediaries of every later market — and the verse empties the market with one sentence. The irreversibility is also the comfort's underside, and the sūrah states both faces without embarrassment: the promise that never fails (13:31) and the judgment none can repeal (13:41) are one attribute — reliability at the scale of verdicts. For the mocked believers watching Makkah's borders quietly move, the clause is a vow: what has begun will not be un-begun. For the complacent inside the shrinking circle, it is the opposite: the process they are calling churn is a signed decision, and signed decisions in this sūrah's world have execution dates (13:38).

**Swift in Reckoning: The Speed of the Accountant**

“And He is swift in reckoning” (13:41). The closing clause is the Qur'an's recurring signature on judgment passages, and its placement here resolves an apparent tension: the reduction is gradual — years of quiet encirclement — yet the reckoning is swift. The resolution is the verse's final teaching. Gradualness governs the warning; swiftness governs the settlement. The border shrinks at the speed of history so that reading remains possible; the reckoning falls at the speed of verdicts so that escape is not. The Book pairs the two tempos exactly this way in its ruin verses: long enjoyment, gradual reduction — then seizure (40:21's “Allah seized them for their sins, and they had no protector from Allah”). For the mocked community, the pairing is a map of their next years: the encirclement they are watching is the slow part, and the slow part is mercy — the era in which the mockers can still count the borders and turn (13:27). For the mockers, it is a countdown they are misreading as background: the verse's question — do they not see — is not about eyesight but about attribution. Seeing without an Owner is just watching; the verse asks for the second step, and the next verse will show what the plotters who never take it discover about the speed at the end.

The verse's question form is its final courtesy: do they not see asks for an admission, not a conviction. A people who answer honestly — yes, the borders are moving — have already taken the verse's first step, because seeing with attribution is the seed of the turn. The question remains open in every generation that reads it, and the borders in question have never once stopped moving for an answer.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:42

> Those ˹disbelievers˺ before them ˹secretly˺ planned, but Allah has the ultimate plan. He knows what every soul commits. And the disbelievers will soon know who will have the ultimate outcome.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Plotting Contest, Already Decided**

“Those ˹disbelievers˺ before them ˹secretly˺ planned, but Allah has the ultimate plan” (13:42). The verse opens by enrolling the Makkans in a series again — the plotting nations before them — and closes the contest before it begins: the plotting of the plotted is answered by a plan from the other side of the table. The Qur'an's fullest statement of the theme uses the strongest available superlative: “They planned, but Allah also planned. And Allah is the best of planners” (8:30) — spoken over the actual conspiracy to capture, kill, or exile the Prophet ﷺ, the plot that dissolved into the migration that founded the community. Another verse states the contest from heaven's chair: “They are certainly devising ˹evil˺ plans, but I too am planning” (86:15–16) — the first person again, the same two hands of 13:32's delay and seizure now working in the register of stratagem. And the pattern's classic instance is stated with the plotters' ignorance inside it: “And ˹so˺ they made a plan, but We too made a plan, while they were unaware” (27:50). Unawareness is the plotters' permanent condition, because the counter-plan's whole method is invisible: the plot that closes on its authors feels, from inside, like success right up to the minute it closes. The verse of this sūrah gives the series its verdict in advance: Allah has the ultimate plan — the outcome is not contested ground.

**The Plot That Circles Home**

The doctrine has a corollary the Qur'an states with geometric clarity: plots return to their makers. Evil plotting, the Book says, circles back on none but its own authors (35:43). The mechanism is visible across the sacred history this sūrah keeps invoking: the plotters of every series built the very machinery of their own exposure — the drowned pursued the sea they feared, the confederates scattered by the wind they mocked, the boycott returned to its architects as the boycotters' own starvation. Plotting is the one crime that requires the victim's cooperation in advance: a plot against God's plan must use the materials God supplies — time, tongue, opportunity — and the materials stay registered to their Owner. That is why the counter-plan never needs to be hurried (13:32's respite): the plot is already carrying its own punishment as cargo, and the delivery is scheduled (13:38). For the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah, the verse is armor against the specific fear that secrecy produces: the town's councils met behind closed doors, and the verse informs him that the doors were open at the other end all along. The ultimate plan is not a forecast; it is sovereignty over the plotting process itself — the ability to let the plot run, fund it with the plotters' own effort, and collect it whole.

**He Knows What Every Soul Commits: The Audit Under the Stratagem**

“He knows what every soul commits” (13:42). The verse inserts the audit clause into the plotting discussion deliberately. Plots are organized around a superstition: that knowledge can be confined — that what happens in the closed room stays there. The clause removes the superstition with the sūrah's standing infrastructure: the Vigilant One of 13:33, the whispering overheard at 13:10, the wombs read at 13:8, the angelic record of 86:4. Every soul's deeds are committed into a record the plotter cannot subpoena or destroy, and the record includes the plot — its meetings, its motives, its drafts. The clause also explains how the ultimate plan can be ultimate without improvisation: sovereignty over outcomes does not require interrupting them if the inputs are all known in advance; a plan resting on total knowledge looks, from inside history, like patience. The verse thereby binds the chapter's two great themes — the total knowledge of its opening movement (13:8–10) and the mission's contested middle (13:27–41) — into one sentence: the plots are known, the knowledge is the plan's raw material, and the plotting contest was over before the plotters left their meeting.

**The Ultimate Outcome: The Verse Gives the Answer Early**

“And the disbelievers will soon know who will have the ultimate outcome” (13:42). The closing clause promises the plotters a discovery, and the commentators read its timing two ways that amount to one lesson: the knowledge may arrive at the plot's unraveling in history — the morning Makkah's borders finish closing (13:41) — and it arrives finally at the audit, when the abodes are assigned. The word for outcome is the same one 13:35 used for the two addresses — the Garden as the outcome of the righteous, the Fire as the outcome of the disbelievers — so the discovery promised here is not a fact but an address: the plotters will learn whose plan was holding their plot the whole time, by learning where each party ends up living. The clause's tone is the sūrah's quietest threat: no description of punishment, no inventory — just the certainty of finding out. And its structure hands the reader the verse's real offer: the outcome is knowable *soon*, which means it is knowable now by inference — the series of 13:32, the borders of 13:41, the promise of 13:31 have already announced how the contest ends. The verse lets the plotters choose their moment of enlightenment: by reading, before the plot closes; or by residence, after.

The verse's three sentences are a complete strategy briefing for the mocked: the pattern is old, the counter is certain, the ledger is exact, and the discovery is scheduled. Nothing in the briefing requires the believer to act except to outlast — the one verb the whole sūrah has been training since its storms.

## Sūrah al-Raʿd 13:43

> The disbelievers say, “You ˹Muḥammad˺ are no messenger.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah is sufficient as a Witness between me and you, as is whoever has knowledge of the Scripture.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Final Objection Is the Bare Denial**

“The disbelievers say, “You ˹Muḥammad˺ are no messenger.”” (13:43). The sūrah's last recorded objection strips every earlier argument down to its floor: no more demands for moved mountains (13:31), no more mockery of resurrection (13:5), no more requests to hasten the torment (13:6) — just the bare denial, said without equipment. The progression is itself the sūrah's argument coming to rest: a position that began with challenges ends with a flat no, because every intermediate position has been dismantled across forty verses. The bare denial is also the confession of exhaustion. The objectors concede, by silence, everything they stopped asserting — the truth of the recitation is no longer disputed on its merits; the signs are no longer requested; the resurrection is no longer debated. What remains is the personal refusal: not "this message is false" but "you are no messenger" — the objection relocated from the content to the man, which is where objections land when the content has stopped being contestable. The sūrah answers it by producing exactly what a personal denial demands: witnesses to the person's office.

**The Witness Who Needs No Introducing**

“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah is sufficient as a Witness between me and you”” (13:43). The answer begins with the Witness above every court: God Himself. The Book has prepared this answer all chapter — the God who knows the whisper and the daylight walk (13:10), who reads every womb (13:8), who attends every soul's record (13:33) — is the one witness whose testimony no cross-examination can touch, because His knowledge is not derived. The Qur'an deploys the same answer against the same denial elsewhere: “Sufficient is He as a Witness between you and me” (46:8) — and that verse adds the assymetrical stakes: if the denial were true, the deniers could do nothing to save the fabricator from God; the witness is also the judge. Another verse widens the witness list to heaven's population: “Allah bears witness to what He has sent down to you—He has sent it with His knowledge. The angels too bear witness. And Allah ˹alone˺ is sufficient as a Witness” (4:166) — angels added, then immediately redundant before the sufficiency of the first. The answer to "you are no messenger" is therefore not a defense but an elevation: the office being denied is attested by the Being whose attestation the deniers' entire system presupposes — they argue about Allah (13:13), they confess Allah's lordship when asked (13:16), and now Allah's testimony is entered on the disputed fact.

**The Second Witness: Whoever Has Knowledge of the Scripture**

“As is whoever has knowledge of the Scripture” (13:43). The second witness stands on the earth: those who know the Book — its contents, its promises, its pattern of prophecy — recognize the messenger when he arrives, because recognizing him is a reading skill. The commentators read the phrase two ways: those who know the Qur'an and its inimitable character, and — the reading with history attached — the learned of the earlier Scriptures, among them the Madinan scholar ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām, who recognized the Prophet ﷺ on the strength of what his own books said about him. The Qur'an confirms the phenomenon as doctrine: the believers of the earlier revelation stand on “clear proof from their Lord, backed by ˹the Quran as˺ a witness from Him, and preceded by the Book of Moses” (11:17) — the Books witnessing to one another like servers of one chain of title. This second witness matters because it defeats the denial on the deniers' own grounds: the Makkans claimed the old Scriptures as their warrant against the new messenger; the verse shows the old Scriptures testifying the other way. Knowledge of the Scripture, in the verse's architecture, is not a privilege that separates its holders from the Prophet ﷺ — it is the qualification that binds them to him, the reading skill whose proper exercise ends in recognition.

**A Chapter That Ends in a Courtroom**

It is fitting that the sūrah closes in a courtroom, because it began as one. Its first verse declared the revelation truth and predicted the disbelief of most people (13:1); its middle sections entered the evidence — the conducted heavens (13:2), the sorted harvests (13:4), the worshipping thunder (13:13), the bankrupt idols (13:14–16), the sorted water (13:17) — and its gallery sorted the audience into two portraits with two addresses (13:19–25). Now, at the end, the last objection is met with the last exhibit: witnesses. The structure hands the reader the case file complete — evidence, verdicts, and testimony — with the verdict's execution scheduled (13:31, 13:38, 13:41) and the appeal window governed by the turn (13:27–28). The Book's own confirmation of the verse's logic is stated in its great witness verse: “Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and so are the angels and people of knowledge” (3:18) — the same witness list, testifying to the same confession this sūrah has been extracting since 13:16. The chapter's final word thus leaves its audience standing where a jury stands: the evidence is in, the witnesses have spoken, and the only remaining motion — belief or denial — is the reader's own, filed where all the sūrah's verses have said it must be filed: in the interior that God has been reading all along.

The verse's last word — witness, not verdict — keeps the chapter's door open to its final line. The testimony has been given; the sentence has not. Between testimony and sentence lies the whole width of the turn, and the sūrah ends by leaving that width, deliberately, where it began: with the reader.
