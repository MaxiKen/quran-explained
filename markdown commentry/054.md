# Sūrah al-Qamar (Chapter 54) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Splitting of the Moon and the Accessible Reminder**

Al-Qamar, "The Moon," is the fifty-fourth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 55 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with an electrifying historical and Last-Day spectacle: *“The Hour has drawn near, and the moon has split (*iqtarabati al-sāʿatu wan-shaqqa al-qamar*)! And if they see a sign, they turn away and say: 'Passing magic!'”* (54:1–2). In authentic prophetic tradition documented in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (3636) and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (2800) by multiple Companions—including ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, Anas ibn Mālik, and Ibn ʿAbbās—the Quraysh demanded that the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ show a visible cosmic miracle. The Prophet pointed toward the night sky, and by divine command, the full moon cleaved into two distinct halves: one half standing directly over Mount Abū Qubays and the other behind it, visible across the Arabian desert. Rather than submitting to the sign, the pagan aristocracy scoffed: *“Muḥammad has cast sorcery over our eyes!”* Characterized by rapid, dramatic staccato verses ending in the resonant rhyme *-ur*, Sūrah al-Qamar is celebrated for its unforgettable, fourfold refrain in verses 17, 22, 32, and 40: *“And We have certainly made the Qur'an easy to remember / easy for reminder, so is there any who will remember? (*Fa-hal min muddakir*)”*.

**The Fourfold Warning and the Ease of Scripture**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Qamar is built around a symmetrical sequence of historical catastrophism:

1. The Prologue and the Day of Emerging (1–8): The moon split, the warning ignored, and the sudden arrival of the Day when the Caller will summon humanity to a terrible event, emerging from the graves with downcast eyes like scattered locusts (*ka-annahum jarādun muntashir*), racing toward the Caller, while the deniers cry: *“This is a difficult day!”*

2. Noah and the Deluge (9–17): Branded mad and threatened with expulsion, Noah prayed: *“Indeed, I am overcome, so help [me]!”* God opened the gates of heaven with torrential rain and caused the earth to burst forth with springs, meeting at a pre-decreed measure, carrying Noah upon an ark of planks and nails (*dhāti alwāḥin wa-dusur*), sailing under divine eyes, sealed with the refrain: *“And We have certainly made the Qur'an easy for reminder, so is there any who will remember?”*

3. ʿĀd and the Screaming Gale (18–22): Denying their warner, struck by a furious, roaring wind on an unceasing day of disaster, plucking people up as though they were uprooted trunks of date palms.

4. Thamūd and the She-Camel (23–32): Objecting that Ṣāliḥ was merely a mortal human from among them, hamstrung the camel, whereupon a single blast (*ṣayḥatan wāḥidah*) reduced them to the dry, trampled twigs of a sheepfold builder (*ka-hashīmi al-muḥtaẓir*).

5. The People of Lot (33–40): Demanding the assault of his angelic guests, their eyes blinded, and struck in the morning by an abiding punishment of raining stones.

6. Pharaoh and his Dynasty (41–42): Seized with the grasp of the Exalted in Might, the Omnipotent. The climactic movement (43–55) turns directly upon the Quraysh: *“Are your disbelievers better than those, or have you an immunity in the scriptures?”* It delivers the stunning military prophecy of verse 45—which ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb recalled reciting at the Battle of Badr when the Qurayshi army was routed: *“Their assembly will soon be defeated. They will turn their backs [in flight]!”* It declares that all things have been created with pre-measured decree (*qadar*, 54:49), and concludes with the sublime eternal reward of the pious: *“Indeed, the righteous will be among gardens and rivers, in a seat of truth (*fī maqʿadi ṣidq*) near an All-Powerful Sovereign (*ʿinda Malīkin Muqtadir*)”* (54:54–55).

## Verse 54:1

> The Hour has drawn near and the moon was split ˹in two˺

**A Claim About Time, Then a Fact About the Sky**

The opening yokes two verbs together, and the order of the yoking is the sūrah's first statement. The first half is a claim about time: the Hour has drawn near. The second half is a claim about a physical event: the moon was split. One half cannot be checked by any human calendar; the other can be checked by any pair of eyes. By placing the checkable half immediately after the unchecked half, the text makes the second the evidence for the first. The claim of nearness is not new in the Book: "˹The time of˺ people's judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away" (21:1), and "The command of Allah is at hand, so do not hasten it" (16:1). What is new here is the pairing. The nearness arrives with a sign, and the sign is the thing that was done, not the thing that was said.

**The Splitting Was a Public Event**

The reports agree on the outline of the event. The Quraysh, after days of disputing with the Prophet ﷺ and demanding a visible proof, pressed the demand again on the plain of Mina. He raised his hand and pointed to the moon, and the moon appeared in two halves with a mountain between them. The narration of Ibn Masʿūd, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, says the moon split into two parts, one on the far side of the mountain and the other on the near side, and that he saw the mountain between the two parts (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3869). The report of Anas, found in both Ṣaḥīḥs, says they saw Mount Ḥirāʾ between the two halves. When the separation closed, the Prophet ﷺ said, "Bear witness!" The call to witness is the detail that matters: the sign was not something to be debated later in a council, it was something to be attested while it was still visible in the sky. Some commentators read the second half of the verse not as a report of a past event but as a forecast of a future one, a sign of the end of time itself. A sign that had not yet happened could not be rejected as "Same old magic" (54:2).

**Proximity Without a Calendar**

The verb "has drawn near" locates the Hour within the reach of human history but not within its schedule. The Prophet's ﷺ own gesture is the clearest expression of the position: "I have been sent and the Hour (is at hand) as these two," holding up two fingers close together (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6503). The image is of two points separated by a short but unmeasurable distance: the event is near, and its date is fixed only by God. "For each community there is an appointed term. When their time arrives, they can neither delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it" (7:34). Nearness, in this reading, is not information for planning. It is information for posture. The one who knows that the Hour is near lives differently from the one who does not, but neither can set the date, and the difference between the two lives is the whole difference between faith and denial.

## Verse 54:2

> Yet, whenever they see a sign, they turn away, saying, “Same old magic!”

**Magic as a Theory of Everything**

In the Qur'an, *siḥr* (magic) is not a category of skill; it is a category of dismissal. When evidence appears, the disbeliever has two boxes available, and the boxes are defined so as to exclude the evidence. The first box is sorcery: what you saw was a trick. The second is divination or poetry: what you heard is a trick of the tongue. The Book records both boxes being used against the same messenger: "Or do they say, '˹He is˺ a poet, for whom we ˹eagerly˺ await an ill-fate!'" (52:30). The label is always aimed at the messenger, never at the content. A message that is examined as a message can be refuted by evidence; a messenger who is labeled a sorcerer cannot be refuted, only avoided. The sūrah's audience is being shown its own account of knowledge: a system in which no observation can enter, because the boxes are defined before the observation arrives.

**A Pattern Repeated Across the Book**

The refusal that follows a seen sign is a standing pattern in the Qur'an, and the Book names it with the same two verbs: reminder and turning away. "And who does more wrong than the one who is reminded of Allah's revelations then turns away from them?" (32:22). "And who does more wrong than those who, when reminded of their Lord's revelations, turn away from them and forget what their own hands have done?" (18:57). The pairing is deliberate and repeated: the sign is not absent when the person turns away; the sign is present, and the face is turned from it. The defect recorded in the pattern is not ignorance. It is direction. The one who turns away has seen, and the seeing is the fact that the turning cannot erase.

**The Travelers' Report and the Question It Raises**

In the accounts of the event, the Quraysh proposed a verification that a sincere skeptic would propose. If the moon had truly been split, they argued, the effect could not have been limited to the people present. Let travelers arriving from far away be asked what they saw, and their report will decide the matter. The reports say that travelers did arrive, and that they confirmed the splitting. The label "magic" was not a hypothesis that could be corrected by a report; it was a verdict that was given before the report and could not be taken back by it.

"Who then does more wrong than those who deny Allah's revelations and turn away from them?" (6:157).

## Verse 54:3

> They rejected ˹the truth˺ and followed their own desires—and every matter will be settled—

**Two Verbs, One Diagnosis**

The first half of the verse gives two verbs, and the order of the two verbs is the diagnosis. "They rejected" comes first, and "they followed their desires" comes after. The second verb is the engine, and the first is its output. The verse does not say that they erred, that they were mistaken, or that the evidence failed. It says that they chose their desires, and the rejection is what the choice produced. The contrast in the Book is always with the alternative: "So do not let your desires cause you to deviate ˹from justice˺" (4:135), and "do not follow their desires over the truth that has come to you" (5:48). Desire, in this usage, is not a neutral appetite. It is a criterion that has replaced the criterion — the *ahwāʾ* that stands where the truth should stand, and the standing is the act of the person, not an accident that happened to him.

**Followed Their Own Desires: A Standing Contrast**

The Qur'an sets "following desires" against "following God and His Messenger" as the two directions available to a person, and the contrast is a standing one. "If you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or help you against Allah" (2:120). "If you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, then you would certainly be one of the wrongdoers" (2:145). The word for desires, *ahwāʾ*, is the plural of *ḥawāʾ* — inclination, taste, preference, the thing a person is drawn to without being compelled. The verse's accusation is not that the Meccans had feelings; it is that they made the feelings into an authority. A desire becomes a criterion when a person arranges belief around what is pleasant rather than around what is true, and the arrangement is complete when the belief that is unpleasant is rejected and the belief that is pleasant is kept, each by the same standard.

**Settlement, Not Just Consequence**

The distinction is important, and the sūrah keeps it. "Settlement" is not only a punishment that follows a crime; it is the property of every affair to reach its proper place. The claim about the Hour is settled. The claim about the moon is settled. The question "which side are you on?" is settled by each person's following of his own desire. The verse so functions as a hinge in the sūrah: the first segment (sign, rejection, desire) rests on this law, and the second segment (the examples of the destroyed peoples) illustrates it. The destroyed peoples are not illustrations of divine anger; they are illustrations of what "settlement" looks like when the desires of a whole people are followed to their end. Each story in the sūrah is a case of settlement executed, and each case ends with the same refrain: "So is there anyone who will be mindful?" (54:15).

## Verse 54:4

> even though the stories ˹of destroyed nations˺ that have already come to them are a sufficient deterrent

**A Deterrent That Arrived Too Late to Deter**

The verse concedes the point it is about to use as a charge. The stories are said to have "already come," and the perfective marks completion: the deterrent was not coming, it had already arrived. The word for the deterrent carries the sense of a rebuke that deters, a lesson that should stop a person before he falls, a reproof that is strong enough to turn a community back from its path. The verse says the deterrent was enough, and the sufficiency is the whole of the sūrah's argument in miniature. If the deterrent was enough, then the failure is not on the side of the evidence. The question the verse raises is not "was it enough?" — the answer is yes — but "why was enough not enough?" The question can only be answered on the side of the one who receives the deterrent. The sūrah's answer is the one it has already given in the previous verse: they followed their own desires.

**The Ruins on the Road**

The stories also had a geography, and the geography was on the Meccans' own trade routes. The caravan that left Makkah in the cold season for Yemen and the caravan that returned in the summer from Syria passed through the very places where the destroyed peoples had lived: the land of the ʿĀd, the valley where the city of Thamūd still stood in stone. The Qur'an fixes this fact elsewhere with a single phrase: "Their ruins still lie along a known route" (15:76). The deterrent was so not only in the spoken word; it was in the ground the caravans crossed, in the walls of the cities the travelers pointed at, in the empty lands that no one farmed.

**Why a Deterrent Can Fail to Deter**

The deterrent carries the logic of its own failure. The destroyed ones were "those before us" — a past tense, a different people, a different age. The Qur'an exposes this distance elsewhere: "Have they not received the stories of those ˹destroyed˺ before them: the people of Noah, ʿĀd, and Thamûd, the people of Abraham, the residents of Midian, and the overturned cities ˹of Lot˺?" (9:70).

## Verse 54:5

> ˹This Quran is˺ profound ˹in˺ wisdom, but warnings are of no benefit ˹to them˺

**The Arabic of Perfection**

The word for the wisdom is *māligha* — complete, perfect, reaching its limit — a judgment brought to its full measure, with nothing left out and nothing left loose. The noun *ḥukm* here is not a single ruling but the judgment as a whole, the total ordering of the message: its law, its narrative, its warning, its promise, all in their places. The line of objection is "your proof is not good enough," and the sūrah's answer is that the proof is the most perfect judgment, and the perfection is not a claim but a description.

**Warnings That Profit Nothing — To Whom?**

The second half needs its scope fixed, and the scope is the whole of the sūrah's case. The verb for "benefit" means to be profitable, to cover a need, to stand in the place of what was wanted. The verse does not say that warnings have no effect, for the effect is a fact — in the Garden and in the Fire, the warnings are being fulfilled. It says that they have no effect on this audience. The warning is a seed, and the verse says the soil of this audience has been sealed. The parallel verse states the same law in the plural, and the plural is the point: "Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Consider all that is in the heavens and on the earth!' Yet neither signs nor warners are of any benefit to those who refuse to believe" (10:101). The word for "warners" here is the same word as in 54:5, and the reading is confirmed: the warnings that profit nothing are the warnings of the messengers, and the audience to whom they profit nothing is the audience that refuses to believe.

**The Benefit That Remains**

The refusal of one audience does not exhaust the warning's effect, and the Book corrects 54:5 with its own voice. The same sūrah, elsewhere, states the law that the benefit of the reminder is located in the believers: "So ˹now˺ turn away from them ˹O Prophet˺, for you will not be blamed. But ˹continue to˺ remind. For certainly reminders benefit the believers" (51:54–55). The two verses together give the complete position: the benefit of the warning is not lost, it is relocated. It has already profited the believers, and it will profit every heart that opens to it, in every age. The "them" of 54:5 is a specific audience — the Meccans who refused — and the "believers" of 51:55 — *“But ˹continue to˺ remind. For certainly reminders benefit the believers”* is the audience that the warning was always meant for. The warning's total range is not defined by its failure in one place; it is defined by its success in every place where the heart is open.

## Verse 54:6

> So turn away from them ˹O Prophet˺. ˹And wait for˺ the Day ˹when˺ the caller will summon ˹them˺ for something horrifying

**The Command That Follows a Diagnosis**

The command to "turn away from them" follows directly from the preceding diagnosis, and the sequence is the point. The warnings have profited nothing to them; the content is perfect and the result is null; the desires have been followed. The logic of the command is clinical, not vengeful: where the tool has stopped working, the practitioner withdraws. The sūrah's own parallel commands make the sense of the withdrawal clear. "And when you come across those who ridicule Our revelations, do not sit with them unless they engage in a different topic" (6:68). "So turn away from them, and put your trust in Allah. And Allah is enough as a Trustee of Affairs" (4:81). The withdrawal is not abandonment; it is a re-aiming. The effort that was spent on an audience that refused is moved to the audience that receives, and the move is the whole of the command. The disbelievers are not punished by the withdrawal; they are left to their affair, which is the affair that will be settled.

**Turning Away, Not Turning Off**

The command is easily misread as "stop speaking," and the Book corrects the misreading with its own voice. The correction comes in the sūrah of the open city, where the two commands are given together and the difference between them is made explicit: "So ˹now˺ turn away from them ˹O Prophet˺, for you will not be blamed. But ˹continue to˺ remind. For certainly reminders benefit the believers" (51:54–55). The two verses, read together, give the complete position: the reclamation of the attention that yields nothing, and the maintenance of the reminder that does. The sūrah does not say "stop"; it says "move the effort." The withdrawal is from them, and the aim is at those who receive. This is the difference between despair of the people and the continuation of the task, and the sūrah's command is the continuation of the task. The reminder goes on; it simply no longer expects a harvest from the field that has been sealed.

**The Day and Its Caller**

The second half of the verse turns the Prophet's ﷺ attention from the present audience to the Day: wait for the Day when the caller will call. The word for the caller is *al-dāʿī* — the one who calls, the announcer, the one whose call is the signal. The commentators identify the caller with the angel who will sound the Trumpet on the Day, and the Trumpet is the instrument of the call: "The Trumpet will be blown and all those in the heavens and all those on the earth will fall dead, except those Allah wills ˹to spare˺" (39:68). The call is not a general summons; it is a summons to a specific thing. The Day is named by its content, and the content is the call and what the call is about. The sūrah's Last-Day description is so built from parts: the Day, the caller, the call, and the thing to which the call sends.

## Verse 54:7

> With eyes downcast, they will come forth from the graves as if they were swarming locusts

**The Exit Described in Two Images**

The verse gives two images at once, and the two images describe the same event from two directions. The first image is individual: eyes downcast — each person, each face, each lowered gaze. The second image is collective: a swarm of locusts — a dense moving mass, a multitude that moves as one body. The sūrah so describes the exit from the graves twice: as a state of each soul and as a state of the multitude. The double description is repeated in the parallel Last-Day passages. The two images are the two images the Book uses for the same event: "˹It is˺ the Day people will be like scattered moths" (101:4), and "Then it will be blown again and they will rise up at once, looking on ˹in anticipation˺" (39:68). The exit is not one picture; it is a state and a movement at once, and the sūrah gives both halves of the description in a single verse.

**Downcast Eyes: The Grammar of Humiliation**

The phrase for the eyes describes a gaze that cannot lift itself. The adjective for the eyes is the adjective of the one who is crushed before the one who has authority, and the detail is deliberate. On the Day, the first thing to be fixed is not the voice but the gaze. Every face that once looked at the sky with pride, that once looked at the messenger with contempt, that once looked at the sign and turned away (54:2 — *“Yet, whenever they see a sign, they turn away, saying, “Same old magic!””*) now looks downward. The posture of the exit is the posture of the judgment: the gaze that was lifted in arrogance is lowered in fear. The sūrah's choice of the detail is a statement about the kind of humiliation that the Day brings: it is not a humiliation of the body but a humiliation of the gaze, and the humiliation of the gaze is the humiliation of the self that once believed it was above the sign. The eye that refused to look at the truth is the eye that cannot look up on the Day.

## Verse 54:8

> rushing towards the caller. The disbelievers will cry, “This is a difficult Day!”

**The Direction of the Rush**

The first part of the verse gives the direction of the swarm described in the previous verse, and the direction is the point. The verb for the rush means to hasten, to run to a thing, to make for a thing with the whole of the body. Al-Ṭabarī records an additional sense of the verb in this context: to look toward, to turn the face. Both senses are needed, and the sūrah uses them together. The body runs, and the face turns, and the two together make the rush. The call that the swarm is answering is the same call named in 54:6 — *“So turn away from them ˹O Prophet˺. ˹And wait for˺ the Day ˹when˺ the caller will summon ˹them˺ for something horrifying”* — the call of the caller, the signal that ends the waiting and begins the assembly. The sūrah's Last-Day description is now complete in its parts: the state (the lowered eyes), the number (the swarm), the cause (the call), and the direction (toward the caller). Nothing in the description is left loose; every element of the scene is fixed.

**The Parallel: A Day That Is Not of Ease**

The Book says the same thing elsewhere, more fully, and the fuller statement is in the sūrah that describes the Day of the binding of the necks. There, the day is named in two verses, and the two verses give the name and its scope: "that will ˹truly˺ be a difficult Day— far from easy for the disbelievers" (74:9–10). The disbeliever's cry on the Day is so not a complaint but an echo of what the Book had already said about that day. They are finally speaking the Book's own description, and they speak it with the voice of one who has been taken by surprise by it. The description they now speak was available to them before, and the availability is the warning.

## Verse 54:9

> Before them, the people of Noah denied ˹the truth˺ and rejected Our servant, calling ˹him˺ insane. And he was intimidated

**The First Precedent: Why Noah**

The sūrah now turns from the Meccans to their predecessors, and it begins with Noah, and the choice of Noah is the first point of the example. The order is not accidental. Noah is the first of the prophets in the Qur'an's own account, the one to whom the prophetic chain is traced back, the one whose people were the first to be destroyed. To begin the examples with him is to say that the pattern of rejection is as old as the prophetic message itself. The phrase "before them" places the Meccans in the line: they are not the first, and they will not be the last. The sūrah's argument is that the Meccan rejection is one link in a chain that has already been tested and already settled, and the link is the same kind of link as the one that broke the people of Noah.

**The Length of the Trial**

The sūrah does not say how long the rejection of Noah lasted, but the Book says it elsewhere, and the length is part of the argument. "Indeed, We sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them for a thousand years, less fifty. Then the Flood overtook them, while they persisted in wrongdoing" (29:14). Nine hundred and fifty years of preaching, and the endpoint is the flood. The length is not a detail; it is a fact about the pattern of rejection. The sūrah's Meccan audience is hearing its own case in this one: the length of the trial is not a sign that the message is weak, it is a sign that the rejection is deep. The pattern is complete: the messenger preaches, the people reject, the charge is made, the cost is paid, and the length of the process is measured in centuries, not in years.

## Verse 54:10

> So he cried out to his Lord, “I am helpless, so help ˹me˺!”

**The Prayer After the Preaching**

The sūrah's account of Noah moves from the public to the private at this verse, and the move is the point. The preceding verse ends with the state of the messenger: rejected, called insane, defamed, and enduring. This verse gives the single act that follows from that state: he cried out to his Lord. The verb for "cried out" is the verb of the prophets in the Book, and it is the move that comes after the argument is exhausted. Noah's case is the first of the sūrah's examples, and it establishes the pattern that the sūrah will repeat: the preaching is complete, the rejection is complete, the charge is made, the cost is paid, and the messenger's response is not anger and not despair but a single prayer. The prayer is the end of the human part of the story, and the settlement that follows is the beginning of the divine part of the story.

**The Same Prayer in the Other Accounts**

The sūrah of Noah gives the call at length, and it shows what the cry in this verse sounded like when it was made. The Book records the man complaining to his Lord that he had called his people night and day and that his calling only drove them further away, that he had called them openly and in private, and that they had plugged their ears and wrapped themselves in their clothes (71:5-7 — *“He cried, “My Lord! I have surely called my people day and night”*). Then it gives the words of this verse in their fuller form: he said, my Lord, I have been overcome, so help me (54:10). The sūrah of the Believers records the same prayer and the answer it received: he called, my Lord, help me, for they have rejected me — and the answer was to build the Ark under God's eyes and His revelation (23:26-27 — *“Noah prayed, “My Lord! Help me, because they have denied ˹me˺.””*), with the instruction that when the command came he should take into it two of every kind and his family, except those already sentenced. The sūrah of Hūd gives the same news from the other side, with God telling him that none of his people would believe except those who had already believed, so he should not grieve over what they were doing (11:36 — *“And it was revealed to Noah, “None of your people will believe except those who already have. So do not be distressed by what they have been doing”*). The human argument ends at this verse, and the water that follows is the answer to the prayer.

**The End of the Human Part of the Story**

The sūrah's own continuation shows what the prayer set off: the gates of the sky opened with pouring water, the earth broke open with springs, and the two waters met for a matter already decreed (54:11-12 — *“So We opened the gates of the sky with pouring rain”*), with the Ark carrying him on planks and nails while the Book says it took its course under watchful eyes as a reward for the man who had been denied (54:14 — *“sailing under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes—a ˹fair˺ punishment on behalf of the one ˹they˺ denied”*). The Book tells the community elsewhere what a messenger does at the point this verse describes. Noah said to his people that if his presence among them and his reminders were hard on them, he had put his trust in God (10:71 — *“Relate to them ˹O Prophet˺ the story of Noah when he said to his people, “O my People!”*), and the Prophet himself was given the same word at the end of a long road: say, my Lord, forgive and have mercy, for You are the best of those who show mercy (23:118 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “My Lord! Forgive and have mercy, for You are the best of those who show mercy.””*). The cry in this verse is the turn from the argument to the appeal, and the Book makes the appeal the thing that opened the sky.

## Verse 54:11

> So We opened the gates of the sky with pouring rain

**The Answer as an Event**

The sūrah's account of the answer to Noah's prayer begins with a single act, and the act is the whole of the divine part of the story in this verse. "So We opened the gates of the sky with pouring water." The verb "We opened" is the verb of the settlement, and the object of the opening is the sky, described as having gates. The water that comes through the gates is not a normal rain; it is a pouring water, a rain that gushes down in abundance, that does not hold to its measure. The sūrah's account is economic: it does not describe the opening in detail, it names it. The event is the answer, and the answer is the settlement, and the settlement is the kind of settlement that the first verse of the sūrah promised. The divine part of the story begins not with a word but with an act, and the act is of a kind that no human hand could perform.

## Verse 54:12

> and caused the earth to burst with springs, so the waters met for a fate already set

**The Earth's Part in the Meeting**

The second half of the flood account is given in this verse, and the half is the earth's part in the meeting. "And We caused the earth to burst with springs." The earth, which was the ground, which held the bodies and the roots and the water of the wells, becomes a source. The verb describes the earth as a vessel that has been opened from within, and the water that comes is not a flood from above but a gushing from below. The two halves of the account — the sky and the earth — together make the meeting complete, and the completeness is the point of the sūrah's description.

**The Meeting of the Two Waters**

The phrase "so the waters met" describes the two waters as two parties that come together, and the image is the point. The verb for "met" is the verb of two things that are moving toward each other and arrive at the same place at the same time. The flood is so not a single event but a meeting: the water from the sky and the water from the earth come together in the middle, and the meeting is the complete settlement. The sūrah's account is a statement about the nature of the divine act: it is not a single act but a coming-together of acts, and the coming-together is the work of the one who opened the two vessels at the same time. The settlement is the result of the meeting, and the meeting is the evidence of the plan behind it. Two separate acts, made at the same time, for the same end.

---

## Verse 54:13

> We carried him on that ˹Ark made˺ of planks and nails

**The Same Word as the Preserved Tablet**

The word *lawḥ* — plank, board — is the same word the Qur'an uses for the Preserved Tablet, the place where the Book is kept: "In a Preserved Tablet" (85:22). The connection is not trivial. The planks of the Ark are boards, and the Preserved Tablet is a board, and the sūrah's choice of the word for the Ark is the sūrah's quiet statement about the kind of preservation it has in view. The Ark preserves a man in the flood; the Tablet preserves the Word in the age. Both are preservations, and both are described with the same word. The sameness of the word is the sūrah's way of joining the two: the preservation of the prophet in the water and the preservation of the message in the sky are of the same kind, and the kind is the kind that does not depend on the thing preserved being strong. The construction of the Ark was itself described in the Book as happening under the watchfulness of God: "And build the Ark under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes and directions, and do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong, for they will surely be drowned" (11:37). The building was under the Eyes, and the sailing is under the Eyes, and the watchfulness is continuous from the first nail to the last wave.

**The Singular of the Saved**

The verse says "him" — the singular — and the singular is the point. The believers who were with him are not named in this verse; they come in the fuller account, where the command is to take into the Ark a pair of every species and his family and those who believe (11:40 — *“And when Our command came and the oven burst ˹with water˺, We said ˹to Noah˺, “Take into the Ark a pair from every species along with your family—except those against whom the decree ˹to drown˺ has already been passed—and those who believe.””*). The sūrah's economy is deliberate: it names the one who cried out, and it carries the one who cried out. The community is implicit in the prophet, and the prophet is the center of the preservation.

**The Ark That Came to Rest**

The sūrah does not say where the Ark came to rest, but the Book says it elsewhere, and the resting is part of the account. "And it was said, 'O earth! Swallow up your water. Withhold ˹your rain˺.' The floodwater receded and the decree was carried out. The Ark rested on Mount Judi, and it was said, 'Away with the wrongdoing people!'" (11:44). The Ark that carried the prophet through the meeting of the waters came to rest on a named mountain, and the naming is the point: the preservation had an end, and the end was a place on the earth, a place that could be pointed at. The sūrah's account ends where the Book's fuller account ends: with the Ark at rest, the flood receded, and the decree carried out. The carrying was not without an end, and the end was the sign that the settlement was complete.

---

## Verse 54:14

> sailing under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes—a ˹fair˺ punishment on behalf of the one ˹they˺ denied

**Under Our Eyes: The Watchfulness That Is Also the Protection**

The first part of the verse describes the sailing of the Ark, and the description is the point: it sailed "under Our Eyes." The watchfulness is not a distant observation; it is the presence of the one who watches over the thing he has made to carry. The commentators read the phrase as the sign of God's protection: the Ark was not left to the sea, it was carried under the Eyes of the one who opened the sky. The sūrah's the teaching about the Last Day is full of eyes, and the eyes here are the opposite of the downcast eyes of the exit from the graves (54:7 — *“With eyes downcast, they will come forth from the graves as if they were swarming locusts”*). There, the eyes are lowered in fear; here, the Eyes are above, watching over the one who is carried. The two images together give the sūrah's full picture of the eyes: the eyes of the one who is judged are lowered, and the Eyes of the one who judges are above, and the distance between the two is the whole of the sūrah's the teaching about the Last Day.

**The Sailing and the Storm**

The verb for the sailing is the verb of the ship that moves through the water with a steady course, the same verb the Book uses for the ships of the sea: "To Him belong the ships with raised sails, sailing through the seas like mountains" (55:24). The contrast is the point: the Ark sailed — a steady, directed movement — while the waters raged — a violent, undirected movement. The means of salvation moved with an ease that the flood denied. The storm was the settlement, and the sailing was the preservation, and the two movements were happening at the same time, in the same water, and the difference between them was the difference between the one who was carried and the one who was taken. The sūrah's method is to put the two movements side by side and let the contrast do the arguing.

---

## Verse 54:15

> We certainly left this as a sign. So is there anyone who will be mindful

**The Sign That Was Left**

The verse says the thing was "left as a sign," and the thing that was left is the point. Some of the commentators take the sign to be the Ark itself, preserved and visible for many generations after the flood, a witness that lasted until the time of the Prophet. The more likely reading is that the sign is the story of Noah, preserved in human memory, passed from generation to generation, a warning that outlived the people it was given to. The two readings agree on the point that matters: the sign was left, and the leaving was deliberate. The flood was not an accident that happened and ended; it was an event that was left behind, a mark in the memory of the world, for the ones who would come after. The sūrah's claim is that the past is not gone; it is left, and the leaving is the work of the one who opened the sky.

**The Sūrah That Was Carried by the Tongue**

The sign that was left was itself carried by a tongue, and the carrying is part of the account. It is reported that the Prophet ﷺ recited this sūrah — the sūrah of the destroyed peoples and their settlements — in the prayer of the two feasts, ʿĪd al-Fiṭr and ʿĪd al-Aḍḥā, the gatherings at which the community is assembled in full. The sūrah of the flood and the wind and the blast was the sūrah of the great assembly, and the choice is the point: the sign that was left for the generations was itself the sign that was repeated at the feast. The leaving and the repeating are the same act: the one who left the sign also repeated it, and the repetition at the feast was the sign made present to the assembly.

---

## Verse 54:16

> Then how ˹dreadful˺ were My punishment and warnings

**The Refrain: A Question That Is Already Answered**

The verse is the first form of the refrain that the sūrah will repeat at the end of each of its cases. The question is "how were My punishment and My warnings?" — an exclamatory question, a question that is already answered by the case that just ended. The sūrah asks "how was it?" to the one who witnessed it, and the answer is the case itself: the flood, the meeting of the waters, the carrying of the Ark, the drowning of the many. The asking is not a demand for information; it is an emphasis, a way of making the listener stand in front of the case and look at it. The refrain is so the sūrah's way of slowing the listener down at the end of each case, of making him stop and look at what happened before he moves on to the next.

**Punishment and Warnings: The Pair That Names the Fairness**

The refrain pairs the punishment with the warnings, and the pairing is the point. The punishment is not named alone; it is named with the warnings that came before it. The pair is the sūrah's way of stating the fairness: the one who was punished was first warned, and the punishment was the end of the warning, not the beginning of it. The sūrah's claim is that the settlement is not arbitrary; it is the completion of a process that began with the warning.

---

## Verse 54:17

> And We have certainly made the Quran easy to remember. So is there anyone who will be mindful

**The Second Refrain: A Mercy Where the First Was a Threat**

The verse is the second refrain of the sūrah, and it is the positive counterpart of the first. Where the first refrain was the question about the punishment — "how were My punishment and My warnings?" — this refrain is the statement of a mercy: the Qur'an was made easy to remember. The two refrains together give the sūrah's two notes, and the two notes are the point. The sūrah does not leave the listener with the threat alone; it follows the threat with the offer. The offer is the ease, and the ease is the standing invitation of the Book. The sūrah's method is to put the two notes side by side — the punishment and the mercy, the threat and the offer — and to let the listener choose between them.

**The Ease That the Prophet Named**

The ease of the Qur'an is not only the sūrah's claim; it is the Prophet's ﷺ own saying, and the saying is the point. He said, "The best among you are those who learn the Quran and teach it to others" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5027), and the saying is the practical form of the ease the verse names. The best of the community are the ones who take up the Book and pass it on, and the taking up and the passing on are the two acts that the ease makes possible. The sūrah's claim is so confirmed by the practice: the Book was made easy, and the ease produced the people who learned it and taught it, and the learning and the teaching are the evidence that the ease is real. The verse and the saying are the same claim in two forms: the claim of the one who made the Book easy, and the claim of the one who received it and found it easy. The purpose of the ease is named in the verse: "to remember." The ease is not given for the sake of reading; it is given for the sake of remembrance, of being held, of being returned to. The remembrance is the opposite of the turning away that the sūrah described at its beginning: the one who turns away presents the back, and the one who remembers turns the face.

**The Question Returns: Joined to the Mercy**

The refrain ends with the same question as 54:15: "So is there anyone who will be mindful?" The question is now joined to the mercy, and the joining is the point. The ease is given, and the question is whether anyone will take it up.

---

## Verse 54:18

> ’Ȃd ˹also˺ rejected ˹the truth˺. Then how ˹dreadful˺ were My punishment and warnings

**The Second Case in Two Verses**

The sūrah moves to its second case, and the introduction is brief: ʿĀd rejected. The brevity is the point. The sūrah does not restate the pattern; it assumes the audience has learned it from the first case. The structure is now a formula: the name of the people, the fact of the rejection, the refrain. What the first case gave in fourteen verses, the second case gives in a single one, and the compression is the sūrah's way of saying that the pattern is established and no longer needs to be explained.

**ʿĀd: The People Who Asked Who Was Stronger**

ʿĀd is the people of strength, and the strength is the point of their case. The Book describes them as the people of Iram with their great stature, unmatched in any other land (89:7–8 — *“unmatched in any other land”*), and it records the question they asked: "Who is superior to us in might?" (41:15). The question is the key to their rejection: they did not reject because the evidence was weak, they rejected because they were strong, and the strength made them certain that no punishment could reach them. The sūrah's choice of ʿĀd as the second case is the sūrah's way of answering the Meccan self-image: the Meccans, like ʿĀd, were a people of strength and standing, and the strength was the reason they were certain they were beyond the settlement.

**The Messenger of Their Own Kind**

The messenger sent to ʿĀd was Hūd, and he was of their own kind: "And to the people of ʿĀd We sent their brother Hūd. He said, 'O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. Will you not then fear him?'" (7:65). The brotherhood is the point, and the sūrah's other cases will make the point again: the messenger is not a stranger, not an outsider, not a rival from another city. The messenger is the one from among them, the one who knows their language and their custom and their strength. The rejection of a brother is a heavier rejection than the rejection of a stranger, and the sūrah's cases are all cases of the rejection of a brother. The Meccan audience is being shown that the rejection of a messenger of their own kind is the rejection that has been settled before, and the settling is the kind that the refrain names. The brotherhood of the messenger is so the heaviest part of the case, because the brother cannot be dismissed as a stranger, and the dismissal is the move that the rejecters have no longer available.

---

## Verse 54:19

> Indeed, We sent against them a furious wind, on a day of unrelenting misery

**The Wind: The Instrument Chosen for the Strong**

The settlement of ʿĀd was a wind, and the choice of the wind is the point. The sūrah's first case used water — the flood, the meeting of the two waters — and the second case uses air, the wind that takes. The change of instrument is not arbitrary; it is measured to the people. ʿĀd was the people who asked who was stronger than they were, and the wind is the answer: the wind is the one that does not stop at a wall, the one that does not bargain, the one that takes the strong as it takes the weak. The sūrah's method is to choose the instrument that answers the specific arrogance of the people: the flood answers the people who were certain of their numbers, and the wind answers the people who were certain of their strength. The instrument is the answer to the question the people asked.

**The Names the Book Gives the Wind**

The Book gives the wind several names, and the names are the point: a furious wind in this sūrah, a furious wind for several miserable days in the sūrah of Fūṣilat — "So We sent against them a furious wind. ˹several˺ miserable days, to make them taste a humiliating punishment in this worldly life" (41:16) — a devastating wind in the sūrah of adh-Dhāriyāt (51:41 — *“And in ˹the story of˺ ’Âd ˹was another lesson,˺ when We sent against them the devastating wind”*), and a furious, bitter wind in the sūrah of al-Hāqqah (69:6 — *“And as for ’Ȃd, they were destroyed by a furious, bitter wind”*). Each name adds a quality to the wind: the fury is the violence, the bitterness is the harshness, the devastation is the sterility, the days are the duration. The wind is not a generic punishment; it is a specific thing, named and described, and the naming is the sūrah's way of making the punishment real. The Meccan audience is being given the wind as it was, not as a symbol, and the reality is the warning.

**A Day of Unrelenting Misery**

The wind came on a day, and the day is described: a day of unrelenting misery, a day in which the misery did not vary, did not break, did not allow a rest. The description of the day is the point: the punishment was not a single blow; it was a day, and the day was full of the misery from the first hour to the last. The sūrah elsewhere describes the duration of the wind for ʿĀd as seven nights and eight days (69:6–7 — *“which Allah unleashed on them non-stop for seven nights and eight days, so that you would have seen its people lying dead like trunks of uprooted palm trees”*). The duration is the kind of duration that does not allow a shelter: the one who survives the first night does not survive the second, and the one who finds a hole in the ground does not find the hole enough. The day of unrelenting misery is so the sūrah's way of describing a punishment that has no interval, no rest, no moment in which the one caught in it can hope that it will stop.

---

## Verse 54:20

> that snatched people up, leaving them like trunks of uprooted palm trees

**The Snatching: The Act the Wind Did**

The verse describes what the wind did, and the description is the point: it snatched the people. The verb for the snatching is the verb of the sudden taking, the taking that does not give a time to move, the taking that happens before the one taken can stand. The wind did not kill the people slowly; it took them, as the hand takes a thing from a table, suddenly and without a struggle. The sūrah's description is economic: it does not describe the sound of the wind or the length of the day, it describes the act — the taking — and the act is the settlement. The taking is the kind of act that leaves no witness who can describe it in detail, because the witnesses were taken with the taken, and the sūrah's economy is the sūrah's way of showing that the settlement was of that kind.

**The Image of the Uprooted Palms**

The image the verse gives is the trunks of uprooted palm trees, and the image is the point. The sūrah uses the same image elsewhere, in the fuller description of the wind of ʿĀd: "so that you would have seen its people lying dead like trunks of uprooted palm trees" (69:7). The image works on two levels at once. The first level is the likeness: the people of ʿĀd were known for their large stature, and the tall palm is the tree of their land, and the likeness is the likeness of the strong to the strong tree. The second level is the taking: the wind takes the palm by uprooting it, not by breaking it, and the taking of the people was of the same kind — the strong were taken whole, in one act, as the trunk is taken from the ground.

---

## Verse 54:21

> Then how ˹dreadful˺ were My punishment and warnings

**The Warnings That Came Before the Wind**

The refrain pairs the punishment with the warnings, and the pairing is the point. The warnings to ʿĀd came through Hūd, over a period of time, and Hūd was of their own kind, their brother. The warnings were not a single call; they were a process, a brother speaking to his people, asking them to worship the one who created them and to seek forgiveness and to be given strength added to their strength (11:52 — *“He will shower you with rain in abundance, and add strength to your strength.”*). The wind was not a surprise; it was the end of the process, the settlement of the warnings that were not taken up.

**The Refrain Returns: The Second Voice of the First Note**

The verse is the refrain again, and this is its second appearance in the sūrah. The first appearance closed the case of Noah; this one closes the case of ʿĀd. The sameness is the point, and the sameness is the argument. The sūrah does not vary the refrain; it repeats it, with the same words and the same force, at the end of each case. The repetition is the sūrah's method, and the method is the point: the pattern is the same, and the pattern is the one the listener is being warned about. The refrain is not a decoration at the end of each story; it is the structure of the sūrah, and the structure is the argument.

**The Punishment Measured to the Pride**

The punishment of ʿĀd was the wind, and the wind was measured to the pride of ʿĀd. The people who asked who was stronger than they were (41:15 — *“As for ’Ȃd, they acted arrogantly throughout the land with no right, boasting, “Who is superior to us in might?” Did they not see that Allah ˹Himself˺, Who created them, was far superior to them in might? Still they persisted in denying Our signs”*) were answered by the one that does not stop at strength: the wind. The refrain asks "how was the punishment?" to the one who asked the question of strength, and the answer is the wind. The sūrah's claim is that the settlement is measured: the pride that said "who is stronger than us?" is answered by the one that is stronger, and the answering is the punishment. The refrain is so the sūrah's way of joining the question and the answer: the question of the proud and the answer of the wind are the same event, seen from the two sides.

---

## Verse 54:22

> And We have certainly made the Quran easy to remember. So is there anyone who will be mindful

**The Ease Stated Elsewhere in the Book**

The ease the refrain names is stated elsewhere in the Book, and the statement is the point: "Indeed, We have made this ˹Quran˺ easy in your own language ˹O Prophet˺ so with it you may give good news to the righteous and warn those who are contentious" (19:97). The two verses, read together, give the complete claim: the Qur'an was made easy, and the ease was to giving good news and warning. The refrain of this sūrah is the same claim, repeated at the end of each case, and the repetition is the sūrah's way of joining the ease to the warning: the ease is not separate from the warning, the ease is the form of the warning. The warning is made easy so that it can be taken up, and the taking up is the act that the refrain asks for. The two verses are the same statement, and the sameness is the argument.

**The Second Refrain Returns: The Mercy After the Threat**

The verse is the second refrain again, and this is its second appearance in the sūrah. The first appearance came after the case of Noah; this one comes after the case of ʿĀd. The sameness is the point: the mercy is repeated, case after case, and the repetition is the argument. The sūrah does not let the punishment stand alone; it follows the punishment with the offer, and the offer is the same each time. The ease of the Qur'an is the sūrah's standing invitation, and the standing invitation is repeated because the audience is repeated: each case is a new audience, and each audience gets the same offer.

**The Question Again: The Third Asking**

The refrain ends with the question "So is there anyone who will be mindful?" — the same question as 54:15 — *“We certainly left this as a sign. So is there anyone who will be mindful”* and 54:17 — *“And We have certainly made the Quran easy to remember. So is there anyone who will be mindful”*, and this is its third appearance. The question is the sūrah's key, and the key is repeated, and the repetition is the argument. The sūrah asks the question after each case, and the question is the one the listener must answer for himself. The sūrah does not answer the question; it asks it, and the asking is the warning. The question is open as long as the listener is alive, and the open question is the sūrah's way of keeping the warning alive. The listener is not asked to believe a claim; he is asked to answer a question, and the answering is the act of the mindfulness the sūrah is asking for.

---

## Verse 54:23

> Thamûd rejected the warnings ˹as well˺

**The Third Case, Introduced in a Single Line**

The sūrah moves to its third case, and the introduction is a single line: Thamūd rejected the warnings. The compression continues, and the compression is the point. The first case, Noah, was given in fourteen verses; the second case, ʿĀd, was given in five; the third case is introduced in one. The sūrah does not restate the pattern; it assumes the audience has learned it, and the assumption is the argument. The audience that has heard the full story of the flood and the wind is now in a position to hear the bare name of Thamūd and to know what the name means: the denial, the charge, the trial, the settlement. The single line is so the sūrah's way of saying that the pattern is established and no longer needs to be explained.

**Warnings, or Warners: The Word That Returns**

The word for "warnings" admits the same double reading as the word in 54:5 — *“˹This Quran is˺ profound ˹in˺ wisdom, but warnings are of no benefit ˹to them˺”*: it can be read as the warnings, the messages, or as the warners, the messengers. The double reading returns, and the return is the point.

**Thamūd: The People Who Came After ʿĀd**

Thamūd is the people who came after ʿĀd, and the succession is the point. The Book records the succession explicitly: "Remember when He made you successors after ʿĀd and established you in the land—˹and˺ you built palaces on its plains and carved homes into mountains" (7:74). The sūrah places Thamūd immediately after ʿĀd, and the order follows the succession of the peoples: the people who came after the people of the wind are the people who carved their homes into the mountains. The succession is the sūrah's quiet argument: the fate of ʿĀd was before the eyes of Thamūd, and the successors still rejected. The warning of the wind was the inheritance of Thamūd, and the inheritance was not taken up.

**The City on the Road**

The traces of this people were on the road, and the road is the point. The Book names the traces of Thamūd and makes them a fact that can be seen: "The ruins of both nations still lie on a well-known road" (15:79). The two nations are ʿĀd and Thamūd, and the ruins of both lie on the road that the caravans travel. The sūrah elsewhere names the people by their city: "Indeed, the residents of the Stone Valley also denied the messengers" (15:80). The Stone Valley is the land of Thamūd, and the residents are the people of this case. The case of Thamūd is so not a story about a far-off people; it is a story about the people on the road, the people whose ruins the caravans pass, the people whose city the sūrah names. The argument of the case is made concrete by the road: the warning is not an abstraction, it is a place, and the place is the one the audience passes on its way.

---

## Verse 54:24

> arguing, “How can we follow one ˹average˺ human being from among us? We would then truly be misguided and insane

**The Charge of Human Stature**

The words of Thamūd give the reason for the rejection, and the reason is the point. The charge is the humanity of the messenger: "How can we follow one human being from among us?" The charge does not address the content of the message; it addresses the person of the messenger. The content of Ṣāliḥ's message — the worship of the one who made them successors, the remembrance of the favours, the forbidding of corruption — is not examined. What is examined is the fact that the messenger is a human being, one of their own kind, from among them. The sūrah's diagnosis is the same as in the case of Noah: the rejection is of the messenger, not of the message, and the rejection of the messenger is the sign that the rejection is not about the evidence.

**The Standing Objection Across the Book**

The objection is not unique to Thamūd; it is the standing objection of every audience that has refused a messenger, and the sameness is the point. The Book records the same objection in its various forms: "And nothing has prevented people from believing when guidance comes to them except their protest: 'Has Allah sent a human as a messenger?'" (17:94). "That was because their messengers used to come to them with clear proofs, but they said ˹mockingly˺, 'How can humans be our guides?'" (64:6). "Yet the deniers are astonished that a warner has come to them from among themselves" (50:2). The objection is constant across the audiences and the ages, and its constancy is the sūrah's argument: this is the default objection, the one every audience produces, and the production is the refusal.

**The Parallel With the Words of the Quraysh**

The sūrah's argument is made visible by the parallel with the words of the Quraysh, and the parallel is the point. The words of Thamūd reflect the words the Quraysh brought against the Prophet ﷺ: "And they say ˹mockingly˺, 'What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺? If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner'" (25:7). The parallel is exact: the objection of the messenger's humanity, the demand for an angel, the insistence on the messenger's otherness. The sūrah is showing the Meccan audience their own words in the mouth of a people who were destroyed, and the showing is the warning. The case of Thamūd is the case of the Quraysh, and the case is the one the Quraysh are making.

**Misguided and Mad: The Two Verdicts**

The charge ends with two verdicts: to follow the human messenger would be to be misguided and to be mad. The two verdicts are the point: the charge attacks both the direction and the sanity of the one who follows. To be misguided is to be led astray, an error of direction; to be mad is to be out of one's mind, an error of sanity. The sūrah's answer is implicit in the law it stated at its beginning: the one who follows his own desire is the one who is misguided (54:3 — *“They rejected ˹the truth˺ and followed their own desires—and every matter will be settled”*), and the one who is called mad for following the messenger is the one whose madness is the refusal.

---

## Verse 54:25

> Has the revelation been sent down ˹only˺ to him out of ˹all of˺ us? In fact, he is a boastful liar.”

**The Second Objection: The Question of the Choice**

The second word of the charge is a question, and the question is the point. "Has the revelation been sent down only to him, out of all of us?" The objection is not about the content of the message or even the humanity of the messenger; it is about the choice of the messenger. The question assumes that the revelation could have been sent to another, and it asks why it was sent to this one. The objection is so a claim about the criterion of the choice: the messenger should be someone other than the one who was chosen. The sūrah's answer, implicit in its examples, is that the messenger is not chosen by the community's preference, and the choice is not the community's to make.

**The Parallel With the Quraysh's Demand**

The sūrah's argument is made visible by the parallel with the demand of the Quraysh, and the parallel is the point. The Quraysh made the same demand in their own words: "And they exclaimed, 'If only this Quran was revealed to a great man from ˹one of˺ the two cities!'" (43:31). The demand is exact: the messenger should be a great man, a leader, one of the two cities' standing families. The sūrah is showing the Meccan audience their own demand in the mouth of Thamūd, and the showing is the warning. The case of Thamūd is the case of the Quraysh, and the demand is the demand that the Quraysh are making. The parallel is the sūrah's way of making the past present, and the presence is the argument.
