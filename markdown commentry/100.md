# Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt (Chapter 100) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Panting War-Steeds and the Ungrateful Human Heart**

Al-ʿĀdiyāt, "The War Horses" or "The Charging Steeds," is the one-hundredth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 11 verses of early Makkan revelation (with some authorities considering it early Medinan). It opens with a cinematic, thundering sequence of five military oaths sworn by Arabian war-steeds in the heat of a desert cavalry charge: *“By the [steeds] that pant with breath, and those that strike sparks of fire [with hooves], and those that charge at dawn, stirring up clouds of dust, and penetrating into the midst of the enemy assembly!”* (100:1–5). In prophetic tradition, Ibn ʿAbbās reported in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī (2895) that Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt is equivalent to half of the Qur'an in its dramatic impact. In eleven rapid, rhythmic verses, the sūrah constructs an astonishing, painful psychological comparison: contrasting the unhesitating, self-sacrificing obedience of the warhorse to its human master against the chronic, stubborn ingratitude of the human being to his Sovereign Creator (*Inna al-insāna li-Rabbihi la-kanūd*).

**The Structural Architecture and the Laying Bare of the Graves**

The architecture of Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt moves across three distinct movements:

1. The Five Oaths of the Cavalry Charge (1–5): Swears by the panting breath of the horses (*ḍabḥā*), the sparks of fire struck by hooves (*qadḥā*), the dawn raid (*ṣubḥā*), the swirling dust clouds (*naqʿā*), and penetrating the center of the enemy ranks (*jamʿā*).

2. The Anatomy of Human Ingratitude and Greed (6–8): Delivers the stinging moral diagnosis of humanity: - *“Indeed, man to his Lord is ungrateful (*la-kanūd*)!”* (Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī explained *al-kanūd* as the person who counts his afflictions while forgetting his blessings). - *“And indeed, to that [ingratitude] he is a witness (*wa-innahū ʿalā dhālika la-shahīd*)!”* (his own conscience and conduct testify against him). - *“And indeed, in his love of wealth, he is intense / ferocious (*wa-innahū li-ḥubbi al-khayri la-shadīd*)!”*

3. The Exhumation of Graves and the Exposure of the Chest (9–11): Issues the urgent existential awakening of verses 9–10: *“Does he not know that when the contents of the graves are overturned and scattered (*buʿthira mā fī al-qubūr*). That which is in the breasts is made manifest and extracted (*wa-ḥuṣṣila mā fī al-ṣudūr*)?”* The chapter concludes with the sovereign reality of divine omniscience in verse 11: *“Indeed, their Lord with them, that Day, is fully Acquainted (*Inna Rabbahum bihim yawma'idhin la-khabīr*)!”*

## Verse 100:1

> By the galloping, panting horses

**An Oath That Puts War-Horses in the Witness Box**

The sūrah opens with a sworn particle and a plural that names animals in motion: *al-ʿādiyāt*, those that run hard until their breath comes short. The word is a feminine plural active participle from *ʿadā*, to rush, raid, or transgress a limit. The panting is not decoration. *Ḍabḥ* is the sound a horse makes when the chest is driven to its edge, a dry rasp between snort and gasp. God does not swear by idle scenery. He swears by a creature that has been trained, mounted, and spent. The oath therefore already contains a moral: value is measured by what a being will exhaust itself to do.

**Charging Souls Without Emptying the Horses**

Al-Bayḍāwī records a reading in which the chargers are souls that, when a light of holiness appears, carry it to others. The text still begins with beasts whose ribs move. The spiritual sense is earned only if the literal sense is kept: God swears by a created service that costs breath. *ʿIlliyyūn* in *“No indeed! The record of the virtuous is in the ʿIlliyyūn”* (83:18) is a later destination, not a licence to dissolve the raid into metaphor on the first line.

**Why God Swears at All**

Qur'anic oaths are not the human kind. A human swears because his word is weak and needs a prop. God swears because the thing sworn by is a sign that already argues, and the oath forces the listener to look at it before the thesis arrives. Here the thesis will be that man is *kanūd*, ungrateful. The five opening pictures (this verse and the four that follow) are the evidence laid out before the verdict. A horse that empties its lungs for a rider is a standing rebuke to a human who will not empty his wealth or his will for the One who made both. The oath also teaches proportion. The Book will later say *“By the dawn”* (89:1) and *“By the fig and the olive”* (95:1). Dawn and fruit are quiet. These horses are loud. The change of register matches the charge that follows: ingratitude is not a private mood; it is a public refusal in a world where even beasts keep faith with their task.

**The Name of the Sūrah Is the First Word of the Oath**

The chapter is called *al-ʿĀdiyāt* because the first audible claim is this participle. Naming a sūrah by its opening oath is common in the last thirtieth of the muṣḥaf. The name does extra work here. It refuses to let the later moral verses float free of the image that paid for them. Every time the sūrah is recited, the congregation is returned to panting animals before it is told that man is ungrateful. That order is pedagogy. The oath in 100:1 is the worship-side of that preparation: a horse already spent is a sign before it is a weapon.

## Verse 100:2

> striking sparks of fire ˹with their hoofs˺

**Hooves on Stone, Not Poetry on Air**

The second oath names *al-mūriyāt*, those that strike fire. The verb *awrā* is the same root used for kindling a fire from a fire-stick. The image is physical: iron-shod or flint-hard hooves hitting rock in a night or dawn gallop, and a spark jumping. Ibn Kathīr and others take this as the plain sense, and it should be kept plain. Desert ground is not all sand. Wadis and lava fields throw stone under a charge. A raiding party that needs speed cannot pick a soft path. Sparks are the tax the earth levies on haste. The translation's bracket, “with their hoofs,” is an interpretive insertion, not extra Arabic, but it is the right insertion. Nothing else in the scene produces fire. The verse therefore continues the first oath's economy: still the same animals, now seen at the point of contact with the ground.

**War, Pilgrimage, and the Ethics of Speed**

Whether the mounts are war-horses or ḥajj camels, speed in this sūrah is not a vice. The vice will be love of gain. Speed in God's service, even when it throws fire from the ground, is something God will swear by. That distinction matters in a culture that can praise both reckless driving and spiritual laziness. The verse does not bless every haste. It blesses haste that has a lawful target and pays in breath and spark. *“And prepare against them whatever you ˹believers˺ can of power and steeds of war”* (8:60) later treats horses as a communal trust. This early oath already assumes that trust. The spark is the audible, visible proof that the trust was not left in a stable.

*“It is Allah Who has created the fire for you from the green tree”* (36:80)

## Verse 100:3

> launching raids at dawn

**Dawn Is Chosen Because Sleep Is Still There**

*Al-mughīrāt ṣubḥan* names those that raid at daybreak. *Aghāra* is to attack, to rush a settlement, to take by sudden force. *Ṣubḥ* is the first light, not mid-morning. Pre-Islamic and early Islamic raids used this hour because guards are tired, flocks are still bunched, and a column can close the last ground under cover of the last dark. The verse does not romanticize slaughter. It names a military fact that every listener in Makkah and Yathrib already knew. God swears by the timing, not by the loot.

**Pilgrimage Dawn as a Second Lawful Scene**

Commentators who read the oaths as ḥajj point to the movement from al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām toward Minā on the morning of the Day of Sacrifice. That rush is also at dawn, and it is also a mass of mounts. *“You may then depart from where the people have departed”* (2:199) belongs to that sequence. The two scenes share hour and motion and differ in aim. One dawn is a raid; one dawn is a rite. The Arabic can carry both because *ighārah* in the raid sense is the primary, while the ḥajj reading treats the same feminine plural as a people or their mounts pressing forward. The stronger first sense remains military, because *mughīrāt* is a raiding word. The ḥajj sense is a serious minority, not a fantasy. What both senses share is this: the creature moves at God's preferred hour toward a goal that is not its own breakfast.

**Dawn as a Qur'anic Hour**

The Book loves *ṣubḥ*. *“By the morning brightness”* (93:1); *“And the dawn as it breathes away the darkness”* (81:18); the command to *“establish prayer at both ends of the day and in the early part of the night”* (11:114). Dawn is when night's cover is lifted and work begins. A raid at that hour is a dark use of a blessed time, unless the raid itself is just. The sūrah does not sort the justice of every historical charge. It uses the hour because the hour is already loaded: what you do at first light tells what you served in the dark. Ingratitude, the coming charge, is also a dawn problem. People wake, take the day's provision, and do not thank. The horses at least spend the first light on the task they were given. Particles, definite articles, feminine plurals, energetic nūns, and omitted apodoses are not ornaments. They are how the threat or the oath actually works. A reciter who only translates into a slogan has dropped the tools. Classical iʿrāb on a verse like this is not a hobby for specialists. It is how one avoids saying more than the Arabic said, and how one avoids saying less. Where the translation uses brackets, those brackets are tafsīr. Dawn motion in rite and dawn motion in raid share a hour; they do not share a law, and this verse must not be used to erase that difference.

## Verse 100:4

> stirring up ˹clouds of˺ dust

**Dust Is the Public Signature of a Charge**

*Fa-atharna bihi naqʿan* continues with a *fāʾ* of sequence: they raise by it a cloud of dust. *Naqʿ* is dust kicked into the air, thick enough to be a screen. *Bihi* is disputed. The pronoun may point to the dawn time, to the place, or to the gallop itself. All three yield the same picture: motion so concentrated that the ground becomes weather. Dust is not a side effect the sūrah is embarrassed by. It is sworn by. In a dry land, a dust-cloud is how a village knows that riders have come before it sees their faces.

**Dust in the Wider Book**

The Qur'an uses dust for origin and for abasement. *“He created you from dust”* (30:20). Faces may be *“covered with dust”* in grief (80:40-41 — *“while ˹other˺ faces will be dusty cast in gloom”*). Here dust is kinetic, not genealogical. It is what happens when speed meets earth. The same earth that man was taken from is now thrown up by animals more obedient than he is. The irony is not cute; it is the sūrah's method. Every physical detail in the oath-block will be reusable as a moral later. Dust now; secrets later. What is stirred up here by hooves will, in another hour, be stirred up by a blast.

## Verse 100:5

> and penetrating into the heart of enemy lines

**The Charge Completes Only When It Is Inside**

*Fa-wasaṭna bihi jamʿan* — they went into the middle, by it, of a gathering. *Wasaṭa* is to be in the centre, not to nibble the edge. *Jamʿ* is a host, a crowd, a collected force. The feminine plural is still the mounts. The raid is not a demonstration outside the walls. It is a breach. Al-Bayḍāwī takes this as horses reaching the centre of enemy troops. The oath-chain has moved from breath to spark to hour to dust to *inside*.

**Collective Nouns and the End of Private War**

*Jamʿ* is a collective. The horses do not pick a single man; they enter a body. War in this picture is not knight-against-knight as a sport. It is a mass meeting a mass. That realism matters. So does the moral limit that other verses will add: *“Fight in the cause of Allah those who fight you, but do not exceed the limits”* (2:190). Verse 5 is not that legal verse. It is the last panel of an oath. Using it to erase limits is a misuse. Using it to deny that early Muslims knew what a charge was is also a misuse. The text assumes knowledge of battle without making battle the sūrah's subject. The subject is still coming, and it is ingratitude.

**Spiritual Centre Without Softening the Raid**

The soul-reading says the chargers penetrate *ʿIlliyyūn* or the gathering of those who live by caprice. Keep that as a sermon, not as a substitute. The verse's first job is to finish a picture of mounts that go all the way. A believer who stops his own run at the edge of a hard command has not yet become like the thing God swore by. *“O believers! Enter into Islam wholeheartedly”* (2:208) is a different sūrah's wording for a similar demand: do not remain on the rim. Verse 5, in its own voice, simply shows animals that did not remain on the rim. The human being has now been given five pictures and no excuse.

## Verse 100:6

> Surely humankind is ungrateful to their Lord—

**The Answer to Five Oaths Is One Diagnosis**

*Inna al-insāna li-rabbihi la-kanūd* is the *jawāb*. After horses, sparks, dawn, dust, and a breached centre, the sūrah names the human being. *Inna* and the extra *lām* on *la-kanūd* are both emphasizers. The claim is not tentative. *Al-insān* is the species, not a single villain. *Rabb* is the Lord who owns, raises, and claims. The shock is the mismatch: animals spent themselves in a lord's service; man withholds from his Lord. The five oaths were not a war documentary. They were a moral contrast drawn in dust and breath. A reader who enjoyed the cavalry and then feels insulted by verse 6 has understood the rhetoric exactly as it was aimed.

**What Kanūd Means When It Is a Person**

*Kanūd* of land is ground that grows nothing. Of a person it is the one who counts troubles and forgets gifts, the miser of thanks. Lexica add: ungrateful, unrewarding, one who cuts off. The translation “ungrateful” is right and slightly thin. The word is a character, not a mood. It is the habit of receiving and not returning. The Qur'an has a family of such words: *kafūr*, *kanūd*, *kafūr* again in *“Indeed, humankind is ungrateful”* (17:67; 22:66; 42:48; 43:15 in related senses). *Kufr* itself is covering a gift. This verse chooses *kanūd* because the image of barren land fits a sūrah that just showed earth throwing dust under obedient hooves. The same earth, in a man, refuses to yield thanks.

**Lordship Makes Ingratitude Personal**

The verse does not say man is ungrateful to “existence” or to “society.” It says *to his Lord*. The pronoun *rabbihi* ties each human to a specific claim. Gifts are not anonymous. Food, time, mounts, children, and the heart itself arrive with an address. Ingratitude is therefore not a failure of manners. It is a failure of recognition. *“And He gave you whatever you asked for. If you tried to count Allah’s blessings, you would never be able to number them. Indeed humankind is truly unfair, totally ungrateful”* (14:34). Verse 6 of this sūrah is that doctrine in a shorter blade. The oaths have already shown what recognition looks like in a horse. The human is now told he is the barren field.

## Verse 100:7

> and they certainly attest to this—

**Attestation Is Not the Same as Repentance**

Witnessing to a crime is not leaving the crime. People admit they are ungrateful in a joke, in a sigh, in a night of fear, and then return to counting goods. Verse 7 does not say they repent. It says they attest. The next verse will show why the attestation does not save them: love of gain is fierce. The human *kanūd* knows in flashes. Self-witness in 100:7 is the short form of that later court in which limbs speak.

**The Pronoun That Splits the Commentators**

*Wa-innahu ʿalā dhālika la-shahīd* — and he / it is surely a witness to that. *Hu* can be God, or the human being. Both readings are old. If God is the witness, the verse says that the Lord who was just named as the object of ingratitude is Himself the one who testifies to man's *kanūd*. That fits a large Qur'anic file: *“Allah is a Witness over all things”* (58:6); *“Surely Allah is a Witness over everything”* (22:17). If man is the witness, the verse says that the ungrateful one knows his own state, in this life by a buried awareness, or in the next by open confession. Al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Ṭabrisī all record the fork. It is not a quarrel to be solved by erasing one side. The Arabic was left able to carry both, and both are true in doctrine even if only one is the first syntax.

**Human Self-Witness Is the Harder Reading**

Taking *hu* as man is harder on the listener, which is often a sign that it is the punch the sūrah wants. Man shows his ingratitude. He does it when he complains of every loss and forgets every gift; that complaint is an inverted testimony. He does it more clearly on the Day when *“they will admit their sins”* (67:11). He does it when skins, hands, and tongues testify: *“On the Day their tongues, hands, and feet will testify against them for what they used to do”* (24:24); *“Today We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to commit”* (36:65). Verse 7, on this reading, is not new information for God. It is the end of the human alibi. The ungrateful man is not a mystery to himself at the last.

**Divine Witness Is the More Comforting Syntax**

Taking *hu* as God makes the pronoun match *rabb* in the previous verse more smoothly for some grammarians. God is witness *to that* — to the ingratitude. The line then stands as a threat: your denial does not create a gap in knowledge. *“Allah is never unaware of what you do”* (cf. 2:74 — *“And Allah is never unaware of what you do”*, 2:140 — *“And Allah is never unaware of what you do”*, 2:144 — *“And Allah is never unaware of what they do”*, 2:149 — *“Wherever you are ˹O Prophet˺, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque. This is certainly the truth from your Lord. And Allah is never unaware of what you ˹all˺ do”*). The comfort is only for the one who wants a just court. For the *kanūd* it is none. A witness who cannot be bribed is not good news for a miser of thanks. This reading also avoids making man the star of his own prosecution in this particular sentence, leaving that role for the Day described in verses 9–11. A commentary that picks one reading and hides the other trains people to think tafsīr is a single key. Here the disagreement has grounds. Grammar: what is the nearest masculine singular? *Insān* is nearest; *rabb* is also available. Theology: both God and man can be called *shahīd* in this Book.

## Verse 100:8

> and they are truly extreme in their love of ˹worldly˺ gains

**Love Named as Violence**

*Wa-innahu li-ḥubbi al-khayri la-shadīd* — he is, for the love of *khayr*, intense. *Shadīd* is strong, fierce, violent. The intensity is not a mild preference. It is a grip. *Khayr* in Arabic is “good,” and most commentators here take it as wealth, because wealth is the good people clutch. The translation's “worldly gains” is that tafsīr made visible. The word can still be wider: every created good that a heart treats as a last end. *“The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people. These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination”* (3:14). Verse 8 is 3:14 — *“These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination”* without the catalogue, with the temperature turned up.

**Why Good Becomes a Trap When It Is Loved as Lord**

The Book does not call wealth evil. It calls certain loves evil. *Khayr* remains a good word. The crime is *ḥubb* at the pitch of *shadīd* when the object is not God. Love is measured by what it will spend and what it will refuse. The *kanūd* will spend the horse and refuse the alms. He will spend the night on inventory and refuse the dawn prayer. The intensity is real; only the qiblah of the intensity is wrong. Prophetic teaching later makes this practical: “The son of Adam says, ‘My wealth, my wealth.’ Yet you have no wealth except what you ate and used up, what you wore and wore out, or what you gave in charity and sent ahead” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2958). Verse 8 is the Qur'anic root of that saying.

## Verse 100:9

> Do they not know that when the contents of the graves will be spilled out

**A Question That Is Not Asking for Information**

*A-fa-lā yaʿlamu* — does he not know? The particle *lā* with the interrogative is a rebuke. Of course the resurrection has been told. The ungrateful lover of goods lives as if he does not know. The verb is knowledge, not feeling. The sūrah treats the last day as a fact that should already be in the file, not as a new myth to be sold. *Idhā buʿthira mā fī al-qubūr* — when what is in the graves is scattered, overturned, spilled. *Baʿthara* is to rummage and throw out the contents. Graves are containers. Their contents are people. The image is not a polite rising. It is inversion, the same family as *“when the earth is shaken”* (99:1) and *“when the graves are overturned”* (82:4).

**Apocalyptic Inversion Has a Moral Job**

The Qur'an's last-day pictures often turn insides out: seas, skies, mountains, graves, breasts. The job of the inversion is to end hiding. A grave is the last human try to put a body out of sight. Spilling the grave is the end of that try. The lover of gains used the world as a hiding place for the self. The grave was supposed to be a further hiding. Verse 9 says the hiding places will be emptied like sacks. *“Allah will resurrect those who are in the graves”* (22:7). The wording here is harsher than “resurrect.” It is rummage. Nothing stored stays stored.

**What Lies in Graves Is More Than Bones**

Bodies, yes. Also the record that went to sleep with them. The next verse will open breasts. This verse opens ground. The two openings are one court. Commentators note the parallel with 82:3–4 — *“and when the seas burst forth and when the graves spill out”* and 84:3–4 — *“and when the earth is flattened out and ejects ˹all˺ its contents and becomes empty”*. The present verse stands on its own as the first half of a temporal clause whose main clause is verse 11. Verses 9–11 are one sentence in the Arabic: when graves are spilled and breasts are exposed, their Lord that Day is fully aware of them. Cutting verse 9 off as a mere horror image misses the grammar. The spilling is a *when*, not a spectacle for its own sake. The fierce lover of *khayr* often claims humility: “who can know about the last day?” The verse refuses that pose. Knowledge has already arrived. What is lacking is the use of knowledge. *Yaʿlamu* in the Qur'an can mean know-and-act. The question is therefore: is he living as a knower?

**Spilled Graves and the End of Lineage Pride**

Graves are also family property. Clans keep them, visit them, boast of who lies there. Spilling them equalizes. The dust that horses raised in verse 4 returns as the dust of a more general unburying. Origin from dust, return to dust, then a spilling of dust's containers: the sūrah's earth-images complete a circle. No pile of *khayr* buys a grave that will not open. *“Every soul will taste death”* (3:185) is the general law; this verse is the visual of what follows the tasting. Spilling and overturning are one inversion; 100:9 chooses the rummage-verb because hiding is the habit of the kanūd.

## Verse 100:10

> and the secrets of the hearts will be laid bare—

**Breasts, Not Only Graves**

*Wa-ḥuṣṣila mā fī al-ṣudūr* — and what is in the breasts is collected, brought out, made present. *Ḥaṣṣala* is to gather what was scattered, to extract ore from rock. The translation of this line (100:10) is a fair English for that extraction. *Ṣudūr* are chests, the seat of intention in Arabic phrase. The sūrah has moved from public containers (graves) to private containers (chests). A man might imagine that even if bodies rise, motives could stay hidden. This verse closes that hope. The court is not only about limbs.

*“On the Day all secrets will be disclosed”* (86:9) is the same doctrine in another sūrah. *Al-sāʾir* and *al-ḥāṣil* are different verbs; the event is one. What people hid from neighbours, from spouses, from their own tongues, is sorted. Good is distinguished from bad, as al-Shanqīṭī and others note on this stretch. The true nature of acts is known. God's knowledge of that nature was never missing. *“He knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth, and knows whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal. And Allah is All-Knowing of what is in the hearts”* (64:4). The new thing on that Day is that the human being can no longer hide from himself.

*Ḥaṣṣala* is used of getting a yield from something that does not give it easily. Intentions are mixed. A chest contains fear, show, love, envy, a little *ikhlāṣ*. Extraction is judgment as analysis, not only as punishment. The scales of other sūrahs weigh deeds; this verb suggests a refining. That is hope for the believer whose outer record is small but whose chest held truth, and terror for the hypocrite whose outer record was large. *“On the Day when Allah will not disgrace the Prophet or the believers with him. Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right”* (66:8) shows chests made visible as light. Verse 10 is the general operation of which that light is one possible yield.

## Verse 100:11

> surely their Lord is All-Aware of them on that Day

**Awareness That Becomes Unavoidable**

*Inna rabbahum bihim yawmaʾidhin la-khabīr* — their Lord, about them, on that day, is surely All-Aware. *Khabīr* is knowledge of inner detail, not a vague watchfulness. Dozens of verses already say God is *khabīr* at all times: *“Allah is All-Aware of what you do”* (63:11 in nearby force; cf. 2:234; 3:153; 17:30; 24:30; 27:88). Why add *on that Day*? Because that Day is when the creatures catch up to a knowledge that never lapsed. The limitation being removed is theirs, not His. They become cognizant that He has always been *khabīr*. Self-deception ends. The ungrateful man can no longer narrate his own story as luck, talent, or victimhood. *Rabbahum* repeats the Lord of verse 6. The one they were ungrateful toward is the one who is *khabīr* of them. The pronoun *bihim* — of them, about them — is comprehensive: bodies from graves, secrets from chests, the fierce love, the attestations. Nothing in the portrait of verses 6–8 falls outside the *khabīr*.

**Why This Is the Last Word of the Sūrah**

The chapter does not end with a picture of fire or garden. It ends with awareness. For some listeners that is milder than *al-Qāriʿah*'s scorching fire; it is not milder in fact. To be fully known by the one you refused to thank is a sentence. For the believer it is also a relief: the same *khabīr* sees the hidden *ṣabr* and the unrecorded alms. The sūrah leaves both possibilities inside one adjective. It has already been severe enough in the spilling and the laying bare. The last line is the name of the knowledge under which those operations happen.

**Disbelievers Who Finally Agree**

Other verses show the damned admitting the truth of the warning: *“They will say, ‘Yes, a warner did come to us, but we denied’”* (67:9); keepers of Hell ask and they answer. *Al-ʿĀdiyāt* does not stage that dialogue. It only states the condition that makes the dialogue inevitable: a Lord who is *khabīr* of them that Day. Admission is the human echo of His awareness. Verse 7's self-witness, if that reading is taken, flowers here as public fact.

**From Horses to Knowledge**

The sūrah's arc is now visible as a whole. It began with animals whose interiors were lungs full of *ḍabḥ*, and it ends with humans whose interiors are opened to a Lord. Motion, cost, ingratitude, love of gain, inverted earth, extracted chests, awareness. A short sūrah can hold that arc because it does not pause to decorate. Reciting it as eleven separate slogans is a waste. Reciting it as one argument — costly loyalty in creation, cheap disloyalty in man, a Day when both the ground and the breast give up what they hold — is what the oaths paid for. Khabīr on that Day is not new data for God; it is the end of human ability to live as if the data were private.
