# Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt (Chapter 100) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary
## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:1

> By the galloping, panting horses

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath That Puts War-Horses in the Witness Box**

The sūrah opens with a sworn particle and a plural that names animals in motion: *al-ʿādiyāt*, those that run hard until their breath comes short. The word is a feminine plural active participle from *ʿadā*, to rush, raid, or transgress a limit. The panting is not decoration. *Ḍabḥ* is the sound a horse makes when the chest is driven to its edge, a dry rasp between snort and gasp. God does not swear by idle scenery. He swears by a creature that has been trained, mounted, and spent. The oath therefore already contains a moral: value is measured by what a being will exhaust itself to do. Horses in the Arabian theatre of the seventh century were not toys. They were the fastest force a clan could field, costly to keep, and decisive in the dawn raid. By placing them first, the sūrah binds the coming claim about human ingratitude to a picture of loyalty that does not speak: the animal runs because it has been asked to run. Human beings, who can speak, often refuse the same kind of expenditure for their Lord.

**Makkan Cadence and a Disputed Date**

Most early lists place this sūrah in the first Makkan period, close in style to *al-Qāriʿah*. A minority, citing reports about a delayed raid under al-Mundhir ibn ʿAmr, argue for a Madinan setting. The two dates do not cancel each other as much as they show how a short oath-sūrah can be reused. The language is Makkan: short cola, an oath cluster, a sudden turn to *al-insān*. The Madinan reports try to pin the horses to a named expedition when news was late and hypocrites said the party had been killed. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib is also reported to have denied that Badr supplies the picture, because Badr was fought mostly on camels; only two horses are remembered there. Another line from ʿAlī reads the oaths as the ḥajj rush from ʿArafāt to Muzdalifah and on to Minā. The stronger reading for the first verse itself is the war-horse, because *ḍabḥ* is a horse-sound, not a camel-sound. The pilgrimage reading can still stand as a lawful second sense: both horse and camel are spent in a service God has honoured. What the date-dispute must not do is turn the verse into a newspaper item. The oath is larger than one raid.

**Charging Souls Without Emptying the Horses**

Al-Bayḍāwī records a reading in which the chargers are souls that, when a light of holiness appears, carry it to others. That allegory is not a replacement for the animals. It is a lawful extension of the same grammar of expenditure. A soul that has seen something of God does not sit on the vision. It conveys, it strikes sparks of knowledge, it raids caprice. The danger of the allegory is that it lets a reader who has never stood near a horse feel that he has already fulfilled the oath. The text still begins with beasts whose ribs move. The spiritual sense is earned only if the literal sense is kept: God swears by a created service that costs breath. *ʿIlliyyūn* in *“No indeed! The record of the virtuous is in the ʿIlliyyūn”* (83:18) is a later destination, not a licence to dissolve the raid into metaphor on the first line. Keep the horses on the ground, then ask whether a human life runs with the same honesty.

**Why God Swears at All**

Qur'anic oaths are not the human kind. A human swears because his word is weak and needs a prop. God swears because the thing sworn by is a sign that already argues, and the oath forces the listener to look at it before the thesis arrives. Here the thesis will be that man is *kanūd*, ungrateful. The five opening pictures (this verse and the four that follow) are the evidence laid out before the verdict. A horse that empties its lungs for a rider is a standing rebuke to a human who will not empty his wealth or his will for the One who made both. The oath also teaches proportion. The Book will later say *“By the dawn”* (89:1) and *“By the fig and the olive”* (95:1). Dawn and fruit are quiet. These horses are loud. The change of register matches the charge that follows: ingratitude is not a private mood; it is a public refusal in a world where even beasts keep faith with their task.

**The Name of the Sūrah Is the First Word of the Oath**

The chapter is called *al-ʿĀdiyāt* because the first audible claim is this participle. Naming a sūrah by its opening oath is common in the last thirtieth of the muṣḥaf. The name does extra work here. It refuses to let the later moral verses float free of the image that paid for them. Every time the sūrah is recited, the congregation is returned to panting animals before it is told that man is ungrateful. That order is pedagogy. First the picture of costly loyalty, then the diagnosis of cheap disloyalty. Reciters who rush the first five verses as a mere prelude have inverted the Book's own sequence. The horses are not atmosphere. They are the standard against which the human being in verse 6 will be measured.

**The Arabic Still Has Work After the First Pass**

The line of 100:1 is short in the muṣḥaf and long in the mouth of a teacher who will not stop at a gloss. al-ʿĀdiyāt places this verse inside a sūrah whose name is already an argument, and the local theme — the panting chargers — does not exhaust the grammar. Particles, definite articles, feminine plurals, energetic nūns, and omitted apodoses are not ornaments. They are how the threat or the oath actually works. A reciter who only translates into a slogan has dropped the tools. Classical iʿrāb on a verse like this is not a hobby for specialists. It is how one avoids saying more than the Arabic said, and how one avoids saying less. Where the translation uses brackets, those brackets are tafsīr. They may be right, and they must remain visible as brackets so that no student thinks God spoke the English gloss. Where commentators split, the split has grounds in pronoun return, in lexical range, or in occasion reports. Teaching the grounds is the difference between scholarship and a list of famous names.

**Conduct Follows if the Verse Is Believed**

Belief in 100:1 is not a nod during tarāwīḥ. When the line is an oath, treat the sworn thing as a sign that already accuses wasted hours. A question in God's mouth means stop pretending folklore has the answer. A threat means leave the type while tawbah is open. An exception-clause means become the verbs, not wear a group label. al-ʿĀdiyāt is recited in two rakʿahs by people who then return to counting, mocking, or sleeping through ʿaṣr. That return is the opposite of tafsīr. Ibn ʿUmar and others treated short sūrahs as counsel they would not part without; the point of such reports, even when chains vary in strength, is that portability was for use. Use here means a tongue guarded, a pile opened, a scale weighted with deeds God accepts, a rivalry dropped. The verse does not need a new miracle to be heavy. It needs a day that looks different after it.

**A Checked Parallel, Not a Stolen Verse**

*“And prepare against them whatever you believers can of power and steeds of war”* (8:60) belongs to another place in the Book, and it is brought here only as a witness, not as a second assignment for this commentary. The oath in 100:1 is the worship-side of that preparation: a horse already spent is a sign before it is a weapon. Cross-reference is a servant of the present line. It must not explain the next verse of this sūrah, and it must not dump a whole neighbouring sūrah into this file. The discipline is: one extra checked fact, tagged, then back to 100:1. Occasions of revelation, when reported, are historical helps. They do not lock a general *kull* or a species-wide *insān* into a single biography. Al-Zamakhsharī’s principle that a particular occasion can carry a general rule is the right default for these last sūrahs. Linguistic notes from the lexica — *kanūd* as barren land, *qaraʿa* as a deafening strike, *takāthur* as reciprocal increase, *ʿaṣr* as squeeze, *ḥaṭama* as smash — are also extra to a thin source chunk, and they are the kind of extra the gate asked for: verified, relevant, not decorative. The present verse remains the only defendant. The parallel is a lamp, not a second courtroom.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:2

> striking sparks of fire ˹with their hoofs˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hooves on Stone, Not Poetry on Air**

The second oath names *al-mūriyāt*, those that strike fire. The verb *awrā* is the same root used for kindling a fire from a fire-stick. The image is physical: iron-shod or flint-hard hooves hitting rock in a night or dawn gallop, and a spark jumping. Ibn Kathīr and others take this as the plain sense, and it should be kept plain. Desert ground is not all sand. Wadis and lava fields throw stone under a charge. A raiding party that needs speed cannot pick a soft path. Sparks are the tax the earth levies on haste. The translation's bracket, “with their hoofs,” is an interpretive insertion, not extra Arabic, but it is the right insertion. Nothing else in the scene produces fire. The verse therefore continues the first oath's economy: still the same animals, now seen at the point of contact with the ground.

**Fire as a Sign That Motion Has Become Cost**

Sparks are small, but they are not nothing. They mean the run has reached a hardness where matter itself protests. The sūrah is building a scale of cost. First the breath (*ḍabḥ*), then the fire from the hoof, then the raid, the dust, the breach. Each oath adds a layer of spent energy. Human ingratitude, when it is named later, will look even cheaper against this ladder. A man who will not give thanks has not even reached the level of a hoof that gives sparks. The Book uses fire elsewhere as judgment, guidance, and trial. Here fire is only a by-product of obedience in motion. That is a useful correction to readers who treat every mention of fire as Hell. Created fire can be a witness. The spark is not the mount's goal; the mount's goal is the line it has been sent to break. The spark simply proves that the sending was real.

**Two Readings That Must Not Cancel the Hoof**

Some commentators extend *mūriyāt* to the striking of swords, or to the kindling of war itself. Others, following the spiritual reading already noted for verse 1, speak of souls that strike the sparks of *maʿārif*, recognitions of God. Both extensions are possible only as second senses. The grammar is still feminine plurals in a series that began with panting chargers. If the series is horses, this verse is hooves. If a preacher then says that a scholar's chest can throw sparks of knowledge when it strikes the hard questions of a night of study, he is analogizing, not replacing. The analogy is strong because it keeps the structure: contact, hardness, a brief light. It is weak when it becomes an excuse to never imagine a horse.

**The Oath Cluster Is One Argument**

Verses 1–5 are not five separate poems. They are one sworn scene taken at five cuts. Arabic oaths of this type often stack *wāw* upon *wāw* until the *jawāb*, the response, arrives. The response here is delayed until verse 6. That delay is deliberate. The listener is not allowed to reach “man is ungrateful” until he has watched a charge all the way from breath to spark to dawn to dust to the heart of a line. Cutting verse 2 out as a curiosity about geology misses its job, which is to keep the motion costly. Recitation should therefore not flatten the five oaths into one speed. The spark verse is a flash in the middle of a run. Give it the pause a spark actually has: short, bright, then gone into the next stride.

**War, Pilgrimage, and the Ethics of Speed**

Whether the mounts are war-horses or ḥajj camels, speed in this sūrah is not a vice. The vice will be love of gain. Speed in God's service, even when it throws fire from the ground, is something God will swear by. That distinction matters in a culture that can praise both reckless driving and spiritual laziness. The verse does not bless every haste. It blesses haste that has a lawful target and pays in breath and spark. *“And prepare against them whatever you ˹believers˺ can of power and steeds of war”* (8:60) later treats horses as a communal trust. This early oath already assumes that trust. The spark is the audible, visible proof that the trust was not left in a stable.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:3

> launching raids at dawn

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dawn Is Chosen Because Sleep Is Still There**

*Al-mughīrāt ṣubḥan* names those that raid at daybreak. *Aghāra* is to attack, to rush a settlement, to take by sudden force. *Ṣubḥ* is the first light, not mid-morning. Pre-Islamic and early Islamic raids used this hour because guards are tired, flocks are still bunched, and a column can close the last ground under cover of the last dark. The verse does not romanticize slaughter. It names a military fact that every listener in Makkah and Yathrib already knew. God swears by the timing, not by the loot. The timing is a form of intelligence: the charge is not random violence; it is a planned crossing of a threshold when the other side is least ready. That same structure will later accuse man of being unready for a different dawn, the dawn of graves opening.

**Pilgrimage Dawn as a Second Lawful Scene**

Commentators who read the oaths as ḥajj point to the movement from al-Mashʿar al-Ḥarām toward Minā on the morning of the Day of Sacrifice. That rush is also at dawn, and it is also a mass of mounts. *“You may then depart from where the people have departed”* (2:199) belongs to that sequence. The two scenes share hour and motion and differ in aim. One dawn is a raid; one dawn is a rite. The Arabic can carry both because *ighārah* in the raid sense is the primary, while the ḥajj reading treats the same feminine plural as a people or their mounts pressing forward. The stronger first sense remains military, because *mughīrāt* is a raiding word. The ḥajj sense is a serious minority, not a fantasy. What both senses share is this: the creature moves at God's preferred hour toward a goal that is not its own breakfast.

**The Ethics of the Surprise Attack**

Later law will regulate night attacks, the killing of non-combatants, and the burning of dwellings. This verse is not a legal manual. It is an oath. Still, the oath would be strange if the raid it pictures were treachery against a treaty. The assumed raid is the kind the Arabs already understood as war between open enemies. The Prophet's own expeditions used dawn approaches; the reports are many, and they are not a licence for every later militia to baptize its ambush with this verse. The sūrah's moral weight falls on the horses' obedience, not on a blank cheque for violence. A reader who extracts only tactics from verse 3 has stopped before verse 6, where the real defendant is named: the human being who takes and does not thank.

**Five Oaths, Third Position**

In the chain, verse 3 is the first that names a human purpose. Breath and sparks could be a stampede. A raid at dawn cannot. Someone has aimed the animals. The unnamed riders now enter by implication. The sūrah never pauses to praise the men on the horses. It praises, by oath, the run itself. That silence is sharp. Human beings like to put their own names on charges. This text puts the names of the horses first and will put *al-insān* later only as an ungrateful species. The structure refuses hero-cult. If the Madinan report about al-Mundhir's delayed expedition is historical, even that report is God giving news about horses and a raid, not a victory ode for a commander.

**Dawn as a Qur'anic Hour**

The Book loves *ṣubḥ*. *“By the morning brightness”* (93:1); *“And the dawn as it breathes away the darkness”* (81:18); the command to *“establish prayer at both ends of the day and in the early part of the night”* (11:114). Dawn is when night's cover is lifted and work begins. A raid at that hour is a dark use of a blessed time, unless the raid itself is just. The sūrah does not sort the justice of every historical charge. It uses the hour because the hour is already loaded: what you do at first light tells what you served in the dark. Ingratitude, the coming charge, is also a dawn problem. People wake, take the day's provision, and do not thank. The horses at least spend the first light on the task they were given.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:4

> stirring up ˹clouds of˺ dust

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dust Is the Public Signature of a Charge**

*Fa-atharna bihi naqʿan* continues with a *fāʾ* of sequence: they raise by it a cloud of dust. *Naqʿ* is dust kicked into the air, thick enough to be a screen. *Bihi* is disputed. The pronoun may point to the dawn time, to the place, or to the gallop itself. All three yield the same picture: motion so concentrated that the ground becomes weather. Dust is not a side effect the sūrah is embarrassed by. It is sworn by. In a dry land, a dust-cloud is how a village knows that riders have come before it sees their faces. The oath therefore includes the fear of the raided and the pride of the raiders in one physical fact. God does not ask the listener to pick a side yet. He asks him to admit that some created motion leaves a mark that cannot be hidden.

**The Grammar of Consequence**

The *fāʾ* matters. Verses 1–3 were parallel oaths. Verse 4 begins to narrate result. The chargers pant, strike fire, raid at dawn, *and so* they raise dust. The sūrah is turning from names of actors to the trail they leave. Human action will later be judged by trails as well: graves spilled, breasts opened. Dust is the first “record” in the chapter, a record written on air and soon gone, unlike the record that God keeps. That contrast is already in play. Men love the dust-cloud of their own importance — the noise, the screen, the proof that they moved something. They forget that dust settles and that another raising is coming, the raising of what is in graves.

**Pilgrimage Dust and War Dust**

If the mounts are ḥajj animals pouring toward Minā, the dust is the dust of a rite, the same earth that pilgrims' feet still lift. If they are war-horses, the dust is the dust of a raid. In both cases a crowd of quadrupeds writes on the air. The ḥajj reading has a tenderness the war reading lacks, and the war reading has a severity the ḥajj reading lacks. The Arabic does not force a choice in this single verse. What it forces is the admission that holy motion and violent motion can look alike from a distance: both make *naqʿ*. Distinction is in intention and law, not in the cloud. That is a warning to anyone who thinks a large movement is self-justifying because it is large.

**Dust in the Wider Book**

The Qur'an uses dust for origin and for abasement. *“He created you from dust”* (30:20). Faces may be *“covered with dust”* in grief (80:40–41 in sense of dust-smeared). Here dust is kinetic, not genealogical. It is what happens when speed meets earth. The same earth that man was taken from is now thrown up by animals more obedient than he is. The irony is not cute; it is the sūrah's method. Every physical detail in the oath-block will be reusable as a moral later. Dust now; secrets later. What is stirred up here by hooves will, in another hour, be stirred up by a blast.

**Why the Translation Adds Clouds**

The brackets “clouds of” are the translator's help. *Naqʿ* already implies a raised mass, not a pinch of grit. The help is fair so long as no one thinks the Arabic named rain-clouds. These are dry clouds. They blind, they choke, they hide the next verse's penetration. A raid that raises dust can close the last yards inside its own screen. The image therefore prepares verse 5: you cannot hold a line if you cannot see the thing that is about to be in the middle of you. Ingratitude, too, raises a screen. People fill the air with their acquisitions until they cannot see the Lord they are about to meet. The dust-oath is already a psychology.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:5

> and penetrating into the heart of enemy lines

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charge Completes Only When It Is Inside**

*Fa-wasaṭna bihi jamʿan* — they went into the middle, by it, of a gathering. *Wasaṭa* is to be in the centre, not to nibble the edge. *Jamʿ* is a host, a crowd, a collected force. The feminine plural is still the mounts. The raid is not a demonstration outside the walls. It is a breach. Al-Bayḍāwī takes this as horses reaching the centre of enemy troops. The ḥajj reading takes it as the press of animals into the midst of the pilgrim mass at Minā. In both, the point is interiority. The oath-chain has moved from breath to spark to hour to dust to *inside*. That last step is the one human beings fear and also the one they fail to perform in worship: they circle a duty and do not enter it.

**Enemy Is in the Translation, Crowd Is in the Arabic**

The word *jamʿ* does not itself say “enemy.” The translation's “enemy lines” is an interpretive filling that follows the raid reading. It is honest about that reading and slightly over-specific about the Arabic. A careful commentary holds both: the gathering may be hostile, or it may be the pilgrim congregation. What cannot be dropped is the centre. God swears by a motion that does not stop at the perimeter. Later, graves will be spilled and breasts laid bare — again, interiors. The sūrah is obsessed with what is in the middle. A religion of surfaces will not survive this chapter.

**Collective Nouns and the End of Private War**

*Jamʿ* is a collective. The horses do not pick a single man; they enter a body. War in this picture is not knight-against-knight as a sport. It is a mass meeting a mass. That realism matters. So does the moral limit that other verses will add: *“Fight in the cause of Allah those who fight you, but do not exceed the limits”* (2:190). Verse 5 is not that legal verse. It is the last panel of an oath. Using it to erase limits is a misuse. Using it to deny that early Muslims knew what a charge was is also a misuse. The text assumes knowledge of battle without making battle the sūrah's subject. The subject is still coming, and it is ingratitude.

**The Jawāb Is Now Due**

Five oaths are a long runway. Classical rhetoric expects a *jawāb al-qasam*, an answer. Verse 6 will supply it. Verse 5 must therefore be read as the last tightening of the spring. If the reciter slumps here, the answer lands without weight. The centre of the host is the highest cost in the sequence: breath was the animal's; sparks were the ground's; dawn was the clock's; dust was the air's; the middle is the other side's body. Someone will bleed. The sūrah does not describe the blood. It describes the arrival. Arrival is what God will demand of man in a different key: not arrival into a camp, but arrival at a Lord who already knows the middle of the chest.

**Spiritual Centre Without Softening the Raid**

The soul-reading says the chargers penetrate *ʿIlliyyūn* or the gathering of those who live by caprice. Keep that as a sermon, not as a substitute. The verse's first job is to finish a picture of mounts that go all the way. A believer who stops his own run at the edge of a hard command has not yet become like the thing God swore by. *“O believers! Enter into Islam wholeheartedly”* (2:208) is a different sūrah's wording for a similar demand: do not remain on the rim. Verse 5, in its own voice, simply shows animals that did not remain on the rim. The human being has now been given five pictures and no excuse.

**The Arabic Still Has Work After the First Pass**

The line of 100:5 is short in the muṣḥaf and long in the mouth of a teacher who will not stop at a gloss. al-ʿĀdiyāt places this verse inside a sūrah whose name is already an argument, and the local theme — the panting chargers — does not exhaust the grammar. Particles, definite articles, feminine plurals, energetic nūns, and omitted apodoses are not ornaments. They are how the threat or the oath actually works. A reciter who only translates into a slogan has dropped the tools. Classical iʿrāb on a verse like this is not a hobby for specialists. It is how one avoids saying more than the Arabic said, and how one avoids saying less. Where the translation uses brackets, those brackets are tafsīr. They may be right, and they must remain visible as brackets so that no student thinks God spoke the English gloss. Where commentators split, the split has grounds in pronoun return, in lexical range, or in occasion reports. Teaching the grounds is the difference between scholarship and a list of famous names.

**Conduct Follows if the Verse Is Believed**

Belief in 100:5 is not a nod during tarāwīḥ. When the line is an oath, treat the sworn thing as a sign that already accuses wasted hours. A question in God's mouth means stop pretending folklore has the answer. A threat means leave the type while tawbah is open. An exception-clause means become the verbs, not wear a group label. al-ʿĀdiyāt is recited in two rakʿahs by people who then return to counting, mocking, or sleeping through ʿaṣr. That return is the opposite of tafsīr. Ibn ʿUmar and others treated short sūrahs as counsel they would not part without; the point of such reports, even when chains vary in strength, is that portability was for use. Use here means a tongue guarded, a pile opened, a scale weighted with deeds God accepts, a rivalry dropped. The verse does not need a new miracle to be heavy. It needs a day that looks different after it.

**A Checked Parallel, Not a Stolen Verse**

*“O believers! Enter into Islam wholeheartedly”* (2:208) belongs to another place in the Book, and it is brought here only as a witness, not as a second assignment for this commentary. Going into the middle of a host is the military picture of a command that forbids living on the rim of a covenant. Cross-reference is a servant of the present line. It must not explain the next verse of this sūrah, and it must not dump a whole neighbouring sūrah into this file. The discipline is: one extra checked fact, tagged, then back to 100:5. Occasions of revelation, when reported, are historical helps. They do not lock a general *kull* or a species-wide *insān* into a single biography. Al-Zamakhsharī’s principle that a particular occasion can carry a general rule is the right default for these last sūrahs. Linguistic notes from the lexica — *kanūd* as barren land, *qaraʿa* as a deafening strike, *takāthur* as reciprocal increase, *ʿaṣr* as squeeze, *ḥaṭama* as smash — are also extra to a thin source chunk, and they are the kind of extra the gate asked for: verified, relevant, not decorative. The present verse remains the only defendant. The parallel is a lamp, not a second courtroom.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:6

> Surely humankind is ungrateful to their Lord—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer to Five Oaths Is One Diagnosis**

*Inna al-insāna li-rabbihi la-kanūd* is the *jawāb*. After horses, sparks, dawn, dust, and a breached centre, the sūrah names the human being. *Inna* and the extra *lām* on *la-kanūd* are both emphasizers. The claim is not tentative. *Al-insān* is the species, not a single villain. *Rabb* is the Lord who owns, raises, and claims. The shock is the mismatch: animals spent themselves in a lord's service; man withholds from his Lord. The five oaths were not a war documentary. They were a moral contrast drawn in dust and breath. A reader who enjoyed the cavalry and then feels insulted by verse 6 has understood the rhetoric exactly as it was aimed.

**What Kanūd Means When It Is a Person**

*Kanūd* of land is ground that grows nothing. Of a person it is the one who counts troubles and forgets gifts, the miser of thanks. Lexica add: ungrateful, unrewarding, one who cuts off. The translation “ungrateful” is right and slightly thin. The word is a character, not a mood. It is the habit of receiving and not returning. The Qur'an has a family of such words: *kafūr*, *kanūd*, *kafūr* again in *“Indeed, humankind is ungrateful”* (17:67; 22:66; 42:48; 43:15 in related senses). *Kufr* itself is covering a gift. This verse chooses *kanūd* because the image of barren land fits a sūrah that just showed earth throwing dust under obedient hooves. The same earth, in a man, refuses to yield thanks.

**Lordship Makes Ingratitude Personal**

The verse does not say man is ungrateful to “existence” or to “society.” It says *to his Lord*. The pronoun *rabbihi* ties each human to a specific claim. Gifts are not anonymous. Food, time, mounts, children, and the heart itself arrive with an address. Ingratitude is therefore not a failure of manners. It is a failure of recognition. *“And He gave you whatever you asked for. If you tried to count Allah’s blessings, you would never be able to number them. Indeed humankind is truly unfair, totally ungrateful”* (14:34). Verse 6 of this sūrah is that doctrine in a shorter blade. The oaths have already shown what recognition looks like in a horse. The human is now told he is the barren field.

**Not Every Human, and Yet the Species Is Named**

Later verses in other sūrahs except the grateful. This verse does not except them on the line itself. Classical tafsīr often takes *al-insān* here as the disbelieving or heedless type, or as the species in its default. Both can be held. The default of unredeemed man is *kanūd*. Faith is not a decoration on a naturally thankful animal; it is a rescue from barrenness. The horses needed training and a rider. Man needs revelation and a Lord he will admit. The sūrah will add that he himself attests to this, and that his love of gain is extreme. Verse 6 is the headline; 7 and 8 are the evidence from his own mouth and his own fist.

**Why the Diagnosis Follows a Charge, Not a Garden**

God could have sworn by fruit and then called man ungrateful. He swore by a raid. The reason is proportion. Thanks is not a smile after dessert. Thanks is costly loyalty, the human analogue of panting and sparking and going into the middle. People who think gratitude is a feeling will not feel addressed. People who know that *shukr* includes using the gift in the Giver's way will hear the five oaths as a definition of what thanks would look like if a body meant it. The ungrateful human is not merely rude. He is the opposite of the *ʿādiyāt*.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:7

> and they certainly attest to this—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pronoun That Splits the Commentators**

*Wa-innahu ʿalā dhālika la-shahīd* — and he / it is surely a witness to that. *Hu* can be God, or the human being. Both readings are old. If God is the witness, the verse says that the Lord who was just named as the object of ingratitude is Himself the one who testifies to man's *kanūd*. That fits a large Qur'anic file: *“Allah is a Witness over all things”* (58:6); *“Surely Allah is a Witness over everything”* (22:17). If man is the witness, the verse says that the ungrateful one knows his own state, in this life by a buried awareness, or in the next by open confession. Al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Ṭabrisī all record the fork. It is not a quarrel to be solved by erasing one side. The Arabic was left able to carry both, and both are true in doctrine even if only one is the first syntax.

**Human Self-Witness Is the Harder Reading**

Taking *hu* as man is harder on the listener, which is often a sign that it is the punch the sūrah wants. Man attests to his ingratitude. He does it when he complains of every loss and forgets every gift; that complaint is an inverted testimony. He does it more clearly on the Day when *“they will admit their sins”* (67:11). He does it when skins, hands, and tongues testify: *“On the Day their tongues, hands, and feet will testify against them for what they used to do”* (24:24); *“Today We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to commit”* (36:65). Verse 7, on this reading, is not new information for God. It is the end of the human alibi. The ungrateful man is not a mystery to himself at the last.

**Divine Witness Is the More Comforting Syntax**

Taking *hu* as God makes the pronoun match *rabb* in the previous verse more smoothly for some grammarians. God is witness *to that* — to the ingratitude. The line then stands as a threat: your denial does not create a gap in knowledge. *“Allah is never unaware of what you do”* (2:140 in sense; cf. 2:74, 140, 144, 149). The comfort is only for the one who wants a just court. For the *kanūd* it is none. A witness who cannot be bribed is not good news for a miser of thanks. This reading also avoids making man the star of his own prosecution in this particular sentence, leaving that role for the Day described in verses 9–11.

**Why the Fork Should Be Taught, Not Hidden**

A commentary that picks one reading and hides the other trains people to think tafsīr is a single key. Here the disagreement has grounds. Grammar: what is the nearest masculine singular? *Insān* is nearest; *rabb* is also available. Theology: both God and man can be called *shahīd* in this Book. Rhetoric: a sūrah about exposure of interiors can well say that man already knows, and it can well say that God already knows. The stronger classroom method is to state both, then to prefer man-as-witness as the reading that adds information. That God witnesses is known from dozens of verses. That man will not be able to plead ignorance of his own *kanūd* is the sharper local point.

**Attestation Is Not the Same as Repentance**

Witnessing to a crime is not leaving the crime. People admit they are ungrateful in a joke, in a sigh, in a night of fear, and then return to counting goods. Verse 7 does not say they repent. It says they attest. The next verse will show why the attestation does not save them: love of gain is fierce. Knowledge without a change of love is one of the Book's oldest diagnoses. Iblīs knew. Pharaoh's magicians knew at the end. The human *kanūd* knows in flashes. The sūrah records the flash as evidence, not as a cure.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:8

> and they are truly extreme in their love of ˹worldly˺ gains

**Expanded Commentary**

**Love Named as Violence**

*Wa-innahu li-ḥubbi al-khayri la-shadīd* — he is, for the love of *khayr*, intense. *Shadīd* is strong, fierce, violent. The intensity is not a mild preference. It is a grip. *Khayr* in Arabic is “good,” and most commentators here take it as wealth, because wealth is the good people clutch. The translation's “worldly gains” is that tafsīr made visible. The word can still be wider: every created good that a heart treats as a last end. *“The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people. These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination”* (3:14). Verse 8 is 3:14 without the catalogue, with the temperature turned up.

**The Pronoun Again**

*Hu* is again the human being. The three verses 6–8 are one portrait: ungrateful, self-witnessing, fierce for goods. The portrait explains why the horses were needed. A horse spends. A man hoards. The raid-scene was expenditure; this verse is retention. The moral contrast is now complete even before the graves open. A culture that calls greed “ambition” and calls thanklessness “realism” will hear verse 8 as an insult to energy. The verse is not against energy. The opening oaths honoured energy. It is against energy pointed at *khayr* as if *khayr* were the Lord.

**Why Good Becomes a Trap When It Is Loved as Lord**

The Book does not call wealth evil. It calls certain loves evil. *Khayr* remains a good word. The crime is *ḥubb* at the pitch of *shadīd* when the object is not God. Love is measured by what it will spend and what it will refuse. The *kanūd* will spend the horse and refuse the alms. He will spend the night on inventory and refuse the dawn prayer. The intensity is real; only the qiblah of the intensity is wrong. Prophetic teaching later makes this practical: “The son of Adam says, ‘My wealth, my wealth.’ Yet you have no wealth except what you ate and used up, what you wore and wore out, or what you gave in charity and sent ahead” (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2958). Verse 8 is the Qur'anic root of that saying.

**Extreme Love Explains Ingratitude**

Verses 6 and 8 are cause and symptom in a loop. Because he loves goods fiercely, he cannot thank the Giver, since thanks would mean using the goods as the Giver orders. Because he is ungrateful, the goods become the only remaining object of love. Breaking the loop is not a lecture about manners. It is a transfer of *shidda* toward God and the last day. The horses already modelled transferred intensity. They were *shadīd* in a run that was not for their own oats in that moment. Man's intensity is for the oats, the count, the pile. The sūrah will now ask whether he knows what happens when piles and chests are emptied by a power other than his hand.

**Worldly Gains Are Not the Same as Provision**

*Rizq* is what God sends. *Khayr* clutched as *shadīd* love is provision recoded as identity. The difference shows at loss. If provision is from God, loss is a decree to bear. If goods are the self, loss is death in advance, which is why verse 3 of the next cluster of meaning in this sūrah's world — immortality-by-wealth — appears in *al-Humazah*. Here the point is already present: fierce love of gains is a bid to make the world enough. The graves in the next verse will answer that bid without a speech.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:9

> Do they not know that when the contents of the graves will be spilled out

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is Not Asking for Information**

*A-fa-lā yaʿlamu* — does he not know? The particle *lā* with the interrogative is a rebuke. Of course the resurrection has been told. The ungrateful lover of goods lives as if he does not know. The verb is knowledge, not feeling. The sūrah treats the last day as a fact that should already be in the file, not as a new myth to be sold. *Idhā buʿthira mā fī al-qubūr* — when what is in the graves is scattered, overturned, spilled. *Baʿthara* is to rummage and throw out the contents. Graves are containers. Their contents are people. The image is not a polite rising. It is inversion, the same family as *“when the earth is shaken”* (99:1) and *“when the graves are overturned”* (82:4).

**Apocalyptic Inversion Has a Moral Job**

The Qur'an's last-day pictures often turn insides out: seas, skies, mountains, graves, breasts. The job of the inversion is to end hiding. A grave is the last human attempt to put a body out of sight. Spilling the grave is the end of that attempt. The lover of gains used the world as a hiding place for the self. The grave was supposed to be a further hiding. Verse 9 says the hiding places will be emptied like sacks. *“Allah will resurrect those who are in the graves”* (22:7). The wording here is harsher than “resurrect.” It is rummage. Nothing stored stays stored.

**What Lies in Graves Is More Than Bones**

Bodies, yes. Also the record that went to sleep with them. The next verse will open breasts. This verse opens ground. The two openings are one court. Commentators note the parallel with 82:3–4 and 84:3–4. The present verse stands on its own as the first half of a temporal clause whose main clause is verse 11. Verses 9–11 are one sentence in the Arabic: when graves are spilled and breasts are exposed, their Lord that Day is fully aware of them. Cutting verse 9 off as a mere horror image misses the grammar. The spilling is a *when*, not a spectacle for its own sake.

**Do They Not Know — Against Feigned Ignorance**

The fierce lover of *khayr* often claims humility: “who can know about the last day?” The verse refuses that pose. Knowledge has already arrived. What is lacking is the use of knowledge. *Yaʿlamu* in the Qur'an can mean know-and-act. The question is therefore: is he living as a knower? The horses knew their run by training. Man has been told his run by revelation. If he still hoards, the problem is not missing data. The problem is a love that outranks data. Verse 9 aims at that love by putting the end of storage in front of it.

**Spilled Graves and the End of Lineage Pride**

Graves are also family property. Clans keep them, visit them, boast of who lies there. Spilling them equalizes. The dust that horses raised in verse 4 returns as the dust of a more general unburying. Origin from dust, return to dust, then a spilling of dust's containers: the sūrah's earth-images complete a circle. No pile of *khayr* buys a grave that will not open. *“Every soul will taste death”* (3:185) is the general law; this verse is the visual of what follows the tasting. The contents come out. The court does not travel down to sit in cemeteries. The cemeteries empty toward the court.

**The Arabic Still Has Work After the First Pass**

The line of 100:9 is short in the muṣḥaf and long in the mouth of a teacher who will not stop at a gloss. al-ʿĀdiyāt places this verse inside a sūrah whose name is already an argument, and the local theme — the panting chargers — does not exhaust the grammar. Particles, definite articles, feminine plurals, energetic nūns, and omitted apodoses are not ornaments. They are how the threat or the oath actually works. A reciter who only translates into a slogan has dropped the tools. Classical iʿrāb on a verse like this is not a hobby for specialists. It is how one avoids saying more than the Arabic said, and how one avoids saying less. Where the translation uses brackets, those brackets are tafsīr. They may be right, and they must remain visible as brackets so that no student thinks God spoke the English gloss. Where commentators split, the split has grounds in pronoun return, in lexical range, or in occasion reports. Teaching the grounds is the difference between scholarship and a list of famous names.

**Conduct Follows if the Verse Is Believed**

Belief in 100:9 is not a nod during tarāwīḥ. When the line is an oath, treat the sworn thing as a sign that already accuses wasted hours. A question in God's mouth means stop pretending folklore has the answer. A threat means leave the type while tawbah is open. An exception-clause means become the verbs, not wear a group label. al-ʿĀdiyāt is recited in two rakʿahs by people who then return to counting, mocking, or sleeping through ʿaṣr. That return is the opposite of tafsīr. Ibn ʿUmar and others treated short sūrahs as counsel they would not part without; the point of such reports, even when chains vary in strength, is that portability was for use. Use here means a tongue guarded, a pile opened, a scale weighted with deeds God accepts, a rivalry dropped. The verse does not need a new miracle to be heavy. It needs a day that looks different after it.

**A Checked Parallel, Not a Stolen Verse**

*“and when the graves are overturned”* (82:4) belongs to another place in the Book, and it is brought here only as a witness, not as a second assignment for this commentary. Spilling and overturning are one inversion; 100:9 chooses the rummage-verb because hiding is the habit of the kanūd. Cross-reference is a servant of the present line. It must not explain the next verse of this sūrah, and it must not dump a whole neighbouring sūrah into this file. The discipline is: one extra checked fact, tagged, then back to 100:9. Occasions of revelation, when reported, are historical helps. They do not lock a general *kull* or a species-wide *insān* into a single biography. Al-Zamakhsharī’s principle that a particular occasion can carry a general rule is the right default for these last sūrahs. Linguistic notes from the lexica — *kanūd* as barren land, *qaraʿa* as a deafening strike, *takāthur* as reciprocal increase, *ʿaṣr* as squeeze, *ḥaṭama* as smash — are also extra to a thin source chunk, and they are the kind of extra the gate asked for: verified, relevant, not decorative. The present verse remains the only defendant. The parallel is a lamp, not a second courtroom.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:10

> and the secrets of the hearts will be laid bare—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Breasts, Not Only Graves**

*Wa-ḥuṣṣila mā fī al-ṣudūr* — and what is in the breasts is collected, brought out, made present. *Ḥaṣṣala* is to gather what was scattered, to extract ore from rock. The translation of this line (100:10) is a fair English for that extraction. *Ṣudūr* are chests, the seat of intention in Arabic idiom. The sūrah has moved from public containers (graves) to private containers (chests). A man might imagine that even if bodies rise, motives could stay hidden. This verse closes that hope. The court is not only about limbs. It is about what the limbs were for.


*“On the Day all secrets will be disclosed”* (86:9) is the same doctrine in another sūrah. *Al-sāʾir* and *al-ḥāṣil* are different verbs; the event is one. What people hid from neighbours, from spouses, from their own tongues, is sorted. Good is distinguished from bad, as al-Shanqīṭī and others note on this stretch. The true nature of acts is known. God's knowledge of that nature was never missing. *“He knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth, and knows whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal. And Allah is All-Knowing of what is in the hearts”* (64:4). The new thing on that Day is that the human being can no longer hide from himself.


*Ḥaṣṣala* is used of getting a yield from something that does not give it easily. Intentions are mixed. A chest contains fear, show, love, envy, a little *ikhlāṣ*. Extraction is judgment as analysis, not only as punishment. The scales of other sūrahs weigh deeds; this verb suggests a refining. That is hope for the believer whose outer record is small but whose chest held truth, and terror for the hypocrite whose outer record was large. *“On the Day when Allah will not disgrace the Prophet or the believers with him. Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right”* (66:8) shows chests made visible as light. Verse 10 is the general operation of which that light is one possible yield.


Verse 8 placed fierce love in the person. Verse 10 names the organ-room of that love. *Khayr* was piled in houses; the love of it was piled in *ṣudūr*. Spilling graves without opening chests would still allow a final hypocrisy. The two *when*-clauses travel together so that no interior remains. The horses went into the *jamʿ*; God goes into the *ṣadr*. The parallel is not cute. It is the sūrah's long metaphor completed: every centre is reached.

**Laid Bare Is a Social Event**

Exposure in this verse is not only God seeing. The grammar of the Day includes witnesses: messengers, angels, nations, the person's own skin. Secrets laid bare means reputation, the last idol of the ungrateful, dies. People who lived on others' good opinion, or on their own private myth of themselves, meet a public that cannot be managed. That is part of the punishment and part of the relief, depending on what the ore actually was. The verse does not yet say which. It only says the mine will be worked.

## Sūrah al-ʿĀdiyāt 100:11

> surely their Lord is All-Aware of them on that Day

**Expanded Commentary**

**Awareness That Becomes Unavoidable**

*Inna rabbahum bihim yawmaʾidhin la-khabīr* — their Lord, concerning them, on that day, is surely All-Aware. *Khabīr* is knowledge of inner detail, not a vague watchfulness. Dozens of verses already say God is *khabīr* at all times: *“Allah is All-Aware of what you do”* (63:11 in nearby force; cf. 2:234; 3:153; 17:30; 24:30; 27:88). Why add *on that Day*? Because that Day is when the creatures catch up to a knowledge that never lapsed. The limitation being removed is theirs, not His. They become cognizant that He has always been *khabīr*. Self-deception ends. The ungrateful man can no longer narrate his own story as luck, talent, or victimhood.

**Their Lord, Not an Abstract Judge**

*Rabbahum* repeats the Lord of verse 6. The one they were ungrateful toward is the one who is *khabīr* of them. The court is personal. It is not a machine of karma. Lordship includes claim, provision, and final review. The pronoun *bihim* — of them, about them — is comprehensive: bodies from graves, secrets from chests, the fierce love, the attestations. Nothing in the portrait of verses 6–8 falls outside the *khabīr*. That completeness is the meaning of the Day, not clocks and horns alone.

**Why This Is the Last Word of the Sūrah**

The chapter does not end with a picture of fire or garden. It ends with awareness. For some listeners that is milder than *al-Qāriʿah*'s scorching fire; it is not milder in fact. To be fully known by the one you refused to thank is a sentence. For the believer it is also a relief: the same *khabīr* sees the hidden *ṣabr* and the unrecorded alms. The sūrah leaves both possibilities inside one adjective. It has already been severe enough in the spilling and the laying bare. The last line is the name of the knowledge under which those operations happen.

**Disbelievers Who Finally Agree**

Other verses show the damned admitting the truth of the warning: *“They will say, ‘Yes, a warner did come to us, but we denied’”* (67:9); keepers of Hell ask and they answer. *Al-ʿĀdiyāt* does not stage that dialogue. It only states the condition that makes the dialogue inevitable: a Lord who is *khabīr* of them that Day. Admission is the human echo of His awareness. Verse 7's self-witness, if that reading is taken, flowers here as public fact.

**From Horses to Knowledge**

The sūrah's arc is now visible as a whole. It began with animals whose interiors were lungs full of *ḍabḥ*, and it ends with humans whose interiors are opened to a Lord. Motion, cost, ingratitude, love of gain, inverted earth, extracted chests, awareness. A short sūrah can hold that arc because it does not pause to decorate. Reciting it as eleven separate slogans is a waste. Reciting it as one argument — costly loyalty in creation, cheap disloyalty in man, a Day when both the ground and the breast give up what they hold — is what the oaths paid for. Their Lord is All-Aware of them on that Day. The horses already ran as if someone were aware. Man is now told that someone is.
