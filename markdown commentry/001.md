# Sūrah al-Fātiḥah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

Al-Fātiḥah, “The Opening,” is the first sūrah in the written Qur’an, although it was not the first revelation chronologically. It is also called *Umm al-Kitāb* and *Umm al-Qur’ān* (the Mother of the Book and the Mother of the Qur’an), and *al-Sabʿ al-Mathānī* (the seven oft-repeated verses), a name connected with Allah’s words: “We have certainly given you the seven oft-repeated verses and the great Qur’an” (15:87). It is recited in every unit of the formal prayer, and a well-known ḥadīth qudsī in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim calls the prayer a division between Allah and His servant because the worshipper answers the praise and petitions of this sūrah.

Most early scholars regarded al-Fātiḥah as an early Makkan revelation. A report that it was revealed again in Madinah led some scholars to say that it was sent down twice, but all Muslims agree on its central place in prayer. Before the detailed laws of the Madinan period, it taught the first believers the basic relationship between the Creator and His servants: Allah is the Lord of every world, the source of mercy, and the Master of the Day of Recompense; human beings worship Him, depend on Him, and ask Him to keep them on the right path.

The sūrah moves from praise to prayer. Its opening verses name Allah and describe His lordship and mercy. The fourth verse turns the reader toward the Hereafter, and the fifth changes from speaking about Allah to speaking directly to Him: “You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.” The final verses make the central request—to be guided along the path of those who received Allah’s favor, not the path of knowing rejection or confused wandering. The plural pronouns are important: even when praying alone, a Muslim asks as part of the community of believers.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

The *basmalah* begins 113 sūrahs; Sūrah al-Tawbah is the only one that does not begin with it. The phrase also appears within the Qur’an in Sulaymān’s letter to the Queen of Sheba (27:30). Early Muslims differed over whether it is a verse of every sūrah it heads or a divinely given separator between sūrahs. Anas ibn Mālik reported that the Prophet ﷺ, Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, and ʿUthmān began their audible recitation with “All praise belongs to Allah,” while Abū Hurayrah recited the *basmalah* aloud and said that his prayer most closely followed the Prophet’s prayer. The schools of law therefore differ over whether it is recited aloud, quietly, or as a verse within al-Fātiḥah, while all regard it as blessed and significant.

The preposition *bi* in *bismillāh* means “in” or “by.” Arabic grammar expects an accompanying verb, such as “I begin,” “I read,” or “I seek help,” but the verb is left unspoken. This makes the formula broad: a person can begin eating, studying, travelling, writing, or working by placing that act under Allah’s name and seeking His help. The omission also teaches that no good action is truly independent of Him.

*Allāh* is the unique proper name of the Creator. The pagan Arabs knew the name and admitted that Allah created the heavens and earth (see 29:61 and 43:87), but they associated other beings with Him in worship. Whether the name is understood as an underived name or as a form related to *al-ilāh* (“the God”), its meaning in the Qur’an is exclusive: no created being can share Allah’s divinity. Other names may be used in a limited human sense—someone may be called merciful or a king—but “Allah” belongs to Him alone.

*Al-Raḥmān* and *al-Raḥīm* both come from the root connected with mercy. The first conveys mercy that is vast and encompassing; the second points to mercy that reaches and cares for its recipients. The Qur’an often joins them to show that Allah’s mercy is not merely an abstract quality: it is active in creation, provision, forgiveness, guidance, and the special care given to those who believe. Beginning every sūrah except one with these names teaches the reader to approach revelation with hope as well as awe.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

*Al-ḥamd* is more than a thank-you for a particular favour. It is praise for excellence and perfection, joined with gratitude for the good Allah gives. Praise ultimately belongs to Him because every blessing comes from Him and every perfection in creation is dependent on His perfection. The Prophet ﷺ taught that a servant’s words of praise are precious, and the Qur’an itself begins with this praise rather than with a request.

“Allah” is the One being praised, and *Rabb* means Lord, Owner, Master, and Sustainer. It includes the meaning of nurturing something through its stages until it reaches its proper development. Allah does not merely create the universe and leave it: He gives it order, maintains it, provides for it, and governs its affairs. The servant who says “Lord of all worlds” acknowledges both Allah’s authority and his own continuing dependence.

The word *ʿālamīn* is plural. It includes every realm and kind of created being—human beings, jinn, angels, animals, the visible heavens and earth, and realities beyond our sight. Some early commentators said that every group of created things is a world in its own right. The point is not to make us catalogue the unseen, but to prevent the narrow idea that Allah is the Lord of one people only. He is Lord of every nation and every existence.

Praise is placed before the requests that follow. This is good manners in supplication, but it is also a correction to the human tendency to notice needs before noticing gifts. The believer starts by recognizing who Allah is and what He has already given. The phrase is also suitable whenever a blessing ends: just as the *basmalah* marks a beginning, “all praise is for Allah” acknowledges the Giver at every conclusion. Even when life contains difficulty, Allah remains worthy of praise for His wisdom, justice, and sustaining mercy.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:3

> The Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

This verse repeats the two names already present in the *basmalah*. The repetition is not needless. Verse 1 teaches the worshipper to begin in Allah’s name and verse 2 identifies Him as Lord of all worlds; verse 3 keeps mercy before the heart before the sūrah mentions judgment. The repetition assures the servant that the Lord whose power reaches everything is also the Lord whose mercy surrounds and cares for His creation.

The two names have one root but different shades of meaning. *Al-Raḥmān* points to an immense, encompassing mercy that reaches all creatures in this life. Every creature receives existence, provision, opportunities, and countless unearned gifts. *Al-Raḥīm* points to mercy that is continually bestowed, and especially to Allah’s saving, guiding, and forgiving mercy toward those who turn to Him. The names should not be reduced to a simple division—Allah’s mercy is one reality—but their pairing reveals its breadth and its particular care.

The Qur’an says that Allah has prescribed mercy for Himself (6:12, 6:54), and that His mercy encompasses all things (7:156). This does not mean that justice disappears. Rather, mercy is the believer’s starting point and the reason repentance remains open. The Prophet ﷺ also taught that Allah divided mercy into a hundred parts, sending only one part into the world and reserving ninety-nine parts for the Day of Resurrection (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Even the affection of a mother for her child is presented as a small sign of that vast mercy.

Mercy in the Qur’an is not permission to ignore wrongdoing. It includes guidance, warning, correction, and forgiveness, just as it includes provision and comfort. The servant therefore does not become careless because Allah is merciful, nor despair because Allah judges. Al-Fātiḥah places the names of mercy immediately before the declaration of the Day of Recompense so that fear of accountability is held together with hope in the One who will judge.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment

The accepted recitations include *Mālik* (“Master” or “Owner”) and *Malik* (“King” or “Sovereign”). Both meanings are authentically transmitted and both are true. A king may rule, while an owner possesses; Allah is both the absolute Sovereign and the absolute Owner of the Day of Recompense. No worldly power will compete with Him then, and no person will possess anything independently of Him.

The *dīn* here means recompense and judgment. It also carries associations of debt: people will receive what their deeds have earned, while Allah’s grace and justice determine the final outcome. In this life, wealth, status, family, and political authority can create the illusion of independence. On that Day, every such illusion disappears. The Qur’an describes the scene: “To whom belongs the kingdom today?” and the answer is, “To Allah, the One, the Overpowering” (40:16).

The Day is called a single day, although its events are vast, because it is one decisive appointment for all creation. People will be raised, gathered, questioned, and judged. The Qur’an repeatedly connects belief in Allah with belief in the Last Day because accountability gives moral weight to choices that may appear small or unnoticed in the present world. The tyrant and the oppressed, the public deed and the hidden intention, are not equal merely because human beings failed to see them.

Remembering the Day of Recompense is meant to reform life now. The Prophet ﷺ said that the wise person is the one who holds himself accountable and works for what comes after death, while the helpless person follows his desires and merely hopes for Allah’s forgiveness (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī). Hope must therefore produce repentance and action, not complacency. After describing Allah’s mercy, the sūrah names His judgment so that worship is built on love, hope, and responsible fear.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:5

> You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help

Here the sūrah turns from speaking about Allah to speaking directly to Him. The change makes the worshipper feel the nearness implied by the preceding names. The object *iyyāka* (“You alone”) is placed before the verbs, a structure that emphasizes exclusivity: we worship no one besides You and seek ultimate help from no one besides You.

Worship, *ʿibādah*, includes prayer, fasting, charity, pilgrimage, remembrance, love, fear, hope, obedience, and every inward or outward act Allah loves. It is not limited to ritual. Work, family care, honest trade, patience, and service can become worship when they are done lawfully and with a sound intention. The verse, however, does not deny ordinary means or human assistance. Muslims may ask people for what they are able to do; they simply do not treat any created being as an independent source of the unseen or as a partner with Allah.

The plural “we” is used even by a person praying alone. It joins the individual to the congregation of believers and prevents worship from becoming a private performance of the ego. It may also include the angels and all servants who worship Allah. The verse thus combines personal responsibility with belonging: each person must worship, but no one is meant to imagine that he walks toward Allah entirely by himself.

“Worship” comes before “help.” This is a commitment, not a bargain: we do not worship only after receiving what we want. Yet the second clause immediately protects the first from self-reliance. The ability to believe, pray, resist temptation, and remain sincere is itself a gift. The Qur’an says, “O humanity, you are the ones in need of Allah, whereas He is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy” (35:15). The believer uses available means while knowing that success comes from Allah.

The three motives often mentioned by the scholars—love, hope for reward, and fear of punishment—are not rival forms of worship. They belong together. Love draws the servant toward Allah, hope keeps him from despair, and fear keeps him from treating sin lightly. The statement “You alone we worship” gathers all three, while “You alone we ask for help” admits that none of them can be sustained by human strength alone.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path

The verb *ihdinā* means “guide us,” but guidance in the Qur’an has more than one stage. It can mean showing the truth, enabling a person to accept it, and then keeping him firm upon it. A person who is already praying still needs guidance: faith can grow or weaken, knowledge can be forgotten, and a straight beginning can be damaged by pride or desire. This is why the Prophet ﷺ frequently asked Allah to keep hearts firm, and why this request is repeated in every unit of prayer.

*Al-ṣirāṭ* is a clear, straight road. The Qur’an warns, “This is certainly My Straight Path, so follow it, and do not follow other ways, for they will lead you away from His Way” (6:153). The image is not of a person inventing his own route to God. The path is defined by revelation, walked through obedience, and protected by sincerity. Straightness also means balance: neither neglecting the body and lawful worldly responsibilities nor allowing them to replace the Hereafter.

The commentators explain the Straight Path in several complementary ways. It is Islam, the Qur’an, obedience to Allah, and the way embodied and taught by the Prophet ﷺ. The Qur’an calls the Prophet a guide to the Straight Path (42:52), and it promises that those who obey Allah and the Messenger will join the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous (4:69). These descriptions do not compete: revelation shows the road, the Prophet demonstrates it, and the righteous walk it.

A well-known prophetic parable describes Islam as a straight path with walls on both sides. The walls have open doors covered by curtains. A caller at the entrance says, “Stay on the path and do not turn away,” while a caller above the path warns anyone who tries to open a door that he will fall through it. The path is Islam, the walls are Allah’s limits, the doors are His prohibitions, the caller is the Qur’an, and the inner caller is the admonition in a believer’s heart (reported in Musnad Aḥmad and other collections). Guidance therefore includes learning the boundaries and resisting attractive deviations.

Some exegetes also connect this verse with the *ṣirāṭ* over which people will pass in the Hereafter. Reports describe it as a bridge over Hell, crossed with speed according to a person’s faith and deeds; some will cross swiftly, while others will struggle. The ethical path in this life and the bridge in the next are not identical, but the connection is meaningful: the one who asks for a straight life asks Allah for safety at the final crossing as well.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

The final verse explains what the Straight Path means by identifying the people who walk it. Allah says that whoever obeys Him and the Messenger will be with the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous—“what an excellent company!” (4:69). Allah’s favour is not defined mainly by wealth or comfort. It is the favour of faith, sound knowledge, obedience, repentance, and a good ending.

The verse then protects the request with two negative descriptions. There are people who know or recognize the truth and then reject it through pride, hostility, or persistent disobedience; and there are people who lose the way through ignorance, confusion, or misguided devotion. Classical commentators often used the Jews and Christians as examples of these two patterns, based on transmitted reports, but the warning is not restricted to a community. Any person can resemble the first by refusing truth after recognizing it, or the second by acting without reliable knowledge. The worshipper asks to be protected from both knowing rebellion and careless wandering.

The two forms of failure need different remedies. Rebellion calls for humility and repentance; wandering calls for learning, honest questioning, and following reliable guidance. A person may also move from one danger to the other, so the prayer remains necessary throughout life. The wording does not ask for an enemy’s destruction. It asks Allah to keep us from their path, which is a prayer for moral safety and a reminder that knowledge without obedience and zeal without knowledge can both mislead.

The verse says “those upon whom You have bestowed favour” rather than “those who have earned the path.” This preserves the balance between effort and grace. A believer must learn, choose, and act, but guidance and steadfastness are gifts from Allah. The whole sūrah has prepared this conclusion: the Lord is merciful, the Judge is real, worship belongs to Him alone, and the servant cannot reach safety without His help.

The word *Āmīn*, commonly said after al-Fātiḥah, is not part of the Qur’anic text, but it is a supplication meaning, “O Allah, answer.” The Prophet ﷺ encouraged it and said that when a person’s *Āmīn* coincides with that of the angels, his previous sins are forgiven (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). It is therefore a fitting seal: after praising Allah and asking for guidance, the worshipper asks that the prayer be accepted.
