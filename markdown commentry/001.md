# Sūrah al-Fātiḥah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

Al-Fātiḥah, "The Opening," is the first chapter of the Qur'an in the codex, though not the first revealed. It has seven verses, and the commentators call it *Umm al-Kitāb*, "the Mother of the Book," because the whole message of the Qur'an — Allah's oneness, His lordship and mercy, the Last Day, worship, and the two final destinations of human beings — is gathered into its few lines. It is also called *al-Sabʿ al-Mathānī*, "the Seven Oft-Repeated Verses," a name the Qur'an itself confirms: *"And We have certainly given you the seven often-repeated verses and the great Qur'an"* (15:87). They are "oft-repeated" because no prayer is valid without them; the Prophet ﷺ said plainly, *"Whoever does not recite Al-Fātiḥah in his prayer, his prayer is invalid"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 756). In the famous *ḥadīth qudsī* in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Allah says: *"I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two equal halves"* — the first half praise, the second half petition.

Ibn Kathir gathers the names the early community gave this surah. In an authentic hadith graded Sahih by At-Tirmidhi, Abu Hurayrah reports the Prophet ﷺ calling Al-Hamdu lillahi Rabbil-Alamin the Mother of the Qur'an, the Mother of the Book, and the seven repeated verses of the Glorious Qur'an. Al-Bukhari explains the mother-name simply: the Qur'an opens with it and the prayer opens by reciting it. Ibn Jarir adds the language behind it: the Arabs call every comprehensive matter an umm — the skin gathering the brain is umm ar-ras, the flag gathering the ranks is an umm, and Makkah is Umm al-Qura as the leader of villages. The surah is also called As-Salah, because no prayer stands without it, and Ash-Shifa and Ar-Ruqyah, the cure and the remedy.

Two reports fix its rank. Muslim and An-Nasa'i record Ibn Abbas saying Jibril told the Prophet ﷺ of a door in heaven never opened before, from which an angel brought glad tidings of two lights given to no prophet before him: the Opening of the Book and the closing verses of al-Baqarah — no letter of them is read but its benefit is gained. And Muslim records Abu Hurayrah reporting the Prophet ﷺ saying three times that whoever prays without Umm al-Qur'an has prayed incompletely; asked what one standing behind the imam should do, Abu Hurayrah said to recite it to oneself.

The strongest view of the early scholars — Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, and al-Ṭabarī — is that it is an early Makkan sūrah, sent down whole near the beginning of the mission. A few reports say it was revealed a second time in Madinah, which is why some scholars count it as both Makkan and Madinan. Either way, its first work was foundational: before any law was given, it taught the small band of believers how to stand before Allah, praise Him, and ask Him for guidance, in a society full of idols and tribal gods.

The sūrah moves in two halves. Verses 1–4 are all praise: the Name of Allah, His lordship over all worlds, His mercy, and His mastery of the Day of Judgment. Verse 5 turns from speaking about Allah to speaking to Allah — *"You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help."* Verses 6–7 make the only request in the whole chapter: guidance. Not wealth, not victory, not long life — guidance alone, because everything good depends on it.

Al-Fātiḥah is also unique in its voice. Everywhere else the Qur'an is Allah speaking to humanity; here it is Allah teaching humanity how to speak to Him. And it is spoken in the plural — *we* worship, *we* ask, *guide us* — so that no one prays for himself alone. Whoever recites it joins a single line of worshippers stretching across the world and across time.

## Verse 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**A Verse or a Divider**

This line is the *basmalah*. It stands at the head of 113 of the Qur'an's 114 chapters — only Sūrah 9 opens without it — and it appears once inside a text, in Sulaymān's letter to the Queen of Sheba (27:30 — *“It is from Solomon, and it reads: ‘In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”*). Because it heads almost every chapter, scholars asked: is it a verse of each sūrah, or only a divider between sūrahs? Both views have early support. Anas ibn Mālik said he prayed behind the Prophet ﷺ, Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, and ʿUthmān, and each of them opened the prayer with *All praise is for Allah* without saying the *basmalah* aloud. Abū Hurayrah, on the other hand, recited it aloud and told his congregation his prayer was the closest to the Prophet's ﷺ. The schools of law divided the same way: the Shāfiʿī and Jaʿfarī schools count it as a verse recited in prayer, while most others recite it quietly. Ibn ʿAbbās explained its importance from the other direction: the Prophet ﷺ did not know where one sūrah ended and the next began until *Bismillāh* came down — the line itself became the punctuation of revelation.

Ibn Kathir maps the disagreement precisely. The view that the basmalah is a verse at the start of every surah except the ninth is attributed to Ibn Abbas, Ibn Umar, Ibn az-Zubayr, Abu Hurayrah and Ali among the Companions, and to Ata, Tawus, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Makhul and az-Zuhri among the Tabi'in, with ash-Shafi'i, Ahmad in one report, Ishaq bin Rahwayh and Abu Ubayd holding it after them; Malik and Abu Hanifah held it is not a verse of al-Fatihah or any surah, while Dawud held it a separate verse before each surah rather than part of it.

On reciting it aloud, Ibn Kathir preserves the reports each side relied on. An-Nasa'i, Ibn Hibban, Ibn Khuzaymah and al-Hakim record Abu Hurayrah reciting it aloud and saying his prayer was the closest to the Prophet's ﷺ — a report Ad-Daraqutni, al-Khatib and al-Bayhaqi graded Sahih. Imam Ahmad, Abu Dawud, Ibn Hibban and al-Hakim record Umm Salamah saying the Prophet ﷺ distinguished each verse in recitation, naming the basmalah first; and ash-Shafi'i and al-Hakim record Mu'awiyah omitting it in Madinah, being corrected by the Muhajirun present, and reciting it aloud at the next prayer. The other side cites what Muslim records from A'ishah — that the Prophet ﷺ opened with the takbir and then All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds — and what the Two Sahihs record from Anas, with Muslim's addition that they did not mention the basmalah at the beginning or end of recitation. Ibn Kathir closes the question as the imams did: the prayer of whoever recites aloud or quietly is correct.

**The Name That Holds Every Attribute**

*Allāh* is the proper name of God. Some asked whether it is underived or a definite form of *al-ilāh*, "the god," but the Qur'an shows the name was already known to the idolaters: ask them who created the heavens and the earth and they say *Allāh* (43:87 — *“If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created them, they will certainly say, “Allah!” How can they then be deluded ˹from the truth˺”*). Their error was not the name but the exclusivity — they acknowledged Allah yet gave partners a share of worship. Al-Ghazālī noted that *Allāh* is at once the most universal of the names, holding every attribute within it, and the most exclusive: a man may be called merciful or a king, but no creature can be called *Allāh*. That is the very point of *"He is Allah—One"* (112:1).

Ibn Kathir notes it is said that Allah is the Greatest Name, because the other names come as attributes beside it (59:22 — *“Knower of the seen and unseen. He is the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”*), and (7:180 — *“Allah has the Most Beautiful Names. So call upon Him by them”*). The Two Sahihs record Abu Hurayrah reporting the Prophet ﷺ saying Allah has ninety-nine names, one hundred minus one, and whoever counts and preserves them will enter Paradise; At-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah record the list itself, with differences between their narrations.

**Beginning Everything in His Name**

Notice what could have been chosen and was not. The formula does not open with the Mighty, the Judge, or the Avenger; it opens with mercy, twice. That sets the tone of the entire Book before a single law or warning appears. And the choice is repeated at a scale no other words reach: the Prophet ﷺ told Abū Saʿīd ibn al-Muʿallā that al-Fātiḥah is the greatest sūrah of the Qur'an — *the seven oft-repeated verses and the grand Qur'an* given to him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4474) — so these names are spoken at least seventeen times a day in the obligatory prayers alone. Their power extends even to healing: a Companions' group recited this sūrah over a tribal chief who had been stung, and he rose healthy; when they brought the sheep they were paid, the Prophet ﷺ smiled and asked, *"How did you know it is a ruqya (healing)?"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5736). The Qur'an itself ties this Name to every other name: *"Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names"* (17:110). To begin in the Name of Allah is to begin at the door that opens onto all the rooms.

Ibn Kathir adds what the Name does in daily use. Imam Ahmad records a rider behind the Prophet ﷺ saying the animal tripped and he cursed Satan; the Prophet ﷺ told him not to say that — it makes Satan grow arrogant — but to say Bismillah, which shrinks Satan small as a fly. An-Nasa'i in Al-Yawm wal-Laylah and Ibn Marduwyah record Usamah bin Umayr's version of the same scene: those words make Satan grow as large as a house, while Bismillah shrinks him to a fly. The basmalah is therefore recommended before every deed: Imam Ahmad and the Sunan compilers record Abu Hurayrah, Sa'id bin Zayd and Abu Sa'id reporting the Prophet ﷺ saying there is no valid ablution for whoever does not mention Allah's Name in it — a hadith graded Hasan; and Muslim records the Prophet ﷺ telling the child Umar bin Abi Salamah under his care to say Bismillah, eat with the right hand, and eat from what is near.

## Verse 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

**What Al-Hamd Means**

*Al-ḥamd* is richer than the English "praise." Thanks (*shukr*) answers a favor received; praise answers what someone *is*, whether or not he has given you anything. Every thanks is a kind of praise, but praise is larger — it reaches past the ledger of gifts to the character of the One praised. The Arabic is also definite: *al*-ḥamd, *the* praise. The commentators read that as a claim of ownership — all praise, wherever it is spoken and whoever receives it, truly belongs to Allah. When a Muslim says *al-ḥamdulillāh*, he is not adding one compliment to the pile; he is saying that every compliment ever paid ends up on one account.

Abu Ja'far bin Jarir defines Al-Hamdu lillah as thanks due purely to Allah alone — not to any idol worshipped besides Him nor any creature — for favors only He can count: creating the limbs that obey Him, the bodies that carry out His commands, the provision of this life, and the warning that shows the road to lasting happiness. Ibn Jarir adds that Allah praised Himself this way to teach His servants to praise Him the same way, as if commanding Say: all thanks is due to Allah — a praise that names His most beautiful names and most honorable attributes. Hafs reports Umar asking Ali: they knew la ilaha illallah, subhan Allah and Allahu Akbar — but what of Al-Hamdu lillah? Ali answered it is a statement Allah liked for Himself, was pleased with for Himself, and loves to see repeated. Ibn Abbas said it is the statement of appreciation: when the servant says it, Allah says His servant has praised Him — a report Ibn Abi Hatim records.

**Why Praise Comes Back With Reward**

Why does the Qur'an open by praising Allah in Allah's own words? The commentators answer: so that we can praise Him with speech that is equal to Him, since our own words fall short. The Prophet ﷺ said in prayer that he could not count the praises due to Allah — Allah is as He has praised Himself. And the praise is answered. In the *ḥadīth qudsī* of the divided prayer, when the servant says *"All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds,"* Allah responds, *"My servant has praised Me"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). The sentence is not spoken into silence. In daily life the same phrase does quiet work: asked how he is — in ease or hardship — the believer answers *al-ḥamdulillāh*, placing health and trial alike inside one praise that does not depend on circumstances.

Ibn Kathir gathers the rewards hung on this sentence. Imam Ahmad records al-Aswad bin Sari offering the Prophet ﷺ words of praise he had collected for his Lord, and the Prophet ﷺ answering that his Lord loves praise. At-Tirmidhi — who graded it Hasan Gharib — An-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah record Jabir bin Abdullah reporting the Prophet ﷺ saying the best remembrance is la ilaha illallah and the best supplication is Al-Hamdu lillah. Ibn Majah records Anas reporting that no servant receives a blessing and says Al-Hamdu lillah but what he gave in praise outweighs what he took in blessing. And Ibn Majah records Ibn Umar reporting a servant who said O Allah, Yours is the praise as befits Your Face and Your authority — words the two recording angels did not know how to write until Allah told them to write them as His servant said them, leaving the reward with Him.

**Lord of Every World**

*"All worlds"* (*al-ʿālamīn*) is a plural the Qur'an deliberately leaves open. Some exegetes said it means humans, jinn, angels, and devils; some said humans and jinn, the two who receive warners — and the Prophet ﷺ was sent as *"a warner to the worlds"* (25:1). Al-Rāzī pushed wider still: beyond this world is space without limit, and Allah can create worlds no mind has imagined. The simplest summary is the best: the worlds are everything that is not Allah. The same word defines the Prophet's ﷺ mission as *"a mercy to the whole world"* (21:107).

Ibn Kathir pauses on both words. Ar-Rabb is the owner with full authority, the master who leads; used alone it belongs only to Allah, while for others it needs a qualifier — rabb ad-dar, master of the house — and it is even reported to be Allah's Greatest Name. Al-Alamin is everything in existence besides Allah, with every generation of creation called an alam. Al-Farra and Abu Ubayd said it means all with minds — mankind, jinn, angels and devils, not animals; Zayd bin Aslam and Abu Muhaysin said all Allah created with a soul; Qatadah said every type of creation is an alam; az-Zajjaj and al-Qurtubi widen it to everything Allah created in both lives — and al-Qurtubi calls this the correct view. The Qur'an itself stages the definition when Pharaoh asks (26:23 — *“And what is ‘the Lord of all worlds’?”*) and Moses answers (26:24 — *“˹He is˺ the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, if only you had sure faith”*). And alam itself comes from alamah, a sign — because creation is the sign testifying to its Creator and His oneness.

## Verse 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**Two Names From One Mercy**

Both names come from one root, *raḥmah* — mercy, the tenderness that moves a stronger being to care for a weaker one. Arabic builds intensity by patterns, and the two names use two different ones. *Raḥmān* follows a form that suggests a state filling its bearer completely: mercy as total nature. *Raḥīm* follows a form that suggests constancy and repetition: mercy that keeps arriving, act after act, creature after creature. One is a flood; the other, a stream from the same rain. Since these two names already appeared in the *basmalah* a line earlier, the repetition makes the reader slow down: before the sūrah says anything about judgment, mercy is named twice.

The two names do not behave alike outside Allah, and the difference is instructive. *Al-Raḥmān* may be applied to no creature at all — it names the mercy by which existence itself is given, and no created thing gives existence. The false prophet Musaylimah's try to take the title struck the early Muslims as theft of a name. *Al-Raḥīm*, by contrast, can describe creatures: the Qur'an says of the Prophet ﷺ that he is *"gracious and merciful to the believers"* (9:128). And the Qur'an sets the first name beside the supreme Name: *"Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names"* (17:110). Sūrah 55 is even titled *al-Raḥmān* and opens by naming mercy's gifts: He taught the Qur'an, created the human being, gave him speech.

Ibn Abbas called them two soft names, one softer than the other — carrying more mercy. At-Tirmidhi, grading it Sahih, records Abdur-Rahman bin Awf hearing the Prophet ﷺ report Allah saying He is Ar-Rahman, created the womb-tie, derived its name from His own, and keeps ties to whoever keeps it while severing whoever severs it — a text al-Qurtubi cites as proof both names derive from mercy. Abu Ali al-Farisi draws the scope from usage: Ar-Rahman gathers every mercy of Allah, while Ar-Rahim effects the believers (33:43 — *“For He is ever Merciful to the believers”*). Uthman bin Zufar put it the same way: He is Ar-Rahman with all creation and Ar-Rahim with the believers.

**Warning, Then Encouragement**

The commentators divide the work between the two names. The mercy of *al-Raḥmān* is universal: it brings everything into existence and provides for every creature — believer and denier, animal and angel. No one is excluded from existing, and existence is the first gift. The mercy of *al-Raḥīm* is particular: the sustained care Allah extends to the believers — guidance in this life, pardon in the next. The breadth of the first is stated without qualification: *"My mercy encompasses everything"* (7:156). Together the two names answer a question every reader eventually asks: if Allah is merciful, why is there suffering? Giving existence and provision is universal; the final mercy of forgiveness follows the choices creatures make with what they were given.

Al-Qurtubi reads the order as deliberate: after Lord of the worlds comes a warning, then an encouragement — Rabb warns while Ar-Rahman Ar-Rahim invites. The Qur'an pairs them the same way elsewhere (15:49 — *“Inform My servants ˹O Prophet˺ that I am truly the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”*), (15:50 — *“and that My torment is indeed the most painful”*), and (6:165 — *“Surely your Lord is swift in punishment, but He is certainly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”*). Muslim records the Prophet ﷺ saying that if the believer knew Allah's punishment none would hope for Paradise, and if the disbeliever knew Allah's mercy none would despair of it.

**Mercy Between Lordship and Judgment**

The mystics built their account of creation on the first name. The archetypes of all things, held in Allah's knowledge, came into being — in the tradition's image — by the *Breath of the Compassionate*: existence itself is an exhalation of mercy. The Qur'an associates the name with cosmic order directly: *"the Most Compassionate, established on the Throne"* (20:5). Even the names of rigor conceal mercy rather than cancel it, for divine power never acts apart from divine kindness.

The verse's position is a claim in itself. Verse 2 declared Allah Lord of all worlds — all being, all "space." Verse 4 will declare Him Master of the Day of Judgment — all time gathered to its end. This verse sits between them: mercy fills the interval. Whatever exists, wherever and whenever, exists inside these two names — so that when judgment is named in the next verse, no one may hear it as menace alone. The *ḥadīth qudsī* marks the shift exactly: at this verse Allah's response rises from *"My servant has praised Me"* to *"My servant has lauded Me"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a).

## Verse 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment

**Owner and King of One Day**

The first word of this verse is recited in two canonical readings, and both go back to the Prophet ﷺ. *Mālik* means owner — the one who holds a thing and disposes of it. *Malik* means king — the one whose command runs over a domain. The commentators argued about which says more: every king is a master but not every master a king, so *malik* seems larger; yet ownership means direct power over the thing itself, while a king may reign over what belongs to others, so *mālik* runs deeper. The disagreement survives because each reading carries something true: on that Day there is no property that is not His and no court but His.

Ibn Kathir anchors the ownership in the names themselves: Allah is the true Owner of everything and everyone, named the King, the Most Holy, the All-Perfect (59:23 — *“the King, the Most Holy, the All-Perfect”*). The Two Sahihs record Abu Hurayrah reporting the Prophet ﷺ saying the most despicable name to Allah is a man calling himself king of kings when there is no owner except Allah. Calling anyone else king in this life is only a figure of speech — as when Allah says He appointed Saul as king (2:247 — *“Allah has appointed Saul to be your king”*), or mentions the king seizing ships by force (18:79 — *“for there was a ˹tyrant˺ king ahead of them who seizes every ˹good˺ ship by force”*), or recalls making the Israelites sovereign (5:20 — *“made you sovereign”*).

**Why That Day Alone Is Named**

Verse 2 declared Allah Lord of all the worlds; why narrow the claim to one day? Because the narrowing enlarges it. In ordinary time, authority is shared and borrowed — owners own, kings reign, judges judge, each within limits and for a term. The Day of Judgment is when every borrowed authority expires. The Qur'an stages the moment (40:16 — *“Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme”*). No other claimant is left standing. Ibn Kathir draws the same conclusion from the silence of that Day: no one will speak there except those Allah permits (78:38 — *“on the Day the ˹holy˺ spirit and the angels will stand in ranks. None will talk, except those granted permission by the Most Compassionate and whose words are true”*), all voices will be hushed before Him (20:108 — *“All voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate.”*), and when it arrives no one will dare speak except with His leave (11:105 — *“When that Day arrives, no one will dare speak except with His permission. Some of them will be miserable, others joyful”*). He also preserves Ad-Ḍaḥḥāk's report from Ibn ʿAbbās that on that Day no one will own anything at all. Naming Allah Master of that Day does two things at once: it guarantees the reckoning will happen — the Day has an Owner who will not let it slip — and it strips every worldly hierarchy of finality. The tyrant's court and the emperor's decree are provisional rentals; one day the lease ends.

Ibn Abbas defines the Day itself: Yawm ad-Din is the Day of Recompense, when Allah reckons creation for their deeds — evil for evil, good for good — except those He pardons. Ibn Kathir adds that several other Companions, Tabi'in and Salaf scholars said the same, since the meaning is plain from the verse.

**A Debt That Falls Due**

*"Judgment"* translates *dīn*, a word tied to obedience and debt — the Qur'an uses its relative *dayn* for a contracted loan (2:282 — *“O believers! When you contract a loan for a fixed period of time, commit it to writing.”*). From the same root comes *al-Dayyān*, "the One Who Repays." The Day of *dīn* is the day a debt falls due: existence, provision. Guidance were advanced to every soul, and the account is settled — *"every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged"* (2:281; see also 24:25 — *“On that Day, Allah will give them their just penalty in full, and they will ˹come to˺ know that Allah ˹alone˺ is the Ultimate Truth”*). Debt language makes the judgment intelligible, not arbitrary: what is repaid then was borrowed here, choice by choice.

The Qur'an gives the imagery of that total possession: *"the ˹whole˺ earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand"* (39:67). A report in the interpretive tradition adds the proclamation: where are the kings of the earth now? Where are the tyrants? Meanwhile the verse presses on the worshipper in a practical way, because this line is recited in every unit of prayer: the believer rehearses the transfer of ownership daily, years before the court sits. Judgment in the Qur'an is never a surprise; it is announced in advance in the most repeated prayer on earth, so that everyone has time to arrange their affairs.

The Qur'an presses the same root elsewhere, asking whether the dead will really be brought to judgment (37:53 — *“will we really be brought to judgment?”*). A hadith states the wise person is whoever reckons himself and works for life after death — holding himself accountable now. Umar said to hold yourselves accountable before you are held accountable, weigh yourselves before you are weighed, and prepare for the great gathering before the One whose knowledge covers every deed (69:18 — *“You will then be presented ˹before Him for judgment˺, and none of your secrets will stay hidden”*). Ibn Kathir gives the chain for that proclamation of the kings: the Two Sahihs record the Prophet ﷺ saying Allah will grasp the earth, fold the heavens with His Right Hand, and proclaim He is the King — where are the kings of the earth, the tyrants, the arrogant.

Read as a sequence, verses 1–4 step down a ladder: *Allāh* names the Essence; *al-Raḥmān* gathers the attributes; *al-Raḥīm* reaches the acts that sustain each creature; the Master names the governance of history itself. The second half of the sūrah climbs back up — from servanthood, through petition, to guidance. The chapter performs what it describes: the names of Allah descend toward the world, and the world, praying, ascends toward Allah.

## Verse 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

**From Speaking About to Speaking To**

For four verses Allah has been spoken *about*. Here the grammar turns and He is spoken *to*. The shift is the hinge of the sūrah, and the *ḥadīth qudsī* marks it precisely: at this verse Allah says, *"This is between Me and My servant, and My servant will receive what he asks for"* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). A described God remains at a distance; an addressed God is present. No priest stands in between — every worshipper on a prayer mat has this direct address built into the words.

Ibn Kathir notes the grammar itself: the speech changes from third person to direct address through the kaf in iyyaka — after praising and thanking Allah, the servant stands before Him speaking to Him. That standing is not optional in prayer: the Two Sahihs record Ubadah bin as-Samit reporting the Prophet ﷺ saying there is no valid prayer for whoever does not recite the Opening of the Book. Muslim's full version of the divided prayer, via Abu Hurayrah, walks through each verse in turn until this one, where Allah says this is between Him and His servant, and His servant shall have what he asked.

**You Alone, in Worship and in Help**

Arabic shows emphasis by word order, and this verse uses the device in both halves. The pronoun *iyyāka* — "You" — is placed *before* the verbs. Normal order would be "we worship You"; fronting the object restricts it: "You, and no one else, we worship." The bracketed *˹alone˺* in the translation is not an addition — it is what the syntax itself says. The first half excludes every partner in worship; the second half excludes every other source of help. Sincere religion in the Qur'an is exactly this double exclusivity: *"worship Allah ˹alone˺, being sincerely devoted to Him"* (39:2).

Linguistically, Ibn Kathir explains, ibadah means subdued — a paved road is mu'abbadah; in religion it means utmost love, humility and fear together. Some of the Salaf said al-Fatihah is the secret of the Qur'an and these words are the secret of al-Fatihah: the first half declares innocence from all polytheism, the second negates all personal power, recognizing every affair lies with Allah alone. The Qur'an repeats the pairing — worship Him and trust Him (11:123 — *“So worship Him and put your trust in Him”*), believe in Him and trust Him (67:29 — *“in Him ˹alone˺ we believe, and in Him ˹alone˺ we trust”*), take Him alone as trustee (73:9 — *“so take Him ˹alone˺ as a Trustee of Affairs”*). Ad-Dahhak reports Ibn Abbas glossing the first half as singling Allah out in fear and hope, none else, and the second as asking His help to obey Him in all affairs; Qatadah says the verse commands sincere worship for Allah and seeking His aid in everything.

**Worship First, Then the Means**

The order of the two clauses is itself a lesson. Worship comes before the request, and the commentators explain why the order cannot be reversed: you do not walk into a king's court with a demand before acknowledging his sovereignty. *ʿIbādah* (worship, servitude) is the lowest humility of the creature before his Lord — and it is the true condition of everything that exists, whether it admits it or not: *"There is none in the heavens or the earth who will not return to the Most Compassionate in full submission"* (19:93). Saying *iyyāka naʿbudu* signs that reality willingly. The creature must be a servant anyway; the only choice is whose.

The second clause has a reflexive edge: the help requested is, first of all, help to do the worship just declared. No one worships Allah by his own power; even the ability to say *iyyāka naʿbudu* truthfully is a gift. *"For Allah is the Self-Sufficient, whereas you stand in need ˹of Him˺"* (47:38). The *ḥadīth qudsī*'s promise — *"My servant will receive what he asks for"* — attaches here, precisely where need is admitted: praise glorifies Allah, but the admission of helplessness opens the hand.

Ibn Kathir explains the order: worship is the objective and Allah's help is the tool, so the more important comes first. And the servant-title itself is an honor: Allah calls the Prophet ﷺ His servant at his most honorable missions — sending down the Book (18:1 — *“All praise is for Allah Who has revealed the Book to His servant”*), standing to invoke Him, and the night journey.

## Verse 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path

**Guidance That Must Continue**

*Ihdinā* — "guide us" — is a prayer for continuation, not first contact. Someone already praying and asking Allah's help does not need to be shown where the road is; he needs to be kept on it. Guidance in the Qur'an has layers: guidance *to* the path, and guidance *along* it — and the second never ends, for Allah *"guides them by their faith"* (10:9) — believers still receive guidance after believing. Some exegetes gloss the verse as "incline our hearts toward You": to be guided is to be led *to* Allah, not merely to a map. And the request repeats at least seventeen times a day, because al-Fātiḥah is required in every unit of prayer. A need asked seventeen times a day is a need that never expires; guidance is not a possession but a supply.

Ibn Kathir answers the obvious question: why does a believer already guided ask for guidance in every prayer? Because without constant asking he cannot stay firm — he has no power to benefit or harm himself except by Allah's leave — so Allah directed him to invoke Him hourly for firmness and success, and the happy person is the one Allah guides to keep asking. The Qur'an commands believers to believe (4:136 — *“O believers! Have faith in Allah, His Messenger”*) not as redundancy but as firmness and continuity, and teaches them to pray that their hearts not deviate after guidance (3:8 — *“Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us. Grant us Your mercy”*). To say Guide us is to ask: keep us firm and do not let us leave the path.

**The Clear Road Without Branches**

*Ṣirāṭ* means a road without bend, deviation, or branching confusion. Ibn Kathir records that Abū Jaʿfar aṭ-Ṭabarī said the community agreed on this meaning — the clear road with no branches, in the usage of the Arabs — and cites the poet Jarīr ibn ʿAṭiyyah using the word that way, so the term is fixed by language before it is fixed by doctrine. He also joins the petition to the two ways Allah showed every soul (90:10 — *“and shown them the two ways ˹of right and wrong˺”*) and to the praise of the people of Paradise, who credit their arrival to guidance alone (7:43 — *“And they will say, “Praise be to Allah for guiding us to this.”*). The Qur'an claims that quality for Allah's way alone (6:153 — *“Indeed, that is My Path—perfectly straight. So follow it and do not follow other ways, for they will lead you away from His Way”*). One path, many ways; straightness against scatter. The commentators also call it a middle way — not drowning in worldliness on one side, not crushing asceticism on the other — outward law joined with inward sincerity. The community formed on it is called *"an upright community"* (2:143), whose moderation is not compromise but geometry: the shortest, surest line between a servant and his Lord.

At-Tabari adds that the Arabs use sirat for every deed and word, righteous or wicked — the honest person is straight, the wicked is crooked — and that the straight path in the Qur'an is Islam. The word guide itself stretches both ways in the Qur'an: Allah chose His friend and guided him to a straight path (16:121 — *“He chose him and guided him to the Straight Path”*), yet the same verb sends the damned onward (37:23 — *“then lead them ˹all˺ to the path of Hell”*). Guidance is direction; this verse asks for direction to success.

**What the Road Is, and Its Parable**

What, exactly, is being asked for? The commentators named the object differently. Some said the straight path is the Qur'an itself. Some said Islam as a whole. Some said the Prophet ﷺ — with textual support, since Allah tells him, *"you are truly leading ˹all˺ to the Straight Path"* (42:52). Shīʿī commentaries often identify the path with the Imams beginning with ʿAlī; some Sunni works read it as the Prophet and his two immediate successors. The readings converge more than they clash: each names what a school trusts to keep a person on the road. And Hūd's words add the deepest layer: *"Surely my Lord's Way is perfect justice"* (11:56) — Allah Himself is upon the straight path, so to be guided along it is to walk with Allah toward Allah.

The Prophet ﷺ pictured the verse in a parable the commentators preserve. Allah sets out a straight path with a wall on each side. In the walls are open doors hung with curtains. A caller at the gate of the path cries: O people, stay on the path, do not deviate. A caller above the path warns whoever reaches for a door: woe to you, do not open it — if you open it, you will fall through. Then the explanation: the path is Islam; the walls are Allah's limits; the curtained doors are His prohibitions; the caller at the gate is the Book of Allah; and the caller above is the admonition Allah places in the believer's own heart. Guidance, in the picture, is not open-country navigation — it is staying between two boundaries while temptation dresses itself as doorways.

Ibn Kathir gives the parable's chain: Imam Ahmad records it in his Musnad from an-Nawwas bin Sam'an, from the Prophet ﷺ.

## Verse 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

**Known by Its Walkers**

Verse 6 asked for the path; this verse names it by its people. A road is known by who walks it. The Qur'an gives the roster in matching terms: *"Whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous — what honourable company!"* (4:69). Four ranks, and one shared description: people upon whom Allah has conferred favor (*niʿmah*). The blessing is not mainly material; the commentators gloss the blessed as those Allah purified so they could reach Him — knowledge without transgression, spiritual experience without excess. Naming the path by its walkers answers a practical danger: anyone can claim straightness. The verse anchors the claim in a known, historical procession — from Ādam through the prophets to the truthful and the martyrs of every age.

Allah closes the roster with its source: this is His favour, and He fully knows who deserves it (4:70 — *“This is Allah’s favour, and Allah fully knows ˹who deserves it˺”*). Ibn Kathir defines these people as those of guidance, sincerity and obedience — adhering to Allah's commands and avoiding His prohibitions.

**Who Earned Anger and Who Went Astray**

A widely reported interpretation identifies those who earned displeasure with the Jews and those astray with the Christians — though the saying behind it is not graded at the highest level of authenticity. Its supporters point to the Qur'an's own usage: wrath-language attaches to episodes of the Children of Israel, as when they complained about *manna* and *"invited the displeasure of Allah"* (2:61), and the warning against *"the vain desires of those who went astray before"* is addressed to the People of the Book (5:77 — *“Say, “O People of the Book!”*). Others refuse the narrowing and read both terms as moral states open to any community — including Muslims. Weighed honestly: the historical application is legitimate where the Qur'an itself makes it, but the general reading suits the prayer. Al-Fātiḥah is recited by Muslims about themselves, in the first-person plural; a petition that only described other religions would be a boast, and the grammar — "guide *us*" — forbids that.

Ibn Kathir gives the chain behind that identification. Imam Ahmad records Adi bin Hatim's long account of coming to the Prophet ﷺ — finding him so approachable he knew he was no king like Kisra or Caesar — and hearing him say those who earned anger are the Jews and those led astray are the Christians; At-Tirmidhi collects it too and grades it Hasan Gharib. Ibn Kathir explains the fit: both groups earned anger and both went astray, but anger is more particular to the Jews (5:60 — *“those who earned Allah’s condemnation and displeasure”*), who knew the truth yet abandoned practicing it, while straying is more particular to the Christians, who sought knowledge but lost it and wandered. He also preserves Zayd bin Amr bin Nufayl's pre-Islam journey to Sham seeking the true religion: the Jews told him he must carry a share of Allah's anger, the Christians told him he must carry a share of discontent — he refused both, stayed on his pure nature worshipping neither idols nor either creed, while his companions became Christians and Waraqah bin Nawfal stayed with them until Allah guided him through the Prophet ﷺ.

**How the Sentence Leaves a Door Open**

One grammatical detail carries theology. For the blessed, an agent is named: those *You* have blessed — Allah's own act, complete and certain. For the first failing group the construction is passive: *al-maghḍūb ʿalayhim*, "those upon whom anger has come" — no one is said to be angry. For the last, simply *al-ḍāllīn*, "the astray" — no agent, no outcome. Blessing is attributed to Allah directly; wrath is left unattributed, echoing the Qur'an's principle that *"Allah does not wrong people — they wrong themselves"* (10:44). And the passive keeps a door open: the verse says such people are under wrath, not that wrath has sealed on them forever, for *"Allah guides to Himself whoever turns"* (13:27). Mercy has left its fingerprint on the very sentence that speaks of anger.

*Ḍalāl* — straying — turns out to be the wider category. It covers the deliberate exchange of truth for falsehood, but also straying by mistake or inexperience: Mūsā says of his early years, *"I did that while I was unguided"* (26:20), and Allah reminds the Prophet ﷺ, *"Did He not find you unguided, then guided you?"* (93:7). Between those poles sits everyone who simply lives far from revelation — not in defiance, but at a distance. Straying rarely announces itself; the slide from knowledge into heedlessness needs no decision. That is why the prayer asks, daily and in the plural, to be kept from it. The Qur'an presses the point with a question: *"Who is more astray than the one who follows his own desire without guidance from Allah?"* (28:50).

Ibn Kathir closes the surah by gathering it: seven verses holding praise, glorification, the most beautiful names, the Hereafter, asking and invoking, all power from Allah, sincerity to Him alone, and the plea for the straight path — held in this life and crossed as the bridge over Hell in the next, leading to gardens in the company of the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous.

The sūrah ends where prayer ends — with a word that is not part of the Qur'an. After the recitation, the worshipper says *āmīn*, and the Prophet ﷺ attached a promise to it: *"When the imam says āmīn, say āmīn, for whoever's āmīn coincides with the āmīn of the angels, his past sins are forgiven"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 780). The word is generally explained as "O Allah, answer" — a seal set on the petition, like a signature at the foot of a letter.
