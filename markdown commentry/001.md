# Sūrah al-Fātiḥah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Mother of the Book**

Al-Fātiḥah, "The Opening," stands first in the written Qur'an, though it was not the first chapter to be revealed. Classical exegesis calls it *Umm al-Kitāb* (the Mother of the Book) and *Umm al-Qur'ān* (the Mother of the Qur'an), because the whole content of revelation is gathered into its seven short verses: who Allah is, His lordship and mercy, the Day of Judgment, worship owed to Him alone, human need, and the two ends of the human road. It is also called *al-Sabʿ al-Mathānī* (the Seven Oft-Repeated Verses), a name the Qur'an itself uses: *"And We have certainly given you the seven often-repeated verses and the great Qur'an"* (15:87). The verses are "oft-repeated" because no valid prayer can be offered without them. Other classical titles include *al-Kāfiyah* (the Sufficient), *al-Shāfiyah* (the Healing), *al-Wāfiyah* (the Complete), and *al-Ṣalāh* (the Prayer) — the last from the famous *ḥadīth qudsī* in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim in which Allah says: *"I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two equal halves."* In seven lines the sūrah covers the ground of all theology: the Divine names and attributes, universal Lordship, sustaining mercy, the Final Judgment, worship offered to Allah alone, human dependence, and the two destinies that await humanity.

**When It Was Revealed**

Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, and al-Ṭabarī all hold that al-Fātiḥah is an early Makkan revelation, sent down whole near the beginning of the prophetic mission, when prayer was first established. A few early reports add that it was revealed a second time in Madinah — once in Makkah to establish the prayer, and again when the *qiblah* (direction of prayer) was changed — but its place at the heart of the prayer from the Makkan period onward is agreed by all. Its first audience was the small band of new believers, surrounded by idolatry and tribal fatalism, who needed a complete way of standing before Allah before any detailed laws arrived. Before a single rule was handed down, the sūrah taught them how to face the Divine: acknowledge His lordship and mercy, and ask Him for the strength to stay on the straight path.

**The Shape of the Sūrah**

The seven verses move from praise to petition, meeting at verse 5. Verses 1–3 are praise: verse 1 opens everything in the Name of Allah, joined with compassion and mercy; verse 2 claims all praise for the Lord and Nurturer of all worlds; verse 3 states mercy a second time, on its own, before judgment is mentioned. Verse 4 names Allah Master of the Day of Recompense, ending every illusion of independence. Verse 5 is the center of the chapter, where the grammar turns from speaking *about* Allah to speaking *to* Him: *"You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help."* Verses 6–7 then ask for the straight path — the path of those Allah has blessed — and to be kept off the two roads of failure: deliberate refusal and aimless wandering.

**Revelation in the Servant's Voice**

What sets this chapter apart is its posture. The rest of the Qur'an is Allah speaking to humanity; al-Fātiḥah is Allah teaching humanity how to speak to Him. Scholars note that praise comes before petition because courtesy demands it: the servant acknowledges the King's majesty and kindness before presenting any request. And the only gift asked for — not wealth, long life, or victory, but guidance — is the one need on which everything else in this world and the next depends. The sūrah also ends as it is spoken throughout, in the first person plural: *we* worship, *we* ask, *guide us*. No one recites it for himself alone; each worshipper is drawn into the company of all who seek the Straight Path.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**A Door Built Into Every Chapter but One**

The *basmalah* — "In the name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful" — stands at the head of 113 of the Qur'an's 114 chapters. Only Sūrah 9 begins without it, and one other occurrence sits inside a chapter rather than above one: the letter Sulaymān sends to the Queen of Sheba (27:30). This odd distribution made scholars ask what the phrase actually is: a verse belonging to al-Fātiḥah and to every chapter it heads, or simply a divider placed between chapters? Both views have support from the Companions. Anas ibn Mālik prayed behind the Prophet ﷺ and behind Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, and ʿUthmān, and heard every one of them open the prayer with *All praise is for Allah*, without saying the *basmalah* aloud. Abū Hurayrah did the opposite: he recited it aloud when leading prayer and told the congregation that his prayer was the closest of all to the Prophet's ﷺ. The schools of law divided along the same lines — the Shāfiʿī and Jaʿfarī schools count it as a verse recited inside the prayer, while most other schools recite it quietly or leave it out of the obligatory recitation without denying its honor. Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq called it the greatest verse in Allah's Book, and Shīʿite scholarship follows him in treating it as a full verse of al-Fātiḥah and of every chapter that carries it. Ibn ʿAbbās went further still: he held that the Prophet ﷺ did not even know where one chapter ended and the next began until this line was revealed. The *basmalah* became the punctuation of revelation.

**The Bāʾ That Carries a Hidden Verb**

The line begins with the preposition *bi*, "in" or "by," and Arabic does not allow a preposition to stand without something to complete it. Exegetes therefore supply a verb: *I begin*, *I read*, *I seek help*. Leaving the verb unstated is the genius of the formula. Because it names no particular act, every speaker completes it with whatever he is about to do — the eater begins in the name of Allah, and so do the reader, the traveler, and the builder. One short phrase can sanctify an unlimited number of deeds, which is exactly how Muslims have used it: meals, journeys, letters, and work all begin with it. Some mystics read a deeper level into the missing verb: Allah speaks first, as if saying, "I began through My Name, so you begin through My Name" — the human phrase imitates a divine act rather than inventing a ritual. Whichever reading one prefers, the effect on the tongue is the same: the coming act is handed over to Allah before it begins.

**Allāh: The Name No One Else Can Wear**

*Allāh* is the name invoked here, and scholars debated whether it is a basic proper name or a definite form of *al-ilāh*, "the god." The Qur'an shows the word was already familiar to the pre-Islamic Arabs: asked who created them, the idolaters themselves answered *Allāh* (43:87). What they denied was not the name but its demands — they acknowledged Allah as Creator while giving shares of worship to others. Al-Ghazālī pointed out what makes this name unique: it is at once the most universal of the divine names, holding every attribute within it, and the most exclusive, because nothing else can be called *Allāh* even figuratively. A man may be described as *raḥīm* (merciful) or *malik* (a king); no man can be called Allah. The declaration *"He is Allah—One ˹and Indivisible˺"* (112:1) rests on exactly this exclusivity. Some scholars therefore call it the Comprehensive Name, the title under which all the other names are gathered — which is why the *basmalah* begins with *Allāh* and then descends to two of His attributes, rather than rising from them.

**Three Names, Descending From Essence to Mercy**

The order of the names is a descent, and Sufi commentators read it as a map of how the Divine relates to creation. *Allāh* points to the Essence itself, beyond every description. *Al-Raḥmān* (the Most Compassionate) gathers the divine attributes into one — a mercy so total that the existence of everything flows from it. *Al-Raḥīm* (the Most Merciful) reaches to Allah's acts: the particular care by which each creature is fed, sustained, and answered. Essence, attributes, acts — the whole structure of Islamic theology stands in these four words. The rest of the sūrah then walks back up the same ladder, from worship and need (verse 5) to guidance and the company of the blessed (verses 6–7), so the opening line does more than invoke: it sketches the path the whole chapter travels.

Why does the formula carry mercy twice — *al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm* — when it could have carried "the Mighty" or "the Judge"? The choice sets the tone of the whole Book before a single law, story, or warning appears. The Qur'an confirms the pairing as two valid addresses to the same God: *"Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names"* (17:110).

**The Sūrah's Weight in the Prophet's Words**

Three well-known hadiths fix the standing of this chapter. The Prophet ﷺ told Abū Saʿīd ibn al-Muʿallā that al-Fātiḥah is the greatest sūrah in the Qur'an — *the seven oft-repeated verses and the grand Qur'an* he had been given (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4474). He also made its recitation a condition of prayer itself: *"Whoever does not recite Al-Fatiha in his prayer, his prayer is invalid"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 756). And its healing power is established in the story of the Companions who recited it over a stung tribal chief and were paid a flock of sheep for the cure; the Prophet ﷺ laughed and approved, asking, *"How do you know that Surat-al-Fatiha is a Ruqya?"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5736). Through such teachings the opening line became the most frequently spoken sentence in a Muslim's day, repeated at least seventeen times in the obligatory prayers alone.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

**Praise Is Wider Than Thanks**

*Al-ḥamd* is usually translated "praise," and something is lost in the translation. Thanks (*shukr*) answers a favor already received; it has a cause you can point to. Praise answers what someone *is*, whether or not he has given you anything. That makes *ḥamd* the larger word: every act of thanks is a kind of praise, but praise goes beyond the record of favors to the character of the One praised. The grammar carries the same claim. *Al-ḥamd* is definite — *the* praise, not *a* praise — and scholars read the definite article as a claim of ownership: all praise, wherever it is spoken and whoever it is spoken of, belongs in truth to Allah. When a Muslim says *al-ḥamdulillāh*, he is not adding one compliment to the pile; he is stating that every compliment ever paid finally belongs to a single account.

**Allah Supplied the Words Because Ours Fall Short**

A line of reflection runs through the commentaries: Allah opened the Qur'an by praising Himself so that human beings could praise Him in Allah's own words, since their own words cannot carry the weight. The point is not that human praise is worthless, but that it is finite — and the finite cannot measure the Infinite. The Prophet ﷺ said as much in prayer, confessing that he could not count the praise due to Allah; Allah is as He has praised Himself. The Qur'an itself records the exchange in the *ḥadīth qudsī* of the divided prayer: when the servant recites *All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds*, Allah answers, *My servant has praised Me* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395). The sentence is not spoken into silence; the Creator receives it and answers it. In daily life the same phrase does quieter work: asked how he is, in any condition, the believer answers *al-ḥamdulillāh*, laying health and hardship alike before the one praise that does not depend on circumstances.

**Rabb: The One Who Owns, Orders, and Raises**

"Lord" translates *rabb*, a word with three centers of meaning. It names a master who is obeyed; it names one who sets affairs in order and keeps managing them; and it names an owner to whom a thing truly belongs. Said of Allah, the three merge: He is the Master without equal, the Arranger of every creature's affairs, and the Owner of them all. A fourth shade comes from the same root: *tarbiyah*, the work of nurturing — raising a child, tending a field to its fullness. Allah as *rabb* is therefore not a distant landlord but the One who develops each thing from seed to maturity, bodies and souls included. English "lord" carries mostly rank and command; *rabb* carries ownership, government, and growth together. The One who is *rabb* of a thing began it, orders its course, and owns its end.

**Worlds in the Plural: Everything That Is Not Allah**

*Al-ʿālamīn* is a plural with no singular form of its own in Arabic, and exegetes filled the gap at different widths. Some counted four communities — humans, jinn, angels, and devils. Some limited it to humans and jinn, since only these two receive warners, and the Prophet ﷺ was sent as a warner to the worlds (25:1). Others widened it past counting: every generation of humankind, every species of creature, every level of existence from the earth to the seven heavens. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī went furthest, noting that space beyond this world has no known limit and that Allah can bring into being worlds no human mind has imagined. On his reading, the verse claims Allah as Lord of all "space" — not just physical space but every mode of being, seen or conceivable. The broadest summary is the simplest: the worlds are everything that exists other than Allah. The same word grounds the Prophet's ﷺ mission, described as being *"only as a mercy for the whole world"* (21:107).

**Praise Placed Before the Asking**

The verse's position in the sūrah is deliberate. Nothing has been requested yet; the first thing the worshipper says is not what he needs but who Allah is. In the ḥadīth qudsī of the divided prayer, the first half of al-Fātiḥah belongs to Allah as praise and glorification, and only the second half carries the servant's requests. The etiquette of supplication keeps the same order everywhere: begin with praise, then ask. The verse also quietly answers an objection — why praise One who has no need of praise? Because the praise changes the one who offers it. A soul that has first said *Lord of all worlds* asks its questions from inside a world already governed, and its asking comes out different: less demand, more trust.

**A Formula for Endings, as the Basmalah Is for Beginnings**

The two great formulas of Muslim life divide time between them. The *basmalah* opens an act; *al-ḥamdulillāh* closes it — after a meal, at the end of a journey, on receiving news. Between the two, no moment falls outside remembrance. The phrase *Lord of the worlds* itself recurs some forty-two times across the Qur'an, in Makkan and Madinan chapters alike, in the prophets' stories and in arguments with idolaters — Mūsā announces to Firʿawn that he is a messenger from the Lord of the worlds. Each return of the phrase redraws the same boundary: everything on one side, Allah on the other, and no third party. Al-Fātiḥah places that boundary at the head of the Book, so that every chapter read after it is read inside a world already owned, ordered, and praised.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**One Root, Two Intensities**

Both names grow from *raḥmah* — mercy with tenderness in it, the pull a stronger being feels to care for a weaker one. Arabic builds intensity by changing a root's vowel pattern, and the two names use two different patterns. *Raḥmān* follows the *faʿlān* form, the shape of words describing a state that fills its subject completely; scholars therefore take it as the more total of the two — mercy as a condition. *Raḥīm* follows the *faʿīl* form, which suggests constancy and repetition — mercy that keeps arriving, act after act, creature after creature. The pairing is not repetition but completion: a flood and a stream from the same rain. And because the two names already appeared in the *basmalah*, their second appearance slows the reciter down: before judgment is named in verse 4, mercy is stated twice — once inside verse 1, and again as a verse of its own.

**A Name Allah Never Shares — and One He Sometimes Does**

The two names behave differently outside the divine vocabulary, and the difference is instructive. *Al-Raḥmān* cannot be applied to any creature, literally or figuratively; it names the mercy by which existence itself is given, and no finite being gives existence. *Al-Raḥīm* can describe creatures: the Qur'an uses it of the Prophet ﷺ, *"gracious and merciful to the believers"* (9:128). Scholars drew the line accordingly — *Raḥmān* belongs to Allah alone, which is why the false prophet Musaylimah's attempt to take the title struck early Muslims as theft of a name. The Qur'an sets *al-Raḥmān* beside the supreme Name as an equal address (17:110, quoted in verse 1 above). Sūrah 55 goes further and takes *al-Raḥmān* as its very title, opening with the One who teaches the Qur'an, creates the human being, and gives him speech — mercy named before mercy's gifts are counted.

**Universal Mercy and Special Mercy**

Commentators divide the work between the two names. The mercy of *al-Raḥmān* is essential and universal: the mercy by which creation exists and everything in it receives its provision — believer and denier, animal and angel alike. No one is excluded from existing, and existence is the first gift. The mercy of *al-Raḥīm* is particular: the continuing care by which each thing is kept, and above all the mercy Allah extends to the believers — guidance in this life, pardon in the next. The Qur'an states the reach of the first mercy without conditions: *"My mercy encompasses everything"* (7:156). The two names also answer a question every reader eventually asks: if Allah is merciful, why does the world contain suffering? Giving existence and provision is universal; the mercy of nearness and forgiveness follows the use creatures make of what they were given.

**The Breath of the Compassionate**

Sufi writers built their account of creation on the first name. The archetypes of all things as they exist in Allah's knowledge — *al-aʿyān al-thābitah*, the "immutable essences" — were brought into being, they say, when Allah breathed upon them what they call the Breath of the Compassionate (*Nafas al-Raḥmān*). On this view the world is not manufactured but exhaled: to exist at all is to be breathed out by mercy. The Qur'an itself connects *al-Raḥmān* with rule over the cosmos: *"the Most Compassionate, ˹Who is˺ established on the Throne"* (20:5). The names of beauty — the Kind, the Clement, the Gentle — are all traces of this mercy, and even the names of rigor conceal it rather than cancel it, since divine power never acts apart from divine kindness. The verse therefore reaches beyond description: the two names explain not only how Allah treats creatures but why there are creatures at all. Mercy precedes the world; the world is mercy's footprint.

**Mercy Set Between Space and Time**

The verse's position is part of its meaning. Verse 2 declared Allah Lord of all worlds — every level of being, all that might be called space. Verse 4 will declare Him Master of the Day of Judgment — all of time gathered to its end. Verse 3 sits between the two, and its placement says what it does not spell out: mercy fills the interval. Whatever exists, in whichever world, at whichever moment before the Day, exists inside these two names. The placement also guards what comes next. "Master of the Day of Judgment," heard alone, could sound like pure menace; the two names set immediately before it insist that the Master of that Day is the Compassionate, the Merciful, here and now. The daily prayer registers the emphasis: in the ḥadīth qudsī of the divided prayer, when the servant recites this verse, Allah's answer rises a step, from *My servant has praised Me* to *My servant has extolled Me* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395). Mercy is stated twice so that judgment, when it is named, arrives in its proper frame.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment

**Two Readings, Both Traced to the Prophet**

The first word of this verse is recited in two canonical forms, and both go back to the Prophet ﷺ. *Mālik* means owner — one who holds a thing and may dispose of it. *Malik* means king — one whose command runs over a domain and its people. Commentators argued over which reading is stronger. One side reasoned that every king is a master but not every master is a king, so *malik* is the larger word. The other side answered that *mālik* is larger, because ownership includes direct power over the thing owned, while a king may rule territories whose every item belongs to others. The disagreement is worth more than its outcome: each reading carries a truth the other cannot fully absorb. On the Day in question both are true at once — there is no property that is not His, and no court but His. This verse also completes the ladder of names built by verses 1–4 — the Essence (*Allāh*), the attributes (*al-Raḥmān*), the acts (*al-Raḥīm*) — by naming the rung that touches creation: governance of a world and its final Day. The second half of the sūrah will climb back up from the servant's side, so the chapter enacts the descent of the names and the ascent of the worshipper.

**Why Mastery Is Claimed Over a Single Day**

Lordship over all the worlds was already declared in verse 2; this verse narrows the claim to one day, and the narrowing enlarges it. In ordinary time, mastery is shared and temporary — owners own, kings reign, judges judge, each within limits and for a term. The Day of Judgment is when every borrowed authority expires, and the verse announces who holds the field when the loans are called in. The Qur'an stages the scene as a question asked over the assembled multitudes: *"Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme!"* (40:16). No answer competes, because no other claimant is left standing. Naming Allah Master of that one Day does two things: it guarantees that the reckoning will come — the Day has an Owner who will not let it slip — and it strips every worldly hierarchy of finality. The tyrant's court, the merchant's ledger, the emperor's decree are all temporary tenures.

**Dīn Carries Debt Inside It**

"Judgment" renders *dīn*, and the words related to it show what kind of judgment is meant. *Dīn* is tied to obedience and to an established way of life, and it sits close to *dayn*, debt — the Qur'an uses *dayn* for a contracted loan (2:282). From the same root comes *al-Dayyān*, the One Who Repays. The Day of *dīn* is the day a debt falls due: existence, provision, and guidance were advanced to every soul, and the Day settles the account. *"On that Day, Allah will give them their just penalty in full, and they will ˹come to˺ know that Allah ˹alone˺ is the Ultimate Truth"* (24:25). The fairness of the settlement is stated from the other side in the same sūrah: *"every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged"* (2:281). Debt-language makes the judgment intelligible without making it arbitrary: what is repaid on that Day was borrowed here, choice by choice.

**The Earth Gripped, the Sky Rolled**

The Qur'an paints the total possession of that Day: *"when on the Day of Judgment the ˹whole˺ earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand"* (39:67). A report preserved in the commentaries adds the proclamation that accompanies the scene — Allah grasps the earth, folds the sky, and asks where the kings of the earth are now, where the tyrants are. But the verse itself already performs the dispossession: the two largest things a human being knows, the ground and the sky, are handled like household objects. For the worshipper the point lands close to home. This line is recited standing in the prayer every day, so the believer rehearses the transfer of all ownership daily, years before it happens. Judgment in the Qur'an is not a surprise; it is announced in advance, in the opening chapter of the Book, so that everyone who prays has had notice and time to settle their affairs before the court sits.

**Reckon Yourself Before You Are Reckoned**

The verse produced a discipline. Asked who is the most intelligent of believers, the Prophet ﷺ answered, in a saying preserved in the commentaries: those who remember death most often and prepare best for what comes after it. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb turned the answer into a rule of daily life: bring yourself to account before you are brought to account, and weigh your deeds before they are weighed. The logic treats the Day of Judgment as a date one works backward from. A debt you know is coming changes how you borrow; an audit you know is scheduled changes how you keep books. And because the Day belongs to the Merciful — the two names of verse 3 stand immediately before it — preparing for it is hope rather than dread: the same Master who will judge has already taught the servant how to ask for pardon.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

**The Sūrah Turns and Speaks to Allah**

For four verses Allah has been spoken *about* — His name, His praise, His mercy, His Day. Here the grammar turns and He is spoken *to*. The shift from third person to second person is the hinge of the sūrah, and the ḥadīth qudsī of the divided prayer marks it exactly: at this verse Allah says, *This is between Me and My servant, and My servant will receive what he asks for* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395). Praise, the tradition implies, matures into address. A God described remains at a distance; a God addressed is present — and the verse is built for presence, with "You" standing at the front of both clauses. From this turn Islamic spirituality draws a consequence that shapes everything else: no priestly intermediary is needed. Every worshipper, alone on a prayer mat, stands in the direct relationship this verse models. Recited in every unit of prayer, the line rehearses the moment when description becomes conversation, and the believer who says it is not reporting a theology but performing a relationship.

**The Object Stands First to Exclude All Others**

Arabic marks emphasis by word order, and this verse uses the device twice. The object pronoun *iyyāka* — "You" — is placed before the verbs "we worship" and "we ask for help." Neutral order would be *we worship You*; putting the object first restricts the action, so the sense becomes "You, and You alone, we worship." The bracketed *˹alone˺* in the translation is not an addition; it is what the syntax itself says. Scholars read the fronting as *tawḥīd* in grammatical form: the sentence does not just state whom the speakers worship — it shuts everyone else out. The two halves then close the two doors in order: no partner in worship, no other source of help. Sincere religion, in the Qur'an's own words, is exactly this double exclusivity: *"worship Allah ˹alone˺, being sincerely devoted to Him"* (39:2). A worship shared with an idol is no worship; and help sought from a saint's grave as an independent power quietly breaks the second clause.

**Servitude Declared Before the Request Is Made**

The order of the two clauses is itself an argument. Worship comes first; the request follows. One does not walk into a king's court and open with demands; acknowledging whose presence you are in is what makes the petition fitting rather than presumptuous. *ʿIbādah* — worship, servitude — means the furthest degree of humility and self-effacement before Allah. It is, in truth, the condition of every creature whether it accepts the condition or not: *"There is none in the heavens or the earth who will not return to the Most Compassionate in full submission"* (19:93). To say *iyyāka naʿbudu* is to sign that reality voluntarily — the creature who must be a servant anyway chooses whose servant to be. The request that follows, *and You alone we ask for help*, is then not the demand of a stranger but a need voiced inside an acknowledged relationship.

**The Plural Voice of a Single Worshipper**

The verbs are plural — *we* worship, *we* ask — even when one person recites the sūrah alone. Two lessons are drawn from this. The first is humility: the speaker folds his private "I" into the ranks of worshippers, standing before Allah as one member of a body that spans all the worlds of verse 2. Only Allah says "I" without qualification; the servant's proper pronoun is "we." The second is representation: the worshipper prays, as it were, on behalf of creation, carrying others into his prayer. Congregational prayer makes the grammar audible — rows of individuals speaking one plural sentence — but the plural does not come from the congregation; it belongs to the sūrah even in solitude. A Muslim praying alone still says *guide us*, and so carries his family, his community, and the whole chain of believers into a prayer that could grammatically have been selfish.

**Fear, Reward, and Love: Three Grades of One Sentence**

Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq sorted worshippers into three classes using this very clause as the test. Some worship Allah out of fear — that is the worship of a slave. Some worship seeking reward — that is the worship of a hired worker. Some worship out of love — that is the worship of the free, and it is the most excellent. All three recite the same words; the words weigh differently on each tongue. The ranking does not dismiss fear and hope — the Qur'an commands both — it puts them in order: service performed for a wage is valid; service performed because the Master deserves it is complete. This is why the verse works as a mirror. Recited honestly, it asks the speaker which grade his worship actually occupies — because the fronted "You alone" allows only one object, and a heart worshipping for the reward alone has quietly made the reward its object.

**Even the Worship Needs the Help That Is Asked**

The second clause turns back on the first. The help requested is, first of all, help to do the worship just declared: no creature worships Allah by his own power, so the ability to say *iyyāka naʿbudu* truthfully is itself a gift. The sentence asks for the very thing it performs — servitude declares itself, then begs for what sustains it. The Qur'an states the dependence without softening it: *"For Allah is the Self-Sufficient, whereas you stand in need ˹of Him˺"* (47:38). Practical exegesis then widens the request beyond ritual to every affair of life — earning a living, resisting a temptation, raising a family — since worship, properly understood, is whole-life obedience. And the ḥadīth qudsī attaches its promise to this half of the sūrah precisely because this half is where need is admitted: *My servant will receive what he asks for* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395). Praise glorifies Allah; admitted helplessness opens the hand.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path

**A Request for Steadiness, Not for a Map**

The petition is *ihdinā* — guide us — and scholars read it as a prayer for continuation, not for first contact. Someone already praying, already worshipping, already asking Allah's help does not need to be shown where the road is; he needs to be kept on it. Guidance in the Qur'an has layers: guidance *to* the road and guidance *along* the road, and the second never ends — *"their Lord guides them by their faith"* is promised to believers still on the way, not held back until arrival (10:9). Some exegetes gloss the verse as "incline our hearts toward You": to be guided is to be drawn toward Allah Himself. The asking repeats because the need repeats — this verse stands in every unit of every prayer, so a practising Muslim voices it many times a day. Guidance, the verse assumes, is not a possession but a supply.

**Ṣirāṭ: A Road With No Bend in It**

The word for path here is *ṣirāṭ*: a road without crookedness, deviation, or confusing forks. The Qur'an claims that quality for Allah's own way in strong language: *"Indeed, that is My Path—perfectly straight. So follow it and do not follow other ways, for they will lead you away from His Way"* (6:153). The contrast inside that verse is the definition — one path, many byways; straightness against scatter. Commentaries also describe the *ṣirāṭ* as a mean: it is not worldliness on one side, nor crushing asceticism on the other, but a middle way that joins outward law to inward sincerity, so that the body serves while the heart stays free. The community formed on it is called *"an upright community"* (2:143), a people of witness whose moderation is not compromise but geometry — the shortest, surest line between a servant and his Lord.

**What the Path Is**

Because the petition is so central, exegetes tried to name its object, and they named it differently. Some said the straight path is the Qur'an itself. Some said it is Islam as a whole. Some said it is the Prophet ﷺ — with textual support, since the Qur'an tells him, *"you are truly leading ˹all˺ to the Straight Path"* (42:52). Shīʿite commentaries often identify the path with the Imams, beginning with ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib; some Sunni works read it as the Prophet and his two immediate successors, Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. Set side by side, the answers converge more than they clash: each names what that school trusts to keep a person on the road. The Qur'an's own usage settles the general case: the path is Allah's (6:153), it is carried by revelation and embodied in the Prophet, and it is walked in company. Hūd's confession adds the deepest layer: *"Surely my Lord's Way is perfect justice"* (11:56) — Allah Himself is on the straight path, so to be guided along it is to walk with Allah toward Allah.

**A Parable of Two Walls and Open Doors**

The commentaries preserve a prophetic parable that pictures this verse. Allah sets out a straight path with a wall on each side. In the walls are open doors hung with curtains, and a caller at the path's gate keeps crying: O people, stay on the path, do not deviate. A second caller, above the path, warns anyone reaching for a door: woe to you, do not open it — if you open it, you will fall through. The interpretation attached to the parable is exact: the path is Islam; the two walls are Allah's limits; the curtained doors are His prohibitions; the caller at the gate is the Book of Allah; and the caller above the path is the admonition Allah places in the heart of every believer. The image does real work: staying guided is not open-country navigation but walking between two boundaries, past temptations dressed as doorways, with the inner caller making the outer Book audible.

**Guidance Asked After Worship Declared**

The order of verses 5 and 6 carries its own lesson. Worship and the plea for help come first; guidance is requested second. The order says that guidance is given to servants, not to spectators — the road is shown to those already committed to walking it. It also protects the prayer from becoming mere curiosity: "show me the straight path" can be asked by someone shopping for ideas, but "guide us along the straight path" is asked by people whose worship has already put them on it. The commentaries connect the two verses directly: the help requested in verse 5 is largely this guidance, since the greatest help Allah gives a servant is to keep him on the road. And because the petition is voiced in the plural, each worshipper asks it for the whole community — a standing reminder that the straight path is traveled in company; solitary guidance is not, in the grammar of this sūrah, a category.

**The Bridge That Tests the Prayer**

A second strand of exegesis reads the verse with the afterlife in view. The straight path has a counterpart at the end of days: the bridge every human being and jinn must cross at the final stage of judgment, before the Garden or the Fire. The reports describe it as finer than a hair and sharper than a sword. Some will cross it like the wind; some will pass over like birds; some will move across like nimble camels; some will only manage to crawl; and some will be dropped from it into the Fire. On this reading the daily petition is a rehearsal: the worshipper asks now for the steadiness of foot he will need then, and the moral straightness of this life decides the footing of the next. Either way — ethical road or eschatological bridge — the mechanics agree: the path is narrow, the deviations are many, and the crossing is not achieved by unaided balance. That is exactly why the verse is a request to Allah rather than a resolution of the will.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

**The Path Is Named by Its People**

Verse 6 asked for the straight path; this verse identifies it by the company walking it. The definition is social before it is geographical — a road is known by who travels it. The Qur'an gives the roster elsewhere in matching terms: *"And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous—what honourable company!"* (4:69). Four ranks, rising in nearness, all sharing one description: people on whom Allah has bestowed His favor. The blessing is not mainly material. Commentators describe the blessed as those Allah purified so that they could reach Him, and those who kept the limits of the Law while tasting spiritual realities — knowledge without excess, experience without license. Naming the path by its walkers answers a practical question the bare request leaves open: anyone can claim straightness. The verse anchors the claim in a named, historical procession — from Ādam through the prophets to the truthful and the martyrs of every age — and makes belonging to that procession the test.

**Two Ways Off the Road, Not One**

The verse then defines the path by negation: not the path of those who earned displeasure, nor of those who went astray. The two categories are genuinely different failures. Some exegetes map them onto kinds of sin: disbelief, which draws wrath, and lesser wrongdoing, which is drifting. Others see two temperaments — the defiant and the heedless. The classical pairing distinguishes those who knew the truth and refused it from those who lost the way without knowing it: the first is will set against knowledge; the second is knowledge lost or never found. The distinction matters to the person praying, because a soul can fail in either direction and the remedies differ — one is cured by repentance, the other by learning. In asking to be kept from both, the worshipper admits both risks: the capacity for rebellion and the capacity for drift live in the same heart.

**The Disagreement Over Who Is Meant**

A widely reported interpretation — though it rests on a narration not graded at the highest level of authenticity — identifies those who earned displeasure with the Jews and those who went astray with the Christians. Its supporters point to the Qur'an's own usage: wrath-language attaches repeatedly to episodes involving the Children of Israel — as in the rebuke over refusing manna and demanding lentils and onions, where they *"invited the displeasure of Allah"* (2:61) — and the warning *"nor follow the vain desires of those who went astray before ˹you˺. They misled many and strayed from the Right Way"* (5:77) is addressed to People of the Book. Other commentators refuse the narrowing and read both terms as moral states open to any community — including Muslims themselves. Weighed honestly, the historical application is legitimate where the Qur'an itself makes it, but the general reading fits the prayer's purpose better. Al-Fātiḥah is recited by Muslims about themselves, day after day; a petition that only described other religions would work as a boast, and the sūrah's grammar — first person plural, *guide us* — rules that reading out.

**Why Wrath Has No Named Agent**

A grammatical detail in this verse has drawn long attention. For the blessed, an agent is named: those *You* have blessed — Allah's own act, in the past tense, finished and certain. For the people of displeasure, the construction is passive: *al-maghḍūb ʿalayhim*, "those upon whom anger has come," with no one named as angry. And for the last group the verse says only *al-ḍāllīn*, "the astray," naming neither agent nor outcome. The asymmetry is theology carried by syntax. Blessing is attributed directly to Allah; displeasure is left unattributed, in line with the Qur'an's principle that Allah does not wrong people — they wrong themselves (10:44). The passive also leaves a door open: the verse says these people are under wrath, not that wrath has sealed upon them. Judgment has not yet fallen while the prayer is still being said, and Allah guides to Himself whoever turns back (13:27). Scholars have read the withheld agent as mercy's fingerprint on the very sentence that speaks of anger.

**Straying Is Broader Than Rebellion**

The second category, *ḍalāl* — wandering, being astray — is the Qur'an's wider term, and its range is surprising. It covers the deliberate exchange of faith for disbelief. It also covers straying by mistake or inexperience: Mūsā says of his early years, *"I did it then, lacking guidance"* (26:20), and Allah reminds the Prophet ﷺ, *"Did He not find you unguided then guided you?"* (93:7). Between those poles sits everyone who simply does not live by revelation — not out of defiance but out of distance. The breadth is one reason the prayer asks, daily and in the plural, to be kept from it: straying rarely announces itself, and the slide from knowledge into heedlessness needs no decision. Read together, the two negations cover the whole territory of failure — refusing the road, losing it, or never finding it — and leave the petition one positive content: to walk as the blessed walk. The Qur'an captures the psychology of the term in a single question: who is more astray than the one who follows his own desire without guidance from Allah (28:50)?

**Āmīn: The Seal the Angels Answer**

The sūrah ends where prayer ends — with a word that is not part of the Qur'an. After finishing *nor of those who are astray*, it is established practice to say *āmīn*, and the Prophet ﷺ commanded it with a promise attached: say *āmīn* when the imam says it, for whoever's *āmīn* coincides with the *āmīn* of the angels, his past sins are forgiven (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 780). The word is generally understood to mean "O Allah, answer" — a seal set on the petition, like a signature at the foot of a letter. Some said it means "so shall it be," close to the Hebrew *amen*; a few even counted it among Allah's names, though the first explanation prevails. Because it is not Qur'anic text, it is kept deliberately outside the recitation, said after a pause so that no one mistakes it for revelation — yet its virtue is prophetic and its timing angelic. The sūrah thus ends as it began, in the liturgy of mercy: a servant asks, the angels answer in the same instant, and the asking itself becomes forgiveness.
