# Sūrah al-Fātiḥah (Chapter 1) — Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Mother of the Book**

Al-Fātiḥah means "The Opening." It stands first in the Qur'an as arranged and recited, though it was not the first part revealed. Classical commentaries call it *Umm al-Kitāb*, the Mother of the Book, and *Umm al-Qur'ān*; the main themes of the whole revelation are gathered into its seven verses — the divine names, mercy, lordship over all creation, the Day of Judgment, worship of Allah alone, and the prayer for guidance. The Qur'an itself names it *al-Sabʿ al-Mathānī*, the seven oft-repeated verses: *"And We have certainly given you the seven often-repeated verses and the great Qur'an"* (15:87). Other titles found in the commentaries include *al-Kāfiyah* (the Sufficient), *al-Shāfiyah* (the Healing), and *al-Ṣalāh* (the Prayer), the last taken from the well-known *ḥadīth qudsī* in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim in which Allah says, *"I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves."* No unit of the daily prayer is valid without it.

**When It Was Revealed**

The dominant view, held by Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, al-Ṭabarī, and others, is that al-Fātiḥah was revealed in Makkah in its entirety, early in the mission, when prayer was first established. Some reports say it came down twice — once in Makkah when prayer began and again in Madinah when the direction of prayer changed — but its place as a Makkan revelation remains standard. Its first hearers were a small group of believers living among idol-worshippers, and before they were given any laws they were taught how to speak to Allah. That is why the chapter contains no rulings, only praise, address, and petition. It trains the tongue to leave the tribal gods behind and turn directly to the Lord of all worlds.

**The Shape of the Sūrah**

The chapter turns at verse 4. Verses 1–3 speak *about* Allah — His name, His praise, His mercy. Verse 4 adds His mastery of the Day of Judgment. Verse 5 shifts from speaking about Allah to speaking *to* Him — "You alone we worship" — and verses 6–7 make the chapter's only request: guidance. Two features of this arrangement shaped Muslim practice. First, praise comes before asking, and this became the rule for supplication in Islam: acknowledge Allah, admit your need, then ask. Second, the request is for guidance alone, not for wealth, health, or victory, because every other good depends on being rightly guided. The chapter is spoken throughout in the plural, so a person praying alone asks for himself and for all believers at once.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**A Verse, or a Line Between Verses?**

The *basmalah* opens 113 of the Qur'an's 114 chapters. Sūrah 9 alone opens without it, and it appears only once inside the text itself, in Sulaymān's letter to the Queen of Sheba (27:30). Its status was debated: is it a verse of every sūrah it heads, or a divider separating one sūrah from the next? Companions reported both practices. Anas ibn Mālik said he prayed behind the Prophet ﷺ and behind Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, and ʿUthmān, and that each of them began with *All praise is for Allah*, without reciting the *basmalah* aloud. Abū Hurayrah recited it aloud when he led prayer and said his was the prayer closest to the Prophet's ﷺ. The schools of law followed the reports: the Shāfiʿī and Jaʿfarī schools treat it as a verse recited in prayer, while most other schools recite it quietly or leave it out of the obligatory recitation without denying its value. Ibn ʿAbbās said the Prophet ﷺ did not know where one sūrah ended and the next began until this line was revealed — in effect, the *basmalah* became the punctuation of revelation.

**The Missing Verb**

The line begins with the preposition *bi* — "in" or "by" — and Arabic grammar does not allow a preposition to stand without something for it to govern. Commentators therefore supply a verb: *I begin*, *I recite*, *I seek help*. The omission is the point. Because no verb is fixed, each speaker fills it with the act he is about to perform. The one eating begins in the Name of Allah; so do the reader, the traveller, and the craftsman. One short formula covers an unlimited number of actions, which is why it is said before meals, journeys, letters, and work. Some Sufi commentators read the omission further: as though Allah says, "I began through My Name, so you too begin through My Name." Whichever reading one prefers, the sentence works the same way on the tongue — it hands the coming act to Allah before the act begins.

**A Name No Creature Can Share**

Scholars debated whether *Allāh* is a proper name with no root, or a contraction of *al-ilāh*, "the god." The Qur'an shows the word was already known to the Arabs before Islam: asked who created them, the idolaters themselves answered *Allāh* (43:87). What they rejected was not the name but its exclusivity — they accepted Allah while giving partners a share in worship. Al-Ghazālī pointed out what makes the name unique: it gathers all the other divine names within it, and yet no other being can be called by it, even figuratively. A person may be described as *raḥīm*, merciful, or *malik*, a king; no one but Allah can be *Allāh*. The declaration *"He is Allah—One ˹and Indivisible˺"* (112:1) rests on exactly this. For the same reason the *basmalah* begins with the name that contains all the rest and then descends to two of its attributes — and the first of those attributes is mercy, not power or majesty, which sets the tone of the whole Book before a single law or warning appears.

**The Most Repeated Sentence in Islam**

The Prophet ﷺ told Abū Saʿīd ibn al-Muʿallā that al-Fātiḥah is the greatest sūrah of the Qur'an — the seven oft-repeated verses and the great Qur'an given to him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4474). He also made its recitation a condition of prayer: *"Whoever does not recite Al-Fatiha in his prayer, his prayer is invalid"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 756). These words are therefore among the most repeated in Muslim life, spoken at least seventeen times a day in the obligatory prayers alone. Their healing use is established too: a group of Companions recited the sūrah over a tribal chief who had been stung, took a flock of sheep as payment, and when they told the Prophet ﷺ he laughed and approved, asking, *"How do you know that Surat-al-Fatiha is a Ruqya?"* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5736). Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq called it the greatest verse of Allah's Book, and Shīʿī scholarship, following him, counts it as a full verse of al-Fātiḥah and of every sūrah that carries it. Behind all these positions is one judgment: the most repeated sentence in Islam was shaped to turn ordinary acts into an acknowledgment of Allah, spoken in a Name no creature shares.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds

**Praise Is Wider Than Thanks**

*Al-ḥamd* is usually translated "praise," and English loses a distinction Arabic keeps. Thanks (*shukr*) responds to a favour received; it is gratitude with a nameable cause. Praise responds to what someone *is*, apart from any gift — it recognises qualities whether or not the one praised has given anything. *Ḥamd* is therefore the wider word: every act of thanks is a kind of praise, but praise reaches past the record of favours into the character of the One praised. The grammar presses the same point. *Al-ḥamd* is definite — *the* praise, not *a* praise — and commentators read the definite article as a claim of ownership: all praise, wherever it is spoken and whoever seems to receive it, belongs in truth to Allah. When a Muslim says *al-ḥamdulillāh*, he is not adding one compliment to a pile; he is saying that every compliment ever paid finally settles on a single account.

**Allah Supplied the Words**

A theme runs through the commentaries: Allah praised Himself in this opening so that human beings could praise Him in His own words, since human words cannot carry the weight. The point is not that human praise is worthless, but that it is finite, and the finite cannot measure the Infinite. The Prophet ﷺ said in prayer that he could not count the praise due to Allah — Allah is as He has praised Himself. The Qur'an stages the exchange in a *ḥadīth qudsī*: when the servant recites *All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds*, Allah answers, *My servant has praised Me* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). Praise spoken by the creature is received and answered by the Creator; the sentence is not spoken into silence. In daily Muslim practice the same formula does quieter work: asked how he is, whatever his condition, the believer answers *al-ḥamdulillāh*, folding ease and hardship alike into a praise that does not depend on circumstances.

**Rabb: Owner, Manager, Nurturer**

"Lord" translates *rabb*, a word with three centres of meaning. It names a master who is obeyed; one who arranges affairs and keeps managing them; and an owner to whom what is owned belongs. Applied to Allah the three merge: He is the Master without peer, the Arranger of every creature's affairs, and the Owner of them all. A fourth shade comes from the same root: *tarbiyah*, cultivation or nurturing, as one raises a child or tends a field to its fullness. Allah as *rabb* is therefore not a distant landlord but the One who develops each thing from seed to maturity — bodies, minds, and souls included. That is why the word resists flat translation. "Lord" in English suggests rank and command; *rabb* carries ownership, governance, and growth at once. The *rabb* of a thing brought it into being, orders its middle, and owns its end.

**"The Worlds": Everything Other Than Allah**

*Al-ʿālamīn* is a plural with no singular of its own in Arabic, and commentators filled the gap in different ways. Some counted four communities: human beings, jinn, angels, and devils. Some narrowed it to humans and jinn alone, since only these two receive warners, and the Prophet ﷺ was sent as a warner to the worlds (25:1). Others widened it without limit: every generation of human beings, every species of creature, every level of existence from the earth through the seven heavens. Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī went furthest, noting that the space beyond this world has no limit and that Allah can bring into being worlds no human mind has imagined; on his reading the verse declares Allah Lord of every mode of being. The simplest summary holds all of them: the worlds are everything that exists besides Allah. The same word grounds the Prophet's mission, sent *"only as a mercy for the whole world"* (21:107).

**Praise Before the Asking**

Nothing has been requested yet. The first thing the worshipper says about Allah is not what he needs but what Allah is, and the *ḥadīth qudsī* of the divided prayer makes that structure explicit: the first half of al-Fātiḥah belongs to Allah as praise, and only the second half carries the servant's petition (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). The etiquette of supplication in Islam generalises the pattern: praise, then admission of servitude, then the request. The verse also answers a question a reader may raise — why praise Allah at all, when He has no need of it? Because the praise changes the one who gives it. A soul that first acknowledges the Lord of all worlds asks its questions from inside a world already governed, and its asking comes out differently: less demand, more trust. The two great formulas of Muslim life then divide time between them. The *basmalah* blesses an act at its start; *al-ḥamdulillāh* acknowledges Allah when the act is complete — after a meal, at the end of a journey, on hearing news. One opens the deed, the other closes it.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**One Root, Two Forms**

Both names come from *raḥmah* — mercy, and more precisely a mercy with tenderness in it, the impulse that moves a stronger being to care for a weaker one. Arabic builds intensity by changing the pattern of a root, and these two names use different patterns. *Raḥmān* follows the form that describes a state filling its subject completely; commentators therefore treat it as the more embracing of the two, mercy as a total condition. *Raḥīm* follows the form that carries constancy and repetition; its mercy keeps arriving, act after act, creature after creature. The pairing is not repetition but complement — a flood and a stream from the same rain. The two names also behave differently outside the divine vocabulary. *Al-Raḥmān* cannot be applied to a creature at all, literally or figuratively; it names the mercy by which existence itself is brought forth. *Al-Raḥīm*, by contrast, can describe creatures, and the Qur'an uses it of the Prophet ﷺ, *"gracious and merciful to the believers"* (9:128). Early Muslims took the claim of the false prophet Musaylimah to the title *Raḥmān* as theft of a name that belongs to Allah alone.

**Universal Mercy and Special Mercy**

Commentators divide the work between the two names. The mercy of *al-Raḥmān* is universal: it is the mercy by which creation comes to be and by which every living thing receives its provision, believer and denier, animal and angel alike. The Qur'an states its breadth without qualification: *"My mercy encompasses everything"* (7:156). The mercy of *al-Raḥīm* is particular: the continuing care by which each thing is kept, and above all the mercy Allah gives the believers as their portion — guidance in this life and pardon in the next. The division answers a question every reader eventually asks: if Allah is merciful, why is there suffering? The names distinguish the giving of existence and provision, which is universal, from the final mercy of nearness and forgiveness, which follows what creatures choose to do with what they were given.

**Why the Names Return Here**

A reader may ask what the second appearance of the names adds, since the *basmalah* already carried them. Part of the answer lies in position. Verse 2 declared Allah Lord of all worlds — every level of being, everything that might loosely be called space. Verse 4 declares Him Master of the Day of Judgment — all of time gathered to its final point. Verse 3 sits between the two, and the position states a claim without stating it: mercy fills the interval. Whatever exists, in whichever world, at whatever moment before that Day, exists within these two names. The *ḥadīth qudsī* of the divided prayer marks a further difference — at these names in verse 3, Allah's answer changes from *My servant has praised Me* to *My servant has lauded Me* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a), praise deepening into extolment. The repetition also guards against a misreading of what follows. Judgment heard alone can sound like menace; the two names placed immediately before it insist that the Master of that Day is the same One who is Compassionate and Merciful now, so that judgment arrives in its proper frame.

**The Breath of the Compassionate**

Sufi cosmology built its account of creation on the first of these names. The archetypes of all things held in divine knowledge — *al-aʿyān al-thābitah*, the immutable essences — were brought into existence when Allah breathed upon them what the tradition calls the Breath of the Compassionate, *Nafas al-Raḥmān*. On this view the world is not manufactured but exhaled by mercy; to exist at all is to be breathed out by compassion. The Qur'an supports the link between *al-Raḥmān* and the governance of the cosmos: *"the Most Compassionate, ˹Who is˺ established on the Throne"* (20:5). All the names of gentleness are manifestations of this mercy, and on this reading even the names of rigour conceal it rather than cancel it, since divine power never acts apart from divine kindness. The doctrine gives the verse its widest reach: the two names do not only describe how Allah deals with creatures, they explain why there are creatures.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment

**Mālik or Malik: Two Readings**

The first word of this verse is recited in two sound forms, both traced back to the Prophet ﷺ. *Mālik* means owner or master, one who holds a thing and disposes of it. *Malik* means king or sovereign, one whose command runs over a domain and its people. Commentators debated which is the stronger claim. One side reasoned that every king is a master but not every master is a king, so *malik* contains *mālik* and exceeds it. The other side answered that *mālik* is more comprehensive, because ownership implies direct power of disposal over the thing owned, while a king may rule a territory whose individual items belong to others. The debate is worth more than its outcome: the two readings together say that Allah owns that Day outright and reigns over it absolutely. A variant reading that survives scholarly disagreement usually does so because each form carries a meaning the other cannot absorb. On the Day in question both are true at once — there is no property that is not His and no court but His.

**Why Mastery Is Claimed Over a Single Day**

Lordship over all the worlds was already declared in verse 2; this verse narrows the claim to one day, and the narrowing enlarges it. In ordinary time authority is shared and borrowed: owners own, kings reign, judges judge, each within limits and for a term. The Day of Judgment is the moment every borrowed authority expires, and the verse announces who holds the field when the loans are called in. The Qur'an stages the scene as a question asked aloud over the whole assembly: *"Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme!"* (40:16). No answer competes, because no other claimant is left standing. Naming Allah Master of that particular Day therefore does two things: it guarantees the reckoning will happen, since the Day has an owner who will not let it pass, and it strips every worldly hierarchy of finality. The tyrant's court, the merchant's ledger, and the emperor's decree are all provisional arrangements. One Day comes when they end.

**Dīn: A Debt That Comes Due**

"Judgment" renders *dīn*, and the word's relatives show what kind of judgment is meant. *Dīn* is tied to obedience and to customary practice, and it is closely related to *dayn*, debt — the Qur'an uses *dayn* for a contracted loan (2:282), and from the same root comes *al-Dayyān*, the One Who Repays. The Day of *dīn* is therefore the day a debt falls due: existence, provision, and guidance were advanced to every soul, and that Day settles the account. The commentators settle on requital — every soul paid what its deeds earned. *"On that Day, Allah will give them their just penalty in full, and they will ˹come to˺ know that Allah ˹alone˺ is the Ultimate Truth"* (24:25). Another verse states the fairness of the settlement from the opposite side: *"every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged"* (2:281). Debt language makes judgment intelligible without making it arbitrary: what is repaid on that Day was borrowed here, item by item, choice by choice.

**The Earth Gripped, the Sky Rolled Up**

The Qur'an supplies the image of that Day's total possession: *"when on the Day of Judgment the ˹whole˺ earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand"* (39:67). A report preserved in the exegetical tradition fills in the proclamation — Allah grasps the earth, folds up the sky, and asks where the kings of the earth are now, where the tyrants are. Whether or not one cites the report, the verse already performs the dispossession: the two largest things human beings experience, ground and sky, are handled like possessions. The point presses on the one reciting al-Fātiḥah in a particular way. This line is spoken inside the prayer, standing, at least seventeen times a day; the worshipper rehearses the transfer of ownership daily. Judgment in the Qur'an is not a surprise sprung on an unprepared world. It is announced in advance, in the opening chapter, in the most repeated sentence of Muslim worship, so that whoever hears it has years to put his affairs in order.

**Reckon Yourselves Before You Are Reckoned**

The verse generated a discipline of self-examination. Asked who is the most intelligent of believers, the Prophet ﷺ answered, in a report preserved by the commentators, that they are those who remember death most often and prepare best for what comes after it. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb turned the answer into a rule: bring yourselves to account before you are brought to account, and weigh your deeds before they are weighed. The logic treats the Day of Judgment as a date one works backward from. A debt you know is coming changes how you borrow; an audit you know is scheduled changes how you keep your books. That is the practical function of this verse inside the prayer. The worshipper who has just praised Allah as Lord of all worlds and recited the two names of mercy is then told that the same Allah owns a Day of settlement. Mercy and mastery are not rivals here: the Day belongs to the Merciful, which is why the reckoning can still end in pardon, and why preparing for it is hope rather than dread.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help

**The Sūrah Turns and Speaks to Allah**

For four verses Allah has been spoken *about*: His name, His praise, His mercy, His Day. In this verse the grammar turns and Allah is spoken *to*. The shift from third person to second is the hinge of the whole sūrah, and the *ḥadīth qudsī* of the divided prayer marks it exactly — at this line Allah says, *This is between Me and My servant, and My servant will receive what he asks for* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a). Praise, in other words, matures into address. A God described remains at a distance; a God addressed is present, and the verse is built for that presence, with "You" placed at the front of both clauses. Muslim spirituality draws the consequence without need of a priestly intermediary: every worshipper, alone on a prayer mat, lives the direct I-Thou relation this verse models. It also explains the verse's liturgical power. Recited in every unit of prayer, it rehearses the moment description becomes conversation, and the one who says it is not reporting a theology but performing a relationship.

**"You Alone": Word Order as Tawḥīd**

Arabic marks emphasis by word order, and this verse uses the device at both joints. The pronoun *iyyāka* — You — is placed before the verbs "we worship" and "we ask for help." Neutral order would say *we worship You*; putting the object first restricts the action, so that the sense is "You, and You alone, we worship." The bracketed ˹alone˺ in the translation is not an addition but the meaning the syntax itself carries. Commentators read the fronting as *tawḥīd* in grammatical form: the sentence does not merely say whom the speakers worship, it excludes everyone else from being worshipped. The two halves of the verse then exclude in sequence — no partner in worship, and no other source relied on for help. Sincere religion in the Qur'an is exactly this double exclusivity: *"worship Allah ˹alone˺, being sincerely devoted to Him"* (39:2). Worship shared with an idol is not worship, and help sought from a created power as though it acted independently quietly undoes the second clause. The verse closes both doors in one line.

**Servitude Declared Before the Request**

The order of the two clauses is itself an argument. Worship is named first; the plea for help follows, and commentators explain why the sequence cannot be reversed. One does not approach a king with a request before acknowledging his authority; the declaration of servitude is what makes the petition proper rather than presumptuous. *ʿIbādah* is understood as the furthest degree of humility before Allah — the posture of the *ʿabd*, the servant, which is the real condition of every creature whether or not it admits the fact: *"There is none in the heavens or the earth who will not return to the Most Compassionate in full submission"* (19:93). To say *iyyāka naʿbudu* is to sign that reality willingly: the creature who must be a servant anyway chooses whose servant he will be. The request that follows — *wa-iyyāka nastaʿīn*, and You alone we ask for help — is then not a bargain struck with a stranger but a need voiced inside an acknowledged relationship.

**The Plural Voice of a Single Worshipper**

The verbs are first-person plural — *we* worship, *we* ask — even when the sūrah is recited by one person alone in a room. Commentators draw two lessons from the plural. The first is humility: the speaker dissolves his private "I" into the ranks of worshippers, standing before Allah as one member of a body that stretches across all the worlds praised in verse 2. The second is representation: the worshipper prays on behalf of others, asking for all what he asks for himself. Congregational prayer makes the grammar audible, rows of individuals speaking one plural sentence, but the plural is not borrowed from the congregation — it belongs to the sūrah even in solitude. A Muslim praying alone still says "guide *us*," and in doing so carries his family, his community, and the whole chain of believers into a prayer that could have been selfish.

**Three Grades of Worshippers**

Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq sorted worshippers into three classes using this clause as the test. Some worship Allah out of fear, and that is the worship of slaves. Some worship seeking reward, and that is the worship of hired workers. Some worship out of love, and that is the worship of the free — the most excellent of the three. All recite the same words; the words weigh differently on each tongue. The classification does not reject fear and hope, which the Qur'an commands, but ranks them: servitude performed for a wage is valid, while servitude performed because the Master deserves it is complete. The verse then works as a mirror. Recited honestly, it asks the speaker which grade his worship occupies, because the fronted "You alone" permits only one object, and a heart worshipping for reward alone has quietly made the reward its object.

**Even the Worship Needs the Help That Is Asked**

The second clause carries a reflexivity the commentators press: the help requested is, first of all, help to perform the worship just declared. No creature worships Allah by his own power; the ability to say *iyyāka naʿbudu* truthfully is itself a gift, so the sentence asks for the very thing it enacts. Servitude comes first, and the plea sustains the servitude. The Qur'an states the dependence plainly: *"For Allah is the Self-Sufficient, whereas you stand in need ˹of Him˺"* (47:38). Practical commentary extends the request beyond ritual to help in every affair, from earning a living to resisting temptation, since all of it falls under worship understood as obedience with one's whole life. The *ḥadīth qudsī*'s promise, *My servant will receive what he asks for* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a), attaches to this half of the verse precisely because this is where need is admitted. Praise glorifies Allah; the admission of helplessness is what opens the hand.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path

**A Request for Steadiness, Not for a Map**

The petition is *ihdinā* — guide us — and commentators read it as a prayer for continuation rather than for first contact. A person already praying, already worshipping, already asking Allah's help in verse 5 does not need to be told where the road is; he needs to be kept on it. Guidance in the Qur'an has layers: guidance to the road, and guidance along the road, and the second never ends — *"their Lord guides them by their faith"* is promised to believers, not withheld until they arrive (10:9). The request is therefore for perseverance and firmness of foot, and implicitly for nearness, since to be guided is to be led *to* Allah. Some commentators gloss the verse as "incline our hearts toward You." The frequency of the asking matches its content: al-Fātiḥah is obligatory in every unit of prayer, so a practising Muslim repeats this petition at least seventeen times a day. A need asked seventeen times a day is a need that never expires; the verse treats guidance not as a possession but as a supply.

**Ṣirāṭ: A Road With No Bend in It**

The word for path here is *ṣirāṭ*, and its sense is exact: a road without crookedness, deviation, or confusing branches. The Qur'an reserves strong language for this quality and claims it for Allah's own way: *"Indeed, that is My Path—perfectly straight. So follow it and do not follow other ways, for they will lead you away from His Way"* (6:153). The contrast inside the verse is the definition — one path, many ways; straightness against scatter. Commentators describe the *ṣirāṭ* as a mean as well: not worldliness on one side and not crushing asceticism on the other, but a middle way that joins outward law with inward sincerity, so that the body stands in servitude while the heart stays free. The community formed on it is called *"an upright community"* (2:143), a witness-people whose moderation is not compromise but the shortest, surest line between a servant and his Lord.

**What the Commentators Say the Path Is**

Given a petition this central, commentators wanted to name its object, and they named it differently. Some held that the straight path is the Qur'an itself. Some said it is Islam as a whole. Some said it is the Prophet ﷺ, and this reading has textual support, since the Qur'an tells him, *"you are truly leading ˹all˺ to the Straight Path"* (42:52). Shīʿī commentaries often identify the path with the Imams, beginning with ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, while some Sunnī works read it as the Prophet and his two successors, Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. Weighed against one another, the readings converge more than they conflict: each names what that school holds to be the reliable means of keeping a person on the road. The Qur'an's own usage decides the general case: the path is Allah's (6:153), it is carried by revelation and embodied in the Prophet, and it is walked by the community. Hūd's confession adds the deepest dimension: *"Surely my Lord's Way is perfect justice"* (11:56). Allah Himself is upon a straight path, so to be guided along it is to walk with Allah toward Allah.

**A Parable of Two Walls and Open Doors**

The commentaries preserve a prophetic parable that pictures this verse. Allah sets out a straight path with a wall on each side. In the walls are open doors hung with curtains, and a caller at the gate of the path cries: O people, stay on the path, do not deviate. A second caller, above the path, warns anyone reaching for a door: woe to you, do not open it — if you open it, you will fall through. The explanation attached to the parable is precise. The path is Islam; the two walls are Allah's limits; the curtained doors are His prohibitions; the caller at the gate is the Book of Allah; and the caller above the path is the admonition Allah places in the heart of every Muslim. The image does real work: guidance along the straight path is not open-country navigation but staying between two boundaries, with temptation dressed as doorways. Every prohibition is a curtained door, and the heart's inner caller is what makes the outer Book audible.

**Guidance Asked After Worship Declared**

The sequence of verses 5 and 6 carries an argument. Worship and the request for help come first; the prayer for guidance follows. The ordering says that guidance is given to servants, not to spectators — the road is shown to those already standing in servitude. It also protects the petition from becoming curiosity. "Show me the straight path" can be asked by someone shopping for ideas; "guide us along the straight path" is asked by people whose worship has already committed them to walking. The commentators connect the two verses reflexively: the help requested in verse 5 is largely the guidance requested in verse 6, since the greatest help Allah gives a servant is keeping him on the road. And the prayer is in the plural, so each worshipper asks guidance for the community along with himself — a reminder that the straight path is a road travelled in company.

**The Bridge That Tests the Prayer**

A second strand of commentary reads the verse with the afterlife in view. The straight path has a counterpart at the final stage of judgment: the traverse every human being and jinn must cross before the Garden or the Fire. The reports describe it as finer than a hair and sharper than a sword. Some will cross it like the wind, some fly over it like birds, others trot across like camels; some will only manage to crawl, and some will be cast off into the Fire. On this reading the daily petition is a rehearsal — the worshipper asks now for the steadiness of foot he will need then, and the moral straightness of this life determines the crossing of the next. Whether one takes the eschatological reading or stays with the ethical one, the two agree on the mechanics: the path is narrow, the deviations are many, and the crossing is not achieved by unaided human balance. That is exactly why the verse is phrased as a request to Allah rather than a resolution of the will.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray

**The Path Is Named by Its People**

Verse 6 asked for the straight path; this verse identifies it by the company walking on it. The definition is social before it is geographic — a road is known by who travels it. The Qur'an gives the roster elsewhere in matching terms: *"And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous—what honourable company!"* (4:69). Four ranks, ascending in intimacy and all sharing one description: people on whom Allah has conferred *niʿmah*, favour. The blessing is not primarily material. Commentators gloss the blessed as those whom Allah purified so they could draw near to Him, and those kept mindful of the Law's limits while tasting spiritual realities — knowledge without transgression, experience without lawlessness. Naming the path by its walkers answers a practical question the bare petition leaves open, since straightness could be claimed by anyone. The verse anchors the claim in a named, historical procession — from Ādam through the prophets to the truthful and the martyrs of every age — and makes belonging to it the test.

**Two Ways Off the Road, Not One**

The verse defines the path by negation as well, and the negation has two parts: not the path of those who incurred displeasure, nor of those who are astray. These are genuinely different failures. Some commentators map them onto kinds of sin: disbelief, which earns wrath, and lesser wrongdoing, which is wandering. Others see moral temperaments — the heedless and the dissolute. The classical pairing, transmitted through many commentaries, distinguishes those who knew and refused from those who lost the way without knowing it: the first term describes a will set against knowledge, the second describes knowledge lost or never gained. The distinction has diagnostic force for the one praying, since a soul can fail by defiance or by drift, and the two failures call for different remedies — one is cured by repentance, the other by knowledge. Asking to be kept from both, the worshipper admits both risks: the capacity for rebellion and the capacity for careless wandering live in the same heart.

**The Disagreement Over Who Is Meant**

A widely reported interpretation, resting on a saying not graded at the highest level of authenticity, identifies those who incurred displeasure with the Jews and those who are astray with the Christians. Its supporters anchor it in Qur'anic usage: wrath-language attaches repeatedly to episodes involving the Children of Israel — as in the rebuke over refusing manna and demanding lentils and onions, where they *"invited the displeasure of Allah"* (2:61) — and the warning *"nor follow the vain desires of those who went astray before ˹you˺. They misled many and strayed from the Right Way"* (5:77) is addressed to People of the Book. Other commentators resist the narrowing and read both terms as moral states open to any community, Muslims included. Weighing the two: the historical application is legitimate where the Qur'an itself uses the language, but the general reading is stronger for the purpose of the prayer. Al-Fātiḥah is recited by Muslims seventeen times a day about themselves; a petition that described only other religions would work as a boast, and the sūrah's grammar — first-person plural, "guide us" — rules that out.

**Why Wrath Has No Named Agent**

A grammatical detail in this verse has drawn sustained attention. For the blessed, an agent is named: those *You* have blessed — Allah's own act, in the past tense, complete and certain. For the people of wrath the construction is passive: *al-maghḍūb ʿalayhim*, those upon whom anger has come, with no one named as angry. For the last group the verse says simply *al-ḍāllīn*, the astray, naming neither agent nor outcome. The asymmetry is theology in syntax. Blessing is attributed directly to Allah; wrath is left unattributed, in keeping with the Qur'anic principle that Allah does not wrong people — they wrong themselves (10:44). The passive also leaves a door open: the verse says these people are subject to wrath, not that wrath has closed over them. Judgment has not fallen while the prayer is being said, and Allah guides to Himself whoever turns back (13:27). Commentators read the withheld agent as a mark of mercy on the very sentence that speaks of anger.

**Straying Is Wider Than Rebellion**

The second category, *ḍalāl* — wandering, being astray — is the Qur'an's broader term, and its range surprises. It covers the deliberate exchange of belief for disbelief. It also covers straying by mistake or inexperience: Mūsā says of himself, before his mission, *"I did it then, lacking guidance"* (26:20), and Allah reminds the Prophet ﷺ, *"Did He not find you unguided then guided you?"* (93:7). Between those poles sits everyone who simply does not live by revelation, whether from defiance or from distance. That breadth is why the prayer asks to be kept from it in the plural and daily: straying rarely announces itself, and the slide from knowledge into heedlessness needs no decision. Read with the verse before it, the pair of negations covers the whole space of failure — refusing the road, losing it, or never finding it — and leaves the petition with one positive content: to walk as the blessed walk. The psychology of the term is captured in the Qur'an's question: who is more astray than one who follows his own desires without guidance from Allah (28:50)?

**Āmīn: The Seal the Angels Answer**

The sūrah ends where prayer ends — with a word that is not part of the Qur'an. When the reciter finishes *nor of those who are astray*, established practice is to say *āmīn*, and the Prophet ﷺ commanded it with a promise attached: say *āmīn* when the imam says it, for whoever's *āmīn* coincides with the *āmīn* of the angels, his past sins are forgiven (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 780). The word is generally understood as "O Allah, answer," a seal set on the petition like a signature at the foot of a letter. Some held it means "so shall it be," close to the Hebrew *amen*; a few even proposed it is a name of Allah, though the first explanation is the standard one. Not part of the Qur'anic text, it is kept deliberately outside the recitation and spoken after a pause so that no one confuses it with revelation — yet its virtue is prophetic and its timing angelic. The sūrah ends as it began, in the liturgy of mercy: a servant asks, the angels answer at the same instant, and the asking itself becomes forgiveness.
