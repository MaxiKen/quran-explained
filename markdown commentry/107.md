# Sūrah al-Māʿūn (Chapter 107) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Denial of the Deen and the Repulse of the Orphan**

Al-Māʿūn, "The Small Kindnesses" or "The Daily Necessities," is the one-hundred-and-seventh chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 7 verses. Classical exegetes note that it bridges the Makkan and Medinan periods: verses 1–3 were revealed in Makkah concerning the cruelty of pagan aristocrats toward orphans, while verses 4–7 were revealed in Madinah concerning the ostentatious prayers and hollow hypocrisy of the Munāfiqūn. It opens with an arresting, provocative moral question: *“Have you seen the one who denies the Recompense / the Religion (*Ara'ayta al-ladhī yukadhdhibu bi-l-dīn*)?”* (107:1). In seven searing, unforgettable lines, Sūrah al-Māʿūn constructs an unshakeable Qur'anic equation: establishing that genuine faith (*Dīn*) is inextricably linked to socio-economic compassion, declaring that external ritual prayer is cursed and worthless if accompanied by ostentation and the refusal to lend a simple household bowl or needle (*al-māʿūn*) to a neighbor in need.

**The Chieftain Who Repulsed the Orphan**

The historical occasions of al-Māʿūn are preserved regarding pagan chieftains such as al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wā'il, Abū Jahl, and Abū Sufyān. Abū Sufyān was accustomed to slaughtering two fattened camels every week for lavish banquets with his aristocratic peers; on one occasion, a hungry orphan boy came to him begging for a morsel of meat, whereupon Abū Sufyān struck the boy across the face with his walking stick and drove him violently from his door! Verses 1–3 descended to brand this behavior: *“That is the one who repulses the orphan (*fa-dhālika al-ladhī yaduʿʿu al-yatīm*), and does not encourage the feeding of the poor!”* The sūrah then shifts its gaze to the hypocrites in Madinah, who stood in the front ranks of the mosque to be admired by men, yet in private refused to assist their neighbors.

**The Structural Architecture and the Curse of Hollow Prayer**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Māʿūn moves across two symmetrical movements:
1. The Denial of the Deen and Social Cruelty (1–3): Exposes the person who claims to have religion or denies judgment day, defining his true spiritual identity by two anti-social vices: violently repulsing the orphan (*yaduʿʿu al-yatīm*) and refusing to encourage the feeding of the destitute (*wa-lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿāmi al-miskīn*).
2. The Curse of Ostentatious Prayer (4–7): Utters the shocking, celebrated divine curse: *“So woe to those who pray (*Fa-waylun li-l-muṣallīn*)! Those who are heedless of their prayer (*alladhīna hum ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn*); those who make show of their deeds (*alladhīna hum yurā'ūn*), and withhold small kindnesses (*wa-yamnaʿūna al-māʿūn*)!”*

**The Liturgy of Compassion: Al-Māʿūn**

The profound philosophical revolution of Sūrah al-Māʿūn is that it redefines *Dīn* (religion). To the shallow mind, religion is merely an external checklist of words, gestures, and formal rituals. But Sūrah al-Māʿūn proclaims *Woe to those who pray!*—declaring that if prayer does not soften the human heart toward the orphan, make the eyes weep for the starving poor, and inspire the hands to lend small household tools (*al-māʿūn*—such as an axe, a pot, salt, or water) to a needy neighbor, then that prayer is not worship; it is theatrical vanity (*riyā'*). Authentic faith is proven not at the front of the congregation, but in the tender mercy shown to the most defenseless members of society.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:1

> Have you seen the one who denies the ˹final˺ Judgment

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Question the Book Uses to Fix the Gaze**

The sūrah opens with the question the Book reserves for the moments when it wants a person held in view. "Have you seen" is a form that does not ask for information; it asks for recognition. The audience knows the type being described, and the question is the instrument that turns a general statement into a portrait. The sūrah of the elephant opened on the same device, directing the gaze to a past event; here the gaze is directed to a present person, and the person is defined in the same breath. The question is the frame, and the rest of the sūrah is the picture inside the frame. Nothing in the verse is a report. Everything in it is a pointing, and the pointing falls on someone the audience can name.

**Dīn: The Religion and the Judgment in One Word**

The translation renders the object of the denial as "the ˹final˺ Judgment," and the Arabic word, dīn, carries more than that. It is the word for the religion as it is sent, the whole system of account and accountability, and it is the word for the judgment itself, the day on which the account is settled. The two meanings are not two readings of the verse; they are the two faces of one denial. The one who denies the judgment denies the final form of the religion, and the one who rejects the religion as a system of account has, in the effect of the rejection, denied the judgment. The exegetes noted the double, and the sūrah needs it: the person described in the verse is identified by a denial that covers the unseen and, through the unseen, everything that follows from it in the conduct of the days.

**A Person Defined by a Single Denial**

The verse identifies a whole human being by one clause, and the choice of the clause is the first argument of the sūrah. The man is not named, not described, not placed in a scene; he is defined by what he denies. The sūrah's claim, stated in its opening, is that the denial of the account is so central to a person that it can stand in for the person. Everything the following verses say, the repelled orphan, the unfed poor, the empty prayer, the withheld kindness, is the shape that the denial takes when it is embodied. The verse is the root, and the rest of the sūrah is the tree. The order is deliberate: the Book states the root first, because a tree that is understood from its root is not misread at its branches.

**The Question Passes Through the Prophet**

The form of the question addresses the Prophet, as the opening of the sūrah of the elephant addressed him, and the address works in the same way. The Prophet is the point through which the question reaches the city of Makkah, and the city is the place in which the person described can be seen. The question is not an abstraction for the audience; it is a man, or a set of men, in the streets of the same city. The sūrah's power comes from the gap between the form of the verse, which is a general description, and its occasion, which is a specific city full of people who fit the description. The "have you seen" is real, because the answer, in Makkah, was a list of names that the audience did not need the sūrah to supply.

**Denial as the Root of the Branches**

The exegetes who read the sūrah as a whole took the first verse to be the root of the sins that follow, and the structure confirms the reading. A person who does not believe in the account treats the orphan as a cost, because no account will be kept of the cost. The same person does not feed the poor, because the feeding has no witness. The same person prays without presence, because the prayer is a transaction and the transaction has no partner. The sūrah does not say "the denier also repels the orphan." It says "that is the one," and the identification is the claim: the denial and the conduct are not two facts about the man; they are one fact, seen from the inside and from the outside. The first verse states the inside, and the rest of the sūrah states the outside. The structure explains the shortness of the sūrah. A text that states the root does not need to argue each branch, because the audience who knows the root will recognize the branches when they are named. The sūrah names the denial and then walks through the conduct, and the walking is enough, because the walking is performed in a city where every step of it can be seen. The six verses are short, and they are complete, because the root carries them.

**The Portrait and Its Order**

The order of the sūrah is the order of a diagnosis. The first verse names the condition; the next verses name the symptoms, one by one, in the order in which they appear in the life of the person: at the gate, at the table, in the prayer, in the eyes of the onlookers, and at last in the hand that withholds the smallest thing. The sūrah moves from the most solemn denial to the most trivial refusal, and the movement is the argument: the denial that begins in the unseen ends in the refusal of a borrowed tool. The first verse sets the direction of the movement, and the last verse of the sūrah, which carries its title, is where the movement arrives. The portrait is complete in six verses, and its first word is the question that holds the gaze.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:2

> That is the one who repulses the orphan

**Expanded Commentary**

**From the Unseen to the Doorstep**

The second verse moves the sūrah from the unseen to the visible in a single step. The first verse named a denial, and this verse gives the denial a face: "that is the one." The demonstrative is the hinge of the sūrah. It takes the abstract figure of the denier and places him at a door, where a child is standing. The orphan is the first test named, and the choice is not arbitrary. The orphan is the person who has no protector, and the treatment of the person without a protector is the cleanest measure of a man's relation to the One who protects. The verse does not say the denier hates orphans; it says he repels one, and the single act, performed on the single child, is taken to stand for the whole character. The demonstrative does a second work. It is the far pointer, the word that places a figure at a distance, and the distance is the distance of the type: the one is not a neighbor to be described but a figure to be seen, held at arm's length by the word that points at him. The sūrah keeps the figure at that distance throughout, and the keeping is the method: the portrait is not brought close enough to be defended, and it is held far enough to be recognized.

**The Verb of Roughness**

The Arabic verb behind "repulses" is kādhara, and it is a verb of roughness. It means to drive off, to push away, to turn back with a rough hand, and it does not describe a man who forgets a child at his door or a man who cannot help it. It describes the act of repulsion itself, the motion of the hand that sends the child back. The choice of the verb is the second argument of the sūrah after the choice of the test. The denier is not passive in this verse; he is active, and the act is performed on the weakest person in the room. The roughness is the point, because the roughness is possible only where there is no answer: the orphan cannot appeal the repulsion, and the man who repels knows that he will not be answered for it.

**Why the Orphan Is the Measure**

The Book returns to the orphan again and again, and the repetition is the weight. In the sūrah that opens the same circle of warnings, the orphan is the first of the charges: "Absolutely not! In fact, you are not ˹even˺ gracious to the orphan" (89:17), and the charge continues with the poor and with the inheritances that are devoured (89:18–19). The orphans' property is a standing subject of the Book's law, and the reason for the standing is the one this verse shows: the orphan's claim is a pure claim, a right with no defender attached to it. A society's relation to its Lord is read in the way it treats the child it has nothing to gain from, and the sūrah of al-Māʿūn takes that reading and makes it the first station of its portrait.

**The Occasions the Tradition Records**

The reports that surround the verse give it occasions, and the occasions are recorded in the two forms the tradition preserves. Some of the early exegetes linked the opening of the sūrah to al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, a man of Makkah whose conduct fitted the description, and another report, carried on the authority of Ibn Jurayj, gives a scene: an orphan came to Abū Sufyān asking for something, and Abū Sufyān struck the child with a stick, and the verse was revealed on that occasion. The two reports differ in the name, and they agree in the point: the sūrah was first applied to a person, and its application to a person does not limit it. The description in the verse is general, and the general is what the sūrah means to carry: the type, not the name, is the subject of the portrait.

**The Repulsion That Answers the Denial**

The second verse is the first branch of the root stated in the first, and the connection is the sūrah's claim. The man who denies the account treats the orphan as a cost, because in his accounting the repulsion has no price. The child at the door is a claim, and a claim is a form of the account, a small present instance of the larger one; the denier cannot see the claim, because he has denied the account that would make the claim visible. The repulsion is therefore not an addition to the denial; it is the denial in the act of being performed. The sūrah's structure states the relation in its grammar: the root, and then "that is the one," and then the act. The act is the root, made visible at the door.

**The Weakest Person as the First Test**

The order of the tests in the sūrah begins with the weakest person in the community, and the beginning is the method. The orphan cannot pay for his own help, cannot threaten, cannot appeal; his only weapon is the claim itself, and the claim is addressed to the conscience and, through the conscience, to the One who keeps the account. The man who repels the orphan is making his position on that claim, and the position is his position on the account, because the claim is a small form of it. The sūrah does not need to say that the denial of the judgment appears in the treatment of the child; it shows it, in a single verb, on a single occasion, at a single door. The first test is the weakest test, and it is placed first, because it is the one that cannot be defended by any interest.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:3

> and does not encourage the feeding of the poor

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Double Absence**

The third verse is the quietest verse of the sūrah, and it contains two refusals in one. The man described does not feed the poor himself, and he does not urge others to feed them. The second refusal is the one that is easy to miss, and it is the one that the verse's grammar foregrounds: the verb is not "does not feed" but "does not encourage," the verb of incitement, of setting another's hand to work. The man blocks the door in two places: with his own hand at the table, and with his silence at the door of the other man. The verse counts both, and the counting is the point: the withholding of the giving and the withholding of the encouragement are two deeds, and the second is as real as the first.

**The Right That Stands in the Wealth**

The verse admits a reading that sharpens it: the man does not urge the giving of the food that belongs to the poor, and the food is theirs in the sense that the poor have a share in it. The Book fixes that share elsewhere as a standing rule. The believers are described as the ones who "give the rightful share of their wealth to the beggar and the poor" (70:24–25), and the wealth of the believer is stated to carry a due for the beggar and the deprived (51:19). The poor in the third verse of al-Māʿūn are therefore not receiving a gift from the man's surplus; they are receiving a portion of what is already, in the accounting of the Book, theirs. The man's refusal is a refusal of a payment, and the sūrah places the refusal in the same column as the repulsion of the orphan, because it is the same act: the withholding of a claim that stands in the wealth.

**Silence as a Deed**

The second refusal of the verse, the failure to encourage, is treated by the sūrah as a deed of its own, with its own weight, and the treatment is the novel part of the verse. A man who gives nothing and says nothing is, in ordinary accounting, a man who has done nothing; the sūrah places him in the column of the doers. His silence is a teaching. The house that does not urge the feeding teaches the neighbor that the feeding is not owed, and the neighbor's hand is cooled by the teaching. The sūrah's judgment of the man therefore covers two generations of effect: what he withholds from his own table, and what his withholding withdraws from the tables of others. The verse is the Book's earliest statement, in this form, that the failure to move another's hand is a participation in the want, and that the teaching of a household counts in the same column as the deed of a household.

**The Excuse the Book Already Rebutted**

The refusal of the third verse has a reason that the Book names and rejects in another place. When the call to give is made, the answer that comes back is theological in form: "Why should we feed those whom Allah could have fed if He wanted to?" (36:47). The excuse is the denial of the first verse in a practical dress: if the provision of the poor is in God's hand alone, the hand of the man is redundant, and the feeding is an interference in the provision. The sūrah of al-Māʿūn does not quote the excuse; it describes the man the excuse produces, the man who neither feeds nor urges. The connection is the argument: the excuse is the root, and the man of the third verse is the fruit, standing at the table with his hand closed.

**The Two Objects of the Claim**

The second and third verses together name the two objects on which the claim of the community is tested: the orphan, who is repelled, and the poor, who is not fed. The first is an act, the second is an omission; the first is rough, the second is quiet; the first is performed on a child, the second on an adult. The pairing covers the whole range of the failure: the man who does harm, and the man who does not help, and the man who does neither himself nor moves another to do it. The sūrah's portrait is built on these two verses, and the rest of the sūrah descends from them to the prayer and to the smallest kindness. The two objects are the children of the first verse's denial, and the sūrah names them in the order of their weakness: the orphan first, because the orphan's claim is the purest, and the poor second, because the poor's claim is the most numerous.

**The Verse That Covers the Bystander**

The last feature of the third verse is the one that makes it wider than its words. The description fits the man who is stingy, and it fits the man who is not stingy with his own hand but is hostile to the giving itself, the man who discourages the feeder, who makes light of the feed, who turns the table of the neighbor against the poor. The sūrah's use of "encourage" captures the second kind, and the second kind is the one the first kind is made of: the open stinger is rare, and the cool voice that teaches the withholding is common. The verse is therefore a judgment on the atmosphere of a house as much as on the hand of a man, and the atmosphere is what the sūrah, in its remaining verses, will show to be the same as the prayer: performed in public, and emptied in private.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:4

> So woe to those ˹hypocrites˺ who pray

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word of the Judgment Arrives**

The fourth verse is the point at which the sūrah stops describing and starts judging. "Woe" is the Book's term of the verdict, a word of the warning that the exegetes connect with the deepest wadi of the fire, and its arrival changes the register of the text. The first three verses were a portrait: the denial, the orphan, the poor. The fourth verse is the sentence. The "so" that opens it is the hinge: the woe follows from what has been described, and it is the consequence of the denial and its fruits. The sūrah has built a chain, and the fourth verse is the end of the chain, the link that turns the description into the judgment.

**The Praying That the Woe Falls On**

The target of the woe is the most surprising word in the verse: the praying. The woe does not fall on the one who abandons prayer; it falls on the one who performs it, and the surprise is the point of the sūrah. The exegetes recorded the range of readings, and the range is wide. The majority held that the verse describes the ones whose prayer brings them no benefit, because they are heedless of it while they perform it. Another reading, common among the early commentators, applies it to the hypocrites, the ones who pray in public and not in private. A third covers the ones who miss the appointed times or leave the bows and the prostrations unfinished. A fourth reaches even further: the ones who perform the prayer in full but perform it without humility, without contemplation, without the remembrance of the one to whom it is offered. The four readings agree on the point that divides them from the abandonment: the outward form is present, and the substance is absent.

**The Prophetic Description of the Empty Prayer**

The form the sūrah describes is given its sharpest picture in a report the tradition received as sound: the one who sits and watches the sun until it is between the two horns of Satan, and then stands and pecks out four prayers, in which he remembers God only a little (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 622). The verb of the report is the verb of a bird, to peck, and the four prayers are reduced to four pecks, the motion without the life. The report is the illustration of the sūrah's verse: the prayer performed at the last moment of its time, in a hurry, without the presence that makes the prayer a prayer, is the prayer the woe is aimed at. The sūrah and the report agree in the diagnosis: the form can be complete, and the prayer can still be empty, and the emptiness is what the woe reaches.

**Why the Prayer Is the Test**

The sūrah chooses the prayer as the station of the judgment, and the choice is the method. The prayer is the act in which the relation to the unseen is performed in the seen: it is the meeting that the day keeps with the Lord, and it is the act that no one can see the inside of. The orphan at the door and the poor at the table can be performed for the eye, and the performance for the eye is itself the vice; the prayer is the act whose eye is God alone, and the sūrah holds the prayer up because it is the act in which the hypocrisy of the first verse, the denial of the account, shows itself in its purest form. A man can feed the poor for the city and still be empty; he cannot pray for the city, because the prayer has no city. The woe falls on the one who brings the city into the prayer.

**The "So" That Carries the Chain**

The conjunction at the opening of the verse is the smallest word in the sūrah and one of its most loaded. The woe is not stated as a new fact; it is stated as the consequence of the three preceding verses. The denial of the account, the repulsion of the orphan, the withholding of the feeding: these are the grounds, and the woe is the sentence that the grounds produce. The sūrah's structure is a syllogism in four verses, and the "so" is the therefore. The form matters, because the sūrah refuses to let the judgment stand alone. A woe without grounds is a threat; a woe with grounds is a verdict, and the grounds are the three verses that precede it. The sūrah's argument is complete at this point, and the remaining verses will do only one thing: specify the form of the prayer that the woe reaches.

**The Wall Between the Two Halves**

The fourth verse is the division of the sūrah. The first half, from the denial to the poor, is the portrait of the man; the second half, from the woe to the last verse, is the specification of the prayer. The division falls exactly where the register changes, from the description of the conduct to the judgment of the worship, and the change is the claim of the sūrah: the conduct and the worship are one, and the judgment of the one is the judgment of the other. The man who repels the orphan is the man whose prayer is empty, and the sūrah states the identity in its structure. The fourth verse is the wall between the two halves, and the word on the wall is "woe," the one word in the sūrah that belongs to the unseen even while it stands in the middle of the seen.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:5

> yet are unmindful of their prayers

**Expanded Commentary**

**The State Named in One Word**

The fifth verse gives the reason for the woe in a single word: the praying ones are unmindful of their prayers. The Arabic is ghāfilūn, the ones in the state of ghafla, the heedlessness, the absence of attention, and the word is one of the Book's standing terms for the condition of the heart that has not received the reminder. The verse does not say the prayers are omitted, or late, or shortened; it says the persons are absent from them. The body is at the prayer, and the person is elsewhere: in the market, in the rank, in the conversation that ended before the standing. The state is the defect, and the state is named because the state is what the woe is aimed at.

**The Heedlessness That Moves the Prayer**

The heedlessness of the verse is not a failure of the form; it is a removal of the person from the form. The prayer remains, in its motions, its recitations, its positions; what is removed is the one who was supposed to be doing it. The sūrah's diagnosis is therefore the inverse of the ordinary one. The common assumption is that the prayer is lost when it is not performed, and the sūrah states the other loss: the prayer is lost when it is performed by a person who is not there. The motions continue, and the prayer is over. The distinction is the hard part of the verse, because it makes the judgment internal: no witness is needed, no count of the prostrations is needed, because the absence is in the one who prays, and the one who prays is the only judge of it.

**The Meeting the Day Keeps**

The prayer, in the Book's own account, is the meeting that the day keeps with the Lord, and the sūrah's verse judges the meeting by the presence of the one who keeps it. A meeting at which one of the parties is absent is not a meeting; it is the shape of one. The unmindful praying one keeps the shape of the meeting through the day, five times, and is absent from all of them, and the sūrah's claim is that the shape, without the presence, is not the thing. The claim is supported by the Book's own contrast, which it states in the description of the believers: the ones who are "consistently performing their prayers" (70:23) and the ones who are "˹properly˺ observant of their prayers" (70:34). The contrast is not between the many prayers and the few; it is between the prayers with the person and the prayers without. The sūrah of al-Māʿūn takes the second column and names its state. The five prayers divide the day into five appointments, and the division is the form of the presence: the day that keeps its meetings is the day in which the person is present with the Lord at five fixed points, and the day that misses them is the day in which the person is alone. The heedlessness of the verse is therefore not a defect in one of the five; it is the absence from the structure of the day, the structure that makes the day a worship and not a sequence.

**The Same Heedlessness at the Gate**

The fifth verse is the explanation of the second, and the connection is the sūrah's method. The man who is absent from the prayer is the man who is present at the gate of the orphan only in his hand, and the hand does the repulsion while the person is elsewhere. The heedlessness is one state, and it appears in two places: in the prayer, where it empties the worship, and at the door, where it permits the roughness. The sūrah does not need to argue the connection; it states the two verses in succession, and the succession is the argument. The state of ghafla is the root of the conduct and the root of the empty worship, and the fifth verse names the state that the second verse showed in the act. The portrait is closed: the denial of the first verse produces the state, and the state produces both the repulsion and the empty prayer.

**The Presence That Is the Whole of the Prayer**

The verse implies its own standard, and the standard is the presence of the person in the act. The Book's description of the prayer of the believers is a description of attention: the bows and the prostrations are given with the body, and the remembrance is given with the heart, and the two are one. The unmindful praying one gives the body and withholds the heart, and the sūrah's woe reaches exactly that division. The standard is not perfection, because the standard is stated in terms of the presence, not the flawlessness; it is the presence of the person in the meeting, the keeping of the appointment with the one to whom the meeting is kept. The verse is the Book's shortest statement of the doctrine that the prayer is a presence, and the doctrine is the one the woe is the expression of.

**The Semicolon That Holds the List**

The verse ends in a continuation, not a stop. The translation marks the verse with a semicolon, and the grammar of the Arabic is the same: the state of the unmindful praying ones is not a complete description; it is the first item of a list, and the next two verses add the items. The list that begins here ends at the last verse of the sūrah, and the list is the specification of the woe: the unmindfulness of the prayer, the performance for the eye, and the withholding of the small kindness. The fifth verse is the first of the three specifications, and its position is the order of the diagnosis: the state first, then the motive, then the final proof in the hand. The sūrah's second half is a list of three, and the list opens on the word for the absence.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:6

> those who ˹only˺ show off

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Motive Named in the Middle of the List**

The sixth verse adds the second item to the list that the fifth opened, and the item is a motive: the praying ones show off. The Arabic term is the term the Book uses for riāʾ, the performance of the act for the eye of the onlooker, and the naming of the motive is the deepening of the diagnosis. The unmindfulness of the previous verse was the absence of the person from the prayer; the showing off of this verse is the presence of the person for the wrong witness. The two verses together cover the two failures of the witness: the prayer without God, and the prayer with the crowd. The list of the sūrah's second half is now two items long, and the two items are the two faces of the same division: the act performed for an audience that is not the Lord.

**The Book's Own Description of the Same Prayer**

The form the verse names is described in the Book in a verse that is its parallel: the hypocrites, when they stand for the prayer, stand half-heartedly, for the seeing of the people, and they remember God only a little (4:142). The parallel is exact in its details: the half-heartedness, the eye of the people, the little remembrance. The two texts state the same prayer from two points, and the agreement is the confirmation of the sūrah's diagnosis. The prayer of the sixth verse of al-Māʿūn is the prayer of that verse of the sūrah of the Women: the form is kept, the motive is the audience, and the remembrance is the residue. The sūrah's short phrase is the name, and the longer verse is the description; together they are the Book's complete account of the prayer performed for the eye.

**The Audience That Replaces the Lord**

The showing off is a transfer, and the transfer is the sin. The reward of the act is moved from the Lord to the onlookers, and the act is performed for the new creditor. The worshipper who shows off has, in the effect of the showing off, made his audience his God, because he gives to the audience the only thing that is given to God: the act itself. The sūrah does not state the transfer in those terms; it names the motive, and the motive implies the transfer. The logic is the logic of the first verse: the denial of the account, here in the form of the denial of the Lord as the witness, produces the conduct that the sūrah lists. The crowd is the witness that replaces the Lord, and the prayer is the act that is sold to the crowd.

**The Two Faces of the Divided Man**

The exegetes who read the sūrah as a portrait of the hypocrite took the sixth verse to be the statement of the division that is the definition of the figure: the prayer in public, and the absence in private. The showing off is the public face, and the unmindfulness of the previous verse is the private one, and the two faces are the two halves of the divided man. The sūrah's list states the two in succession, and the succession is the claim: the man who performs for the eye in the congregation is the man who is absent from the prayer in the heart. The two verses are not two descriptions of two men; they are two views of one, and the view from the street is the showing off, and the view from the heart is the absence. The sūrah's method is the method of the diagnosis: the outside first, and then the inside.

**The Contrast in the Book's Own Words**

The Book states the opposite of the sixth verse in the description of the believers, and the contrast is the standard. The believers are the ones who are "consistently performing their prayers" (70:23), the ones for whom the prayer is a state and not an event, and the sūrah of al-Māʿūn states the ones for whom the prayer is an event and not a state, an occurrence that happens in front of the crowd and does not happen in front of the Lord. The contrast is the same act with the witness moved, and the moving of the witness is the whole of the difference. The sūrah does not argue the point; it names the motive, and the motive is the difference. The consistent performer is the one whose witness is the Lord; the show-off is the one whose witness is the crowd, and the two are the two columns of the Book's account of the prayer.

**The Item That Leads to the Hand**

The sixth verse is the second of the three items of the sūrah's second half, and its position is the order of the diagnosis. The state is named in the fifth, the motive is named in the sixth, and the final proof will be named in the seventh, in the hand that withholds the smallest kindness. The motive of the showing off is completed in the act of the withholding, because the man who performs for the eye is the man who keeps his account with the eye, and the eye is not paid in the hand that gives. The list of the sūrah moves from the absence to the motive to the hand, and the movement is the claim: the division that begins in the witness ends in the giving. The sixth verse is the middle of the movement, and it is the verse that names the motive, and the naming of the motive is the heart of the diagnosis.

## Sūrah al-Māʿūn 107:7

> and refuse to give ˹even the simplest˺ aid

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Verse Carries the Name of the Sūrah**

The seventh verse is the verse that gives the sūrah its title, and the title is the word for the smallest form of the mutual help. The Arabic is māʿūn, and the verse places the word at the end of the sūrah, in the last item of the list, in the position of the final proof. The choice of the position is the method of the sūrah: the list descends from the unseen, the denial of the account, to the seen, the orphan and the poor and the prayer, and it ends in the smallest seen, the loan of the tool. The sūrah's last word is therefore its most concrete word, and the concreteness is the point: the judgment of the sūrah reaches to the bottom of the visible, and the bottom is the bucket, the pot, the pickax.

**The Smallest Kindness as the Final Test**

The majority of the early exegetes took the word to mean the small loans that the people of the desert made one to another: the bucket for the well, the pot for the cooking, the pickax for the work, the items of the shared life that moved from house to house in the lending. The reading is the natural one, and it is the one that makes the verse's choice of the end so sharp. The sūrah does not end on the large refusal, the withholding of the wealth, or the refusal of the zakāt; it ends on the small refusal, the withholding of the tool. The reason is the reason of the test: the large giving is possible for the proud man, because the large giving builds the pride, and the small lending exposes the heart, because the small lending has no pride in it. The sūrah closes on the test that cannot be passed by the performance, and the test is the lending of the thing that is needed for a day.

**The Wider Readings and Their Common Point**

The exegetes recorded the wider range of the word, and the range is worth the space. Some took the refusal of the sūrah to be the refusal of the obligatory giving, the zakāt that is the due of the wealth. Others took the word to be the word for the property in general, in the dialect of the Quraysh, so that the verse is the refusal of the wealth itself. Others yet took it to be the refusal of the hospitality, the feeding of the guest who comes at the door. The three readings differ in the object, and they agree in the subject: the hand that will not give. The sūrah's claim is not about the size of the gift; it is about the direction of the hand, and the direction is the refusal. The final verse is therefore the widest verse of the sūrah in its reach, because the hand it names is the hand of every refusal, from the zakāt to the bucket.

**The Stinginess of the Two Refusals**

The last verse completes the pair that is the second half of the sūrah. The first half of the pair is the prayer: performed for the eye, unmindful, empty of the presence. The second half is the giving: refused, withheld, the hand closed. The two refusals are the two faces of the same state, and the sūrah states them in the order of the Book's own description of the hypocrites, who come to the prayer reluctantly and give reluctantly, never giving except with the hand of the unwilling (9:54). The parallel is exact: the half-hearted worship and the half-hearted giving are the two motions of one body. The sūrah of al-Māʿūn is the short form of that verse of the sūrah of the Disbelief, and the agreement of the two texts is the confirmation of the diagnosis: the state that empties the prayer closes the hand.

**The Mirror of the Sūrah**

The seven verses of the sūrah form a closed figure, and the figure is the argument. The first verse names the unseen, the denial of the account; the second and third name the fruits of the denial in the two claims, the orphan and the poor; the fourth names the judgment, the woe; and the fifth, sixth, and seventh specify the judgment in the three items of the list, the unmindfulness, the showing off, and the withholding. The first verse is the root, and the last verse is the fruit in its smallest form, and the figure between them is the tree. The sūrah is a mirror in which the unseen is read in the seen, and the mirror is complete: every verse is a face of the first, and the last face is the hand that will not lend the bucket. The name of the sūrah is the name of the last face, and the naming is the claim: the smallest kindness is the truest measure.

**The Verse That Ends on the Hand**

The sūrah ends on the hand, and the ending is the method. A sūrah that began on a question about the unseen ends on an act of the hand, and the movement from the question to the hand is the movement of the whole of the Book's teaching: the unseen is made real in the hand. The hand that repels the orphan, the hand that does not feed the poor, the hand that withholds the bucket: the three hands of the sūrah are one hand, and the one hand is the state of the heart that the first verse named. The last verse does not add a new charge to the list; it closes the list, and the closing is the verdict. The man of the sūrah is complete, and the completeness is the portrait: the denial, the conduct, the empty worship, and the closed hand, all one, in seven verses, ending on the smallest word of the kindness.
