# Sūrah al-Qalam (Chapter 68) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:1

> Nũn. By the pen and what everyone writes!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Letter Left Without a Translation**

The sūrah opens with a single letter, and no one is asked to translate it. *Nūn* belongs to the group called *al-muqaṭṭaʿāt*, the cut-off letters, which stand at the head of twenty-nine chapters and are recited as they are written. The mainstream of Muslim scholarship has always refused to fix their meaning, holding that the letter is addressed to God's knowledge rather than to ours; other readers have searched for what they might abbreviate and produced proposals, but no proposal has ever commanded the field. Their function as opening is not obscure. A Book that begins with a bare letter announces at once that it is a recitation, an assembly of sounds that carries sense — and it stops the reader's habit of consuming a text quickly.

This chapter has a further distinction in that regard. It is the earliest in the order of revelation among the sūrahs that carry such letters, which places the device at the very beginning of the Qur'an's public life rather than at its maturity. The letter stands there before any claim is made, before any warning is delivered; it simply names the material that will carry the message.

**The Readings That Have Been Proposed**

Commentators who wanted more than the letter itself found several doors. The letter's shape in Arabic script resembles the inkwell from which a scribe's pen draws ink, and the reading was reported on that basis. *Nūn* is also an ordinary Arabic noun for a large fish, which has led some to connect the letter with the prophet whose story closes this sūrah — Yūnus, the Man of the Whale. Others heard in it the initial of a divine name: al-Nūr, the Light, or al-Nāṣir, the Helper. Each reading is a proposal, and the proposals do not exclude one another; they share a single feature in that all of them point away from the letter itself toward writing, aid, or the sea, the three elements of this chapter's opening.

The wiser course is the one taken by the exegetes who stopped where the text stopped. A revelation whose first word in this place is a letter does not need a meaning attached to the letter; it needs a reader who notices that the letter is standing at attention, and that the oath that follows it is already in progress.

**An Oath Sworn by a Created Instrument**

God then swears by the pen. Qur'anic oaths are testimony: when heaven swears by the dawn or the sun or the fig, the created thing is summoned as a witness to what the speaker is about to assert. That God should bind His statement to a piece of wood cut for writing gives the instrument a status the ancient world gave to oracles and kings. The point is not the pen's material; it is the act of writing itself, and the chapter is written.

The same equivalence appears in the sūrah revealed first among all of them: *“Read, ˹O Prophet,˺ in the Name of your Lord Who created—”* (96:1), and then, after the mention of creaturely pride, *“Who taught by the pen—”* (96:4). In that order, teaching by the pen stands beside creation itself as a divine act: God brings a thing into existence, and He teaches what He has brought into existence to preserve knowledge beyond itself. Careful attention to the two verses shows why this chapter opens with an oath and not an argument. The quarrel is about a book, and the chapter begins by naming the two instruments that make books possible.

**"What Everyone Writes": The Recorders Never Stop**

The oath is not sworn by the pen alone but by what those who write write down. The Arabic leaves the writers general, and the generality is the discipline of the verse. All writing is included: the contracts of daily trade, the poetry of the tribes, the letters of rulers, and, decisively in this Book's understanding of itself, the record kept over every person. The Qur'an describes those keepers in several places with a precision that leaves no room for comfort: *“Or do they think that We do not hear their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks? Yes ˹We do˺! And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording ˹it all˺.”* (43:80); *“As the two recording-angels— ˹one˺ sitting to the right, and ˹the other to˺ the left—note ˹everything˺, not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺.”* (50:17–18); and over the whole of it, *“honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺. They know whatever you do.”* (82:11–12).

The oath therefore places the sūrah's first readers inside a file that is already open. A man who is being told that he writes only what he chooses should remember the writers who never stop working behind him.

**The Pen That Wrote Before There Was Anyone to Write For**

Behind every human pen stands an older one. The Prophet ﷺ described it in a report carried by ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit, who passed it to his son: the first thing Allah created was the pen, and He said to it, write — and when the pen asked what it should write, He said, write what was decreed about everything until the Last Hour comes (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4700, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī). The decree was written before any creature held a pen, and the pen of this oath is therefore not merely a tool of memory but the earliest servant of God's command.

That background explains the weight of the accusation the chapter is answering. The Book recited to Quraysh was traced by them to a man's imagination. The chapter opens by invoking two pens — the one that wrote the measure of all things, and the one that records what every creature writes — and puts the revelation they are hearing inside that structure. Whatever else the letter *nūn* may mean, it stands in front of a claim about where words come from.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:2

> By the grace of your Lord, you ˹O Prophet˺ are not insane.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charge and Why It Was Available**

The Makkans had one accusation that cost them nothing and could be repeated indefinitely. Against the Qur'an they would have to argue, and against the Prophet's honesty they had nothing to say — a fact the Book states outright: *“It is not your honesty they question—it is Allah’s signs that the wrongdoers deny.”* (6:33). So they attacked the channel instead of the content. The sūrah answers a charge stated in an older revelation: *“They say, “O you to whom the Reminder is revealed! You must be insane! Why do you not bring us the angels, if what you say is true?””* (15:6–7). The same denial appears across the Makkan sūrahs with small variations — *“So ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺. For you, by the grace of your Lord, are not a fortune-teller or a madman.”* (52:29); *“And your fellow man is not insane.”* (81:22); *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I advise you to do ˹only˺ one thing: stand up for ˹the sake of˺ Allah—individually or in pairs—then reflect. Your fellow man is not insane...””* (34:46). Each time, the Book answers the diagnosis rather than the disease.

The charge had a logic from the pagans' side. A man claiming that the Creator speaks to him, in a city where the gods were stone and the poets were inspired by jinn, fits one of two categories: prophet or possessed. They had excluded the first, so the second remained.

**An Oath Sworn by the Grace, Not by Power**

The verse does not swear by God's might or by the angels or by the Book; it swears by *the grace of your Lord*. *Niʿma* in Arabic is a favour received without being earned, and the commentators identified the favour here with prophethood — the sending down of the mission itself. That choice of oath is the argument. The thing the accusers hold responsible for his state, the revelation that has come upon him, is the very thing by which God swears that the state is sound. In a single clause, the verse turns the evidence of the prosecution into the evidence of the defence.

Notice too what the oath accomplishes socially. The Prophet ﷺ is not told to defend himself, nor to produce a counter-accusation. The denial is issued by the One who granted the favour, and it is issued first, before the accusers can be answered by anything they might respect — no sign, no demonstration, just the flat statement of a Lord about His servant.

**The Grammar of a Categorical Denial**

Arabic can say "you are not mad" in more than one way, and the verse chooses the heaviest. The construction *mā anta bi-majnūn* adds the *bāʾ* to the predicate, a particle that strengthens the negation and closes the door that a bare denial leaves open. Grammarians treat this form as the language's way of saying that the charge is not merely incorrect but outside the range of possibility. The word *majnūn* is itself significant: its literal sense is "jinn-struck," a person overtaken by a hidden spirit. The verse denies the occult mechanism by name, exactly the mechanism the pagans assumed. Revelation, in their model, is possession by a spirit; in the Qur'an's model, it is speech brought by an angel from God.

The denial is also personal in a way that a general statement could not be. God swears by His own favour to His own Messenger: the second person is used throughout, and the sentence reads like the beginning of a legal deposition in which the highest possible witness has already spoken.

**Grace Given, Not Earned**

The word chosen for the oath deserves its own look. *Niʿma* is a favour, and Arabic uses it for benefits that arrive without being deserved or paid for; the root also gives *naʿīm*, the ease and delight of the Hereafter. When the verse swears by *the grace of your Lord*, it places the Prophet's prophethood in that category, a gift handed over rather than an achievement. The accusation against him assumed the opposite: that a man had manufactured a claim, that the message was ambition wearing a religious mask. A claim built by the claimant would indeed be a private project, and private projects are the business of the ambitious, not of the mad. The oath settles the question of origin: what came to him came from outside him.

The Qur'an keeps this framing in every dispute about prophethood. Prophets are not described as masters of a technique but as recipients of a favour, and their task is to deliver what they were given without adding to it or taking from it. The pagan charge of insanity implied that something had gone wrong inside a man. The answer implies that something had been given to him — and that the giver, not the receiver, stands behind the fact. From that angle the accusers' question, asking why he did not bring angels down, was answered before it was asked, since the whole affair from beginning to end was never his to conduct.

**What the Verse Refuses to Argue**

The reply does not cite the Prophet's lineage, his poverty in a city that admired wealth, his reputation for honesty, or the content of what he recited. Every one of those would have been a strong argument, and the sūrah passes all of them by. Instead it locates the answer in the relationship between the Lord and the servant: a grace given, and a mind intact to receive it. The reasoning behind that restraint is worth stating. If the truth of the message depended on the messenger's credentials being accepted by his opponents, its truth would be hostage to their standards; the verse leaves them with a fact they cannot test and cannot refute, and waits.

The chapters around it in this part of the Qur'an say the same thing in different words, and one of them adds the public dimension: the man is not deluded but sent, *“Have they not ever given it a thought? Their fellow man is not insane. He is only sent with a clear warning.”* (7:184). The Book's persistent move is to invite reflection rather than to win the exchange, and it trusts reflection to reach the end of the road.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:3

> You will certainly have a never-ending reward.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Reward That Cannot Be Cut Off**

The phrase rendered *never-ending* is *ghayr mamnūn*, and *mamnūn* comes from a root with two closely related senses. The first is cutting: a rope is *mamnūn* when it has been severed, so the reward promised here is one that nobody and nothing will cut. The second sense is the counting and reminding of a favour given, the reproach of a benefactor who keeps the gift in front of the receiver's face. Both senses are relevant, and the commentators accepted both, because both describe something a person cannot suffer from God: the wage will not be interrupted, and it will not be spoiled by a reminder of the generosity behind it.

The Qur'an uses the same words for the same promise in several places, and always with the same purpose. Believers who do good are told they will have *“a never-ending reward”* (41:8), and the closing sūrahs repeat it for the same class of people (84:25; 95:6). The formula belongs to the ordinary believer as much as to the Prophet ﷺ, which tells us something about the economy the sūrah is describing: God's payment is not a finite fund shared out among favourites, but a standing arrangement.

**The Wage That No Employer in This World Can Pay**

*Ajr*, the word behind *reward*, is the ordinary Arabic term for wages — what a worker is owed for the labour he has performed. It is a deliberately undignified word to use of God's dealings with a prophet, and the Qur'an uses it constantly. The message is being described as work, and the worker is described as hired: a man who rose at night, stood in front of a city that insulted him, and kept speaking for twenty-three years. The honour lies in the employer, not in the terminology.

The Qur'an returns to the point from another angle, asking whether the Prophet ﷺ ever asked his audience for payment — a question that assumes they know the answer (68:46). A message carried without a bill produces nothing in this world for the messenger: no revenue, no rank, no safety. What remains payable is owed only by God, and the verse states it as a settled fact, certain in the future tense. Since the currency of Makkah refused him, the wage was deposited with the One who sent him.

**Why the Promise Is Made in This Sūrah**

This chapter is answering a campaign of defamation, and the accusations were material as much as theological. A man who claims prophethood and gains nothing by it presents his city with a puzzle — either he is a fool, or the thing he sells is worth more than what they offer. The sūrah's answer is that the account is not closed. The reward is described as unceasing precisely because the visible ledger looked empty: no wealth, no following among the powerful, no protection.

The Qur'an keeps this contrast in view elsewhere with a symmetry that is hard to miss. Whatever a person accumulates in this world is temporary by construction: *“Whatever you have will end, but whatever Allah has is everlasting.”* (16:96), and the whole earth is marked for removal: *“Every being on earth is bound to perish. Only your Lord Himself, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain ˹forever˺.”* (55:26–27). Against that measurement, a reward that does not terminate is not one item on a list of blessings; it is the only kind of possession that survives its owner.

**Why the Promise Is Built as a Negative**

*Never-ending* is a description made of what something is not, and Arabic uses that technique when a good exceeds the words available for it. Instead of promising a reward of a certain size, the verse promises a reward with no terminus; instead of describing its contents, it removes the one thing that would ruin any contents — the possibility of their ending. Human beings fear loss more than they fear scarcity, and the fear is justified: a joy that must be surrendered is a joy that is already being surrendered in advance.

The Qur'an applies the same description three times to the reward of ordinary believers (41:8; 84:25; 95:6), and the repetition is deliberate. Paradise in this Book is not only described by what it contains — gardens, rivers, shade, nearness to God — but by the impossibility of eviction. Every reader knows the other story: a garden once given to human beings and then lost through a single act of disobedience. The unceasing reward is the answer to that memory, and it is stated in the plainest possible way, without a single ornament.

**The Same Word for Every Worker**

A detail worth pausing on: the identical phrase, *ajrun ghayru mamnūn*, is given to all believers rather than reserved for the Prophet ﷺ alone. The Book does not maintain two pay scales, one for messengers and one for ordinary people who believe and do right. This levels the promise in a way that disciplines the reader's ambition. What is on offer is not a higher grade of existence for religious professionals, but the same unbroken recompense for anyone whose deeds are accepted.

There is also a warning inside the promise, and the Qur'an states it in the vocabulary of charity, where a gift destroyed by a reminder of it becomes worthless: *“O believers! Do not waste your charity with reminders ˹of your generosity˺ or hurtful words.”* (2:264). Human benefactors ruin their own gifts; the giver in this verse does not. A wage that will never be cut off and never be thrown back in the worker's face is the highest available description of security, and the verse leaves it standing as a fact about the One who hired him.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:4

> And you are truly ˹a man˺ of outstanding character.

**Expanded Commentary**

**khuluq and khalq: Two Words, One Root, Two Directions**

The Arabic behind *character* is *khuluq*, and it stands beside *khalq*, the word for physical creation, with only one letter separating them. The root is the same; the vowels decide whether the word describes a body's shape or a soul's disposition. Arab philologists used the pair as a measure of man: *khalq* is the form that others see, *khuluq* is the form that others live with, and a person may be well made in one and misshapen in the other. The Prophet ﷺ himself prayed with this pairing in view, asking God who had beautified his created form to beautify his character as well (Musnad Aḥmad, al-Muwaṭṭaʾ).

The verse therefore praises something invisible. Nothing in the sentence mentions miracles, eloquence, lineage, or the number of those who followed him. It names the settled disposition that governed his dealings when no one was watching and no revelation was being delivered — patience with a rude questioner, fairness to an enemy, restraint after victory. In a sūrah that opened by answering a charge of madness, this is a strange and pointed defence: the opposing party could not fabricate a character they had lived beside for years.

**ʿĀʾishah's Answer: The Character Was the Book**

The most quoted comment on this verse comes from the Prophet's wife, and it survives inside a long narrative about a man who travelled to Medina to ask her questions. Saʿd ibn Hishām requested that she describe the character of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. She did not answer with adjectives; she asked him whether he read the Qur'an, and when he said yes, she said: *the character of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ was the Qur'an* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 746). The reply is a definition by identity. His patience was the patience of the sūrahs about patience; his forgiveness was the forgiveness commanded in them; his mercy, his restraint in anger, his refusal to repay insult, were all text turned into conduct.

Her answer also makes a claim about the Book. A revelation that produces a life like that is not merely a set of instructions; it is a pattern that can be inhabited, and the person who repeats its commands and its silences becomes its living commentary. ʿĀʾishah had the closest view of him, and she chose to interpret what she saw through what he recited — the reverse of a magician's trick.

**A Vast Character, Stated With Emphasis**

The grammar of the sentence carries weight before any doctrine does. *Innaka la-ʿalā khuluqin ʿaẓīm* piles emphasis upon emphasis: the particle *inna* with the attached pronoun, the *lām* of asseveration, and the preposition *ʿalā*, "upon," which is used of standing on ground rather than of possessing an attribute. He stands on a vast character the way a man stands on solid land. The adjective *ʿaẓīm* is the same word used for mighty things — a vast army, an immense sin, a great Day — and here it is attached to disposition.

Perhaps the strongest mark of the verse's confidence is its placement. The denial of madness came in the second verse; the description of character comes in the fourth, and the sūrah does not argue the point beyond stating it. The mind that the Makkans called disordered produced, over twenty-three years, a life that even hostile chroniclers found hard to fault.

**Why Conduct Outweighs a Sign as Evidence**

A demand runs through the Makkan sūrahs: show us a sign, bring the angels, turn the mountains into gold, break the sky in pieces — *“Why do you not bring us the angels, if what you say is true?”* (15:7). The Qur'an answers the demand by pointing elsewhere, and here it points to a man's conduct. The choice is rational. A sign would be a spectacle, and the audience for spectacles is a crowd; character is verified in private and over time, by people who knew him when he was tired, when he owed money, when his children died, when he was hungry. A city can check a reputation; it could not check a miracle performed for its benefit.

The verses of this chapter keep circling the same contrast: the accusers speak, and the accused is described. Their words are cheap and repeatable; his character is expensive and consistent. The sūrah sides with the slower evidence.

**The Character Announced in Advance**

The Book presents the Prophet's ﷺ conduct as something that had been described before he appeared, and the description it gives is a list of habits rather than of titles: *“˹They are˺ the ones who follow the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, whose description they find in their Torah and the Gospel. He commands them to do good and forbids them from evil, permits for them what is lawful and forbids to them what is impure, and relieves them from their burdens and the shackles that bound them.”* (7:157). A man who orders good, restrains evil, and removes burdens is a man whose manners are visible in the street, which is why this sūrah can praise a character rather than a doctrine.

**A Compassion That Cost Him Something**

The Book's portrait of him is consistently that of a person whose mercy is exercised at his own expense. One verse states it plainly: *“There certainly has come to you a messenger from among yourselves. He is concerned by your suffering, anxious for your well-being, and gracious and merciful to the believers.”* (9:128). Another defines his mission by the same quality: *“We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a mercy for the whole world.”* (21:107). The mercy named in both verses is not a mood; it is the practice of a man who grieved over the stubbornness of his own people and continued to deal gently with them.

For the community that received this sūrah, an insulted messenger was not an abstraction. The verses immediately before this one have described the mockers and their accusations, and the answer given to him is a statement about his manners rather than a promise about his enemies. The Book does not tell him that the mockery will stop; it tells him what he is.

**A Standard for Anyone Who Claims to Follow Him**

The Prophet ﷺ described the purpose of his mission in the vocabulary of this verse. He said, in a report recorded in Musnad Aḥmad and al-Muwaṭṭaʾ, that he had only been sent to perfect noble traits of character. That single sentence defines prophecy as a repair project aimed at conduct, and it also sets the rank of character among religious concerns: above ritual performance when the two conflict, since ritual without character has nothing to perfect. The Book says of the messenger himself that he reached the highest measure of it.

For those who follow, the consequence is immediate and uncomfortable. Whoever claims allegiance to this man has taken on the obligation of resembling him in the area where the description was given, which is the ordinary business of dealing with people. The next verses will turn to those who can neither imitate the character nor leave it alone, and they will ask who is actually mad.

[UNVERIFIED: the report “I was only sent to perfect noble character” is widely quoted (al-Muwattaʾ, Musnad Aḥmad, al-Adab al-Mufrad 273) but no formal grade was confirmed during this session, so it is cited without one.]

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:5

> Soon you and the pagans will see,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence Suspended On Purpose**

This verse has no object. It says that both sides will see, and it holds the answer back, leaving the sentence balanced on the edge of the next line. That suspension is a technique the Qur'an uses when it wants an audience to supply the missing word from its own conscience. The reader who has followed the sūrah from the letter and the oath, through the denial of madness and the description of character, already knows what is coming, and being made to wait for it does more work than the announcement would have done.

The temporal particle, *sa-*, marks the future as near. Arabic uses it for what is about to happen, and the Book applies it repeatedly to events that will take decades or a lifetime to arrive: the vindication of a messenger, the collapse of an arrogant city, the settled verdict of the last day. The claim is not that the event is imminent on a calendar. It is that the event is certain, and that certainty compresses time.

**The Courtroom Structure of the Verse**

Notice who is named as witnesses. The verse gives the Prophet ﷺ first place — *you will see* — and adds the accusers second: *and they will see*. Two parties, one event, two viewings. This is the shape of a verdict rather than a curse, and the difference is worth holding onto. A curse expresses a wish; a verdict states what will happen when the evidence is finally read out. The Makkans had spent years issuing the conclusion — the man is mad — and this verse tells them the case is still open and that they too will be shown the finding.

That mutuality is the reason the disbelievers' mockery never moved the Prophet ﷺ from his course, and the reason the sūrah can be so calm about insults. The dispute had a deadline attached to it, and no one had the authority to read the verdict early.

**Why the Qur'an Risks a Future Tense**

Promising an outcome in the future is a hazardous rhetorical move; it asks to be tested by events, and events can go the other way. The Makkan sūrahs take that risk constantly. The Book tells the Prophet ﷺ that his opponents will see; it tells the mocking crowds that they will know which of them was in error; it tells the powerful that their monuments will not save them. Each such sentence is a wager on the reliability of God's government of history.

What makes the wager safe is not that every prediction is fulfilled within a particular person's lifetime. The Book locates part of the outcome beyond death by design, and the verses that follow in this chapter speak of gardens, records, and a Day when the hidden becomes plain. Anyone assessing the claim must include that final court in his account, which is why the Qur'an can state a distant verdict as a near fact.

**Sight as the Arrival of Certainty**

In the Arabic of the Book, *seeing* often means more than the eye's report; it is the moment when a matter stops being a topic and becomes a fact to the person confronting it. The opposition saw the Prophet ﷺ every day in the streets of Makkah, and as far as they were concerned they had seen everything there was to see. The verse tells them that another seeing is coming, of a different kind, and that it will settle what argument could not.

The pattern repeats at the end of this very chapter, where the disbelievers are described as nearly cutting the Prophet ﷺ down with their eyes when they hear the Reminder, calling him a madman (68:51). Their eyes were already doing their worst. The verse under discussion promises a different look with a different result, and it is the reason the sūrah can leave its opponent in possession of the last insult for now.

**The Accused Is Addressed First**

The order of the clause is not indifferent. God addresses the Prophet ﷺ before He mentions his opponents: *you will see, and they will see*. A promise made to a man under daily insult is worth more than a threat issued to his mockers, and the verse supplies the comfort first. Anyone who has carried an unpopular responsibility knows the particular loneliness of not knowing how the story ends, and the Book addresses that condition directly, with a sentence in the second person singular, spoken to the one who needs it.

The same ordering appears across the Makkan revelation, which regularly steadies its messenger before it engages his adversaries. The Prophet ﷺ is told what will happen to him and to those with him; the opponents are then told that they will witness it. This is why the sūrah can ask him, a few verses on, to keep working. Patience in the Qur'an is never sustained by willpower; it is sustained by an assurance about the ending, delivered in advance to the person who will need to explain his patience to others.

**The Mercy Hidden in the Delay**

Something has been given to the deniers by the postponement. The verse does not name the punishment; it names a time of seeing, which is a time in which a person may still change what will be seen. The Book elsewhere makes that possibility explicit and wide: *“Say, ˹O Prophet, that Allah says,˺ “O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins. He is indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.””* (39:53).

Read that way, the unfinished sentence is not only a threat held back for effect. It is an interval, and the sūrah addresses people who are living inside it. Whoever sees now, before the seeing that will be forced on everyone, has gained the entire benefit of the delay.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:6

> which of you is mad.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Charge Handed Back Intact**

The Makkans had a word for the Prophet ﷺ, and this verse takes it from them and puts it in the middle of the table. He is not accused here of anything new, and no new defence is mounted for him. The statement is only that when the matter is finally displayed, the two parties will find out which one the description actually fits. The accusation is returned to its sender as a question that time will answer, and the Book does not answer it on the spot.

The restraint is a rhetorical strength. A revelation that rushed to say "you are the mad ones" would be trading insult for insult. Instead it holds the word aloft and lets the audience look at it — at the man whose character it has just described, and at the people who composed the description.

**The Root That Means Both Trial and Madness**

The Arabic word behind *mad* allows a second reading, and the older exegetes noted it. The root *f-t-n* covers testing, trying by fire, and being led astray; *maftūn* is one who has been tried, and in the usage of the tribe it could also mean deranged. So the sentence can be read as *which of you is the one put to trial*, and the sūrah's context supports both senses at once. The whole of chapter 67, immediately before this one, described human life as a test of who would do best; here the test's outcome is drawn out and laid over the accusation of insanity.

The double sense is not a translator's puzzle but part of the verse's argument. The two parties each think the other has been struck by something — one says a spirit has taken the Prophet's ﷺ mind, the other says a delusion has taken theirs. Both sentences use the same vocabulary of affliction, and the verse says the day is coming when the diagnosis will be assigned to the correct patient.

**A Verdict Reserved to God's Knowledge**

The sūrah does not pronounce on the sanity of its opponents at all. It puts their question in a queue and attaches it to the day when every hidden matter is examined. The pattern is consistent in the Book: accusations are not always refuted in this world, and the vindication offered to the wronged is often the statement that the record will be read in public. The faithful are asked to accept that arrangement, which requires them to leave a matter unresolved for as long as God leaves it unresolved.

That is a demanding instruction, and the sūrah demonstrates it in its own voice. It refuses to be goaded. The calm of the verse is the model: a person convinced of the outcome does not need to win the exchange that begins it.

**What the Delay Shows About the Accusers**

The sentence also says something about the people who used the charge. They lived beside the Prophet ﷺ for years. They heard him speak, traded with him, entrusted him with their goods — the reports of his reputation for honesty among them are not a Muslim invention but an embarrassment to their own case. Calling such a man possessed was not an assessment they reached after study; it was a weapon chosen because it required nothing of them. The Book had already answered the substance of the claim by describing his conduct, and this verse leaves them the word they preferred.

A verdict postponed is not a verdict withdrawn. Both sides are told they will see the answer, and the verse specifies only that the answer will not be given by them. Meanwhile the accusers continue to live in the city of the accused, to trade with his companions, and to hear the recitation in the open; the interval they spend mocking is the same interval in which the question is still open for them, and nothing in the verse forecloses that possibility while the sentence remains unfinished.

**The Charge That Boomerangs in the Book**

The Qur'an records the same accusation in several other places, and it treats it as evidence against those who made it rather than against the man accused. One verse states the conclusion plainly: *“Or ˹because˺ they say, “He is insane?” In fact, he has come to them with the truth, but most of them are resentful of the truth.”* (23:70). The resentment is given as the motive, and the diagnosis of madness as the excuse. That is the order of events in the sūrah as well: character described first, then the proposed verdict examined.

The reader who watches this pattern across the Makkan chapters sees a consistent method. The Book refuses to match insult with insult; it puts the accusation on record, states the facts about the accused man, and defers the verdict to a place where no one can lie. Then it leaves the accusers with the burden of their own word. That word is what this verse pins to the wall, and the next verse names who in fact knows which way a person is heading.

**The Standard the Word Leaves Behind**

By leaving the question open, the verse invites a reckoning that a reader can perform on himself. The word *majnūn* originally described someone overtaken by a hidden force, acting without control of his own reason. Measured by that definition, the man described in the fourth verse of this sūrah — settled, generous, patient, consistent over decades — is the least likely candidate for the description. Whichever way the reader takes the word, the sūrah's contrast is now complete: one party accused, one party assessing, and a room in which the accused has already been described. There is no space left in the sentence for a third option, and the next verse states who in fact knows which way a person is going.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:7

> Surely your Lord ˹alone˺ knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Case Is Referred to Its Proper Court**

The sūrah has just left a question hanging: the two parties will see which of them is mad. This verse supplies the ground on which such a question can be left hanging without injustice. Judgment about guidance is not a matter of votes, reputations, or who shouts loudest in the marketplace of Makkah; it is knowledge, and this knowledge is God's. The sentence does not tell the Prophet ﷺ that his opponents are astray. It tells him that the One who decides the question is not his adversary and not an audience of Quraysh elders, and that ends the debate as a practical matter.

Anyone who has argued in public knows the relief contained in such a statement. It removes the burden of proving one's own guidance and the other man's error, which is the burden every honest debater eventually finds impossible to carry. The Book repeatedly lifts that weight from its messenger and places it where it belongs.

**"Knows Best": an Exclusive Claim, Not a Comparison**

The Arabic construction is *aʿlamu bi-* — knows best about — and its elative form can mislead an English reader into thinking it means that God knows better than others who also know something. In the usage of the Qur'an the sense goes further: the matter is His knowledge, and human estimates of it are not a smaller version of the same thing. The point is driven home by the same sentence being repeated almost word for word in other sūrahs: *“Indeed, your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided.”* (6:117), and again in the sūrah of the Star, *“This is the extent of their knowledge. Surely your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided.”* (53:30).

That repetition is a feature of the Book's method. Where a claim appears in several places with identical wording, the reader is meant to treat it as a fixed point of the revelation rather than an occasional remark, and this claim fixes the limits of human assessment of other people's religion.

**Two Roads, and the Vocabulary of Each**

Both key words in the sentence are drawn from travel. *Ḍalla* describes a person or animal that has wandered off the road, and the broader Arabic sense adds the idea of being lost and perishing in the wilderness; *ihtadā* means to be shown the way and to follow it. The verse therefore pictures human lives as journeys conducted on a network of paths, where the failure to arrive is not the absence of a road but the choice to leave it. The sūrah's own opening oath had already raised the question of writing, and the Book's habit of joining wayfaring metaphors to moral ones rests on the same assumption: a path exists, and directions have been given.

The two participles also describe states rather than acts. A person does not cease to be guided by one bad stretch of road, and he does not become guided by one good intention; the verse speaks of the settled direction of a life, known entirely to the One who knows what is in the breast.

**Both Parties Were Making the Same Claim**

The sūrah's first audience is a city in which each side regarded the other as lost. The Makkans considered the believers deviant from the way of the ancestors; the believers considered the Makkan cult a departure from the way of Abraham and Ishmael. When two groups make mirror claims about each other, the immediate effect is a stalemate that neither can win by assertion. This verse breaks the stalemate by naming where the truth of the question is kept, and it does so without ever declaring the verdict.

The restraint is instructive. The Qur'an does not ask its readers to adjudicate between the parties on the strength of their respective confidence; it asks them to think about the road itself, and to take the fact of a final verdict seriously enough to prepare for it.

**Where the Question Is Finally Settled**

God's knowledge is not a private file. The verse says He knows, and the rest of the Book says that the knowledge will one day be displayed: the sūrah has already told both parties that they will see, and every passage about the last day is a public reading of what had been known from the beginning. The wording chosen here also appears in a verse addressed to the Prophet ﷺ during a period of exile and danger, where the same knowledge is offered as a guarantee about his future: *“Most certainly, the One Who has ordained the Quran for you will ˹ultimately˺ bring you back home ˹to Mecca˺. Say, “My Lord knows best who has come with ˹true˺ guidance and who is clearly astray.””* (28:85). The identical formula serves two purposes there — a promise of return and a refusal to argue with his accusers — which tells us how the Book uses this sentence: as the place where disputes are parked until they can be settled properly.

For the believers of Makkah, that meant living with unresolved insult for years. For their opponents, it meant that the last word had not been spoken, and that the record was being kept by someone with full information.

**What the Messenger Is Not Asked to Do**

A consequence of this verse shaped the whole shape of the Prophet's ﷺ work in Makkah. He was not appointed to apportion his audience between the guided and the lost; he was appointed to deliver a message. An assignment to sort people would have required him to read hearts, and the Book never gives him that ability; it tells him, in effect, that the sorting is done by the One who knows. This is what allowed him to keep preaching to the same hostile faces for years — the outcome was not his to produce.

That division of labour also protects the audience. Nobody's guidance is delivered by the Prophet's ﷺ verdict on him, and nobody's perdition is sealed by his opinion. Every person stands in relation to the message, and the knowledge of how he stands is held elsewhere, which is why the next instruction in the sūrah can be given to the messenger as a rule of conduct rather than a judgement about people.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:8

> So do not give in to the deniers.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Instruction Built on the Previous Sentence**

The particle that opens this verse is a *fāʾ* of consequence: *so* do not give in. The command is not a fresh subject introduced after a digression; it is the practical conclusion drawn from the statement that God alone knows who is guided and who has strayed. If the assessment is His, then the pressure of a hostile majority is not evidence about anything. The sūrah's logic runs from knowledge to conduct, and the Book never reverses it: the Qur'an does not first demand loyalty and then look for a reason to justify the demand.

Readers who know the Makkan history understand the force of the instruction. The verses were revealed to a man whose city had the power to starve, exile, or kill him, and whose followers included slaves and the poor who had everything to lose. A command to stand firm addressed to people in that position is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the whole difference between a movement that survives pressure and one that dissolves into private opinion.

**"Do Not Obey": the Command Targets Submission, Not Agreement**

The Arabic is *fa-lā tuṭiʿ*, do not obey, and the verb is the one used for following instructions rather than for holding a view. The choice matters. The verse does not say "do not agree with the deniers" or "do not be convinced by them", because conviction is not something a person decides to have. It says: do not hand them the obedience they are demanding. The Book locates the danger at the level of submission, which is precisely the level at which a person has control.

The same verb returns in the passage that follows, when the sūrah lists the character types whose orders are not to be accepted. The repetition is deliberate: the warning against the pressure of an entire city is first issued in general, and then applied to particular men whose lives displayed exactly what the pressure was made of.

**What the Deniers Were Asking For**

The demand was not philosophical. The Makkan leaders wanted the Prophet ﷺ and his community to return to the religion of their fathers — the inherited cult of the sanctuary, its rites, its pilgrim traffic, and its tribal consensus. Ancestral practice was the strongest argument they had, and it is the argument the Book challenges wherever it appears, since custom proves only that a thing is old. The verse's refusal is therefore a refusal to convert authority into seniority: something is not true because the majority of one's grandparents believed it.

This is the point at which the small community's isolation becomes most acute. To break with the ancestors is to break with the entire social architecture built on them — burial, marriage, alliance, inheritance, and reputation all rested on the inherited cult. The command is measured against that cost, and it stands.

**A Demand That Concession Never Satisfies**

The Book's experience with this kind of pressure is recorded in a sentence of startling bluntness: *“Never will the Jews or Christians be pleased with you, until you follow their faith.”* (2:120). The statement is about a different set of communities, but the mechanism it describes is universal. The demand made of a messenger by those who dislike his message is never a partial adjustment; it is complete assimilation, and every concession made along the way simply relocates the line of the demand. The verse under discussion therefore refuses the first step rather than managing a series of steps, since the logic of the pressure guarantees that the last step is the same as the first.

**Refusal Without Hostility**

The Qur'an's model of refusal is not belligerence, and the short sūrah that closes the recitation of the Book states it with unmatched clarity: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O you disbelievers! I do not worship what you worship, nor do you worship what I worship... You have your way, and I have my Way.””* (109:1–6). Separation is declared, agreement is not pretended, and no threat is issued. The Prophet ﷺ is told that he cannot satisfy them, and that he is not required to; he is required to keep speaking.

The same confidence is stated as a promise elsewhere: *“O Messenger! Convey everything revealed to you from your Lord. If you do not, then you have not delivered His message. Allah will ˹certainly˺ protect you from the people.”* (5:67). The duty is delivery; the protection is God's concern. An instruction built that way frees a messenger from the impossible task of managing the reactions of his audience, and history shows what that freedom produced in a city that had decided to destroy him.

**Obedience, Not Agreement, Is the Line Being Drawn**

A distinction runs through this command which later generations of Muslims have found indispensable in dealings with hostile majorities: the verse prohibits obedience, not association. The Prophet ﷺ continued to trade with the Quraysh, to receive their delegations, to visit their sick, and to honour their agreements while refusing to accept their religious authority. Nothing in the command required him to treat opponents as enemies, and nothing in it permitted him to concede what he had been sent to deliver. The line was drawn at submission, and moving it either way would have distorted the mission.

The distinction survives because it corresponds to how belief actually works. A person can be told to disobey an instruction, and he can be told to disobey a whole community's expectation; he cannot be told to disbelieve a conclusion he has reached, and the Book never asks it. It addresses conduct, agency, and the practical weight a person allows others to place on his own convictions. Where that weight is refused, arguments can continue for years without the truth being surrendered; where it is granted, the argument is already over.

**What Yielding Would Have Cost**

A final reason for the command lies in what the Prophet ﷺ had to offer. Nothing in the revelation was his own, and a man who cannot revise the text cannot strike a bargain over it. The Book has him say as much when the demand for amendment was put to him directly: *“Say ˹to them˺, “It is not for me to change it on my own. I only follow what is revealed to me. I fear, if I were to disobey my Lord, the punishment of a tremendous Day.””* (10:15). The refusal to give in, in other words, is not stubbornness of character. It is the only position available to someone who has been handed something he does not own.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:9

> They wish you would compromise so they would yield ˹to you˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb dahana: Softening at the Edges**

The word rendered *compromise* is *tudhinu*, from a root that gives Arabic *duhn*, oil. To *dāhana* someone is to make things smooth for him — to slacken where one should hold firm, to soften a boundary so that friction disappears. The metaphor is not about disagreement in principle; it is about the easing of a stance for the sake of peaceable relations. The Book's objection is not to courtesy, which it commands, but to the kind of flexibility that leaves the message itself undefended while the relationships stay pleasant.

The commentators noted that the word sits between two other senses in Arabic: to demean something by taking it lightly, and to slide away from a commitment. Both are present in the one verb, and both describe what happens to a religious position when it is treated as negotiable for social advantage — it stops being a doctrine and becomes a preference.

**A Bargain With Two Halves**

The sentence is built as a trade: if you slacken, they slacken. Two conditional halves locked together, each dependent on the other. This is the shape of every religious compromise offered by a hostile majority, and it is worth naming what is wrong with it. The offer assumes that the truth of a message is proportional to how much resistance it generates, so that lowering the resistance raises the truth. The sūrah rejects the premise by naming the offer for what it is: a wish, not a theological argument.

The second half of the trade is the instructive part. What the other side would concede is described without detail, because in practice the concession would be as vague as the offer itself: a little less hostility, a little more tolerance, an agreement to live and let live. The Book's audiences have always found this offer attractive, and the verse records it as a temptation rather than a debate.

Something else follows from the symmetry of the sentence. If each side's firmness is adjusted to the other's, then the party that cares less about the outcome controls the terms. The Makkans cared about the message only insofar as it threatened their arrangements; the Prophet ﷺ cared about it as a trust from God. In any negotiation of that kind, the side with the lighter stake sets the price, which is exactly why the sūrah refuses to enter the negotiation at all.

**The Proposal Reported From Makkah**

The reports about the Prophet's ﷺ mission record the concrete form the offer took. The leaders of Quraysh, according to the accounts transmitted about this period, proposed a rotation: that he worship their gods for a period, and that they worship his God for a period, so that each side could claim the other's respect. Other versions have them offering him wealth, marriage into the leading houses, and authority in exchange for softening his preaching. The sūrah's verbs fit both reports, and the reply preserved in the Book is a sūrah of its own, in which the Prophet ﷺ is told to declare that worship is not divisible: *“I do not worship what you worship, nor do you worship what I worship”* (109:2–3).

The proposal was rational from the standpoint of tribal politics. Compromise would have ended the boycott, restored the family alliances, and given the new religion a respectable place in the sanctuary's economy. The Book declines on the grounds that what is being divided is the worship of God, which cannot be apportioned by a negotiation between parties who do not own it.

**Why There Is No Middle Position**

A message that says God is one, that the dead are raised, and that a messenger has been sent cannot be half accepted. The Book states the negative form of that claim to its messenger: *“˹O Prophet!˺ Follow what is revealed to you from your Lord—there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and turn away from the polytheists.”* (6:106). The instruction to turn away is not an instruction to hate; it is the recognition that two positions exist and cannot be blended into a third.

There is a second reason for refusing the middle position. A religion that bargains publicly teaches its own followers that its claims are negotiable, and no community survives that lesson. The sūrah's audience included slaves and the poor whose entire hope rested on the truth of the resurrection; for them, a deal struck by their leader would have been an evacuation of the ground they were standing on.

**The Wish Behind the Offer**

The verse's most unsettling word is *wish*. It is not describing a debate or an argument; it describes a desire, and desire in the Book is often the tell. What the opponents wanted was not the Prophet's ﷺ agreement on a point of theology; they wanted the pressure to end without the truth being accepted. Their comfort required only that the recitation become less sharp.

Read that way, the offer is a compliment in disguise. A message that can be safely tolerated does not threaten the arrangements people have built; a message that cannot be tolerated is doubted by the fact that it needs to be. The sūrah draws its instruction from this reality: since the pressure exists because the message is real, the response is not to alter the message but to endure the pressure.

**What the Verse Does Not Do**

Nothing here commands hostility, and nothing authorises the sort of religious argument that ends in insult. The verse records a wish and issues a refusal; it leaves the opponents' dignity untouched and their property unmentioned. That is a model the sūrah's later verses will develop, when they turn from the pressure of a whole city to the conduct of particular men. The difference between a message that is refused and a messenger who refuses to be pleasant is a distinction this verse keeps intact.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:10

> And do not obey the despicable, vain oathtaker,

**Expanded Commentary**

**From a Whole City to a Single Man**

The command to resist the deniers, issued in general terms a few verses earlier, now narrows to a portrait. The sūrah stops describing the pressure and describes the kind of man who applies it. The shift tells the reader what was actually at stake in the Makkan bargaining: the obstruction of the message was not an abstract social force but the work of particular people with names, families, businesses, and reputations, and the Book names their habits one by one.

The exegetes disagreed over the identity of the man described. Most identified him with al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, an elder of the Quraysh whose wealth, sons, and standing made him one of the loudest voices against the Prophet ﷺ; others read the passage as a composite portrait covering several of the leading opponents, including Abū Jahl, al-Aswad ibn ʿAbd al-Yaghūth, and al-Akhnas ibn Sharīq. Both readings agree on the point that matters: the list is meant to be recognised, so that no follower mistakes this character type for leadership.

**The Portrait Opens With the Tongue**

The first trait named is the habit of swearing. The Arabic *hallāf* is an intensive form, a pattern that describes a person who does a thing repeatedly, and the adjective beside it, *mahīn*, adds contemptibility. So before the sūrah mentions the man's schemes, his wealth, or his mockery, it mentions his oaths. In an oral society, an oath is the instrument by which a man without a written contract can be trusted, and the man who swears constantly is the man who has made God's name a piece of conversational machinery.

Something of the offence is visible in the balance of the sentence. The Book's gravest language is reserved elsewhere for those who invoke God lightly in transactions; here the light invocation is not a small matter attached to a villain's crime but the first item in the list, as if the cheapening of the divine name were the root from which the other traits grow.

**The Oath in Arabia and the Value of a Word**

Arabia before Islam ran on spoken commitment. Alliances, truces, marriages, and debts were secured by a promise sworn before a god, and a tribe's reputation rested on whether its men kept their oaths. The vice of excessive swearing was therefore not merely irritating; it dissolved the mechanism that made ordinary life possible, because a word repeated too easily stops carrying weight. The Book regulates the practice directly and warns against carelessness in it: *“Allah will not call you to account for your thoughtless oaths, but He will hold you accountable for deliberate oaths...”* (5:89), then names the expiations for a broken vow and closes by instructing the reader to keep them.

A man who swears habitually has no reliable yes and no left. When such a man accuses a prophet of madness, his testimony is worth exactly what his oaths were worth.

The Book itself swears frequently, which shows that the problem is not the oath but its weight. Divine oaths in the Qur'an are reserved for the gravest matters and are taken by created things to turn attention back to their Creator; the difference between that practice and the oath-monger's is the difference between a witness summoned deliberately and a word scattered in ordinary conversation. One calls God to testify; the other uses Him as filler.

**The Character Type the Sūrah Warns Against**

The command *do not obey* attached to this description has a social meaning that outlasts the sūrah's first audience. Every religious community contains men whose public profile is built on words: the assertive oath, the quick verdict, the slander repeated as a jest. The Book warns that such people will seek to be followed, and the warning is addressed to believers who may be tempted to give them influence because their wealth and standing make them look like leadership. The account of the earlier nations makes the pattern general: whenever a messenger came, *“its ˹spoiled˺ elite would say, “We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are walking in their footsteps.””* (43:23), and the elite were the obstacle.

So the verse is a test of judgment as much as a moral portrait. It instructs a community to measure its would-be leaders against their tongue, their dealings, and their treatment of the weak, rather than their standing.

**The Paradox in mahīn**

The word rendered *despicable* is *mahīn*, from a root meaning weak or contemptible. Applied to an elder of Quraysh, the term is deliberately jarring. In the estimation of his city this man was a pillar: wealthy, well connected, surrounded by sons. In the estimation of the Book he is weak, because everything he has consists of things that can be taken from him, and his influence rests on no moral foundation whatever.

That reversal runs through the sūrah's whole argument. The Makkans looked at the man with the following and asked why God had not sent a message to a great man of the two cities; the Book looks at the same man and describes him as worthless before the description of his fortune arrives. The scale of valuation is not the market's, and the verse tells its readers so before it lists the man's sins.

**What the Verse Achieves by Silence**

One thing the verse does not do is prescribe a response to the man beyond withholding obedience. It does not authorise retaliation, boycotting, or reprisal; nothing is asked of the believers except that they not hand him authority over their religion. The restraint is deliberate and practical: in a city where such men controlled access to food, water, and safety, the command to withhold obedience was already the maximum that could be sustained. The rest of the passage builds the full portrait, and the reader is left to recognise how much of a man's public weight is nothing but his willingness to swear.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:11

> slanderer, gossip-monger,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Portrait Returns to the Tongue**

The list of traits continues, and it returns to speech. The man under description has already been introduced as a habitual swearer; he is now named as a spreader of calumny. The pairing is deliberate. A person who treats oaths as ornament has nothing invested in the accuracy of what he says, and a person with nothing invested in accuracy becomes the ideal instrument for carrying reports between people. The first vice supplies the raw material for the second.

Both traits involve the same organ, which is why the sūrah spends two of its seven descriptions on the mouth. A person's tongue is the part of him that other people meet most often, and the part with which a community is either built or dismantled. The Book's moral teaching consistently scores speech as action rather than as commentary on action; a sentence spoken can destroy a reputation, a marriage, or a truce as effectively as a hand raised.

**Namīmah: Carrying Words Against Their Owners**

The Arabic term behind *gossip-monger* is *nammām*, from a root describing the passing of talk from one ear to another in order to set people against each other. It is not simply idle chatter. Idle chatter wastes time; calumny rearranges a community. The carrier is usually accurate, which is what makes him dangerous: he takes a true sentence spoken in one gathering, delivers it in another where it will land as an insult, and steps back to watch the two sides harden.

The reports on this sin are unusually severe. In one warning the Prophet ﷺ stated that a tale-carrier will not enter Paradise (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 105). The statement has troubled readers who notice that the same Book holds out forgiveness for far worse sins, and the reconciliation is straightforward: the promise of paradise belongs to the community whose members can trust one another, and the tale-carrier's trade is the systematic manufacture of distrust inside that community. Forgiveness remains available to him, as it does to anyone, but the practice and the destination cannot both be maintained.

**The Damage Done by True Words**

The sūrah's target is a human type, and the type is not a liar in the ordinary sense. He does not need to invent anything, because his method is distribution: he takes what he has heard, removes it from the context that explained it, and installs it where it will do the most harm. The Book addresses the receiving side of the same trade in a verse that has shaped Muslim social ethics: *“O believers! Avoid suspicion as much ˹as possible˺, for suspicion in some cases is a sin. And do not spy on one another or backbite...”* (49:12). The instruction forbids the snooping that feeds gossip and the repeating that discharges it, and it places suspicion alongside them because a suspicious community is the hospitable environment calumny needs.

The image the verse uses for the receiver's side is deliberately brutal — the flesh of a dead brother — because gossip is a meal: it is consumed with appetite, it requires a body, and it produces nothing. Together the two verses enclose the practice from both ends. One tells the community not to carry stories and not to consume them; the sūrah under discussion tells them not to follow the men who do.

**Why Such Men Seek Influence**

The verses assume that a person with these habits will want to be obeyed, and the anatomy of that ambition is visible in the description. A man who cannot build a reputation on his own integrity can build one on his usefulness to others — the information he supplies, the enemies he identifies, the quarrels he manages. In a city where rival clans watched each other constantly, this was a real form of power, and the Book's warning is addressed to a community that could be captured by it.

The same pattern appears in every generation of religious history. Where the truth of a message is settled, the ambitious find scope in the spaces around it: who is loyal, who is suspect, what was said in private. The sūrah's answer is not to argue with such a man but to withhold obedience from him, which deprives the habit of its audience.

**The Tongue and the Scale of Deeds**

The Book treats speech as weighted matter. Its promises and threats attach to what people say with an insistence that surprises readers who expect religion to be concerned only with acts of worship: the careless oath, the fabricated report, the mocking nickname, the slander repeated for entertainment. This is not because words are magical. It is because in a community bound by revealed obligations rather than by state enforcement, the word is the load-bearing element, and the Book protects it by attaching the gravest consequences to its abuse.

The sūrah's portrait uses this reasoning implicitly. Its list of vices does not begin with violence or theft; it begins with the tongue, moves to greed, then returns to speech, and only then arrives at the qualities that made the man feared in the market.

**What the Two Words Together Establish**

Read together, the pair of terms at the head of this verse describes a man who is both the source and the transmitter of false reports: he invents when invention suits him and carries when carrying suits him. That combination is what the earlier command was guarding against, and it explains why the instruction was phrased as a prohibition of *obedience* rather than of association. Believers may live beside such a man, trade with him, and treat him with the ordinary decency the Book requires; what they may not do is accept his account of who is trustworthy, which is the only commodity he has to sell.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:12

> withholder of good, transgressor, evildoer,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Man Who Blocks What Costs Him Nothing**

The Arabic *mannāʿ al-khayr* is an intensive form: not a man who happens to be stingy on an occasion, but one who is habitually a blocker of good. The phrase covers more than money. Good in this vocabulary includes every benefit a person can hand to another — relief, a hearing, a fair word, access, protection — and the blocker is the one who stands between people and these things as a matter of course. There is a special ugliness in the vice when what is being withheld costs the giver almost nothing, and the sūrah's portrait is of a man whose obstruction was a daily practice.

The commentators gave the term two applications, and both fit the Makkan setting. One reading is the miser whose hoarding is contagious, a man who taught his circle to fear generosity; the other is the man who blocks the way to faith, using his standing to prevent people from listening to a messenger. The second reading makes the phrase continuous with the portrait around it, since the man's whole influence was exercised at the point where access could be denied.

**Access as a Weapon in a Tribal City**

Makkah ran on access. Access to the sanctuary, to the caravan seasons, to clan protection, to the hearing of an elder. A leader who could withhold those things held a power that no court had given him, and the opponents of the Prophet ﷺ used exactly this instrument: threats against traders, pressure on families, boycotts aimed at communities with no reserves. The portrait here names the person who institutionalised that pressure, and it names him in religious terms, because his refusal was not merely a business decision.

The Book's answer to such men never rests on their wealth. It goes to the question of what wealth is for, and it treats withholding as a form of taking: the poor have a right in what the wealthy hold, and to keep it back is to seize something that was not theirs to keep.

**The Vow, the Withholding, and the Consequence**

A passage in the sūrah of repentance traces the psychology of this vice in three movements. First the promise: *“And there are some who had made a vow to Allah: “If He gives us from His bounty, we will surely spend in charity and be of the righteous.””* (9:75). Then the failure: *“But when He gave them out of His bounty, they withheld it and turned away indifferently.”* (9:76). Then the sentence: *“So He caused hypocrisy to plague their hearts until the Day they will meet Him, for breaking their promise to Allah and for their lies.”* (9:77). The withholding of what was promised is not a private vice with a private cost; it hardens the character until the person cannot be reached.

That sequence explains why the sūrah's portrait places the withholder among men who end up actively fighting a messenger. Withholding begins as a relationship with property and becomes a relationship with God.

**Muʿtadī and Athīm: Two Charges in One Breath**

The two words that follow the withholder describe two different offences. *Muʿtadī* is a transgressor — one who crosses a boundary that belongs to someone else, the vocabulary of wrongs done against people. *Athīm* is a sinner — one who violates the law of God, the vocabulary of wrongs done against the divine claim. The pairing tells the reader how the Book counts offences: it does not allow a man to be a tyrant in the market and a saint in the mosque, and it does not allow a man to be religiously observant while holding his community to ransom.

The order is also worth noticing. Transgression comes first, and sinfulness follows, which mirrors the way the Book usually narrates corruption: conduct is damaged, then belief is damaged, and the two damage each other in turn.

**Why Withholding Is Classified as Aggression**

A reader accustomed to thinking of stinginess as a minor failing should register the sūrah's taxonomy. It puts the withholder in a list that ends with the most contemptible descriptions available, and it does so because the harm is structural. In a community where the wealthy hoard, the poor must borrow at ruinous terms, families fall into dependency, and resentments accumulate that the next conflict will ignite. The Book protects the community by attaching moral weight to the movement of wealth, and it names those who obstruct that movement among the people whose leadership must not be obeyed.

The strongest statement of the principle treats the poor not as recipients of generosity but as holders of a legal share: *“And in their wealth there was a rightful share ˹fulfilled˺ for the beggar and the poor.”* (51:19), a description the Book repeats when it lists the qualities of the successful: *“and who give the rightful share of their wealth to the beggar and the poor”* (70:24–25). Wealth, on this account, arrives with an obligation already attached to it; the man who blocks the flow is not refusing a request but withholding a due.

**The Portrait's Fourth Item and What It Prepares**

Something is building in this list. The sūrah has moved from the tongue, to the hand, to the boundary, to the law, and each item has made the man's social world smaller and more dangerous. What remains is the quality of his temper and the question of his origins, and those two final items will complete a picture that the sūrah intends its first readers to attach to a face they knew in the market. The purpose is not revenge on a personal enemy; it is the protection of a small community from a big man's gravity.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:13

> brute, and—on top of all that—an illegitimate child.

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿUtull: Strength That Everyone Else Pays For**

The word rendered *brute* is *ʿutull*, and Arabic dictionaries describe a man who is rough, harsh, quick to anger, and physically intimidating. The term is used of an animal that will not be led, and of a person whose strength of body has never been matched by any discipline of temper. Everything the earlier items in the list had in common now becomes visible: the man who swears easily, carries tales, blocks the good, and oversteps boundaries is not a subtle schemer at heart. He is a bully whose social position gave his temper room to operate.

The picture matters for a community under pressure. Believers tempted to admire the confidence of their opponents are shown what that confidence is made of. What looks like authority in the market is a man who cannot be reasoned with, and the Book identifies the trait before it identifies the man, so that the type can be recognised wherever it appears.

**Zanīm: the Word About Origins**

The final word of the list, *zanīm*, is the harshest. It describes a person of uncertain or illegitimate descent — someone who cannot be attached to a recognised household. The sūrah's use of it has troubled some readers, who point out that a person does not choose his birth, and that the Book elsewhere forbids holding a man's origins against him. No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another is a Qur'anic principle stated without qualification, and *“No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another.”* (17:15) is not the sort of sentence that permits contempt for a man's parentage.

What resolves the difficulty is the audience. In this list the accusation is not that the man suffers from his origins but that he traded on lineage he could not prove, using tribal standing as an argument against a message whose truth had nothing to do with anyone's descent. The Book reaches for the one fact that would strip him of that argument in front of his own people, and it places the phrase at the end of the list, after the man's own character has been described at length.

**A Wordplay That Adds Contempt**

Alongside the genealogical sense, Arabic uses a related word for the fleshy flaps that hang below a goat's ears: *zanamah*. The classical lexicographers noted that such appendages serve no useful function, and from that image *zanīm* acquired the sense of something attached to a body without contributing to it. If the sūrah intends that overtone, the final word describes a man who is not merely of doubtful origin but surplus to the world: present, loud, and of no use to anything. The Book's sharpest vocabulary is reserved for those who obstruct its message, and there is no softer way to read this line of it.

**What the Book Weighs Instead of Descent**

The Qur'an's treatment of lineage is consistent, and it gives the sūrah's use of this word its proper frame. Descent is not a virtue in the Book; it is a fact, and the only measurements it applies to people are belief and conduct. The sūrah's own portrait confirms the standard by what it does with wealth. The man's possessions and sons are not evidence of God's favour, and the Book dismisses them in the verse that follows: *“the Day when neither wealth nor children will be of any benefit. Only those who come before Allah with a pure heart ˹will be saved˺.”* (26:88–89). A pure heart, not a strong lineage, is what carries weight.

So the sūrah enumerates everything the man relied on and pronounces each item worthless in turn: his wealth, his children, his oaths, his information, his temper, and finally his name.

**The Portrait as a Warning to Believers**

Six descriptions now stand together, and the community is told not to obey the man they fit. The instruction is not directed at the man; it is directed at anyone tempted to treat him as a leader. The Book is protecting its readers by making the character type legible, and the list is as notable for what it leaves out as for what it contains. It contains no accusation of poverty, no criticism of low birth, no complaint about weakness. Every item is a vice of power misused: speech, wealth, boundaries, temper, and standing.

That selection tells the reader where the danger to a religious community usually comes from. It is rarely the poor and the powerless who obstruct a message; the Book's historical record is consistent that the opposition comes from those with something to protect.

**What the Community Was Being Told About Leadership**

The instruction attached to this portrait is a negative one — do not obey — and the portrait itself supplies the criteria by which a community should judge those who seek to lead it. A leader whose word is cheap, whose tongue carries tales, whose hand withholds what is due, who crosses boundaries, and whose temper frightens his own people is disqualified by his own habits before any question of doctrine is raised. The Book's method here is to describe rather than to legislate, which leaves the judgment with the reader and makes the lesson portable: the same description can be applied to any candidate in any century.

That method also protects the community from a particular temptation. Wealth and lineage are the two most visible forms of social weight, and they are precisely the two things this man had in abundance; the Book exposes them because they are the two things a person cannot help noticing. What it asks its readers to notice instead is conduct over time.

**How the Sūrah Ends the Portrait**

Having built the description to its harshest word, the sūrah does not soften it or explain it. It moves to the man's wealth and children in the next verse, then to his mockery of the revelation, and then to the threat of a branding that will expose him before everyone he impressed. The structure is that of a diagnosis followed by a prognosis: this is the character, and this is the end of it. The believer reading it is meant to take away a single practical lesson, which is that public standing is not evidence of anything, least of all of guidance.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:14

> Now, ˹simply˺ because he has been blessed with ˹abundant˺ wealth and children,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Fortune as Evidence in His Own Mind**

The verse explains why the man behaves as he does, and the explanation is one sentence long: he has wealth and sons, and he has concluded from them that he can afford his contempt. The exegetical reports identify the figure as al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, a man whose household and holdings made him a power in Makkah, and the sūrah shows the reasoning by which such a man arrived at his position: the gifts must mean something, and what they must mean is approval. The verse does not bother to refute the logic in detail. It states it, and leaves the reader to see how thin it is.

Thin, because it proves far too much. If possessions were a verdict, then every wealthy scoffer in every generation would be vindicated and every destitute believer condemned, and the Book's own messengers — most of whom lived with little — would be exactly what their opponents said they were. The argument from success collapses the moment it is applied consistently.

**The Book's Refusal to Read Providence Without a Scale**

The Qur'an states the counter-principle plainly: distribution in this world is not a ranking. *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Surely ˹it is˺ my Lord ˹Who˺ gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills. But most people do not know.””* (34:36). The verse puts abundance and scarcity in the same sentence, assigns both to the same will, and closes by noting that the arrangement is generally misread. The misunderstanding is not a modern one; it is the standard human error that revelation exists partly to correct.

The reason the arrangement cannot serve as a verdict is that it has a different purpose. Things are given to be used, kept, and accounted for; they are the material of a test rather than the announcement of a result. The Book says as much in a phrase: *“Your wealth and children are only a test, but Allah ˹alone˺ has a great reward.”* (64:15).

**Children as the Deeper Illusion**

Wealth can be spent; children feel like permanence. A man with strong sons has defenders, heirs, and a name. The Book recognizes the strength of that attachment and repeatedly warns against it, because it is exactly the attachment that makes a person resistant to a message that asks him to set his own standing aside. A verse addressed to the believers states the danger in terms of priority: *“O believers! Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah. For whoever does so, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.”* (63:9). The warning is not against having children; it is against the inversion in which the family becomes the standard by which revelation is judged.

A second verse supplies the corrective in a comparison: *“Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope.”* (18:46). Adornment is not worthless; it is decorative, and it belongs to the surface of a life whose depth lies elsewhere. The sūrah of mutual rivalry states the cost of the alternative in four lines: competition for gain occupies a person until he arrives at his grave, and the account of what he enjoyed is still owed (102:1–8). A man who has built his identity out of his possessions is not simply mistaken; he has mortgaged his sense of self to something that is going to be taken from him.

**The Precedent of Men Who Trusted the Same Evidence**

The Qur'an preserves the record of an earlier prophet whose people made exactly this argument, and it reports the outcome in a way that leaves the pattern unmistakable: Noah complained to his Lord that his opponents had followed those elite whose abundance only increased their loss (71:21). The phrase the Book uses — wealth and children increasing a people in loss — is the whole of this verse's claim in advance, and the historical record supplies the ending that the Makkan argument lacked. What looks like proof of divine favour can be the very instrument of ruin.

The Makkan elders of this generation knew the ruins of earlier nations on their trade routes. The lesson was available; the confidence of wealth made it unnecessary to learn.

**The Alternative Reading and What It Adds**

The exegetes recorded a second way to construe the grammar, producing the sense: is it because he has wealth and children that, when the signs are recited to him, he says *ancient fables*? On that reading, the clause supplies the motive for the mockery rather than the ground of the man's self-importance. The two readings arrive at the same account of the man, and they agree on the mechanism: his position in the world made the message dismissible, because accepting it would have demoted him.

The verse's own form confirms this. It does not say that he was punished for being rich; the punishment that follows is attached to his behaviour after the mention of the fortune, not to the fortune itself.

**What the Sūrah's Readers Were Meant to Take Away**

A small community in a city of traders needed this correction more than it needed almost anything else, and the sūrah supplies it in the middle of a portrait of a powerful opponent. The message is practical: do not measure the truth of a claim by the size of the house its opponent lives in, and do not measure your own standing by the number of your sons. Both measurements were being applied to the Prophet ﷺ by his enemies, and both were being applied to themselves. The following verses complete the portrait with the words the man used when the revelation reached him.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:15

> whenever Our revelations are recited to him, he says, “Ancient fables!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Words Against the Whole Recitation**

The man's entire response to revelation is recorded in two words, and the sūrah quotes them as he said them. *Asāṭīr al-awwalīn* — fables of the ancients — reduces the recitation to folklore and its messenger to a storyteller repeating what he has heard. It is a dismissal rather than an argument, and its appeal lies precisely there: no counter-claim has to be defended, no evidence produced, no text examined. A man of the position described in the previous verses could afford that kind of remark, and the Book preserves it as the whole of his contribution.

The dismissal also has a target beyond the words. If the Qur'an is old material, then the God who is said to have sent it never spoke, and the question of obedience disappears. The phrase is therefore not a literary judgement; it is a theological position that avoids the trouble of being stated as one.

**The Fuller Version of the Accusation**

Other sūrahs record how the accusation sounded when the men of Makkah developed it, and the fuller versions are worth setting beside this one. They claimed transcription and coaching: *“And they say, “˹These revelations are only˺ ancient fables which he has had written down, and they are rehearsed to him morning and evening.””* (25:5). They claimed they could match it if they chose: *“Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they challenge ˹you˺, “We have already heard ˹the recitation˺. If we wanted, we could have easily produced something similar. This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but ancient fables!””* (8:31). And they claimed, in the most economical version, that it was nothing they had not been told before: *“And when it is said to them, “What has your Lord revealed?” They say, “Ancient fables!””* (16:24).

Set side by side, the claims contradict each other: the Book is simultaneously an unmatched miracle and an easily imitated imitation of old stories. The Book keeps both on file and lets them stand.

**Why "Old" Is Not an Argument**

The centre of the accusation is the age of the material, and the Book's response is to refuse the premise that age decides truth. A fact does not become false by being ancient, nor true by being new; if it did, the history of prophecy would be a record of error, since every messenger brought what his people already considered old. The irony is that the same men who dismissed the revelation for resembling old stories rested their own religion on the practices of their forefathers, which was the oldest thing in the city. Antiquity was an argument when it supported them and a defect when it did not, and the Book's repeated exposure of that inconsistency is aimed at the inconsistency rather than at the age.

What the phrase does show is that the audience had heard something. The accusation refers to the narratives of earlier prophets that the recitation contained, and acknowledging the resemblance meant acknowledging that the material was known to be from the tradition of revelation.

**A Formula Recorded in Eight Places**

The Book reports the same phrase from the mouths of deniers in eight other verses, and the repetition is itself a statement. The Qur'an is presenting the history of how revelation is received: the same objection, in the same words, offered by generation after generation, from the people of Noah to the audience in Makkah. A reader who encounters the formula repeatedly stops hearing it as a clever remark and begins to see it as a reflex, which is what the Book's insistence on recording it accomplishes.

The formula also has a counterpart in the sūrah's next line, where the response to the mockery is given in a single sentence. The Book pairs the record of the insult with the record of the answer, and the answer does not take the form of a rebuttal of the phrase.

**What Mockery May Cost the Man Who Used It**

The sūrah describes a man surrounded by wealth and sons, unaccountable to anyone in his city, delivering two-word verdicts on a text that thousands would later memorise. Nothing forced him to examine the recitation, and nothing in his position rewarded close attention. That is the practical cost of contempt: it closes the file. A person who decides in advance what something is will never find out what it is, and the Book's account of his end will not be a discussion of his opinion.

Another sūrah describes what the habit does to the person who maintains it, and the description is not about the ears but the heart: *“Whenever Our revelations are recited to them, they say, “Ancient fables!” But no! In fact, their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit!”* (83:13–14). The dismissal is not the cause of the stain; it is evidence of it. Repeated mockery leaves a residue, and after enough of it the text cannot reach the place where it would have to be judged.

The same pattern recurs as long as there are powerful men and revealed texts. Reducing a message to its supposed origins is a way of ending the conversation, and it works until the conversation is taken up elsewhere.

**The Book's Own Answer to the Charge**

The Qur'an answers the accusation of human authorship not by defending itself against each detail but by pointing to the future. *“We will show them Our signs in the universe and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that this ˹Quran˺ is the truth. Is it not enough that your Lord is a Witness over all things?”* (41:53). The claim is staked on display rather than on argument, and the audience is asked to wait and watch — an unusual position for a dismissed text to take, and an answer that only works if the text is what it says it is.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:16

> We will soon mark his snout.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Metaphor of the Brand**

The sentence is built on the practice of branding animals. A mark is burned onto the nose of livestock to show who owns it, and a nose is the most visible point on the face of a beast being led. The verse takes that image and applies it to a man of great standing in his city: the one who spoke of revelation as folklore will be marked like an animal, in public, permanently. Nothing in the description is subtle, and nothing about it is intended to be. The Book's answer to mockery is not a jest in return but an image that cannot be laughed off.

The word for this branding in Arabic is also used for a stigma, a mark that identifies something as disgraced, and that double sense gives the verse its range: what is applied to the body of a beast is also applied to the reputation of a man.

**Three Readings of the Promise**

The exegetes offered more than one account of what the verse promises, and the differences are instructive. On the first reading, it is an idiom for irreversible disgrace: the man will carry a mark that people recognise, and his standing among his own tribe will never recover. On the second, taken more literally, the promise was fulfilled at the Battle of Badr, where the reports describe him receiving a wound to the nose — the exegetical tradition identifies the man with one of the prominent opponents who fell at that engagement. On the third, the branding is the exposure of a hidden reality: he will be shown to be exactly what he is, as the Book says of the condemned generally, *“The wicked will be recognized by their appearance, then will be seized by ˹their˺ forelocks and feet.”* (55:41).

The readings are not mutually exclusive, and the third gives the verse its widest application, since the humiliation described is a permanent condition rather than a single event.

**A Man Who Claimed Not to Be Property**

The image's theology is more pointed than its cruelty. This is a man whose entire posture in Makkah rested on not being owned by anyone: not by the messenger, not by the message, not even by the reckoning. He was an elder, a patron, a man whose word opened doors and closed them. The verse answers by describing him with the vocabulary of livestock, marked on the nose by a master. The reduction is the message. Autonomy of the kind he claimed does not exist in the Book's account of the world; the only question is whose mark a person carries.

The Book makes the same point from the other direction when it tells the believers that nothing in creation falls outside its owner's knowledge, and when it describes God as the inheritor of everything the powerful consider theirs.

**The Course of the Reports**

The early reports about the man described in this passage record that his end came within a short time of the revelation and that it was remembered by his own people as an unexpected and humiliating one. The reader of the sūrah does not need to adjudicate the details in order to grasp what the sūrah itself claims: the word *soon* is a promise with a date attached, and the Book's habit is to keep its promises in this world and in the next. The sureness of the wording is the point. A man whose position looked unassailable is told that a mark is coming and that no protector can prevent it.

The detail that would most have embarrassed the pagans of Makkah was not the wound but the visibility. Their society ran on reputation, and a branded man is a public possession.

**Exposure as the Shape of Judgment**

Across the Qur'an, the punishment of the arrogant takes the form of disclosure. What they concealed about themselves becomes legible; what they boasted of becomes the record against them. This verse belongs to that pattern, and its image is the most compact expression of it: a mark on the face, impossible to cover, identifying its bearer to everyone he met. The Book's last word on a man who traded his entire life on appearances is that the appearances will be taken over by someone else and rewritten.

**The Mark on the Face, and Why the Face**

A brand is placed where it cannot be hidden, and the Book's choice of the face is not incidental. Social standing in Makkah rested on the face: the face one presented in the market, at the sanctuary, in the negotiation of marriages and alliances. A man with a damaged or marked face carried his disgrace in front of him, and everyone who met him read it first. The humiliation designed here is therefore proportionate to the crime in a precise way. A man spent his fortune and his influence manufacturing an impression; the impression is confiscated and replaced with one he cannot correct.

The Book uses faces throughout its descriptions of the last day, darkened or brightened according to what their owners did, and it treats the face as the public summary of a person. The branding promised in this verse takes that summary out of the owner's hands.

**"Soon" and How the Book Measures Time**

The particle *sa-* marks the future as near, and the Book applies it to events of very different distances. It is used of the Hereafter, of the fall of cities, and of the vindication of the wronged, and in each case its function is the same: it removes the option of treating the outcome as remote. The reader cannot say that the matter is far off, since the Book's grammar refuses to say it. The sureness of the promise and its proximity are the same statement, and this verse leaves the man whose snout is to be marked with no interval in which to feel secure.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:17

> Indeed, We have tested those ˹Meccans˺ as We tested the owners of the garden—when they swore they would surely harvest ˹all˺ its fruit in the early morning,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Parable Announced as a Test**

The verse does not introduce a story for entertainment. It states the frame first: the Makkans are being tested, and they are being tested in the same way that a group of gardeners were tested. The comparison tells the audience what kind of event their own situation is. They had been hearing the recitation for years and measuring their response to it by their interests; the verse informs them that the arrival of a messenger is an examination, and that wealth and harvest are also examinations. Everything they had was a test with a deadline.

The plural *those* refers to the people being addressed, and the pronoun does the work of making the parable personal before a single detail of the story is told.

**The Owners of the Garden in the Reports**

The exegetical tradition supplies the setting, and the details are consistent about the essentials. The garden belonged to a man who had left it to his sons, and his custom during his lifetime had been to set aside a portion of every harvest for the poor who came to its edges. After his death the brothers counted their obligations, looked at the size of their families, and concluded that the old arrangement was no longer affordable. Their father's rule was a habit they could not see the point of, and the produce was theirs to decide about.

The reports differ on the number of brothers and their country. What the sūrah itself supplies is enough: a group of owners, a garden, a poor population accustomed to its yield, and a plan to gather the crop before anyone could ask.

**The Early Harvest and the Purpose of the Hour**

The detail chosen from the plan is the hour. They swore to harvest in the early morning — before the town woke, before the gleaners arrived, before anyone could stand at the gate and make a claim. The sūrah will report their instruction to one another on the road, that no poor person should be allowed in that day, and the timing is the implementation of that instruction. This is not a plan to sell early or to beat the heat; it is a plan to be unobserved.

The Book treats that intention as the heart of the offence. The brothers were not stealing from anyone, and the harvest was legally theirs; what they were doing was arranging their affairs so that a duty they had inherited would be impossible to fulfil.

**The Due of the Harvest in the Qur'an**

An instruction in another sūrah states the obligation these men were evading, and states it in the context of the gift itself: *“He is the One Who produces gardens—both cultivated and wild — and palm trees, crops of different flavours, olives, and pomegranates— similar ˹in shape˺, but dissimilar ˹in taste˺. Eat from the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful.”* (6:141). The command to pay at the moment of harvest leaves no season in which the poor can be told to come later; the claim travels with the fruit.

The sūrah's audience understood the arrangement. Gleaning rights and shares for the poor were part of the agricultural life of the region, and a landowner who abolished them was altering a social rule in order to alter the terms of a divine one.

**Wealth as a Test Rather Than a Prize**

The verse's frame does more than introduce a story; it states the theology the story illustrates. Produce comes from rain, soil, and seed, and the owners contributed the last of those only: *“Have you considered what you sow? Is it you who cause it to grow, or is it We Who do so? If We willed, We could simply reduce this ˹harvest˺ to chaff, leaving you to lament.”* (56:63–65). The conclusion follows: no one should congratulate himself for what he did not produce and pay no attention to what he did.

Their harvest was theirs in law and God's in fact, and the interval between those two facts was exactly the life they were living.

**Why the Book Tells This Kind of Story**

The Qur'an's parables are not decorative and the Book says so plainly: *“Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito or what is even smaller. As for the believers, they know that it is the truth from their Lord. And as for the disbelievers, they argue, “What does Allah mean by such a parable?” Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray, and guides many.”* (2:26). An illustration in this Book is a test in its own right, and revelation states without apology that the testing is intentional. A reader who dismisses the story as an irrelevant tale for peasants has already answered the parable's question about himself.

The same principle appears where the Book describes the effect of its own material on the audience: *“And We have made what We brought you to see as well as the cursed tree ˹mentioned˺ in the Quran only as a test for the people. We keep warning them, but it only increases them greatly in defiance.”* (17:60). A parable that fixes a lesson in the mind and a warning that hardens a heart are the same revelation meeting two different people.

**How the Sūrah Uses the Story**

The parable is planted in the middle of a sūrah about a man who mocked revelation because he was rich. The Makkans are asked to hear a story about a garden and recognise the arithmetic of their own situation: a good thing received, a duty attached to it, a decision to keep it all, and a confidence that nothing could interfere. The story's outcome is what makes it a warning rather than a moral tale, and the sūrah reports it in detail — the oath they swore, the exception they failed to make, the night that came, and the voices they used when they saw what was left.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:18

> leaving no thought for Allah’s Will.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Clause Their Oath Was Missing**

The verse adds a small detail to the plan reported in the previous one, and the detail is the whole of their crime at the level of intention. They swore that the harvest would be taken in the morning, and they did not add *if God wills*. The Arabic term for that addition is *istithnāʾ*, the placing of an exception beside a determination, and the Qur'an makes it a rule of speech for believers: *“And never say of anything, “I will definitely do this tomorrow,” without adding, “if Allah so wills!” But if you forget, then remember your Lord, and say, “I trust my Lord will guide me to what is more right than this.””* (18:23–24). The formula is not a verbal charm; it is the acknowledgement that the future is not held by the person planning it.

What the brothers had done was to swear an oath about the future as if the future were a tool in their hands, and the Book reports the omission because it exposes the state of mind that produced the plan to exclude the poor.

**Why the Formula Is Not Superstition**

A reader could mistake *if God wills* for a polite hesitation, and the Book's treatment of it rules that out. The clause appears at the heart of decisions, not at their edges; it belongs to the moment when a person states what he is going to do, and its function is to keep that statement inside the truth about who decides. The Qur'an returns to this reality from many angles, including the assertion that God's will is never thwarted by human intention alone and that His delay in punishing was itself a willed postponement.

Seen that way, the missing clause was not a grammatical omission. It was the technical expression of a presumption that had already taken hold: the owners of the garden believed, in a way they had never had to examine, that tomorrow belonged to them.

**The Alternative Reading: No Portion Left for the Poor**

The commentators offered a second way to read the phrase, taking the exception to refer not to the divine will but to the harvest: no portion was excepted for the poor. On that reading the verse says simply that they left nothing out of their claim. The two readings fit the story's logic without competing, since the exclusion of the poor and the exclusion of God are the same error in two registers. A man who has removed God from the operation of his property will find no reason to leave a share of it for people who cannot pay for it.

The rest of the passage supplies the demonstration. Their whisper on the road is that no poor person should enter the garden that day, and the sentence is the practical outworking of a decision already made when the oath was sworn.

**Oaths That Had Already Cost Them Nothing**

The Book has already used this sūrah to criticise a man who swore habitually; here an entire group swears with the same lightness, and the pattern is worth noticing. An oath that is used to secure a plan against interruption is not worship; it is a technique for making arrangements feel inevitable. The men of Makkah who took oaths casually and the brothers in the garden produced the same product in different quantities: a confidence in their own arrangements that no longer admitted any contingency.

Revelation's response to that confidence is consistent throughout the Book, and it is not a lecture. It is the arrival of events the planners had not scheduled.

**Planning, and the Line Between It and Presumption**

Nothing in this passage condemns preparation. The brothers were not criticised for harvesting, for keeping accounts, or for wanting a good yield; agricultural work requires exactly those things. What the verse identifies is a step beyond planning, in which a person treats his arrangements as controlling rather than contingent, and it identifies that step by its smallest visible trace — the absence of two words.

The Qur'an's guidance here is a discipline of speech that produces a discipline of mind. A person who says *if God wills* whenever he describes what he intends is training himself to hold his plans as plans, and the training protects him from the particular blindness that prosperity produces.

**The Clause Says Something About Time**

Behind the formula lies a fact about human knowledge that the Book states in several places: the future is not available to the planner. *“Indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ has the knowledge of the Hour. He sends down the rain, and knows what is in the wombs. No soul knows what it will earn for tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die.”* (31:34). A person who has no knowledge of tomorrow's earnings and no knowledge of the place of his own death is in a poor position to swear that a harvest will be taken at dawn.

That is the substance of the clause, and it explains why the Book attaches it to speech about action rather than to speech about belief. Belief is about what has already been revealed; action is about a future that no one holds. Saying *if God wills* is therefore a way of keeping a person's confidence in his own arrangements from hardening into a claim about the world.

**What the Verse Teaches About Ownership**

The underlying subject of the sentence is property, and the sūrah's treatment of it rests on a claim it states elsewhere in the same Book: what a person holds arrives as provision, and provision arrives with conditions attached. The owners of the garden had inherited both land and custom; they kept the land and cancelled the custom, then swore to gather the whole yield before anyone else could take a share of it. The missing exception is the hinge on which the whole story turns, and the night that follows it does not come as an accident.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:19

> Then it was struck by a torment from your Lord while they slept,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Night They Had Reserved for Their Own Plan**

The story's timing is exact. The brothers had decided to harvest at first light, before the town stirred, so that no one would see the produce leave the garden and no request could be made. The verses fall into the darkness they had chosen for their own purposes, and the vineyard is destroyed while its owners are unconscious. Nothing could state the lesson more economically. The men who believed they controlled the coming day were asleep when the day was decided.

The Book's narrative method here is worth noticing: it does not include a voice from heaven, a warning, or an argument. The event simply arrives, and the owners learn about it the way everyone else does — by looking.

**The Arabic Word Behind the Torment**

The noun rendered *torment* in this line is *ṭāʾif*, formed from the verb for circling, and it describes something that comes round a place, the way a night patrol or a wandering fire comes round before it settles. The word suggests a movement that encircles its target, and the exegetes understood it as the scourge appointed for the garden, arriving at the hour it was sent for. The phrase *from your Lord* completes the sense: what came round the walls that night was not a random occurrence in nature but something dispatched by the owner of the garden's owner.

**Reports on What Came**

The classical commentators, filling in the story, report that a fire descended on the garden and burned it to the ground, leaving the fruit destroyed and the trees blackened. The evidence for their reading is internal: the verses that follow describe the owners arriving at dawn to gather and finding a place that answers to no description they can recognise, and later verses in the same Book use precisely this image for a harvest reduced to chaff. Whatever the mechanism, the sūrah's own words are enough: a visitation came, and what had been a garden became something the owners could not identify from a distance.

The Book describes this same hour of arrival in precisely these terms in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf: *“Did the people of those societies feel secure that Our punishment would not come upon them by night while they were asleep?”* (7:97), followed immediately by the daylight version: *“Or did they feel secure that Our punishment would come upon them by day while they were at play?”* (7:98). The owners of the garden were struck in the first of the two conditions the passage names, and the sūrah's wording marks it: the visitation arrived while the household was asleep.

**"From Your Lord" — the Attribution the Planners Omitted**

The verse attaches the disaster to its author in three words: *from your Lord*. The phrase answers the omission of the previous verse. The brothers had made no exception for God's will when they planned; the sūrah now supplies the will they had ignored, and it does so with the same possessive construction that runs through the whole passage. The garden was theirs by inheritance; the visitation was His by right.

The Bible and the Qur'an agree that a person may labour and plant, and that the increase is not his to command. Where they differ from much human speech is in the refusal to describe this as an accident of nature. The Book's world is too thoroughly governed for that.

**Slept: the Hour When Claimants Are Most Helpless**

There is a reason the destruction comes at night, and the verse names it in the last word. Sleep is the one state in which a human being's claim to control his own affairs is visibly false; he lies down in a house he did not build, on ground he cannot hold, and stops being awake to guard anything. The Book elsewhere points to sleep as one of the ordinary mercies by which lives are maintained, which makes its appearance here more pointed: the same sleep that is a gift is also the demonstration of the receivers' dependence.

The men of Makkah who heard this story trusted in the arrangements they had made for the morning. The verse's image asks them to consider what their arrangements were worth during the hours when they were not awake to supervise them.

**The Pattern of Judgements That Arrive Unannounced**

The Book repeats this form of punishment across its narratives, and the repetition gives the verse its wider resonance. The people of Lot met their end before dawn, with a rain of stones: *“We unleashed upon them a storm of stones. As for ˹the believers of˺ Lot's family, We delivered them before dawn”* (54:34). The cities of the plain were overturned by a command that came as they slept. The army of the Elephant was destroyed on the road. In each case the blow fell on people who had made their plans and had not expected to be interrupted.

That consistency is the message. What happened to the garden was not an unusual punishment reserved for stingy farmers; it is the ordinary manner in which a delay ends.

**The Night as the Book's Recurring Hour of Judgement**

The Qur'an returns to the hours of darkness for its narrations of sudden punishment, and the choice seems deliberate. The night is when human beings delegate their affairs to their own arrangements — locked doors, walls, darkness, and sleep — and it is at that point that the Book has judgement arrive. The cover of night, which the owners had chosen to give their plan its advantage, is turned into the cover under which the loss occurred. Whatever protection the darkness was supposed to provide worked in the opposite direction, and the men who depended on not being seen were the ones who saw nothing.

That reversal is the sūrah's quiet comment on the plan. The brothers wanted an unobserved harvest; the observation they avoided was not the kind that mattered.

**What the Owners Could Not Defend**

Nothing in the passage suggests that the brothers were attacked by an enemy, outwitted by rivals, or punished by the legal authority of their district. Their walls, their title deeds, their family, and their plans were all in place, and none of them had any bearing on what came. The next verse will report the result in one image — a garden swept bare — and the verses after that will record the argument the brothers had among themselves. What was lost in the night could not be recovered by any of the resources they had been counting on, and the sūrah is careful to show them awake and talking rather than struck down, because the point is to display their helplessness, not to end their story.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:20

> so it was reduced to ashes.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Image in the Arabic: a Garden Reaped**

The word translated *ashes* is *ṣarīm*. In the ordinary vocabulary of agriculture it means a field that has been cut and cleared — a place where the harvest has been taken and nothing is left standing. That is the first shock of the verse: the garden the owners intended to strip that morning had already been stripped. What they thought they were going to do to the fruit had been done to the entire property, and it had been done by someone else, in the dark, without arrangements.

The word can also mean the blackness of night, and the commentators noted that the wreckage of the garden could be described as something dark spread across the ground. Either way the owners arriving at dawn were looking at a surface rather than an orchard.

**What the Owners Were About to Do, Done to Them**

The offence being punished in this passage is exclusion. The brothers intended to take everything before anyone could ask, and they wanted the poor kept outside the gate. What they found was a garden from which everything had been taken by a hand they could not identify — a total harvest of which they received nothing. The Book's punishments often take this matching shape, and the sūrah leaves the mirroring unstated because stating it would weaken it. The reader sees the plan and the result in adjacent verses and draws the conclusion himself.

The same logic governs the Book's wider treatment of hoarding. A person who refuses to let his wealth move is visited by a movement he did not choose, and the vocabulary the Book uses for divine seizure elsewhere is drawn from the same family of images: the taking of a thing in its entirety.

**The Silence of the Verse About the Method**

The verse does not name fire, wind, a plague, or an army. It reports only the result, which is the destruction of the whole. That restraint is characteristic of the Qur'an's treatment of divine punishment, and it serves a purpose: an unexplained event compels the reader to look for the author rather than the mechanism. The verse that follows records the owners' words at dawn, and their first reaction is complete disorientation — they could not identify the place, and then could not accept the loss. The reader, who has been told that a visitation from the Lord came upon it while they slept, knows more than they do at that moment, and the gap is the point.

Modern readers sometimes ask what the physical cause was, and the text refuses to satisfy the curiosity, as it does with the armies of the Elephant and the fire of Lot's towns. The Book's narratives of judgement report the arrival and the result; the machinery is left where it belongs.

**A Reversal That Leaves Nothing Behind**

The brothers had counted on a garden that would keep producing: sons to inherit it, harvests coming round every year, the value of the land rising. The word *ṣarīm* ends the arithmetic in one stroke. A productive estate becomes a surface; an asset becomes evidence; a plan for the next generation becomes a conversation about what went wrong. The Book uses this collapse repeatedly because it is the most direct refutation of the confidence that wealth produces in a person who has forgotten where it came from.

What the owners still had was their lives, their families, and one another — and the verses that follow show those assets under immediate strain.

**The Punishment That Leaves the Lesson Behind**

Unlike the towns of a previous era, the brothers survived, and the sūrah keeps them alive to speak. Their argument among themselves, their realisation, and their hope in the next verses turn the story into something more useful than a ruin: a case study in repentance after a loss that cannot be recovered. The destruction is total at the level of property and partial at the level of the people, and that combination is exactly what the sūrah's Makkan audience needed to consider, since they were still in the position the brothers had occupied the night before.

**The Ruin That Matches the Region's History**

An image from another sūrah gives this one its wider frame. The Book describes the fate of societies that persisted in wrongdoing, and it chooses the vocabulary of abandoned agriculture and empty construction: *“Many are the societies We have destroyed for persisting in wrongdoing, leaving them in total ruin. ˹Many are˺ also the abandoned wells and lofty palaces!”* (22:45). A garden reduced to a dark surface belongs to the same family of pictures as a well that no one draws from and a palace with no one in it. What the Book presents as the end of arrogance is not always fire and flood; frequently it is property that outlives its owners in a useless state.

The brothers had built their plan on the assumption that the garden would outlast them, that its value would pass to their children and their children's children. The verse leaves them standing in front of it at dawn, and the chapter's judgement is that what they had was never theirs to bequeath in the first place.

**Where the Story Turns Next**

The garden is gone in a single sentence, and the sūrah immediately moves to the morning: the owners call each other, decide to hurry, walk out whispering their plan, and reach the gate. Everything they do on that road is an illustration of the previous day's confidence, and the verses let the reader watch a plan being executed by men who do not yet know it is irrelevant. The rest of the story belongs to the following verses, and its shape is already fixed: a garden that kept nothing will now correct the people who meant to keep everything.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:21

> Then by daybreak they called out to each other,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Call That Began the Working Day**

The verse moves the story out of the night and into the first activity of the day. The brothers woke and began summoning each other, which is what every household of landowners did on a harvest morning: the news travelled from bed to bed, the tools came out, and the day took the shape that had been planned for it. Nothing in the sentence suggests doubt, hesitation, or a bad night; the men had gone to sleep with a decision and they woke up with it intact.

That ordinary quality is what makes the next verses so effective. The story of a family's ruin is told through the routines of a normal morning, and the reader watches the routine continue right up to the moment it meets the ruined garden.

The Arabic verb behind *called out to each other* is reciprocal in form, built to describe an action that people perform on one another. The brothers did not merely call; they roused each other in turn, and the shape of the word tells the reader that the household was working as a unit. The same pattern serves the Book for people who advise one another, who question one another, and, in other passages, for people who turn on one another once a loss has to be explained. In this verse the activity is innocent; within a few lines the same family will be using its speech against itself.

The sentence reports no reply and names nobody. What the reader receives is the noise of an organised morning, and the placement of the verse makes the reader hear it from the wrong side of the event, since the visitation and the ruined garden have already been described. The men calling to each other do not know what the audience knows.

**Morning as the Hour of Worship in the Book**

The Qur'an places the earliest hours among the times when creation is asked to acknowledge its Lord. One passage joins the morning to the evening as a refrain of praise: *“So glorify Allah in the evening and in the morning— all praise is for Him in the heavens and the earth—as well as in the afternoon, and at noon.”* (30:17–18). The Prophet ﷺ was instructed to keep the same rhythm: *“So be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say. And glorify the praises of your Lord before sunrise and before sunset, and glorify Him in the hours of the night and at both ends of the day, so that you may be pleased ˹with the reward˺.”* (20:130). Even a sign given to Zakariyyā took the form of an instruction to fill those hours with remembrance: *“Remember your Lord often and glorify ˹Him˺ morning and evening.”* (3:41).

The brothers used the hour for something else. The Book does not criticise early work; it lets the reader notice which voice was heard in the garden that morning and which was not.

**Why Dawn Is Also the Book's Hour of Judgement**

The hour chosen for the harvest is the hour the Book most often assigns to sudden punishment. Lot's people were destroyed before daybreak, with the believers among them delivered at the same time: *“We unleashed upon them a storm of stones. As for ˹the believers of˺ Lot's family, We delivered them before dawn”* (54:34). The visitation on the garden came while its owners slept, which means it came during the hours before this verse. The men waking and calling one another are therefore calling from the far side of a decision that has already been carried out.

Dawn serves both purposes in the Book: it is when the day's work is taken up, and it is when judgements tend to arrive. The two are not unrelated. A morning is the moment when a person commits to the plan he made overnight, and the Book has a habit of showing what that plan was worth.

**The Voice That Organised the Family**

The verse gives the scene one detail and no dialogue. Someone was first awake; that person called, and the rest answered. The household machinery of the ancient world ran on such calls, and the Book's decision to report this one places the reader inside the ordinary competence of men who were good at managing an estate. Everything in the passage up to this point has described capable farmers: they kept accounts, they timed their harvests, they coordinated their workforce. Their competence was real, and the story's point is not that they were foolish.

Their competence is precisely what made the plan attractive, and the verses have allowed the reader to watch it become the reason for the disaster.

**What the Sūrah Lets the Reader Hear**

The reader knows more than the men speaking. The temporary visitor of the previous verse has already come, the garden is already gone, and the call to work is being issued by owners who have lost the thing they are calling about. The gap between what the characters know and what the audience knows is the sūrah's device for teaching.

That is the ordinary condition of anyone receiving a warning about the future, and the sūrah does not confine the lesson to the garden. The Book tells its readers what the end of a life looks like while they are still able to arrange their affairs, and the men of this passage are an illustration of what happens when the arrangement is completed first.

**The Difference Between This Morning and the Morning They Expected**

The brothers had planned for the twenty-four hours ahead in detail, and their plan assumed a garden. The verse's quiet description of a family waking up becomes the last moment of that assumption, and everything after it in the sūrah is the sound of a plan being dismantled. The day will still have its hours; the work will not be done.

For a reader, the scene does one more thing. It shows how thoroughly a person can be wrong about his own position and how normal the wrongness looks from the inside. Nobody in this passage suspects anything, and the sūrah lets the reader feel how ordinary that composure is before it takes the garden away.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:22

> ˹saying,˺ “Go early to your harvest, if you want to pick ˹all˺ the fruit.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Words That Were Said Out Loud**

The morning call is reported in detail, and what the brothers said to each other was straightforward farm talk: get out early, the crop is ready, pick it all. There was nothing in the sentence that a neighbour would have found strange — this is how a harvesting day begins in any century. The sūrah gives the reader the ordinary instruction and then, in the next verse, the whispered version of the same plan. The two are one decision stated twice, once for the workforce and once among the owners.

**"If You Want to Pick All the Fruit"**

The clause carries the weight of the verse. The instructions the brothers gave were about totality: every fruit, no remainder, nothing left standing for anyone else. The Book's word for the payment owed at harvest is tied to the same moment, and the command that establishes it says: *“Eat from the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful.”* (6:141). The brothers did not intend to waste anything — they intended to keep everything, which is the failure the verse of the harvest due was revealed to prevent.

**The Due at Harvest: One-Tenth and One-Twentieth**

The obligation the gardeners were evading is not left to common sense in Islamic law. A well-known report from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, sets the rate according to how the land is watered: one-tenth of the yield when the land is irrigated by rain or natural channels, and half of one-tenth when the water has to be lifted by effort (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1483; Muslim 981). The payment is due when the crop is harvested, and it is made from the produce itself.

Two features of the rule belong to this verse. The first is the moment: the claim travels with the fruit, so a harvest cannot be reorganised to postpone it. The second is the graded rate: land that receives rain pays more than land that must be irrigated, because the gift received is larger. A bookkeeping system built on gratitude looks like this.

**The Poor in the Middle of the Harvest Field**

Alongside the obligatory share, the practice of the region left gleanings and specific claims to those who came to the field: the edges of a crop, the fallen produce, and the portion given to the poor at the time the harvest was taken. Landowners who honoured the custom were treating their property as a trust with designated beneficiaries, and the brothers' scheme was aimed precisely at the window of time in which those beneficiaries could present themselves.

This is why the exclusion planned for the next verse is described as whispering. A right that everyone acknowledged could not be revoked out loud; it had to be defeated by timing, and the crime being punished in this passage is committed with a schedule rather than with a weapon.

**Plans That Require Silence to Work**

A person who intends to do what everyone would approve of does not lower his voice. The sūrah's structure makes the observation without stating it: the public call — go early, take everything — is followed by a private conference whose content is a restriction. The two sentences together show a family that knew the difference between what its neighbours would say and what its members had decided to do.

The Book reports this instinct often enough to have made it a diagnostic. Secrecy about a good deed is modesty; secrecy about a distribution of property usually means the distribution has been decided against the people who are not present.

**What the Brothers Understood About Ownership**

The men in this passage were not confused about the law. They had a garden in their name, a crop on the trees, and a claim they could defend in front of any arbiter in the district. The Book does not dispute any of that; it disputes the inference the brothers drew from it. Ownership of a yield is granted with terms, and the terms arrive with the harvest.

The Book's treatment of property throughout its revelation holds the two claims together: what a person holds is his in law, and what he holds is a gift in fact. Where the two are separated, the result is a man who thinks nothing can reach his garden, and the verses following this one exist to demonstrate what such a man will find at the gate.

**Rank as a Test of What Was Given**

An earlier sūrah states the principle the brothers were violating, and it states it in terms of rank: *“He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you. Surely your Lord is swift in punishment, but He is certainly AllForgiving, Most Merciful.”* (6:165). Superiority in the Book is never a title to be enjoyed; it is material for an examination, and the examination consists of what the better-placed person does with the difference.

The brothers held land, water rights, and the labour force that made a large garden produce. Their neighbours included people whose only claim on the crop was the custom of sharing it. The verse quoted above does not ask the landowner to regard his position as an accident to be grateful for privately; it asks him to treat it as a test whose result is being recorded. Everything the two verses after this one report — the whispering, the early start, the closed gate — is that examination being taken and failed.

The final clause of the verse pairs two attributes and holds them together for a reason. A reader told about a swift punishment needs to know that forgiveness remains available; a reader presuming on forgiveness needs to hear that the reckoning arrives quickly. Both attributes belong to the owner of the garden in this story, and the passage displays both.

**The Shape of the Story's Irony**

A harvest that was meant to be first became a harvest that never happened. The brothers hurried so that no one would take a share; what they found was that everything had been taken. The Book does not explain the irony; it arranges the verses so that the reader assembles it and then moves on to the morning's confusion at the garden gate.

The word the sūrah uses for this garden is the same word it uses for Paradise in the verse that follows the story's close: *“Indeed, the righteous will have the Gardens of Bliss with their Lord.”* (68:34). The brothers were guarding one *jannah* against the poor, and the Book then names the *jannah* that is given to those who give. Read in sequence, the passage does not merely say that hoarding failed; it says that the thing being hoarded was a borrowed copy of a gift that generosity would have preserved. That is why the story can end without a moral attached: the arrangement of the verses is the moral.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:23

> So they went off, whispering to one another,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Walking and Whispering at the Same Time**

The brothers set out for the garden and talked on the road, and the sūrah reports the fact before it reports the content. A group that is confident in its plan does not lower its voice; the whispering tells the reader that these men had already understood what the town would think of them. The next verse supplies the single instruction they exchanged, and this verse is the picture that surrounds it: a decision being carried out by people who wanted it kept quiet, spoken between men who were walking briskly toward their own ruin.

**The Book's Word for a Private Conversation**

The verb used here belongs to the family of *najwā*, the Arabic term for a private exchange between two or more people, and the Qur'an has a good deal to say about the practice. One verse removes any possibility of privacy from the minds of those who depend on it: *“Do you not see that Allah knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth? If three converse privately, He is their fourth. If five, He is their sixth. Whether fewer or more, He is with them wherever they may be. Then, on the Day of Judgment, He will inform them of what they have done. Surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.”* (58:7). The audience of this sūrah has already been told, a few verses earlier, that a record is being written; here a family proceeds as though the record were not being kept.

**Secret Talks That Are Heard Before They Are Spoken**

Other passages make the same point in different forms. The Book reports that it knows what the opponents said when they thought no one was listening: *“We know best how they listen to your recitation and what they say privately—when the wrongdoers say, “You would only be following a bewitched man.””* (17:47). Another verse ends the discussion with a question that has no counter-argument: *“Or do they think that We do not hear their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks? Yes ˹We do˺! And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording ˹it all˺.”* (43:80).

Secret deliberation is a recurring feature of the Book's narratives. The magicians of Egypt, facing Moses, conferred out of public hearing (20:62), and the notables of Makkah plotted against the Prophet ﷺ in the same manner. The whispering in this verse belongs to a category the audience could already recognise, and the company it keeps is not flattering.

**The Standard the Book Sets for Private Counsel**

The Qur'an does not forbid private conversation as such; it attaches a condition to the subject matter. *“There is no good in most of their secret talks—except those encouraging charity, kindness, or reconciliation between people. And whoever does this seeking Allah's pleasure, We will grant them a great reward.”* (4:114). The exceptions are precise: a whispered conversation is good when it arranges help. The brothers' whisper is arranging the withdrawal of help, and the verse's permitted list — charity, kindness, reconciliation — reads like a description of the three things they agreed to prevent.

Discretion has an honoured place in the Book, but it protects the dignity of the poor and the sincerity of the giver. A donor who hides his gift is not concealing a wrong; he is refusing a credit. The two kinds of silence belong to different moral worlds, and the sūrah's first audience would have felt the difference without being told about it.

**Who Was Being Kept Outside**

The people excluded by this plan were not strangers from another district. The Book describes the poor of the community in terms that fit them closely: *“˹Charity is˺ for the needy who are too engaged in the cause of Allah to move about in the land ˹for work˺. Those unfamiliar with their situation will think they are not in need ˹of charity˺ because they do not beg. You can recognize them by their appearance. They do not beg people persistently.”* (2:273). A group that arrives at a garden's edge on harvest day and waits without shouting is exactly the population described in that verse, and the brothers' plan rested on the assumption that no one with authority would ever speak for them.

**The Kind of Family That Could Decide This**

Everything in the passage suggests a household that had inherited both a property and a policy. The garden came from a father who had set aside a share for the poor, and the brothers inherited the trees, the water, and the obligation together. What they did on the road was revise the terms of an estate they had received, and they did it as a group rather than as individuals — which is how most such decisions are made and why they are so difficult to reverse. Each man could tell himself that the plan was the family's business and that his own share of the responsibility was small.

The Book turns that reasoning inside out a few verses later when the same men begin blaming one another, and the sequence is worth noticing: a decision that was collective in the planning stage becomes individual in the accounting. Nothing in the sūrah suggests that any one of them had proposed the exclusion; the entire group is treated as its author.

Economic pressure may well have contributed. A garden shared among brothers supports more households than it once did, and each household has its own children. The step from a reduced share to no share is easy to describe in a family meeting and difficult to defend in public, which is exactly why the conversation happened on the road with the voices lowered.

**What Secrecy Says About a Plan**

A decision that must be hidden from the people it affects has already been judged by those making it. The sūrah does not need to add a rule about whispering in gardens; it shows the brothers hurrying to work with their voices lowered, and the lowered voices are the testimony. The rest of the passage will supply the sentence they were protecting and the correction they had not expected, and the reader is left holding one observation: the plan was never strong enough to be said aloud.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:24

> “Do not let any poor person enter your garden today.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Instruction in Plain Words**

The whisper is finally reported, and it turns out to be a single sentence about who may pass through the gate. There is no argument in it, no reference to need or merit, and no mention of God. The men are speaking among themselves about their own property in the tone of a household giving orders, and the sūrah preserves the wording so that the reader can see how ordinary the plan sounded to those who made it.

The sentence does not shut a poor person out forever; it shuts him out for one day, and the day is the harvest. That qualification is where the wrongness sits. A garden closed to visitors on an ordinary afternoon harms nobody; a garden closed on the morning the crop is taken is closed against the one moment in the year when a claim could be presented. The brothers had not abolished a custom; they had made it unreachable, and the word meaning *today* is the instrument.

**A Rule the First Audience Already Knew**

The obligation they were evading is stated in a verse that ties the payment to the moment of gathering: *“Eat from the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful.”* (6:141). A landowner who pays at the moment of harvest has done what was asked; one who arranges the harvest around the avoidance of that moment has made the schedule itself the offence.

**A Ban on People, Not on Produce**

The sentence forbids entry to persons. It does not say the fruit may not be taken; it says the poor may not come in, and the difference matters. A wealthy man who keeps everything can still be argued with; a wealthy man who will not let the hungry stand on his ground is guarding something other than a crop. The Book's attention to this distinction is characteristic of it, since its judgments fall not only on forbidden acts but on the small architecture of arrangements built to keep a claim out of reach.

The population at a garden gate on harvest morning would have included the people named in another passage, where the acts of the righteous are listed and food is given in a time of famine to an orphaned relative or a poor person in distress (90:14–16). The sūrah of the Frown, a few pages away from this one, describes the man who repulses the orphan and does not encourage the feeding of the poor (107:1–3). Those portraits are not decoration around the story of the garden; they describe the world in which the whisper makes sense, and the sentence on the road is the same refusal spoken at a lower volume.

**The Selfishness the Book Names**

The Book has a word for the condition the brothers were in, and it treats the condition as the real opponent of generosity: *“So be mindful of Allah to the best of your ability, hear and obey, and spend in charity—that will be best for you. And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful.”* (64:16). The success in that verse is attached not to the amount given but to the escape from an inner pressure that urges a person to hold everything.

What awaits the person who does not escape it is described without softening: *“And do not let those who ˹greedily˺ withhold Allah's bounties think it is good for them—in fact, it is bad for them! They will be leashed ˹by their necks˺ on the Day of Judgment with whatever ˹wealth˺ they used to withhold.”* (3:180). The image converts the garden into a rope. What the brothers meant to keep for themselves is exactly what will be fastened to them, and the closed gate they planned is the shape their own condition will take.

**The Reward the Book Attaches to Giving**

The Book promises a return on the produce that is released at harvest, and the image it chooses is agricultural: *“The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills. For Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.”* (2:261). The same Book sets the measure of generosity as high as the thing a person values most: *“You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish.”* (3:92). The brothers had a garden of grapevines and palm trees, and they were asked for a fraction of its yield; the arithmetic being offered to them was seven hundred grains for every one.

**The Right in Wealth and the Gift From It**

The Book treats the poor as holders of a claim rather than as recipients of whatever a landowner decides to release. One passage lists the marks of the faithful and includes among them a standing obligation in their property: *“and who give the rightful share of their wealth to the beggar and the poor;”* (70:24–25). Another verse from the same period makes the same point in the language of shares: *“And in their wealth there was a rightful share ˹fulfilled˺ for the beggar and the poor.”* (51:19). The claim exists before any request is made and does not depend on the owner's mood on the day.

Set the brothers' instruction beside those verses and the offence takes its full shape. They were not refusing a favour; they were closing the door on a right. The Book's warning about how such giving may be spoiled runs in the opposite direction: charity followed by reminders of generosity or by injury cancels itself, like a bare rock with a film of soil washed off by rain (2:264). A man who gives and then humiliates the recipient loses the reward; a man who refuses him entry and keeps everything has done neither part of the transaction.

**How the Verse Sounds to a Later Reader**

Farms in an industrial age are seldom harvested with a crowd at the fence, but the sentence has not lost its application. Produce is still gathered at a moment when claims can be honoured or bypassed, and the poor are still the ones who notice which of the two is happening. The Book's decision to quote the whisper is an act of care for its readers: the exact words are preserved so that an identical plan can be recognised while it is still only a plan.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:25

> And they proceeded early, totally fixated on their purpose.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Steps of a Confident Plan**

The journey ends where it was meant to end. The brothers walked the road at first light with their decision already made and their voices already lowered, and the verse describes the state of mind in which they arrived rather than the distance they covered. What the reader is shown is not indecision or guilt but plenty of purpose: men who had settled an argument among themselves and were now doing the work. Everything in the passage until this point has been preparation, and this verse is the moment at which the preparation is finally in position to be executed. The sūrah gives the walk one line and moves on, because the ruin has already been described and the reader knows what the men will find.

**What the Arabic Phrase Carries**

The phrase rendered *totally fixated on their purpose* has been understood by the commentators in two directions, and both are alive in the language. One reading describes men who were determined and confident, carrying out a plan with full commitment. The other describes men who had overestimated their own ability, believing that they could decide who would receive the produce of their garden and who would not. Together the two readings give the verse its direction. This is a portrait of human power at the exact moment when it is being exercised over something it does not control, and the second reading names the mistake that the whole passage has been building toward.

**Power Treated as a Possession**

The Book consistently refuses to let wealth or strength be described as a personal achievement. *“Whatever blessings you have are from Allah. Then whenever hardship touches you, to Him ˹alone˺ you cry ˹for help˺.”* (16:53). The same Book reports the words of a man who attributes his good fortune to himself: *“When one is touched with hardship, they cry out to Us ˹alone˺. Then when We shower Our blessings upon them, they say, “I have been granted all this only because of ˹my˺ knowledge.” Not at all! It is ˹no more than˺ a test. But most of them do not know.”* (39:49). The brothers had not said that sentence out loud. The sūrah leaves the reader to notice how closely their behaviour matches a man who had, and how ordinary the mistake looks when it is worn as competence rather than spoken as a boast.

**The Two Gardens and the Words They Missed**

The Book's other garden story supplies the sentence these men were missing. A wealthy man walked into his vineyard and declared that he did not think it would ever perish; his companion answered with a rebuke that culminates in a single line: *“If only you had said, upon entering your property, ‘This is what Allah has willed! There is no power except with Allah!’ Even though you see me inferior to you in wealth and offspring”* (18:39). The ruined owner's reaction is then recorded in detail: *“And so all his produce was ˹totally˺ ruined, so he started to wring his hands for all he had spent on it, while it had collapsed on its trellises. He cried, “Alas! I wish I had never associated anyone with my Lord ˹in worship˺!””* (18:42).

The two stories teach the same lesson from different angles. One man was warned while there was still time and refused the correction. The other group arrives at its garden with the warning already behind it, and the words that would have been said on entering the property are nowhere in the passage.

**Determination Is Not the Problem**

Nothing in this verse criticises resolve. The Book asks for firmness in more places than can be counted, and it attaches trust to decision rather than separating the two: *“Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah. Surely Allah loves those who trust in Him.”* (3:159). A determined man who has handed the outcome to God is being encouraged in this verse's terms. The complaint is about a determination that has excluded that handing over, which is precisely what the oath in verse seventeen lacked. The brothers were not too committed; they were committed to an outcome they had no authority over.

**The List of Things They Meant to Control**

The brothers believed they could govern the hour of the harvest, the identity of the people present, the speed of the work, and the distribution of the result. Their confidence about the last item was total. What they left out of the list was the will of the One who had provided the rain, the soil, and the trees, and the story was arranged so that the omission would be answered overnight. The verse lets them walk toward the garden carrying a purpose that has already been absorbed into a larger one, and it does not pause to explain the irony.

Their mistake was also visible without any revelation. Rain, blight, a late frost, a fire, or a night of strong wind could have taken that crop at any point in the year, and the men who farmed for a living knew the calendar of risks better than anyone. What the passage exposes is not a gap in practical knowledge but a habit of the heart: the risks were counted for the season and then set aside on the morning that mattered, and the arrangements they had made were treated as though they had removed the last of them.

**Why the Punishment Is Not Named**

No verse in this passage identifies the agent of the destruction beyond the words *from your Lord*, and none of the brothers is told why it happened while he stands in the ruin. The Book's method in such narratives is to describe the blow and leave the explanation to the reader, who has already been given the plan and the whisper. This verse is the last moment of confident movement in the story. The sūrah lets it pass in a single line so that the contrast with the next scene is as sharp as the language allows.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:26

> But when they saw it ˹devastated˺, they cried, “We must have lost ˹our˺ way!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Sentence at the Gate**

The brothers reach the garden and speak before they understand. What they say first is that they must have taken the wrong road, which is a confession of disorientation rather than of sin. The men who had planned the morning in such detail could not identify their own property, and the sūrah preserves the reaction because it is the most accurate description of their situation that anyone will utter in the passage. They were lost, though not in the way they meant.

**Two Readings of the Confession**

The commentators offered two explanations of the line, and both fit the words. On one reading the men genuinely suspected the route, convinced for a moment that they had come to a neighbour's plot or a stranger's field. On the other, the sentence is the first movement of conscience: they had gone astray months earlier, when they agreed to keep the harvest, and the words at the gate were truer than they knew. The sūrah allows both readings to stand, and the verses after this one supply the correction that makes the second necessary. A sentence spoken in confusion becomes, a few lines later, an admission.

**A Companion Parable: the Two Gardens**

The Book tells another story about a garden and its owner, and the parallels are close enough to be deliberate. A man owned two vineyards, planted with grapevines and surrounded by palms, with a river running between them; he boasted to a poorer companion about his wealth and his manpower, then entered his property and said that he did not think it would ever perish (18:32–35). His companion's reply is a rebuke built on the same foundation as this sūrah: *“If only you had said, upon entering your property, ‘This is what Allah has willed! There is no power except with Allah!’”* (18:39). The owner's end is then reported without comfort: *“And so all his produce was ˹totally˺ ruined, so he started to wring his hands for all he had spent on it, while it had collapsed on its trellises.”* (18:42).

The companion then names the two forms the ruin could take, and both are ordinary events in that landscape: *“perhaps my Lord will grant me ˹something˺ better than your garden, and send down upon your garden a thunderbolt from the sky, turning it into a barren waste. Or its water may sink ˹into the earth˺, and then you will never be able to seek it out.”* (18:40–41). Fire from above or water lost below; a vineyard is vulnerable from both directions, and the man warning his neighbour says so in plain agricultural language.

The same lesson is taught twice in one Book, once as an argument between two men and once as a family's morning at a gate. What is missing from each is the acknowledgement repeated in the other. The phrase *what Allah has willed* appears in the rebuke of the first story and is the exact omission that the sūrah of the pen reports in its own.

The story of the two gardens also ends with a man speaking at the point of loss, and his words resemble the brothers' first sentence in their bewilderment. He wrings his hands over what he had spent, watches the trellises collapse, and wishes he had never associated anyone with his Lord. The Book records that regret without softening it, and it supplies no sentence in which the ruined owner blames his companion's warning.

**Loss as a Word With Two Meanings**

The Arabic term rendered *lost our way* also carries the sense of straying from a path, and the Book uses it throughout for the condition of people who have missed the road God has marked. The brothers use the word about a track through farmland; the Book uses it about lives. The overlap is the story's method of moving from the visible fact to the invisible one, and the following verse completes the movement by naming what had actually been lost.

Two different kinds of mistake sit inside the brothers' sentence. The first is about geography: they thought a wrong road could explain the wreckage. The second is about ownership: they had spent the previous day arranging a garden as though the plan were the same thing as the fact. The word the Book puts in their mouths resolves the first and leaves the second intact, which is why the correction has to follow so quickly.

The confusion itself is instructive. A person who has organised his life around a possession finds it difficult to recognise that possession when it has been stripped, because the object in front of him no longer answers to the description under which he was holding it. The garden was still the garden; the fruit was gone. What the men could not process was a familiar place that had been emptied of the only feature they could see in it.

**What They Had Not Yet Said**

Nothing in the sentence assigns the disaster to anyone, and nothing in it mentions God. The first reaction of men who had decided that the harvest belonged entirely to them is to treat the ruin as an accident of navigation, and the sūrah gives them the space to say it before they are corrected. That sequence is a small portrait of how human beings process a loss. The instinct at the moment of discovery is to look for a mistake in the route, because a wrong road is a problem that can be fixed.

A narrative that silenced the brothers instantly would have made a statement about severity and left the audience with nothing to imitate. The Book lets them talk, argue, interpret, and hope, and the ruined garden stays exactly as it is while the conversation continues. The lesson is not that words change the outcome. It is that the words available to a person after a disaster reveal what he had been excluding before it, and the three verses that follow show a family working through the sentences it should have said the week before.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:27

> In fact, we have been deprived ˹of our livelihood˺.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Correction at the Start of the Sentence**

The brothers' confusion is answered by their own words. The first attempt blamed the road; this one begins with a marker of correction and then names the actual situation: they have been deprived. Nobody in the group says who did the depriving, and the sentence does not yet concede that anyone had the right to. What has changed is the category. A wrong turn is a mistake; deprivation is a condition, and the second word puts the men in the position of people dealing with an act rather than an accident.

**The Same Sentence in Another Sūrah**

The Book uses these words almost exactly elsewhere, in a passage about the harvest that every farming audience knows: *“Have you considered what you sow? Is it you who cause it to grow, or is it We Who do so? If We willed, We could simply reduce this ˹harvest˺ to chaff, leaving you to lament, “We have truly suffered a ˹great˺ loss. In fact, we have been deprived ˹of our livelihood˺.””* (56:63–67). The final line of that passage is the sentence in this verse, placed in the mouths of farmers whose crop has been turned to chaff.

Revelation gives the same complaint twice because the event it describes is not unusual. Everything that grows can be reduced overnight by the one who makes it grow, and the vocabulary of the ruined garden in this sūrah is the vocabulary prepared for anyone who has watched a harvest fail.

**What the Repetition Teaches**

The overlap carries a further lesson. If the ending of the brothers' story is also the ending written for every harvest, then the story of the garden is not a curiosity about four men and a walled property. It is the account of a relationship that all wealth-holders are in, localised into one morning so that the pattern can be seen.

The Book frequently repeats its sentences across sūrahs for exactly this purpose. A phrase that appears twice in the revelation stops being a detail of one narrative and becomes a description of the world.

The sūrah's own frame is the nearest example. It opens by denying a charge — *“By the grace of your Lord, you ˹O Prophet˺ are not insane.”* (68:2) — and it closes by putting the same word in the mouths of the mockers (68:51). Between those two statements the accusation has travelled from a claim the revelation rejects to a symptom of the people who make it, and the repetition is what makes the journey visible. The same principle governs the brothers' sentence: spoken once, it is a complaint about a bad morning; repeated where planting and harvest are described, it becomes a law of possession.

**The Width of the Word "Livelihood"**

The term rendered *livelihood* is wider than a crop. It covers everything by which a creature is sustained: rain, food, offspring, health, and the means by which a person earns. The brothers' loss at the garden included much of that list. Their estate was gone, the harvest they had counted on to feed their households was gone with it, and the ground they had inherited no longer produced anything they could rely on. What they named at the gate was not the destruction of one year's fruit; it was the removal of the arrangement that had kept their families.

The Book elsewhere treats provision as a measured quantity rather than an open tap: *“We send down rain from the sky in perfect measure, causing it to soak into the earth. And We are surely able to take it away.”* (23:18). A household that has lived through a run of good seasons begins to treat the flow as a property of the world. The word chosen at the gate puts the flow back where the Book locates it, in a measure that someone keeps.

**Deprivation and Design**

The word the brothers chose concedes more than they intended. To be deprived is to have something withheld, and a thing is withheld by someone. The passage has already told the reader who that someone is: a visitation came from *your Lord* while they slept. The men at the gate had not yet arrived at that thought, and the sūrah allows them to speak two sentences in a row without mentioning God. The third of the group will supply the missing name in the next verse.

**The Lament the Book Prescribes Instead**

The Book's instructions for dealing with loss take a different form from the brothers' complaint. Believers are told to expect the very things these men had lost: *“We will certainly test you with a touch of fear and famine and loss of property, life, and crops. Give good news to those who patiently endure— who, when faced with a disaster, say, “Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return.””* (2:155–156). The difference between the two reactions is not the amount of grief. It is where the words are directed. The sentence in this verse ends with the men and their property; the sentence the Book teaches ends with the One who owns both.

A reader who has followed the story from the oath on the road to this gate has been shown a complete descent:

A reader who has followed the story from the oath on the road to this gate has been shown a complete descent: from confidence to schedule, from schedule to whisper, from whisper to a garden whose crop was already gone, and now to the plain statement of deprivation. What follows belongs to a different register. The next verse contains a question rather than a complaint, and the question comes from the one man in the group who had tried to attach the exception to the oath. The passage is not finished with the brothers yet.

The Book could have described the destruction of the garden and moved on. Instead it records, over four verses, what the owners said when they arrived, and the record is not flattering to them. That choice is instruction. A community reading these lines learns that a person's own words after a loss are part of the account being kept, and that the sentences spoken at the gate become the material by which the following verses can answer them.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:28

> The most sensible of them said, “Did I not urge you to say, ‘Allah willing.’?”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The One Who Had Already Said Something**

The silence at the gate is broken by the one member of the family who had spoken up earlier, and his first words are a reminder rather than a reproach about the garden. He had asked his brothers to attach the exception when they swore their oath, and the question now is whether he had been answered. The sūrah places his sentence at the point where blame begins among the group, which tells the reader that the family's internal argument started long before the road.

**What "Most Sensible" Means Here**

The Arabic term rendered *most sensible* can also be read as the most balanced, the most equitable, or the most just among them, and the commentators leaned on both senses. The word describes a person who occupies the middle of a matter instead of an extreme, and the sūrah applies it to the man whose counsel earlier would have prevented the disaster. A group in crisis contains people of different quality, and the Book is precise about which kind speaks. What he says is not an accusation about the harvest; it is an appeal to something he had offered before there was any reason to remember it.

The quality the verse praises has a public use in the Book. The community is told that it has been made an upright one so that it may stand as a witness over humanity: *“And so We have made you ˹believers˺ an upright community so that you may be witnesses over humanity…”* (2:143). The word behind *upright* carries the same sense of the middle that the verse gives to *most sensible*. Balance in this vocabulary is not the avoidance of a position; it is the footing a person needs in order to say the true thing while the people around him are stumbling.

**The Advice That Had Been Dropped**

His question refers to the rule the Book lays down about statements concerning the future: *“And never say of anything, “I will definitely do this tomorrow,” without adding, “if Allah so wills!” But if you forget, then remember your Lord, and say, “I trust my Lord will guide me to what is more right than this.””* (18:23–24). The practice is not a formula for softening a promise. It is the acknowledgement that the person speaking is not the one who decides which morning arrives, and it belongs most naturally to a man making plans about a harvest.

His brothers had sworn the oath without it. The objection he raised at the time may have sounded to them like a scruple about wording; the morning at the garden turned it into the exact point on which their confidence had been built.

**Why He Asks a Question Instead of Preaching**

He does not begin with the doctrine and he does not say what he believes about God's decision. He refers to a prior conversation and asks them to remember it, which places the responsibility where the passage has already located it. The Book's reminders often take this form. A servant of God who has been asked to counsel someone does not always need to explain the principle again; drawing attention to advice that was already given is frequently the more penetrating course, because it lets the hearer supply the argument himself.

**"Why Do You Not Glorify?"**

In the fuller wording of the tradition the question is asked with the verb for glorification, and the commentators understood it as an appeal to give thanks and to attribute the blessing to God rather than to the family's own effort. The Book's diagnosis of the opposite habit is stated in a passage about prosperity and scarcity: *“Now, whenever a human being is tested by their Lord through ˹His˺ generosity and blessings, they boast, “My Lord has ˹deservedly˺ honoured me!” But when He tests them by limiting their provision, they protest, “My Lord has ˹undeservedly˺ humiliated me!””* (89:15–16). Both sentences in that passage are readouts of a single mistake: the assumption that the size of a person's provision is a verdict on the size of his worth.

Glorification is the practice that removes the assumption. To say God is free of any deficiency is also to say that the servant's circumstances are not his own creation, and the brothers had finally reached a situation in which that admission cost nothing to make — which is why the Book has them make it here rather than a week earlier.

**Reminders Remembered at the Gate**

The Book reports that human beings call on God when hardship arrives and forget the appeal once it is lifted: *“Whenever someone is touched by hardship, they cry out to Us, whether lying on their side, sitting, or standing. But when We relieve their hardship, they return to their old ways as if they had never cried to Us to remove any hardship!”* (10:12). The pattern in the garden runs in the opposite direction and is equally familiar. Advice heard while life is comfortable is filed away as a technicality and recalled at the moment its meaning becomes unmistakable.

The man at the gate is not congratulating himself on being right. The verses that follow show the group turning on one another and then turning, collectively, toward their Lord, and the question of this verse is the hinge that makes the second turn possible.

**A Practice That Entered Muslim Life**

The doctrine this verse turns on has shaped the way Muslims speak about the future in every generation since. A plan is stated with the exception attached, a promise is made with the acknowledgement that the speaker is not the guarantor of his own tomorrow, and a man who forgets to add the phrase is instructed by the Book to remember his Lord and restate his intention. What the brothers' story adds to the rule is a demonstration. The exception is not a mannerism of speech; it is a claim about the world, and the world on this occasion was arranged so that the claim could be seen to be true.

**Why the Story Gives the Rebuke to a Brother**

The lesson of the passage is delivered inside the family and by one of its members, not by a messenger or a judge. That choice keeps the story's accusation close to home for every reader who holds something valuable and a group of relatives who have a view about it. The man who speaks here had lived with the same inheritance, the same pressures, and the same conversation on the road, and the sūrah identifies him by one quality: he had remembered God when the plan was being made.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:29

> They replied, “Glory be to our Lord! We have truly been wrongdoers.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Right Sentence in the Story**

The family that whispered on the road and argued at the gate finally says something that is entirely true. The words pair two movements. The first is the glorification of God, which declares Him free of any fault in what happened. The second is an admission about themselves: they were wrongdoers, and the sentence does not qualify the claim with excuses. After three verses of confusion, argument, and mutual blame, the group arrives at the vocabulary the Book has been preparing them for. The sūrah reports the sentence in the plural, so the confession is made by the whole family rather than by one of its members.

**Glorification and Confession Joined Together**

The two halves of the sentence belong to each other. Praise directed at God removes the possibility that the disaster was unjust, and the confession directed at themselves gives up the possibility that they were merely unlucky. The Book uses the combination in the mouths of nations that had already been destroyed: *“Their only cry—when overwhelmed by Our torment—was, “We have indeed been wrongdoers.””* (7:5). A similar scene appears in the sūrah of the Prophets: *“They cried, “Woe to us! We have surely been wrongdoers.” They kept repeating their cry until We mowed them down, ˹leaving them˺ lifeless.”* (21:14–15).

One further verse makes the point with an image that could have been written about the brothers: *“If they were touched by even a breath of your Lord's torment, they would certainly cry, “Woe to us! We have really been wrongdoers.””* (21:46). The confession is the human being's most natural response to being caught, and the Book records it in the same words in every century.

**What the Word "Wrongdoers" Claims**

The term is an ethical judgment rather than a description of misfortune. The commentators explain the brothers' guilt in concrete terms: they prevented the poor from receiving what was due to them. Nothing else in the story is named as the offence — not the size of the estate, not the wealth of the family, not the oath by itself. The failure was a refusal of a claim, and the confession at the gate is accurate because it puts the wrong where the earlier verses had already placed it.

The Book's use of the same word for the person who wrongs himself adds another layer. A wrongdoer of this kind damages his own case, and the brothers' loss of a garden is treated in the passage as the visible portion of a much larger loss that they have just begun to measure.

The term also settles a question the family had been unable to settle on the road. If the withholding of a share was merely a business decision, the men were entitled to make it and the loss was an accident. Calling themselves wrongdoers removes that reading and leaves them with the version in which they were the authors of their own condition. Anyone reading the passage in later centuries can test his own disputes against the same standard: a person who can name the point at which he wronged someone has found the place where his account begins.

**Praise That Clears God and Blame That Stays Home**

The Book's descriptions of people under judgement include a great deal of blame-shifting, and the passages are collected in this commentary's next section. What the flood and the destroyed nations did was to deflect responsibility onto patrons, ancestors, and leaders. The sentence in this verse takes the opposite route. It clears God of any charge and keeps the fault on the speakers, and that double movement is what distinguishes a confession from a complaint.

A complaint about a loss can be entirely honest and still leave the speaker where he was. The brothers' earlier sentences were honest in that way. The sentence in this verse changes their position because it accepts a verdict instead of questioning one.

**The Confession in This Life and the Confession in the Fire**

The Book gives the same words to people whose situation is already decided, and it is careful about the difference. Confession in this world is the door out of a wrong; the same words spoken in the next world are simply the record being agreed to. The sūrah's brothers have lost their garden but not their lives, and the passage that follows this one contains a hope that would have no place in a scene of final judgement: they trust that their Lord will give them something better than what they had (68:32).

The distinction explains why the Book keeps reporting confessions made too late. It is not mocking the ruined nations. It is showing the reader a sentence that is available now, while it still does work.

**The Arc of the Family's Speech**

Speech in this passage follows a curve. The brothers began by whispering on the road, then called to one another at daybreak, then argued about the wrong road and the ruined harvest, then turned on each other, and now confess together. Every stage of the story is marked by what the men said, and the movement from secrecy to blame to acknowledgment is the sūrah's way of showing what is happening inside them.

The Book treats speech as the visible index of a condition, and this sequence is one of its clearest illustrations. A family that had organised its life around a plan that could not be said aloud ends the morning saying the one thing that had been missing from the oath.

The word they now use in public is the same word they had been avoiding since the harvest was counted. On the road the point of the whisper was to keep the plan from being described accurately; at the gate the plan is described, and it is their conduct rather than their property that the description covers. The story's ending belongs to the family's speech, which is why the sūrah gives the confession a verse of its own and lets it stand without a reply.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:30

> Then they turned on each other, throwing blame.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument That Follows the Loss**

The confession of the previous verse does not arrive first; blame does. The brothers turn toward one another and begin assigning fault, and the sūrah reports the turn before it reports any words. The commentators explain the dispute in the same terms as the crime itself: the family had reproached one another for having failed to help the poor. It is a striking detail, because the argument is not about who lost the estate. The accusation being traded is about conduct, and the group that had decided together is now trying to distribute the responsibility it had shared.

**Blame as the Book's Signature of the Exposed**

The Qur'an repeatedly describes people under judgement as occupied with each other's faults. The followers in the Fire appeal to the leaders who had misled them: *“We were your ˹dedicated˺ followers, will you then shield us from a portion of the Fire?”*, and the answer they receive is a refusal: *“We are all in it! ˹For˺ Allah has already passed judgment over ˹His˺ servants.”* (40:47–48). In another passage the lowly and the arrogant are set against each other in the same way: the followers say that without their leaders they would have believed, and the leaders reply that they had not hindered anyone from guidance (34:31–32). The exchange continues until the followers accuse the leaders of plotting by day and night, and the verse ends by noting that all of them conceal their remorse when they see the punishment (34:33).

The Book records a version of the same scene between relatives: friends who were partners in wrongdoing become each other's accusers, and the exception is the righteous (43:67).

**Why Reproach Makes the Torment Worse**

The Book treats mutual blame as part of the punishment rather than as relief from it. Two verses make the point in the plainest terms: those who misled others will disown their followers when the torment appears and every bond between them will be cut (2:166), while the followers will wish for a second chance so that they could disown their leaders as they were disowned, and God will make them remorseful of their deeds without letting them leave (2:167). Nothing in the exchange changes the situation of either party, and nothing in it consoles anyone.

The reason the arguing adds to the punishment is the same in this world as in the next. A group that has lost something together can recover in two directions: it can accept the loss and work out what to do, or it can spend its remaining strength deciding who is to be resented. The brothers chose the second, and the sūrah reports it without comment.

There is also a social cost that the passage leaves for the reader to calculate. Every hour spent in reproach was an hour in which the household's actual situation went unaddressed: what remained of the estate, what the families would eat, whether any part of the ground could be replanted. The Book's descriptions of the Hereafter use the same material for a different reason, showing that blame in the presence of a settled verdict is not merely unhelpful but a continuation of the offence that produced the verdict.

**The Dispute That Has Already Been Answered**

Another passage shows what such arguments sound like when the record is produced: *“Allah will respond, “Do not dispute in My presence, since I had already given you a warning.””* (50:28). The subject of the argument is closed before anyone speaks, and the parties are left in the position of men continuing a case whose verdict they are holding in their hands.

The sūrah's own version of that closed case is the visitation that came while the brothers slept. Their dispute at the gate is therefore not really about the facts; it is about how much of the fault each man is willing to carry, and the Book lets that discussion run for exactly one verse before it collapses into the confession that follows.

**What Households Argue About After a Disaster**

There is a recognisable human anatomy to what the passage describes. A family facing a serious loss rarely argues about the original decision; it argues about who first suggested it, who failed to object, and who should have known better. The man who gave good advice (68:28) has already reminded the group of what he had said, and the argument that follows his reminder is an attempt to respond to that reminder without accepting it.

Practical questions often lie underneath the argument: what will each household live on now, how will the children be fed, who owes what to whom. The sūrah does not report those questions, but the sense of strain in the verse is consistent with them.

A reader who has heard the story from the beginning can see what the brothers cannot. The dispute about blame is being conducted on ground that was entirely their own creation, and the accusation they trade — that someone failed to help the poor — is the one criticism of which every member of the group is guilty. The argument therefore cannot be won by anyone in it, and the verse's brevity leaves no impression that the sūrah wants the reader to take a side.

**The Turn the Next Verse Makes**

The blame the brothers exchange does not last long. The following verse gives them one voice again — a lament that they were transgressors — and the argument disappears from the story. The structure of the passage moves from deflection to admission in a single step, and the result is that the last thing the ruined family does in the narrative is agree about themselves.

For readers of the sūrah, the sequence is the point. A group that can be brought to a shared confession has a future, whatever it has lost, and the same group's arguments are shown to be a stage rather than a destination. The Book's handling of the owners of the garden ends in that agreement, and the verses that follow shift their attention outward to a lesson for the Makkans hearing this passage recited in their streets.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:31

> They said, “Woe to us! We have certainly been transgressors.

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Woe to Us": the Cry of a Recognised Verdict**

The word rendered *woe* is the Book's own exclamation of regret, and it appears wherever people discover that a matter has been settled against them. When the record of deeds is laid open, the wicked cry out in the same terms and ask what kind of book leaves nothing out: *“They will cry, “Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?” They will find whatever they did present ˹before them˺. And your Lord will never wrong anyone.”* (18:49). At the resurrection the same cry goes up as people are raised from their resting places (36:52), and at the arrival of the Day of Judgement it is the first thing the disbelievers say (37:20).

The brothers use the word over a ruined garden rather than over a record of deeds, and the Book's choice of vocabulary links the small scene to the large one. The sentence being spoken at the gate is the sentence of people who have just recognised a verdict.

**The Word "Transgressors" and the Step Beyond Wrongdoing**

Their earlier confession called them wrongdoers; this one calls them transgressors, and the second term is heavier. Transgression in the Book means crossing a limit that has been set, and the sūrah's own account of the situation supports the description: the limit was the due attached to the harvest, and the brothers crossed it by scheduling their gathering around the moment it could be claimed. A wrong can be repaired by restitution; a limit that has been crossed describes a habit of movement, which is why the language deepens as the family comes to understand what it has been doing.

The Book applies the term to those who exceed what they were given to use, and it sets them in opposition to the thankful — the two categories between which human beings divide.

There is also an irony in the timing of the word. The men who called themselves transgressors at the gate were the same men who had described nothing in the previous week as a limit; a due attached to a harvest is only a boundary if a person acknowledges the one who set it. The confession is therefore not a new fact about the family. It is the first time they have named something they had been treating as an inconvenience.

**A Confession With No Request Attached**

Nothing follows the admission. There is no plea for the garden, no offer to compensate anyone, and no attempt to bargain over the outcome. The Book contains confessions of that other kind, including the one that is expressly excluded from acceptance: the repentance of a person who continues in his conduct until death arrives and then says that he repents (4:18). The brothers' sentence belongs to the same family of statements, with one important difference — the men are still alive, and the verses that follow show what the Book does with a confession made in time.

The narrative gives the family several attempts at understanding what happened: they thought they had taken the wrong road (v26), then named their deprivation (v27), then called themselves wrongdoers (v29), and now transgressors. Each sentence is a step deeper into the same event, and the sūrah reports all of them because a loss of this size is not processed in one movement. The Book's narratives often work this way, letting a scene be described from several angles until the reader has both the event and the reaction to it.

**Regret That Arrives in Time**

The Book distinguishes sharply between regret that can be acted on and regret that cannot. A verse states the door plainly: *“Whoever commits evil or wrongs themselves then seeks Allah's forgiveness will certainly find Allah All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”* (4:110). Another describes the repentance that is accepted as the repentance of those who err and then turn soon afterwards (4:17). The brothers at the gate have lost their harvest and have not lost their lives, and the sūrah places their cry on the right side of that line without making a speech about it.

Regret in the Book is therefore measured by what it produces rather than by how strongly it is felt. The cry of *woe* can belong to a person who never repents at all — it is the sound the wicked make when the record is opened — or it can be the first sentence of a life that changes direction. What decides the case is whether anything follows the sentence, and in this story something does.

**How the Confession Deepens the Family's Language**

Three words carry the story's inner movement: deprivation, wrongdoing, transgression. The first is a description of a condition, the second is an ethical judgment, and the third names a pattern of conduct that has been going on longer than the week of the harvest. By the end of the sequence the brothers are no longer talking about a garden at all. They are describing themselves, and the vocabulary they use is the vocabulary the Qur'an reserves for people who have crossed a boundary.

**What the Reader Is Meant to Do With the Cry**

A reader who has followed the passage from the oath to the gate has heard the family say everything that a person says when it is too late to change the outcome. The Book's purpose in reporting those sentences is not to make the reader feel the men's regret; it is to put the same words in front of him while he still has options. The revelation asks for the turn that the brothers have just made, and it asks for it earlier: *“O believers! Turn to Allah in sincere repentance, so your Lord may absolve you of your sins and admit you into Gardens, under which rivers flow.”* (66:8).

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:32

> We trust our Lord will give us a better garden than this, ˹for˺ we are indeed turning to our Lord with hope.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hope Arrives With the Confession**

The family's turn produces a sentence about the future. Having admitted what they were, they state their expectation of their Lord, and the verse puts the two movements in a single line so that neither stands alone. The Book consistently joins the confession of a wrong with an opening rather than a verdict, even where the consequences of the wrong remain in place. A ruined garden is not undone by repentance; a relationship is restored by it, and the verse treats the second restoration as the one that matters at the gate.

The order of the two halves is worth keeping in view. The brothers' expectation of something better comes after their admission and not before it, so the hope in the verse is not a way of avoiding the subject of what they did. Had they spoken about a replacement garden on the road, the sentence would have been a plan rather than a trust. Spoken at the gate, with the wreckage in front of them and their own words of confession behind them, it becomes the first statement of the passage in which God is the subject of an action rather than an omission.

**"Better Than This" and the Book's Arithmetic of Loss**

The claim is bold in its setting. What the brothers lost was a productive estate, and they say in the middle of the wreckage that they expect their Lord to give them something better. The Book makes the same offer repeatedly, and it does so with the contrast between what passes and what remains: *“Whatever you have will end, but whatever Allah has is everlasting. And We will certainly reward the steadfast according to the best of their deeds.”* (16:96). The next verse of that sūrah widens the promise to cover the conditions of living: *“Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, We will surely bless them with a good life, and We will certainly reward them according to the best of their deeds.”* (16:97).

Other passages state the same principle for people recovering from serious failure: those who believe and do good will have their misdeeds replaced by something better (29:7), and forgiveness is followed by a reward measured by the best of what a person has done (39:35). The brothers' hope is not optimism about weather or land; it is confidence in the character of the one they are addressing.

**What the Reports Say About the Outcome**

The exegetical tradition adds a detail that the sūrah's own words do not require. It is reported that the family was in fact given a better garden in place of the one they had lost, an outcome that some commentators connected with a garden in this world and others with the Garden of the Hereafter. Both readings preserve the verse's own sense, which is that the petition of a repentant household is directed at a provider who is not constrained by what has already been ruined. The story does not need the report to make its point, but the report shows how the first generations read the verse.

**"Turning to Our Lord" and the Practice Behind the Word**

The Arabic term rendered *turning* describes a movement of return that is expected to repeat rather than to occur once. The same description is given to Abraham in the Qur'an, who is called ever turning to his Lord (11:75), and it recurs in the Book as a title for people whose direction of travel has been corrected. The brothers describe themselves with that word rather than with the vocabulary of a completed transaction. What they are announcing at the gate is a change of orientation — away from the estate and the plan and toward the one who owns both.

The word also explains why the sentence can be spoken by men who have just lost everything. A person who has turned has somewhere to go, and the direction he is facing is no longer the ruin behind him.

**A Family Turning Together**

Every stage of this story has been collective: the oath was sworn by the group, the whisper was exchanged on the road, the exclusion was agreed among brothers, and the confession is spoken in the plural. The final verse of the sequence keeps the same shape. The household that had organised itself around a private plan now organises itself around a shared return, and the sūrah's choice of the first person plural is exact. There is no individual hero in the passage, and no individual culprit; the family is treated throughout as one body whose members were responsible for each other.

A reader who has lost an opportunity through his own conduct finds in these verses the sequence the Book recommends. The loss is acknowledged rather than explained away, the wrong is named in the language of transgression, and the response is an expectation directed at God, spoken in the middle of the ruin and not after its repair. Nothing in the passage suggests that the brothers' repentance restored the garden or returned the year. What it restores is their standing with the one whose decision the harvest had been, and the Book presents that as the more substantial of the two possessions.

**The Story That Began With a Garden Ends With a Lord**

The parable opened with an oath about fruit and closes with a sentence about trust, and the change of subject is the story's real conclusion. A household that had measured its security in trees, water, and a walled property ends its appearance in the sūrah with a statement about the disposition of its Lord toward those who turn to Him. Readers who have followed the passage from beginning to end are meant to notice how little of the final scene is about a garden, and how much of it is about the relationship that the garden's owners had forgotten was the source of everything they once held.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:33

> That is the ˹way of Our˺ punishment ˹in this world˺. But the punishment of the Hereafter is certainly far worse, if only they knew.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Story Steps Out of the Garden**

The narrative pauses and speaks to the reader directly. What happened to the owners of the garden is summarised in three words — such is the punishment — and then set beside a larger event that the sūrah describes as far more serious. The parable is over, and its lesson is being generalised. Everything the passage described in agricultural terms now becomes a statement about how God deals with people who withhold what they owe, and the audience in Makkah is given the second half of the comparison before the chapter moves to its closing arguments.

**Punishment in This World as a Warning**

The Book treats loss and hardship in this life as instruments that are meant to bring people back rather than to finish them. One verse states the intention explicitly: *“We will certainly make them taste some of the minor torment ˹in this life˺ before the major torment ˹of the Hereafter˺, so perhaps they will return ˹to the Right Path˺.”* (32:21). Another verse assigns the cause of affliction to human conduct while noting how much is forgiven: *“Whatever affliction befalls you is because of what your own hands have committed. And He pardons much.”* (42:30).

A ruined garden is therefore not the end of a story but one of its turns, and the brothers' response in the preceding verses shows what the turn is for. They were given a sentence that they could read.

**Miserliness as the Charge Against the Deniers**

The commentators connect the parable's crime to the fate described in this verse and explain that the disbelievers are being warned about their own hoarding. The Book's portrait of the miser is one of its sharpest: *“Woe to every backbiter, slanderer, who amasses wealth ˹greedily˺ and counts it ˹repeatedly˺, thinking that their wealth will make them immortal!”* (104:1–3). The final clause identifies the underlying belief. A person who hoards is not simply failing to share; he is treating his store as a guarantee against the mortality he cannot escape.

The warning to the same behaviour appears in the sūrah of Repentance with an image that matches the brothers' situation: those who hoard gold and silver without spending it in God's cause are told that their treasure will be heated in the fire and used to brand their foreheads, sides, and backs, with the words *“This is the treasure you hoarded for yourselves. Now taste what you hoarded!”* (9:34–35). What a person refuses to release is returned to him as an instrument of his own punishment.

**Why the Hereafter's Punishment Is Called Greater**

The comparison in this verse is not between two penalties of similar kind but between two orders of magnitude. A farmer who loses a crop loses a season; the accounting that follows this life concerns the whole of what a person was. The Book makes the contrast in a sentence that borrows the same structure as the verse before us: *“So Allah made them taste humiliation in this worldly life, but far worse is the punishment of the Hereafter, if only they knew.”* (39:26). The humiliation mentioned there is one nation's experience of being brought down in the world, and the Book's point is that the same people face something considerably heavier.

For the sūrah's first audience, the visible scale mattered. Makkan society measured standing in camels, gardens, and alliances, and the verse insists that the accounting on the other side is not measured in those units at all.

The Book adds a second reason the later punishment is heavier, and it concerns duration. Everything in this world ends, including its penalties, and the loss of a harvest is followed by other seasons. The accounting of the Hereafter is not followed by anything, which is why the Book's warnings about it repeatedly use vocabulary of permanence rather than of intensity. A person who has learned to think in terms of years can be forgiven for underestimating a sentence measured in something else.

**"If Only They Knew"**

The verse ends with a clause the Book uses often, and the clause changes the warning into an invitation. The information is available; the audience has not taken it. Nothing in the sentence suggests that the matter is obscure or that the evidence is missing, and the phrase leaves the choice where the whole sūrah has been placing it — with the hearer, who is being told what he does not yet know.

**What the Makkan Traders Were Meant to Hear**

The parable is planted in a sūrah addressed to wealthy merchants, and the connection is not difficult to see. The people being reproached in the surrounding verses are the men who mocked the Prophet ﷺ, blocked the road, and trusted in their assets; the story of the garden shows them how quickly assets are converted into evidence. Their own city lived by trade, and the sūrah's comparison between a ruined harvest and a final accounting gives them a measure of what they were risking for the sake of a surplus they intended to keep.

The verse ends the parable on a note that concerns them directly: the punishment they were watching in the story is the smaller of the two being described.

There is also a practical argument inside the comparison. A merchant who understood risk would ask what a man gains by withholding a share of a harvest when the withholding produces a loss of the whole. The Book does not present the parable as an isolated punishment for stinginess; it presents it as the ordinary operation of an arrangement in which everything a person holds is already on loan. The verse's phrase about the Hereafter being worse then tells the audience where the loss ends if the lesson of the story is not taken.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:34

> Indeed, the righteous will have the Gardens of Bliss with their Lord.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Promise in One Line**

After the warning comes the counter-statement, and the sūrah sets it out without argument or elaboration: the people who were mindful of their Lord will have gardens, and the gardens are described by their quality rather than their contents. The verse marks the turn of the chapter. From this point the sūrah addresses the claims of the wealthy men of Makkah about their own standing, and the opening statement of the new section is a promise that has nothing to do with camel herds, harvests, or tribal rank.

**What the Word "Bliss" Names**

The gardens in this verse are gardens of *naʿīm*, and the commentators explain the term as blessing that is pure and unmixed, in contrast to the goods of this world, which arrive attached to anxieties, conditions, and the risk of loss. The Book uses the word repeatedly for the opposite of punishment: the virtuous are described as being in bliss while the wicked are in a blazing fire (82:13–14), and the same picture is drawn in the sūrah of the Defrauders, where the virtuous are seated on couches and the glow of delight is visible on their faces (83:22–24). In another sūrah the people of that bliss are described as fully pleased with what their striving produced, and the sūrah names their dwelling as an elevated garden (88:8–10).

One passage places the same word in the middle of the journey rather than at its end, promising serenity, fragrance, and a garden of bliss to the soul of the person brought near to God (56:88–89). Bliss in the Qur'an's vocabulary is a condition, not merely a place.

**"With Their Lord": the Phrase That Fixes the Value**

The gardens are not described as a distant inheritance but as being *with* the Lord of the righteous, and the Book's descriptions of the reward return to that nearness as the highest part of it. The people of paradise are said to be at the seat of honour in the presence of the Sovereign (54:55), and other passages describe faces that are pleased because they are looking at their Lord. A reader who understands the gardens as a set of pleasures has missed the phrase in this verse, which locates the reward in a relationship before it locates it in a landscape.

That is also the answer to the sūrah's earlier story. The garden in the parable was a possession that its owners stood outside of; the gardens promised here are described in relation to their owner as much as their occupants.

**The Objection the Verse Reportedly Drew**

The exegetical reports describe a reaction to this promise. When the verse was recited, the disbelievers of Makkah boasted to the Muslims that the favours they enjoyed in this life were a sign of what awaited them in the next; the following verse, with its question about whether those who submitted to God should be treated like the wicked, was revealed as the answer. The story fits the chapter's setting closely. The Makkans had already been shown a parable about a garden's owners, and their response was to claim that their own gardens were evidence of favour rather than material for a test.

**Righteousness Defined by Conduct, Not by Wealth**

The verse names the people who receive the gardens as the righteous, and the Book elsewhere explains what the term covers. A long verse sets out the definition while explicitly denying that it can be reduced to ritual direction: *“Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west. Rather, the righteous are those who believe in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books, and the prophets; who give charity out of their cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives; who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and keep the pledges they make; and who are patient in times of suffering, adversity, and in ˹the heat of˺ battle.”* (2:177).

The list is worth reading beside the parable. The categories named in it — relatives, orphans, the poor, travellers, beggars, captives — are the people the brothers of the garden intended to keep outside the gate, and the definition of righteousness in that verse is largely a description of what they refused to do.

**The Gardens That Answer the Garden**

The sūrah's structure makes an argument by placement. A story about an earthly garden destroyed in a single night is followed, a few verses later, by a promise of gardens that are not subject to a visitation. The first garden supported a family for a generation and then vanished with its owners' consent; the second is announced as a reward that survives the people who receive it.

That contrast explains the choice of imagery in a chapter that could have spoken of paradise in any number of ways. The Book takes the thing its audience valued most, shows it failing, and then offers a version of it that cannot fail.

**What the Promise Meant to the First Believers**

The community hearing this sūrah held little of what Makkah counted as wealth. Its members were drawn from the poorer families of the city, from those without protection, and from a few men who had given up their standing. A promise of gardens with their Lord was an answer to their circumstances which did not involve becoming like the men who opposed them, and the Book reinforces the point elsewhere by promising that God will never waste the work of anyone who migrates, is expelled, or is persecuted for His sake, and by naming the reward as gardens beneath which rivers flow (3:195).

The verse is therefore not a general statement about the afterlife placed here by accident. It is the answer to the story the sūrah has just told, addressed to the people who had lost their own portions of this world's goods for the sake of the message.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:35

> Should We then treat those who have submitted like the wicked?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question That Supplies Its Own Answer**

The sūrah asks whether the people who submitted to God can be handled in the same way as the people who rejected Him, and the question is framed so that no reader can answer it in the affirmative. The Book uses this technique repeatedly: instead of arguing for a distinction, it places the two categories side by side and lets the audience's own sense of justice do the work. What is being addressed in this verse is not a point of information but an assumption, held by the wealthy men of Makkah, that their position would be preserved into the next world exactly as it was in this one.

**The Same Question Elsewhere in the Book**

Other passages make the same comparison with variations in vocabulary, and the repetition shows how central the argument is. One verse asks the question in two forms at once: *“Or should We treat those who believe and do good like those who make mischief throughout the land? Or should We treat the righteous like the wicked?”* (38:28). Another states the matter as a plain fact rather than a question: *“Is the one who is a believer equal ˹before Allah˺ to the one who is rebellious? They are not equal!”* (32:18). A third keeps both pairs together: *“Those blind ˹to the truth˺ and those who can see are not equal, nor are those who believe and do good ˹equal˺ to those who do evil. Yet you are hardly mindful.”* (40:58).

The Book also extends the comparison into imagery of light and darkness, heat and shade, and life and death (35:19–22), each pair making the same claim about the difference between two conditions that look similar from outside.

One more verse places the same distinction in a command rather than a question, and it directs the audience to the standard rather than the appearance: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Good and evil are not equal, though you may be dazzled by the abundance of evil.””* (5:100). The sūrah's question in this verse is the same question with its details removed, which is why it can be answered by anyone who has ever watched an unjust man prosper and found the sight intolerable.

**The Two Words: Submitted and Wicked**

The verse contrasts people who have *submitted* with people it calls *wicked*, and the second term is a legal and moral category rather than an insult. The commentators note that the word for the submitters here is the same word used for Muslims, and some of them observe that this may be the earliest appearance of the term in the order of revelation. The pairing is therefore exact: on one side are people who handed their affairs to God, and on the other are people whose conduct made them answerable for a crime. The verse asks whether the two are to be equivalent, and the question assumes that the audience will find the idea unacceptable.

The verse belongs to a thread running through the sūrah about the difference between outward standing and inward reality. The men of Makkah were judging by what they could see: wealth, ancestry, numbers, and the respect a man commanded in the marketplace. The Book's response is to show how consistently those measures fail. A rich family can own a garden and lose it in a night; a man of no standing can be the one whose conduct God accepts. The question in this verse is the point at which the two systems of measurement are forced to disagree with each other in public.

**Justice as the Ground of the Question**

Nothing in the verse defends the distinction it draws by appealing to privilege. Its argument is the opposite: the Book treats the difference between the two groups as a requirement of justice. A judge who punished the obedient and the criminal alike would be regarded as unfair by every human standard, and the verse uses that common standard against the people who were demanding to be judged differently. The final accounting in the Book is described in the language of scales and exact weights, with the assurance that no soul will be wronged by so much as a mustard seed (21:47).

That appeal to justice also explains why the question in this verse is put to the wealthy rather than to the poor. The men being addressed had spent their lives insisting that merit produced their position; the Book accepts the premise and turns it around. If merit matters, then the distinction between the obedient and the criminal cannot be dissolved in the next world, and the last group that should expect to be waived past the accounting is the group that made its arrangements in defiance of the one who will conduct it.

**What the Verse Answers in the Sūrah's Argument**

The Makkans had been claiming that their prosperity was evidence of their standing, and the sūrah had already told them a story about a garden that was destroyed in a single night. This verse applies the lesson to the long term. If God treated the arrogant and the humble identically in the next world, the flood and the fire, the warnings and the messengers, and the entire moral order of the revelation would be meaningless. The verse asks the audience to notice what their own expectations imply about the character of the one who will judge them.

**Why the Question Is Put to the Deniers**

The Book's most characteristic form of address to a hostile audience is a question, and the sūrah has used it from its opening verses. A question does not silence a man; it requires him to answer, and it leaves his answer on the record for him to live with. The deniers of Makkah are being asked, in this verse, to state in public that they believe the righteous and the wicked should be treated alike, and the sūrah supplies the answer before they can speak it. What they are left with is the question itself, and a further one about their capacity to judge.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:36

> What is the matter with you? How do you judge?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Rebuke in Two Questions**

The verse follows the comparison with an expression of astonishment, and it directs the astonishment at the audience rather than at the argument. The first question asks what has happened to them; the second asks about the quality of the judgment they are applying. In two short sentences the sūrah moves from the content of the claim to the condition of the people making it, which is where the Book usually locates the real problem. The audience has just been told that the righteous and the wicked are not alike, and their objection to the statement is itself being placed under examination.

Questions of this kind recur in the Qur'an whenever a group substitutes its own preferences for a standard. The Book asks what has happened to an audience's reasoning, and then asks how they have arrived at their verdict. The sūrah has already used the second question once in a related form, and it will return to the same challenge in the verses that follow, where the audience is asked whether they possess a scripture, an oath, or a guarantor that supports their expectations (68:37–41). The series works as a courtroom sequence in which the defendants are asked to produce their documents and cannot.

**The Faculty They Were Supposed to Use**

The Book's appeal is not to blind obedience but to a capacity the audience was given and knows it has. One verse states the contrast with the same vocabulary the sūrah has used earlier: *“˹Are they better˺ or those who worship ˹their Lord˺ devoutly in the hours of the night, prostrating and standing, fearing the Hereafter and hoping for the mercy of their Lord? Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Are those who know equal to those who do not know?” None will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason.”* (39:9). The Book elsewhere describes wisdom itself as a gift from God and adds that only people of understanding take the reminder (2:269).

The verse in this sūrah belongs to the same argument. The audience cannot claim that the question is beyond them; the comparison was constructed from material they use every day in courts, markets, and family life.

An audience given hearing, sight, and understanding cannot argue that the earlier comparison exceeded its equipment. The sūrah has already reminded its readers of those gifts and of the accounting attached to them, and this verse asks them to use the very faculty whose misuse the earlier passage described. The Book's confidence in putting such a question is itself a statement about human beings: the capacity to recognise the difference between the messenger and the men who opposed him was present in Makkah, and the obstruction lay somewhere else.

An earlier verse of the Book describes the danger of judging by what is plentiful rather than by what is true: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Good and evil are not equal, though you may be dazzled by the abundance of evil. So be mindful of Allah, O people of reason, so you may be successful.””* (5:100). The Makkan elite were dazzled by abundance in the most literal sense. They had the numbers, the income, and the alliances, and they concluded from the size of their position that the position was correct.

This verse asks them what instrument they used to reach that conclusion. The Book's criticism is not that they judged without information but that they judged without a measure, and the two are different failures.

The chapter has been constructing a case that the deniers cannot see what is in front of them, and this verse marks the point at which their competence as judges is questioned in the open. Everything placed before them in the sūrah — the pen and the record, the character of the messenger, the parable of the garden, the promise to the righteous — is evidence they have been treating as noise. The question about how they judge is a summary of the whole passage: the material was sufficient, and the verdict they reached was produced by something other than the material.

**Why the Rebuke Is Addressed to the Group**

The verse speaks to a community rather than to an individual, and the plural is important. The Book's arguments about the next world were being rejected in Makkah by an informal council of the same wealthy men, and the pressure of agreement among them had turned a set of private doubts into a settled public position. The question in this verse is addressed to all of them at once, which means that each man is being asked to describe his own reasoning rather than to appeal to the group's conclusion.

**The Question That Remains Open**

The sūrah does not close the matter here. The verses that follow continue the examination, asking whether the audience has a scripture in which their expectations are written, an oath binding God to their preferences, or a guarantor for their claims. The question about how they judge is a first move in that sequence, and its purpose is to make the audience aware that the burden of proof is on them. Once a person has been asked to produce his standard, the absence of one becomes visible, and the Book's next questions are built on that absence.

The tone of the verse is sharp, and its purpose is not humiliation. A rebuke from the Book is addressed to people who can still change the answer they give, and the sūrah continues to address its audience as parties to a discussion rather than as prisoners awaiting sentence. The men being questioned at this point in the chapter are the same men to whom the Prophet ﷺ had been reciting for years, and the question about their judgment is an invitation to use it.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:37

> Or do you have a scripture, in which you read

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question of the Document**

The sūrah moves from asking how the deniers judge to asking what they are reading. The question is whether they possess a revealed book that supports their expectation of being treated differently from the righteous, and it is put in the form of a challenge rather than a proposition. The commentators explain the verse in the same terms, and they connect it to a series of similar challenges in other sūrahs. The audience is being asked to produce its authority, and the audience has nothing to bring.

**The Book's Habit of Asking for Proof**

This kind of challenge recurs throughout the Qur'an whenever a group claims a position that the revelation does not grant it. One verse demands the evidence directly: *“Bring me a scripture ˹revealed˺ before this ˹Quran˺ or a shred of knowledge, if what you say is true.”* (46:4). Another turns the claim back on the claimants with the same instrument: *“Then bring ˹us˺ your scripture, if what you say is true!”* (37:157). A third asks whether such a book exists at all and whether its owners are holding to it: *“Or have We given them a Book ˹for proof˺, before this ˹Quran˺, to which they are holding firm?”* (43:21).

Two further passages supply the context of the Makkan claim. The Book notes that no scripture had been sent to these people to study and no warner had come to them before (34:44), and it asks their associate-gods to demonstrate what they have created, ending with the observation that the wrongdoers promise each other nothing but delusion (35:40).

**A Community Without a Book of Its Own**

The Makkans of the Prophet's lifetime were heirs to the memory of Abraham's religion but not to a written scripture. Their city had no revealed text in Arabic, and the Qur'an's arrival among them was the first time such a book existed in their language. The audience of this verse therefore knew the answer to the question before it was finished. What they possessed were inherited practices, poetic tradition, and the opinions of their elders, none of which could be produced as a document in a dispute about the world to come.

The Book states its own status in the same connection: it was revealed as an authority in Arabic, and following the desires of a community after knowledge has arrived leaves a person without a protector (13:37).

**The Book They Were Ignoring**

The sūrah's question lands differently when read beside the verses that describe what a scripture demands. One passage defines the people who actually possess revelation as those who follow it as it should be followed (2:121), and another asks what message an audience will believe in after denying God and His revelations (45:6). The Makkans had no book of their own to consult about the next world, and they had access to the one that had been recited in their streets for years. The question in this verse simply makes that arrangement explicit.

**The Two Ways the Sentence Can Be Read**

The commentators note a small variation in the reading of this verse and the next. One way of reciting the passage places a stop at the end of this verse, so that the question is whether the audience has a book at all, and the following verse describes what such a book would contain: a promise that they will have whatever they choose. The other way joins the two verses into a single sentence about a book in which they read that promise. Both readings produce the same challenge, because the claim being tested is the same in each: that a document exists in which their preference has been guaranteed.

The difference between the two recitations is a small matter of breath and a substantial matter of argument. In one, the audience is asked whether it possesses a revelation at all; in the other, the content of the imagined revelation is included in the same sentence. The sūrah's first hearers received the passage recited rather than written, and the pause was the only punctuation available to them. Either arrangement leaves the hearer with the same task: name the book that promises you your choice.

**Reading Selectively From a Book**

The idea of a scripture that grants a reader his choice appears elsewhere in the Book as a description of how some communities handled their revelations. One verse describes people who know nothing of the Book except what they wish to be true, and who deal only in conjecture (2:78). Another addresses a group that believed part of the scripture and rejected the rest, asking what reward such conduct deserves beyond disgrace in this world and the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment (2:85). A third reports those who distorted the words of the Book with their tongues and attributed the result to God (3:78).

The question in this sūrah belongs to that family of criticisms. It asks whether the audience's expectation of exemption came from a text they had received or from a preference they had brought with them.

**What the Challenge Leaves Standing**

By the end of the verse the audience has been deprived of any authority to cite. The sūrah's sequence of challenges — a book, an oath, a guarantor, associate-gods — is designed to remove each support in turn, and this is the first of them. What remains after the removal is the position the sūrah has been describing from the beginning: a group of people who have taken a conclusion from their own interests and who are being asked, one item at a time, to say where it came from.

The removal of the supports is also a service to the audience, and the Book does not conceal the fact. A person whose position rests on an authority he cannot name is in a weak position whether or not he has noticed it, and these questions are designed to make the weakness visible while there is still time to repair it. The verses that follow continue in the same manner, moving from a book to an oath and from an oath to a guarantor, and the audience is left with the one authority it has been refusing to consider.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:38

> that you will have whatever you choose?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Claim of Unlimited Choice**

The verse describes the content of the imaginary scripture: a text promising its readers that they will receive whatever they select. The commentators explain the promise as a reference to the Hereafter and, more particularly, to the Garden, and they observe that the audience's expectation was exactly what such a text would produce. The sentence is written as a continuation of the previous question, so the audience is being asked to name a book that guarantees their preferences — a request designed to fail, since no revealed text has ever made such a promise.

**The Method of Taking a Claim to its Conclusion**

The Book's treatment of the deniers is often conducted by following their assumptions to a destination they would not have chosen. The sūrah has already asked whether the righteous and the wicked are to be treated alike; here it asks whether the audience's position rests on a document granting them whatever they want. Neither question invents a claim. Each one states the logical outcome of what the audience already believed, and the sūrah lets the absurdity of the outcome speak for itself.

The audience's real position is one the Book names in another sūrah: those who follow nothing but assumptions and do nothing but lie (6:116).

The verse raises a question that runs through the whole revelation about the source of a person's judgments. A verse warns against declaring things lawful and unlawful with the tongue in order to fabricate claims about God (16:116), and another asks whether the audience is seeking the judgment of pre-Islamic ignorance when a better judge exists for people of sure faith (5:50). The Book's objection in both places is the same as the objection here. A standard that changes with a person's wishes is not a standard; it is a preference wearing the clothes of a rule.

The Makkan audience would have recognised the argument, because their own commercial law depended on standards that did not bend according to the parties involved.

**"Whatever You Choose" and the Property of Other People**

The phrase about choosing belongs to the sūrah's larger concern with what people claim from the possessions of others, and the garden story is the reason it lands here with force. The brothers in the parable chose to keep an entire harvest, and their choice was defeated in a night. The audience being addressed in this verse has made the same move with a larger claim: exemption from judgment itself. The Book does not treat the two as different in kind. Both begin with a person deciding that the terms do not apply to him.

**The Hereafter Is Not Open to Negotiation**

Several verses in the Qur'an place the final judgment beyond the reach of human preference. One asks whether the audience seeks a judge other than the One who revealed the Book explained completely, while those who received earlier scriptures know the revelation to be true (6:114). Another states that following the desires of others after knowledge has arrived leaves a person with no protector and no shield (13:37). A third compares the two parties in the clearest possible terms: *“Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Can any of your associate-gods guide to the truth?” Say, “˹Only˺ Allah guides to the truth.” Who then is more worthy to be followed: the One Who guides to the truth or those who cannot find the way unless guided? What is the matter with you? How do you judge?””* (10:35).

That verse ends with the same rebuke the sūrah under discussion has just used, which shows how closely the two passages are related in method and in subject.

The insistence has a practical consequence for the way the Book's readers evaluate religious claims. Any teaching that makes the reward of the next life depend on a person's own selection is disqualified by this verse before it is examined on any other ground, because the offer it makes is precisely the offer the sūrah ridicules. Revelation in the Qur'an's understanding tells a person what is true and what is required, and it leaves him free to accept or to refuse; it does not adjust the account to the preferences of the reader.

**What the Followers of Revelation Receive Instead**

The alternative to choosing is described in the Book as submission, and the term is not a surrender of dignity. The people who are given the Book and follow it properly are described as the ones who truly believe it (2:121), and a verse about the prophets explains that a man blessed with scripture would never instruct people to worship him; he would call them to be devoted to their Lord in accordance with what is read and taught (3:79). The submission the Book asks for is directed at a text and a Lord, and it leaves the person who practises it with more certainty about his future than any audience of self-made promises has ever had.

The contrast being drawn is between a document that would serve its readers and a document that would rule them. The first kind exists only in the fantasy of the audience under examination here; the second is what the Book claims to be. A community that expects its scripture to confirm its decisions has placed itself above the text, and the question about the choice the audience imagines receiving is a way of asking which of the two relationships it wants with the revelation that had already reached it.

**Why the Question Is Left Unanswered**

The sūrah does not pause for an answer. The next verses continue the interrogation by asking whether the audience has oaths binding on God until the Day of Judgment and whether any of them can guarantee the claim. The sequence works because each question removes a prop that the audience had not previously examined, and the reader is allowed to watch a position being dismantled from the inside. The verse ends in the middle of that process, with the audience holding a claim they cannot source.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:39

> Or do you have oaths binding on Us until the Day of Judgment that you will have whatever you decide?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Prop: a Pledge Drawn From God**

The interrogation moves from a document to a contract. Having asked whether the audience possesses a scripture supporting its expectations, the sūrah now asks whether it holds a pledge that binds God to their terms until the Day of Judgment. The claim being tested is the most serious one a creature can make: not merely that a book grants an exemption, but that God has committed Himself to whatever the claimants decide. The commentators explain the verse as a challenge to the assumption that the disbelievers could reject the revelation and the prophethood while retaining a promise of better treatment than the believers.

**Books Read as Guarantees of Safety**

The attitude this verse attacks has a history in the Book. One passage reports a claim of limited punishment, and the answer it receives is exactly the question being asked here: *“˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, “The Fire will not touch us except for a number of days.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Have you taken a pledge from Allah— for Allah never breaks His word—or are you ˹just˺ saying about Allah what you do not know?””* (2:80). The question distinguishes sharply between two cases. A pledge from God is unbreakable and must therefore be found somewhere in what He has revealed; a claim for which no such pledge exists is a statement about God made without knowledge.

Another passage extends the same challenge to the Makkans and their claim of superiority: *“Now, are you ˹Meccan˺ disbelievers superior to those ˹destroyed peoples˺? Or have you ˹been granted˺ immunity ˹from punishment˺ in divine Books?”* (54:43). Immunity of that kind appears nowhere in the scriptures, and the sūrah's question assumes that the audience knows it.

**Oaths in the Book's Portrait of Makkah**

The Book reports the oaths of this community with a consistency that amounts to a portrait. People swore their most solemn oaths that God would never raise the dead (16:38); they swore that a warner would make them better guided than any other community, and the warning made them worse (35:42); they swore that a sign would make them believe (6:109); they swore to the believers to please them (9:62); they swore that if commanded they would march forth (24:53). The hypocrites of Madinah likewise used their oaths as a shield and swore to lies knowingly (58:14, 58:16; 63:2).

Against casual swearing the Book sets a severe valuation of the oath itself: whoever trades God's covenant and his oaths for a fleeting gain will have no share in the Hereafter (3:77), and deliberate oaths carry an expiation for the person who breaks them (5:89). An oath in this society was not a figure of speech, which is why the question in this verse is so damaging.

**The Difference Between God's Promise and Human Terms**

Nothing in the verse denies that God makes promises. The Book is built on them, and it states that the pledge of resurrection is a true promise binding on Him (16:38). What the verse denies is the possibility of a promise whose content is fixed by the person receiving it. The audience being questioned has arrived at the position that their standing in the next world is settled because of who they are, and the sūrah asks them to produce the document. What God actually promised is described throughout the Book with conditions attached, and the conditions are never the continuation of a person's own choices.

**The Oath the Sūrah Has Already Described**

Readers of this passage have met an oath before in the same chapter. The brothers of the garden swore that they would harvest in the morning and left the exception out of their vow, and the story demonstrated what that kind of confidence is worth when the future belongs to someone else. The verse under discussion widens the same lesson to the largest possible claim. A human being may swear about a harvest and be overtaken by a night; a community may swear about the Last Day and find that its oath was never heard by anyone who grants exemptions of that kind.

The chapter's opening words are themselves an oath, and the direction of the two arrangements is what this verse exposes: *“Nũn. By the pen and what everyone writes!”* (68:1). God swears by what He has made and recorded, and His oaths seal what He has already determined. The claimants want the traffic to run the other way, with a creature's decision made binding on the One who decides everything, and the verse leaves them holding a contract with no counterparty.

**"Whatever You Decide" and the Fantasy of Self-Rule**

The closing phrase ties the verse to the question in the previous one. There the audience was imagined choosing whatever it wanted; here it is imagined deciding, and the difference between the two words is the difference between desire and authority. The Book's own account of authority is stated in a sentence from the sūrah of the Cattle: the punishment the mockers wished to hasten is not within any human power to deliver, and it is God alone who decides its time (6:57). A community that had arranged its expectations as though the decision were its own is being asked to locate the agreement in which that arrangement was recorded.

**What the Audience Is Left Holding**

The verse leaves the claim where the previous two left theirs: unsupported. Each challenge in the sequence has removed a prop — a book, then a pledge — and the audience is being driven toward the position the sūrah has described from its opening verses. What follows is a demand for a guarantor, which completes the examination by asking whether any person among them would stand up and take responsibility for the claim in public.

That sequence is itself instructive for readers in every generation. A belief that cannot be traced to revelation and cannot be warranted by anyone has been accepted on a basis the believer would not accept in a market transaction, and the sūrah's questions are designed to make the difference visible.

The demand for evidence is one the Book makes of every community that has staked a claim on the Hereafter: *“The Jews and Christians each claim that none will enter Paradise except those of their own faith. These are their desires. Reply, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Show ˹me˺ your proof if what you say is true.””* (2:111). The claim in each case is the same in shape — a group treating its identity as a title deed — and the answer is the same request for the paperwork.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:40

> Ask them ˹O Prophet˺ which of them can guarantee all that.

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Documents to Persons**

The sūrah shifts the demand from texts to people. Having asked whether the audience has a book or a pledge, the verse instructs the Prophet ﷺ to ask whether any of them will stand behind the claim, and the commentators explain the word rendered *guarantee* as a form of the verb for taking responsibility for what one has said. The three supports the audience might have cited have now been identified: a revelation, a promise from God, or a superior reward in the next life. The question asks who among them has received any of it.

**The Claim Confronted With Its Claimants**

A group's position often looks stronger than the individuals who hold it, and the sūrah separates the two. Asking which of them can vouch for the expectation reduces a communal conviction to a personal undertaking, and the Book uses this method in several places. When the Makkans were asked about the creation of the heavens and the earth, they answered that the Almighty, the All-Knowing had created them (43:9); asked to whom the earth and everyone on it belonged, they answered that it was God's (23:84–85). Their own testimony was available to them in matters they could see, and the guarantee they were asked for in this verse concerns a matter they had no access to at all.

The audience's error was not a lack of information about God's existence; it was the assumption that their relationship with Him had been settled in their favour without terms. The Book records the Prophet's ﷺ own position on the limits of his knowledge in a verse from another sūrah: knowledge of the Hour rests with God alone, and the messenger's task is to warn (79:44–45). A man who stated that limit about himself was in a poor position to accept a guarantee from men who claimed no limit at all.

**Guarantors Withdrawn From the Scene**

One further observation belongs to this verse, and the Book makes it repeatedly. The men of Makkah who treated their own judgment as sufficient would have conceded, if pressed, that some matters lie beyond human knowledge; the sūrah's question simply applies their concession to the claim they were making. The distinction between an opinion and a guarantee is one that traders understand better than most, since a caravan's goods are insured against events that no merchant can foresee. The Makkans had built their commercial world on exactly that caution, and the verse asks them why the terms of the next world had been given none of it.

The Book's descriptions of the Last Day systematically remove the figures whom people had counted on to speak for them. Intercession is said to belong to God alone (39:44), and the day is described as one on which no friendship will be of use (2:254). The sūrah's next verse continues the same process by asking the audience whether they have associate-gods and inviting them to produce them. The demand for a guarantor in this verse is therefore not a rhetorical flourish; it is the first step in a process that the sūrah completes over the following verses.

**Why Nobody in the Audience Could Answer**

The men being addressed were the leading families of Makkah, and their authority rested on wealth, ancestry, and the alliances they could command. None of those assets gave any of them access to the terms of the next world, and the sūrah's question forces the point into the open. Had one of them claimed to hold such a guarantee, he would have been required to show where it came from; had the group named another man, the same demand would have moved to him. The question cannot be answered because the thing being asked for does not exist.

**The Promise God Did Make**

The Book does contain promises, and it states their conditions. One verse describes the consequence of trading God's covenant and oaths for a temporary advantage: no share in the Hereafter, no speech from God, no glance, no purification, and a painful punishment (3:77). Another states the positive form: whoever fulfils the pledge he has made to God will receive a great reward (48:10). What the sūrah's audience wanted was a guarantee without a pledge of their own, and the Book's promises run in the opposite direction.

A guarantee of the kind being demanded would have required two things the audience did not have: a source and a witness. The source would have been a revelation containing the promise, and the previous verses have already asked for it. The witness would have been a person willing to say in public that the promise had been made, and the Book's courtroom scenes in other sūrahs show how such testimony fares on the day it is false. What was left to the Makkans was a claim held by general agreement, unsupported by a text and unbacked by any individual, and the sūrah's question exposes the arrangement by asking someone to sign his name to it.

**The Guarantee Offered Where a Guarantee Was Refused**

The rejection of the message was itself a decision about guarantors. The wealthy men of Makkah had chosen as their security their clan, their investments, and their standing in the sanctuary, and those assets are exactly what the sūrah's earlier parable removed in a single night. The verse asks them to name the person who has warranted the terms of the next world, and the Book's account of the next world answers that no created being stands in that position. The only guarantee available is the one offered by the revelation they were rejecting, and its terms begin with acknowledgment of the one making the promise.

The instruction to ask the whole audience rather than a single leader is deliberate. A position held by a community is easier to defend than a position held by an individual, because the members can each assume that someone else has examined it. By requiring them to nominate a guarantor, the sūrah converts a shared assumption into a personal responsibility, and it does so in front of the believers. That public shift of the burden is the rhetorical achievement of the verse, and it prepares the ground for the next question about the associate-gods the audience associated with God.

**A Question That Closes a Door and Leaves Another Open**

Sharp as it is, the question in this verse is not an ending. The sūrah's method throughout the passage has been to take each support on which the audience relied and ask for its documentation, and a person who follows that process honestly arrives at the position the revelation has been recommending from the beginning: trust placed in the only party who controls the outcome. The verse next asks about the associate-gods the audience associated with Him. The invitation to produce them is genuine, and the audience's silence is the answer the sūrah is seeking.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:41

> Or do they have associate-gods ˹supporting this claim˺? Then let them bring forth their associate-gods, if what they say is true.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Prop and the Invitation to Produce It**

The examination reaches its final item. Having asked for a book, a pledge, and a guarantor, the sūrah asks whether the audience has partner-deities who back the claim, and it follows the question with an invitation to produce them. The commentators read the partners in two ways: the idols and other beings the Makkans associated with God, or the influential men among them whose judgment they treated as sufficient. Both readings fit a verse that is dismantling every form of borrowed authority, and the invitation at the end is the same challenge the Book issues elsewhere.

**Gods That Cannot Create or Help Themselves**

The Qur'an's descriptions of the idols strip them of every capacity their worshippers assumed. One verse lists what they cannot do in a single sentence: *“Yet they have taken besides Him gods who cannot create anything but are themselves created. Nor can they protect or benefit themselves. Nor can they control life, death, or resurrection.”* (25:3). Another pairs the charge with two questions: *“Do they associate ˹with Allah˺ those ˹idols˺ which cannot create anything, but are in fact created; which cannot help them, or even help themselves?”* (7:191–192).

The sūrah of the Spider supplies the image the Book uses for the whole arrangement: those who take protectors other than God are like a spider spinning a shelter, and the flimsiest of shelters is that of a spider (29:41). The picture is not of wickedness but of structural weakness, and the verse under discussion asks the audience to lean on the structure in public.

There is a practical dimension in the audience's own city, since the idols of the sanctuary were also a trade. The men who carved them, sold them, and received the offerings of pilgrims had an income from the arrangement as well as a belief in it, and the Book's argument touches both. A god that must be carried into position and protected from theft is of limited use as a patron, and the sūrah's invitation to produce him in public is a way of asking what such a being could actually do for the people who relied on him.

**Intercessors Who Cannot Even Testify**

The Makkan claim was not usually that the idols created or ruled; it was that they carried requests to God. The Book answers that claim in a series of verses that remove the intercessors from the picture. One verse reports the audience's own explanation of their worship and asks whether they are informing God of something He does not know (10:18). Another asks whether they have taken intercessors who have neither authority nor intelligence (39:43). A third observes that the beings people call upon besides God cannot even respond, and are unaware that they are being called (46:5).

The last detail is the sharpest. An intercessor who does not hear the request is not a mediator; he is a name.

**The Day They Are Called and Do Not Answer**

The Book describes what happens when the arrangement is finally tested. In one scene the disbelievers are told to call upon their associate-gods, they call, and no answer comes (28:64); in another the same summons produces silence and a shared doom (18:52). A third passage records the moment when the partners are asked where they are, and the worshippers themselves deny the association they had maintained (41:47–48), while the gods they invoked turn on them and call them liars (16:86).

The invitation in this verse to bring the partners forward is therefore an invitation to a demonstration that the Book has already described from the other end of time.

The witness the Book recommends instead is named in a short exchange recorded elsewhere: asked who is the best witness, the Prophet ﷺ is instructed to answer that God is, and to state that He is a witness between him and the audience, and that the revelation came to warn them and whoever it reaches (6:19). The verse is precise about where valid testimony is kept. A community that spent its arguments assembling supporters is being shown the only testimony that will be entered in the case.

**The Wise Men of the Tribe as Guarantors**

If the partners are read as people rather than idols, the verse's target is the community's reliance on the judgment of its elders. The Book reports that argument in the words of its users: told to follow what God revealed, they answered that they would follow what their forefathers practised, and the reply asks whether they would do so even if their forefathers had no understanding or guidance (2:170). A similar exchange is reported of the elites of earlier societies, who said that they had found their ancestors on a way and were walking in their footsteps (43:22–23), and another verse records the community's claim that what their forefathers practised was good enough for them (5:104).

The sūrah's question puts the same reliance under examination. A tribe's elders may be wise about weather, trade, and war; none of them has been given the terms of the next world, and a community that treats their collective opinion as a guarantee has confused experience with authority.

**The Confidence Behind the Challenge**

The invitation to produce the partners is issued without fear, and the Book records a moment when the challenge became explicitly confrontational. In one passage the Prophet ﷺ is told to address the idolaters directly: *“Do they have feet to walk with? Or hands to hold with? Or eyes to see with? Or ears to hear with? Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Call upon your associate-gods and conspire against me without delay!””* (7:195). The servants of the idols are invited to assemble whatever support they have and use it.

That confidence rests on the sūrah's whole argument. The claim being tested had no book, no pledge, no guarantor, and now no partners, and the verse leaves the audience at the end of its resources with the question still standing.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:42

> ˹Beware of˺ the Day the Shin ˹of Allah˺ will be bared, and the wicked will be asked to prostrate, but they will not be able to do so,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Idiom of the Bared Shin**

The phrase translated *the Shin will be bared* is an Arabic expression for a matter of extreme seriousness, and the commentators explain its origin in the clothing of the desert. A man wearing a long garment who sets out on strenuous work, or who finds himself in battle, gathers the cloth up so that his lower leg is exposed, and the idiom describing a crisis — that the war broke out upon the shank — comes from that picture. The verse therefore announces the arrival of a moment which no one can meet casually, and it announces it in the idiom of the audience's own daily life rather than in the abstract language of theology.

The word has a concrete counterpart elsewhere in the Book. When the queen of Sheba is brought into Solomon's palace and mistakes the crystal floor for water, the translation describes her as baring her legs (27:44) — a person exposed before a prophet in a moment of complete surprise. The Qur'an uses the limb in its ordinary sense in that scene and reserves the idiom here, where what is exposed is not a body but a situation.

**The Readings and What They Change**

The exegetes record several ways of taking the phrase. On one reading God bares His shank, an act signalling the beginning of the judgement and demanding prostration from every creature. A variant recitation states the same in the first person plural, so that the verse reads that We lay bare the shank. A third reading takes the exposure as a figure for the severities that the resurrection will uncover, and a fourth connects it with the revealing of secrets that a person had kept hidden.

Each reading agrees on the effect of the verse, and that effect is the point: the scene is the moment when the outward appearance of a life is stripped away and what remains is asked to submit.

The differences among the readings concern what the phrase refers to rather than how serious the moment is. On every interpretation, something hidden is brought into the open and the creatures present are required to respond to it, and the verse's function in the sūrah is to move the audience from arguments about documents to the sight of a matter they cannot question. The Book elsewhere affirms what God has described of Himself without comparing Him to anything created, and scholars discussing this verse have kept to that rule while differing over the details.

**The Report About the Prostration**

The exegetical tradition reports a ḥadīth in connection with this verse, describing the baring of the shank, the prostration of the believers, men and women, and the attempt of those who had prostrated in the world only in order to be seen, whose backs become stiff and cannot bend. Whatever the reader concludes about the report's details, its picture matches the verse exactly. The prostration is available to everyone on that day, and it is impossible precisely for the people who had practised the gesture for an audience.

**Prostration as the Habit Being Tested**

The following verse explains the situation in a sentence that removes any suggestion of arbitrariness: they were called to prostrate while they were fully able and declined. The sūrah under discussion is addressed to men who had heard that call for years — the Qur'an's own prostrations, the prayer of the Prophet ﷺ, the visible submission of the believers in their city — and whose response had been mockery. What the Day brings is not a new requirement but a repetition of a summons they had already refused, with the difference that their bodies no longer cooperate.

**The Body Used to Display a Spiritual Condition**

The Qur'an often indicates an inner state through the body's posture, and this passage is one of its clearest examples. In another sūrah the people who denied the truth are described as being dragged into Hell on their faces and as furthest from the right way (25:34); the physical position matches the fact that they spent their lives with no view of the road. Elsewhere the Book describes faces that are bright and laughing beside faces covered in dust and gloom (80:38–41), and it separates the two groups by their expressions before it reports anything they said (3:106–107).

A person's habits in this world become his capacities in the next, and the inability to prostrate in this scene is the completion of a refusal rather than a punishment invented at the moment.

The same picture appears in another sūrah, where the humbling of the eyes and the covering of disgrace are described for those who are summoned (70:44), and the Book's consistent method is to let the posture carry the verdict. Faces are bright or dust-covered, bodies are able or unable, and the physical detail is never an arbitrary addition to the sentence. It records the state of a person whose conduct has been travelling toward that condition for the whole of his life.

The men addressed by this sūrah had been called to prostrate in the ordinary course of their days. The prayer of the believers was visible in Makkah, the recitation included its own prostrations, and the posture they found humiliating was offered to them as a practice that would have made prostration on that day the most natural movement in the world.

**Why the Scene Follows the Courtroom Questions**

The sūrah has spent a sequence of verses demanding documents: a book, an oath, a guarantor, partner-gods. The passage now shows the moment when the argument is over and the claim is tested by an action that everyone present will attempt. The men who had answers for every question in Makkah are given one instruction on that day, and the verse reports that they cannot perform it.

That transition from debate to event is the sūrah's closing strategy. The verses that follow turn to the Prophet ﷺ with comfort and instruction, which means the sequence ends where the whole chapter has been leading: the verdict belongs to God, and the community's task is patience.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:43

> with eyes downcast, totally covered with disgrace. For they were ˹always˺ called to prostrate ˹in the world˺ when they were fully capable ˹but they chose not to˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Detail of the Scene**

The verse completes the picture with two images: eyes lowered and disgrace covering its owners. The first image removes the possibility of defiance, since a person whose gaze is fixed on the ground has already conceded the argument. The second turns the condition into something visible and total. The Book uses the covering of disgrace in several descriptions of the judgement, and the effect is always the same: the interior state of a person is displayed on his surface for everyone to read.

A sūrah that opens with the news of an overwhelming event describes the same expression: faces downcast on the day it arrives (88:1–2). The Book's portraits of that day regularly begin with the face, because the face is the part of a person that cannot be rearranged once the matter has been decided. What the verse under discussion adds is the second layer, so that the humiliation is not merely visible in a man's features but wrapped around him.

**Why the Refusal Explains the Inability**

The final clause supplies the reason, and the reason is a matter of history rather than of physical power. The men in the scene were called to prostrate while they were fully capable and declined, and the inability that follows is the settled form of that decision. The commentators make the connection explicit: one reading of the passage treats the prostration of the previous verse as the prostration of the Hereafter and the prostration of this verse as the call to prayer in this life, so that the punishment matches the refusal exactly.

**The Posture the Arabs Objected To**

The exegetes report that some of the Arabs objected to prostration on the grounds that the position was humiliating — knees on the ground and a forehead in the dust — and the Book's answer is contained in this scene. The men who found the posture beneath them were given a far greater humiliation to carry, and the refusal itself is being identified as the reason they are now unable to perform the act that would have saved them from it. The objection had been to a gesture; what the verse describes is a condition.

The complaint being reported was about status rather than effort. Prostration places a man's face level with the ground beside whoever stands next to him, and in a society organised by clans and by the ranking between them, the posture made a chief and a servant equal for the duration of the prayer. Islam's answer is that the equality is the truth about them, and the scene in these verses shows the same men encountering the fact at a moment when they cannot avoid it.

**The Ability They Had**

The clause about capacity is the part of the verse that addresses the audience directly. It does not describe people who never heard the call or who were prevented from answering it; it describes men who had years of opportunity. The prayer of the Muslims was performed in the streets of Makkah, the recitation of the Qur'an included prostrations of its own, and the Prophet ﷺ had prostrated among his people for as long as they had known him. Nobody in that city could claim that the summons had been concealed, and the verse says so in a few words that leave no room for a defence.

The call had reached the households of Makkah through the public prayer of the believers, and it arrived in the recitation itself, which contains places at which a listener is invited to fall down. Prostration was not an unfamiliar practice in the city; it was the gesture that marked the new community most visibly. The clause about capacity closes that line of defence as firmly as anything in the chapter, and the men it describes were the ones who had watched the believers prostrate in the sanctuary and had chosen to stand.

The Arabic used for the covering suggests a garment or shade laid over a person, and the Book uses related imagery for both conditions in the next life. Faces that are bright are described with light, and faces that are ruined are described with dust and gloom (80:38–41), while the people who are kept from the sight of their Lord are described as covered by a veil. The covering is not decoration; it is the closest the Book comes to a portrait of shame as a physical environment.

**What Worship Was Supposed to Do**

The passage treats prostration as a practice with cumulative effects, and the Book's teaching about worship follows the same logic throughout. A servant who places his forehead on the ground in private and in public is training a capacity, and the sūrah shows what the capacity is for. The refusal to practise the movement in this world produces the inability to make it in the next, which means the punishment is not an arbitrary penalty imposed on a person who simply neglected a ritual. It is the completed form of the choice he made when the ritual was available.

The same pairing of a call and a capacity appears in a parallel description of that day, where the humbling of the eyes is presented as the destination of people who had been warned about it all along (70:44). The Book's warnings seldom introduce a new requirement; they remind a person of an instruction he already had. What is lost in the scene is not the opportunity but the ability to use it.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:44

> So leave to Me ˹O Prophet˺ those who reject this message. We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command to Hand the Matter Over**

The sūrah turns from the interrogation of the deniers to an instruction for the Prophet ﷺ. The command is to leave those who reject the reminder to God, which means ending the effort to force a verdict out of them rather than abandoning the work of warning. The Book issues the same instruction at other points in the Makkan revelation, and its purpose in each case is to relieve the messenger of a responsibility that was never his. The argument had been made; the outcome belongs to the one who will raise the question again on a day the audience does not control.

The object of the denial is described here as this message, and the commentators identify it with the Qur'an itself. The audience in Makkah had spent years hearing the same recitation, and their rejection was not of an unfamiliar claim but of a text whose verses they knew well enough to mock. The sūrah's earlier passages have listed what the deniers said about the messenger and the reminder; this verse names the response in a single phrase and moves on.

**Drawn On Gradually**

The phrase translated as *gradually draw them on* describes a process in which a person is moved forward step by step without noticing the movement. The source of the expression is the fisherman's or the traveller's experience of being brought along a gradient, and the Book uses it for the ease with which a person who reads prosperity as approval is allowed to continue. The idea is stated plainly in the sūrah of the Heavens: those who deny are left to be drawn on gradually from a direction they do not know (7:182–183).

The Book explains why the arrangement works: *“Do they think, since We provide them with wealth and children, that We hasten to ˹honour˺ them ˹with˺ all kinds of good? No! They are not aware.”* (23:55–56). The gifts themselves become the instrument of the process, because the recipient reads them as evidence about his standing.

The same sentence recurs in the Heights, where the drawing on of the deniers is followed by a statement about the delayed end and the flawless planning (7:183). The two verses form a pair there, and the sūrah under discussion repeats them as a pair as well. Such repetition suggests that the temptation being addressed is constant rather than occasional: comfort, success, and years of unbroken routine make a delay look like a verdict.

**The Report About Respite**

The exegetical tradition connects this verse with a report in which the Prophet ﷺ said that God grants the wrongdoer respite, but that when He seizes him there is no escape, after which he recited the verse about the seizing of towns while they were doing wrong and described that seizing as painful and severe (11:102). The report belongs to the same idea as the verse under discussion. The delay is not a gap in the divine administration of affairs; it is part of the method, and its purpose is not acquittal.

**Why Gradual Ruin Is Harder Than Sudden Ruin**

There is a reason the Book describes this process as worse than a sudden judgement arriving on time. A punishment that arrives immediately leaves the offender in the middle of his career with a clear knowledge of what has happened. A man allowed to continue has the opportunity to add to what he will be asked about, and every month of continuation confirms him in the belief that nothing is being recorded. The Book states the underlying arithmetic elsewhere, warning the deniers not to imagine that the respite granted to them is good for them: they are granted time only to increase in sin, and their punishment will be humiliating (3:178).

**What the Prophet Is Asked to Do Instead**

The instruction to leave the matter to God does not end the passage; it changes the posture from which the rest of the sūrah is spoken. The verses that follow repeat the interrogation of the deniers in a cooler key and then turn to the Prophet's ﷺ own comfort, ending with a description of what the Qur'an is for. The messenger is told to be patient with the decree, to avoid the impatience of the man of the whale, and to remember that he has neither asked the people for a reward nor claimed a share of the unseen. The command in this verse is the first step of that closing movement.

The instruction also settles a question that had been pressing on the believers: whether the continued prosperity of the mockers was evidence that their position had been accepted. The verse answers that question with a description of a process rather than a judgment about the men. They are being moved forward, and the movement is not toward the place they believe they are going.

**The Comfort Inside the Command**

The phrase about drawing them on gradually is addressed to a man who had been watching his opponents prosper for years, and the sentence tells him that their prosperity is not evidence of anything except the working of a process he cannot see. The Book's way of consoling a messenger under pressure is not to promise him the destruction of his enemies but to describe accurately what is happening to them. The audience is being led along a route whose end they do not know, and the Prophet ﷺ is told that this route is the one he is asked to leave alone.

The sūrah does not ask the Prophet ﷺ to conceal his grief over the answer he was receiving. It asks him to place the timing of that answer outside his own management and to keep working, and the distinction between grief and control runs through the Makkan years of his mission. A messenger is permitted to feel the weight of a refusal; he is not permitted to take over the timetable of its reversal.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:45

> I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Delay Named in the First Person**

The verse continues the previous sentence and speaks in God's own voice. The interval that the deniers experienced as security is described here as a delay granted by the one who is deciding the matter, and the second clause explains what is being done during it. The audience in Makkah had watched years pass without the punishment they mocked, and the verse names the arrangement directly rather than leaving the silence unexplained.

The word rendered *delay* covers more than time passing. It describes an extension granted to a debtor, and the commercial flavour of the term suits a sūrah addressed to traders who understood exactly what an extension meant. An extension is not a cancellation; it is time granted by a creditor who has decided to wait, and the decision belongs to him. Nothing in the arrangement changes the debt or the obligation to settle it.

**The Same Sentence in the Heights**

The Book states almost this same sentence in the sūrah of the Heights, immediately after the statement that those who deny the signs will be drawn on gradually without understanding: *“As for those who deny Our signs, We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend. I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless.”* (7:182–183). The repetition of the wording is a signal. When the Qur'an uses an identical sentence in two places, the second occurrence gives the reader a way to recognise the pattern in the first, and here the repetition establishes the delay as a standing feature of how the Book describes the treatment of denial.

**Mistaking Delay for Acquittal**

The Book addresses the mistake directly and in advance: *“Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment.”* (3:178). The arrangement described there is the one in this passage. The added time is not neutral; it is time in which a record continues to be written, and the person receiving it is the author of what is being added.

The audience in Makkah had a specific reason to make the mistake. Their prosperity was the visible evidence with which they answered the message, and each year of that prosperity strengthened the argument in their own eyes. The Book's answer is that evidence of that kind was never being offered.

**"My Planning Is Flawless"**

The term rendered *planning* is the Arabic *kayd*, a word the Book also uses for the stratagems that human beings devised against the messengers. Its use here is carefully placed. The men who were organising opposition to the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah were matching themselves against a plan made by the one who arranges every cause in the world, and the Book records the same contrast at the moment of the migration: they planned, and God also planned, and God is the best of planners (8:30).

Elsewhere the Book applies the same vocabulary to the plotters of every generation, noting that they made a plan while a plan was being made for them without their knowledge (27:50), and asking the question that closes the subject: could anyone have felt secure against God's planning, when none feels secure except those who are lost (7:99)? The clause about the plan being flawless adds that nothing in it can be blocked, hurried, or redirected by the people it is directed at.

**The Psychology of an Interval**

An interval of quiet produces a particular kind of confidence in people whose conduct is under examination, and the Book describes the mechanism more than once. A man who is not corrected concludes that he is correct, and a community that prospers concludes that its prosperity has been approved. The verse lets its audience see what they had been living inside. From their side the years were evidence; from the side of this sentence the years were an allowance.

The gradual quality of the process is what makes it effective, since nothing about it feels like a punishment while it is happening. The Book's language for the process suggests a gentle movement rather than a violent one, and the very mildness is what allows a man to continue without alarm.

A reader in any century can test the description against his own experience of a delayed consequence. The delay does not usually produce reflection; it produces forgetfulness, and the forgetfulness is what the verse names when it says the process is incomprehensible to the people inside it.

**What the Sūrah Asks of the Believer Watching the Delay**

The instruction given to the Prophet ﷺ in the previous verse remains in force, and this verse supplies the reason it can be obeyed. A messenger who understands that the delay is planned is not required to explain it, to defend it, or to force an ending; the closing verses of the sūrah turn instead to patience and to a comparison with an earlier prophet whose haste cost him years.

The verse therefore does two things at once. It describes the situation of the deniers, whose apparent safety is being administered by the one they were defying, and it relieves the community of any obligation to produce the ending itself. What the believers are asked for is the patience that the sūrah's final section will describe.

That patience has a practical shape in the sūrah's next verses. The Prophet ﷺ is told to wait for his Lord's decree, to avoid the example of the man of the whale, and to remember that he had neither asked his people for a payment nor claimed a share of the record. The instruction is not to be indifferent to the mockery. It is to keep working while the timing remains where it was always kept.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:46

> Or are you asking them for a reward ˹for the message˺ so that they are overburdened by debt?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Turned Back on the Messenger**

Having examined the claims of the audience, the sūrah asks about the messenger's own position. The question is whether he is charging them for the message, in a way that would burden them with an obligation and explain their refusal to accept it. The form is deliberately public. Nobody in Makkah could name a price they had been asked to pay, and the answer to the question was already known in every household that had heard him recite.

**A Debt That Would Have Explained a Refusal**

The image chosen is commercial, and the audience was qualified to weigh it. A message that arrived with an invoice would put its hearers in the position of debtors, and a debt owed to a man would be a plausible reason to resist his claim. The verse removes that explanation before the deniers can use it. The Prophet ﷺ had asked them for nothing, which means the objection was never about a cost.

In a city where patronage ran on reciprocity, the absence of a price was itself unusual, and the sūrah treats it as part of the message.

The question also closes a line of attack that the deniers had been working in the opposite direction. They had accused the Prophet ﷺ of wanting status, of seeking a following, and of aiming at leadership over the city; the verse asks whether he had ever billed them for what he was delivering. The Book does not merely deny the accusation. It restates the accusation in the form of a question whose answer everyone present already knew.

**The Book's Repeated Statements of the Same Point**

The Qur'an clears its messenger of any charge in several sūrahs, which suggests how persistent the accusation was. One passage asks the same question in a different form: *“Or ˹is it because˺ you ˹O Prophet˺ are asking them for tribute? But the reward of your Lord is best, for He is the Best Provider.”* (23:72). Another joins the absence of a charge to the purpose of the revelation: *“even though you are not asking them for a reward for this ˹Quran˺. It is only a reminder to the whole world.”* (12:104).

The identical question appears word for word in another sūrah (52:40), and the Book elsewhere invites its audience to keep whatever reward they imagine was asked of them, since the messenger's reward comes from God alone and He is a witness over everything (34:47). In one passage the Prophet ﷺ is told to state the alternative arrangement plainly: he asks for no reward, and whoever wishes may take a path to his Lord (25:57).

**Why the Answer Matters for the Message**

A teacher paid by his students is accountable to them, and the Book's insistence that its messenger took nothing protects the content of what he delivered. Had he been paid, the message would have been open to the suspicion of tailoring, and his audience would have had a reason to examine which parts of it were designed to please the customers. The reminder arrived unattached to any human interest, and the Book treats that quality as part of its authority.

The one request the Qur'an does attach to the message is not a payment. In one verse the Prophet ﷺ is told to ask for no reward except affection among kin (42:23), and in another the reward he mentions is offered back to the people themselves. The relationship being proposed was never a transaction, and the verse under discussion removes the last suggestion that it was.

The final verse of the sūrah describes the revelation as a reminder for all the worlds, and this verse prepares that description by taking away every trace of a price. A reminder is given freely. A person who is being reminded of something important may resent the reminder, but he cannot claim that the one reminding him is collecting a fee, and the Book keeps its own account of the matter clear in passage after passage.

**What the Merchants of Makkah Would Have Understood**

The trading community of the city understood commission, credit, and the difference between a gift and a contract, and its members had dealings with soothsayers, clients, and allies that ran on reciprocity. When a man arrived among them saying that he expected nothing, the claim was unusual enough that the revelation had to repeat it in several places. The question in this verse asks the merchants to notice that the messenger had not entered into such a relationship with them at all.

**The Question's Connection to the Next**

The following verse asks whether the audience has access to the unseen and copies it down, which returns to the accusation that the Prophet ﷺ was inventing his message. The two questions work as a pair: one clears the messenger of a motive, and the other asks the deniers to account for their own access to the information they claimed. Between them, the sūrah leaves the audience with nothing to say about where the Qur'an's content came from.

The pair also explains why the sūrah's closing verses insist that the revelation is a reminder for all the worlds. A message delivered without a fee and without a claim to the unseen can only be described as a reminder, and a reminder offered to everyone is precisely what the deniers did not want to hear. The question about the reward quietly identifies the real objection: not what the messenger was taking from them, but what the message was asking them to give up.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:47

> Or do they have access to ˹the Record in˺ the unseen, so they copy it ˹for all to see˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Claim to Have Read the Unseen**

The interrogation reaches its highest point. The verse asks whether the audience has access to the hidden record and transcribes from it, which is a claim far beyond anything a reader might have imagined. The commentators explain the question as a challenge to the idea that the deniers' statements about the next world were as good as revelation, since a person who copies from the record in the unseen would have exactly the standing they claimed. The Book describes that record in another sūrah as being preserved in a tablet (85:22).

**The Ladder the Book Refuses Them**

The image of access to the unseen is developed elsewhere with the same bluntness. One passage asks whether the audience owns the treasuries of God or controls anything, and then supplies the only method by which the knowledge they claimed could be obtained: *“Or do they have a stairway, by which they eavesdrop ˹on the heavens˺? Then let those who do so bring a compelling proof.”* (52:38). A listener who wanted information from the sky needed a ladder and a hearing that passed the guards, and the verse invites the claimants to produce their evidence.

The Book repeatedly describes the heavens as guarded against attempts of that kind, and the sūrah of the Pen's own earlier verses belong to the same picture.

**The Unseen Is Not Distributed**

The revelation is consistent about where knowledge of the hidden is kept. God alone possesses the keys of the unseen; the Book assigns to Him the knowledge of the Hour, the rain, the womb, and what a person will earn tomorrow (31:34). Even the messengers receive only what they are given, and the Qur'an's own reports of the next world are announcements made by the one who knows rather than discoveries made by anyone who looked.

The Quran's own reports of the next world are therefore a different kind of statement from a conclusion about the future. A person who describes what he has been shown is reporting; a person who reasons from his circumstances to what will happen to him afterwards is guessing and calling the guess knowledge. The sūrah's question is aimed at the second kind of statement, and it asks where a copy of the record could have been obtained.

The image used against the audience is that of copying. A scribe copies from a text placed in front of him, and the question asks where such a text would be in their case. The sūrah opened with an oath by the pen and a record being written, and the contrast is deliberate. There is a book being written about them, and there is no book being copied from by them; what they had were conclusions, and the verse's final clause about copying for all to see leaves them without a source.

Against that background the question in this verse is not a request for documentation; it is a demonstration of absurdity. The audience had been speaking about the terms of the Hereafter with the confidence of men who had read the file.

**The Illusion of Self-Sufficiency**

The source of that confidence is named by the Book in a very short passage: *“Most certainly, one exceeds all bounds once they think they are selfsufficient.”* (96:6–7). The sentence describes the psychological mechanism behind every claim of the kind being examined here. A person who believes he needs nothing from anyone else will not accept that a category of knowledge exists which he cannot reach, and he will fill the gap with the conclusions that suit him.

The men of Makkah had the wealth and the standing to make that belief comfortable, and the sūrah has already described what was waiting for them.

The image used against them is copying. A scribe copies from a text placed in front of him, and the sūrah's question asks where such a text would be for the audience. The sūrah opened with an oath by the pen and the record being written, and the contrast is deliberate: there is a book being written about them, and there is no book being copied by them. What the audience had were conclusions, and the verse's final clause about copying for all to see leaves them without a source.

**What the Deniers Were Actually Asserting**

The Makkan case against the message had a positive content, not merely a rejection. Its holders were certain that their position would continue, that the men the Prophet ﷺ was gathering would not be vindicated, and that the ancestors whose religion they followed had not been mistaken. Each of those claims concerns the unseen, and each was held with a certainty that only access to the unseen could justify. The verse asks where that access came from.

**A Question That Ends the Examination**

With this verse the sequence of challenges closes. The audience has been asked for a book, a pledge, a guarantor, partner-gods, and now for the record itself, and each question has removed a support. What remains at the end of the list is the position the sūrah has been driving toward since its opening verses: a revelation that is either accepted as it stands or rejected without a substitute.

The examination has also shown the shape of the audience's confidence. It was not built on evidence they could produce, decisions they had made, or authorities they could name; it was built on the comfort of their circumstances and the agreement of their neighbours. A position of that kind survives every argument it cannot answer, which is why the sūrah's questions are followed by a description of a day when the posture of the body will settle what the discussion could not. The closing verses turn from the examination to the comfort of the messenger, and they begin by naming him a reminder for the worlds.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:48

> So be patient with your Lord’s decree, and do not be like ˹Jonah,˺ the Man of the Whale, who cried out ˹to Allah˺, in total distress.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Instruction of Patience**

The sūrah turns to the Prophet ﷺ with a command that summarizes everything the preceding verses have established. The decree of his Lord will arrive; the delay belongs to God; the mockers are being drawn along; the messenger's task is to wait for the outcome without forcing it. The command is followed by a prohibition, and the prohibition is illustrated by the example of an earlier prophet whose impatience took him out of his assignment.

**The Man of the Whale**

The prophet being cited is Jonah, and the Book devotes a passage to his story in the sūrah of those ranged in ranks. He was one of the messengers (37:139), he fled to a loaded ship (37:140), the lot was drawn and he was thrown overboard (37:141), and the whale swallowed him while he was blameworthy (37:142). His survival is attributed to a habit he had maintained: *“Had he not ˹constantly˺ glorified ˹Allah˺, he would have certainly remained in its belly until the Day of Resurrection.”* (37:143–144). He was then cast onto an open shore, worn out, and a plant was made to grow over him (37:145–146).

The comparison is aimed at a specific temptation. Jonah left his people when the response to his message did not come, and the Book records that his departure was a flight rather than an ending of his mission. The man who fled was later described as having stormed off in anger, thinking that he would not be restrained (21:87), and the account leaves no doubt that the impulse behind the journey was impatience rather than obedience. The Book calls his departure a flight, which means it was not presented as the completion of a commission. The correction that followed him was therefore a correction of a messenger's working decision, and the sūrah's readers in Makkah were being shown the difference between waiting for a decree and walking away from one. Muhammad ﷺ was under pressure of the same kind: years of rejection, mockery in the streets, the demand for proofs, and no visible change in the attitudes of the men he was addressing. The verse tells him to stay where he is and let the decree arrive in its own time.

The comparison is not an accusation. The Book places the example of a prophet directly beside the instruction given to the Prophet ﷺ, and the effect is to normalise the strain he was under while ruling out the response it was producing. A man being told not to behave like Jonah has been told that his situation resembles Jonah's, and the resemblance is a kind of company.

The story also illustrates how much of a messenger's work is waiting. Jonah is described in the Book as one of the messengers (37:139), and the account of his mission consists almost entirely of his departure and his recovery, with the response of his city mentioned in a single verse from another sūrah (10:98). The work between the call and the response takes up no narrative at all, which is exactly the part of the assignment that this verse is addressing.

**The Cry in Total Distress**

The adjective describing Jonah's call describes a man in the grip of grief, and the Book records what he said: *“And ˹remember˺ when the Man of the Whale stormed off ˹from his city˺ in a rage, thinking We would not restrain him. Then in the ˹veils of˺ darkness he cried out, “There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except You. Glory be to You! I have certainly done wrong.””* (21:87). The prayer combines the confession of God's uniqueness with an admission of his own fault, and the next verse reports the answer: *“So We answered his prayer and rescued him from anguish. And so do We save the ˹true˺ believers.”* (21:88).

The Prophet ﷺ is being shown a messenger who had to be rescued, and the sūrah's prohibition is a warning against putting himself in the position of needing such a rescue.

**The Prophet's Own Constraint**

The Book supplies the same instruction in other places with different reasons attached. One verse tells him that his patience is only possible with God's help and that he should not grieve over the disbelievers or be distressed by their schemes (16:127). Another reminds him of the pattern of earlier messengers, who were rejected and persecuted and endured until help arrived, with the assurance that God's promise is never broken (6:34). The command in this verse belongs to that family of instructions, and it comes at the end of a sūrah whose whole argument has been that the timing was never his to manage.

**Patience and Withdrawal: Where They Diverge**

Patience in the Book is not the same as leaving the field. Jonah's error was a departure, and the correction given to Muhammad ﷺ is the opposite movement: he is told to remain and to keep reciting. The verses that follow describe the mockery he was facing and the charge of madness, and the sūrah's answer to both is the message itself. The messenger stays; the decree arrives when it arrives; the men who were mocking him are described in their own terms on the final page of the sūrah.

The command in this verse is therefore continuous with the message itself. The Prophet ﷺ is not asked to become indifferent to his people or to stop caring what happens to them; he is asked to continue delivering the reminder while their response remains their own decision. That is the arrangement the sūrah has been describing from its opening lines, and the closing verses give it a name.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:49

> Had he not been shown grace by his Lord, he would have certainly been cast onto the open ˹shore˺, still blameworthy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Grace That Made the Difference**

The verse describes what would have happened to Jonah and attributes the difference to grace. The construction is a counterfactual, and the Book uses it to separate two things that look alike: the mercy that rescued him from the sea and the grace that changed his standing. Being brought out of the fish alive would not by itself have settled his case. The verse says that without the favour of his Lord he would have been thrown onto the shore in the same condition in which he entered the water.

**"Still Blameworthy"**

The description is of a man whose error remained attached to him. The Book had already used a related term for his arrival in the whale's belly while he was blameworthy (37:142), and this verse carries the word forward to the shore. The fault being referred to is the departure from his people and the flight before the decree had arrived, which is precisely the action the sūrah forbids to the Prophet ﷺ in the preceding verse.

The Book does not soften the diagnosis even though it is speaking about a prophet. A messenger whose conduct is recorded by revelation is a messenger whose mistakes are also recorded, and Jonah's story is told with the fault in the foreground.

**The Shore and the Plant**

What actually happened to him is described with attention to his condition after the rescue. He was cast onto an open shore, worn out, and then a plant was made to grow over him (37:145–146). The Book's care for the mechanism of recovery is characteristic. The man was not merely extracted from the sea; he was given shade and a place to recover, and the mercy in the passage is physical as much as legal.

The sūrah of the Prophets reports the response to his prayer in the same spirit: his call was answered, he was rescued from anguish, and the Book adds that this is how the believers are saved (21:88).

The Book's refusal to leave the man without provision after the rescue is worth noticing in a passage about patience. His impatience had taken him away from his assignment, and the correction began with extraction from the water and continued with shade, food, and time to recover. Nothing in the account suggests that he restored himself; the only act attributed to him in the darkness was the admission of his fault, and everything after that was given.

A reader can follow the same sequence as a description of how the Book treats repentance generally. The person acknowledges the wrong, is taken out of the consequence, and is given a place to stand afterwards. The sūrah under discussion presents the same movement in the story of the garden's owners, who lost everything and then confessed, and it presents it here in the life of a prophet who lost his position among his people and received a new one from his Lord.

The passage shows a pattern that appears throughout the Book. Rescue is the first stage, and rehabilitation is the second. A person is brought out of the consequence of his conduct, and then work is done on him so that he is fit for what comes next. Joseph was brought out of the well and the prison before he was given authority; Moses was brought out of the river before he was given the mission; Jonah was brought out of the whale and then shaded and fed.

The verse's counterfactual marks the difference between the two stages.

It also fixes the limit of what a rescue can accomplish by itself. A person may be brought out of a bad situation and remain in the condition that produced it, and the sentence says exactly that about a prophet: without the favour of his Lord, the man on the shore would have been the same man who went over the side. The distinction between surviving a consequence and being changed by grace is the lesson the passage is teaching, and it explains why the next verse can speak about selection. Extraction from the sea was already accomplished by the time the shore appeared, but the man on the shore was still blameworthy, and grace is what dealt with that.

The Book's teaching on this point is consistent across its narratives: a fault that is repented and forgiven is removed, and the person is treated afterward as though the fault had not been charged to him. The verses about Jonah move from the admission of wrongdoing in the darkness (21:87) through rescue to a fresh commission. Nothing in the sequence requires that the consequences of his flight be erased from the record of his life; what it requires is the standing that makes his later work possible, and that standing is described in the next verse.

**Why the Story Is Told Here**

The Prophet ﷺ is being shown the case of a messenger who had to be rescued from a situation created by his own impatience, and the moral of the comparison is contained in its outcome. He was rescued, and the community of his own city was later given a reprieve that no other community received: they believed, and the punishment of disgrace was lifted from them in this world before it fell (10:98). The story's ending is therefore a reason for patience rather than a warning against hope, and it shows the messenger under pressure in Makkah what time can do when it is left alone.

The verse does not describe the grace itself, and the sūrah gives no account of how God dealt with the man's fault in detail. What the audience receives is the fact that the difference existed and that it determined everything. A reader is left to infer the condition of his own case from the same information, and the Book's silence about the mechanism keeps the attention where it belongs, on the one who grants the favour rather than on the process by which it arrives.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:50

> Then his Lord chose him, making him one of the righteous.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Election After Failure**

The verse completes the story with an act of selection. The man who had fled, been swallowed, and been rescued was then chosen by his Lord and placed among the righteous, and the sūrah's order is deliberate. Election comes after the episode, not before it, and the sequence is what the Book presents to a messenger under pressure in Makkah. A fault that has been dealt with does not close a man's file. It becomes the setting of the next appointment.

The Arabic verb rendered *chose him* is the one the Book uses for God's selection of individuals for a task, and it appears in connection with a small number of prophets. Being chosen in this vocabulary is not a reward for merit already demonstrated; it is an assignment granted by the one entitled to grant it. The same word stands behind the selection of the prophets generally, and its use here in the aftermath of Jonah's mistake is the point of the verse.

A second observation belongs with it. The choosing is attributed to his Lord, and the possessive construction is the sūrah's way of keeping the relationship in view. The man's fate after the whale is not described as the outcome of his recovery programme or of the prayers he had offered in the darkness; it is described as a decision made by the one he had disobeyed. The story's last movement is therefore an act of grace rather than a reward, and the sūrah has been preparing its audience for that distinction from its opening verses.

The mercy that balances the warnings is also the reason the verse can end the story where it does. A revelation that threatened without offering rescue would misrepresent the One who sent it, and the sūrah's final pages are carefully arranged so that the listener hears the warning and the way out in the same sitting.

**"One of the Righteous"**

The commentators explain the phrase as meaning that he was placed among the prophets, and the phrase's simplicity carries a considerable claim. The man is not described by his rank, his miracle, or the size of his following. He is described by a moral category, and the audience is left with the fact that a prophet who had made the mistake recorded in the earlier verses is nevertheless placed in the ranks of the righteous by the decision of his Lord.

Jonah's story is not the only one in which the Book records a prophet's error and then describes his restoration. Adam received words from his Lord and was forgiven; Moses asked for forgiveness after his anger in the camp, and was told that the tables would be restored; David was told that his fault was forgiven and that he held a place of nearness. The Book reports these episodes because their audience needs them, and it reports the forgiveness because the same audience needs that too.

What the Book never does in these passages is deny that the error occurred. The record is stated plainly, and the repair is stated in the same chapter.

The purpose of such reporting is stated by the Book itself in the middle of one of those narratives, where the reader is told that the accounts of the messengers are related in order to strengthen the heart of the one receiving them. The stories function as evidence about the way God deals with people, and the evidence includes failures and their correction. A community under pressure learns from them that a setback in the middle of a mission is not the end of the mission.

The same reasoning governs the use made of these stories in the sūrah under discussion. Its audience was watching a small community in a hostile city, and the example of Jonah told them that a messenger who had to be rescued from the sea was later chosen and placed among the righteous by the same Lord who had sent him.

**What the Sequence Offers the Prophet in Makkah**

The sūrah's closing section is addressed to a man who had been mocked for years in front of his own city, and the example placed before him is a prophet who left his post and was still chosen afterwards. The lesson is not that leaving is acceptable. The lesson is that the one who called him to the work is not limited by a setback and that the end of a mission is not determined by the middle of it.

**The Mercy That Balances the Warnings**

The sūrah contains a great deal of warning: a destroyed garden, a courtroom of unanswerable questions, the baring of the shin, and a punishment described as far worse than anything in this world. The verses about Jonah give the passage its other half. The same Book that describes the seizure of the mockers also describes the recovery of a prophet who ran away, and both halves belong together, because a revelation that threatened without offering rescue would misrepresent the One who sent it.

The story of the garden was about people who lost everything by refusing to share; the story of Jonah is about a man who lost his position and got it back by grace. Between them the chapter sets out the two outcomes facing every audience, and the verses that follow address the last of the Prophet's ﷺ difficulties directly. The mockers are looking at him; the reminder he is reciting is what he is; and the sūrah ends by saying so.

The Book's habit of ending a difficult passage with a story of recovery is worth noticing as a pattern. The sūrah of the Pen has been severe: a garden destroyed, an interrogation that strips its victims of every support, a day when the body will not obey, and a punishment described as far worse than anything in this world. Its last section hands the reader a prophet who failed, was rescued, and was chosen. Both halves belong to the same message, and the closing verse describes the whole thing as a reminder for everyone who hears it.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:51

> The disbelievers would almost cut you down with their eyes when they hear ˹you recite˺ the Reminder, and say, “He is certainly a madman.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Gaze That Almost Strikes**

The verse describes hostility that has become physical without becoming violent. The eyes of the disbelievers, fixed on the Prophet ﷺ as he recites, are said to come close to cutting him down, and the image treats a look as an instrument capable of damage. The context is a public recitation: the message being recited is the reminder, and the men listening are not indifferent to it. Their reaction is intense enough that the Book describes it in the language of an attack.

The Book treats looking as an act with moral weight of its own. Believers are commanded to *“lower their gaze and guard their chastity”* (24:30), an instruction that assumes a person answers for what he fixes his eyes on. When the same Book describes the mockers' eyes almost cutting the reciter down, it is reporting a hostility that has already begun in the will and is only waiting for the power to complete itself.

**The Report About a Man Who Could Harm With a Look**

The exegetical tradition connects this verse with accounts of the evil eye, including a report about a man among the Arabs who would abstain from food for days and then point his influence at a passing herd, after which some of the animals would fall dead. According to the report, the disbelievers asked this man to turn his eye on the Messenger of God, and the verse was revealed as a protection for him.

Whether or not the report's chain is accepted by a given reader, the interpretation reflects how the earliest community understood the verse. The passage is not describing ordinary dislike; it is describing hostility that was believed to carry a force of its own.

**The Evil Eye in the Prophet's Teaching**

The Book's answer to the danger is the reminder itself, and the reported teaching of the Prophet ﷺ on this subject is consistent with the verse. He is reported to have instructed his companions to seek refuge with God, saying that the eye is real, and to have told them that when one of them sees something in his brother, or in himself, or in his property that he admires, he should mention God, since the eye is real. The practice of Muslims to this day connects the final two sūrahs of the Qur'an — known as the two protectors — with protection from harms of this kind.

The instruction in those reports is directed at the person who might be harmed and at the person who might cause harm. The one who looks at something he admires is told to mention God, which converts admiration into an acknowledgement of the giver and removes the sense of possession that envy feeds on. The one who fears an eye is told to seek refuge with God, which places the remedy where the sūrah places every other outcome.

**Why the Mockery Is Described as Bodily**

The Book frequently renders spiritual opposition as something physical, and this verse is one of its clearest examples. The same hostility that produced arguments about documents and oaths appears here as a stare that could almost kill. Naming the desire in bodily terms is a form of disclosure: the men who had been presenting themselves as rational critics of a claim are shown as people who wanted the claimant gone.

**The Charge of Madness Returns**

The verse reports the accusation that had been made against the Prophet ﷺ from the earliest days in Makkah, and the sūrah's opening had already answered it: the Prophet ﷺ is addressed at the start of this chapter as not being, by the grace of his Lord, a madman, and is described as having an outstanding character. The charge appears at the beginning and at the end of the sūrah, which is how the Book frames a smear it intends to leave dismantled.

The charge of madness was a way of describing a man's inner state, and the Book answers it by describing his outward conduct, just as the sūrah opened by defending his character. A community that had exhausted its arguments could still try to disqualify the speaker, and the reply is held back until the end of the chapter: the man they were calling mad was reciting a reminder addressed to them and to everyone else. Dismissal of that kind has followed warnings in every generation, and the Book records this instance so that its readers can recognise the move when they meet it.

**What the Verse Confirms About the Recitation**

The setting of the verse is a recitation being heard, and the reaction of the audience is evidence about the text they were hearing. Men do not respond with that intensity to a story they consider trivial, and the reports of the period describe crowds gathering to listen even while they mocked. The Book's description of their faces records both facts at once: they listened, and they hated what they heard.

There is also a practical setting behind the sentence. Recitation in Makkah took place in public, at the sanctuary and in the streets, and the men who gathered were the same men whose objections the sūrah has been answering. The verse captures the moment when a hostile audience hears words it cannot refute and responds with a look rather than an argument, and the Book's decision to report that moment has given later readers a picture of what the message faced at the point where argument had run out.

The accusation of madness belonged to the same repertoire. A man whose words could not be answered could still be dismissed, and the sūrah's opening had already dealt with the charge by describing the character of the man being accused.

**Where the Messenger Stands in the Passage**

The Prophet ﷺ is told none of this in order to alarm him. The sentence stands in a sūrah that has just instructed him to be patient, and its function is to name the difficulty accurately. What he faced was not a debate he was losing but a public hostility that had already reached the stage of wishing him harm. The sūrah's last verse answers the charge by describing what he was actually bringing.

## Sūrah al-Qalam 68:52

> But it is simply a reminder to the whole world.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Last Word of the Sūrah**

The chapter that began with an oath by the pen and a defence of the Prophet's ﷺ character ends with a description of the message he was reciting. The verse answers the accusation of madness by naming what the thing in his mouth actually is. A madman produces words that concern only himself; a reminder is addressed to everyone who hears it, and the verse states the difference in a single clause.

The Book describes its own message as a reminder in passage after passage, and the word carries a specific claim. A reminder assumes that its hearer already knows something he has stopped attending to: the existence of his Lord, the accountability of his conduct, the reality of judgement. The revelation is not presented as an invention of new information but as the restoration of knowledge that human beings carry and neglect, and the sūrah of the Pen has been acting on that assumption throughout, appealing to what its audience already admitted about creation, provision, and their own faculties.

**For the Whole World**

The reminder is not addressed to one clan or one language, and the Book states the universality of the mission in several places. The Prophet ﷺ is described as having been sent as a mercy for the whole world (21:107), and the same expression used here appears in the sūrah of Joseph, where the message is described as a reminder to the whole world in the middle of an argument about its messenger's honesty (12:104). The Book's own account of its audience has never been tribal, even when its first hearers were members of a tribe.

The universality claimed here is not a general statement about the worth of human beings; it is a statement about the scope of the warning. Every community that receives the message is being told what it needs to know about its own condition, and the sūrah has addressed its audience on that basis throughout, moving from the Makkans' own admissions about creation to arguments they could follow about their own trade. The reminder operates in the language and reasoning of the people it reaches, which is why the Book describes its messengers as speaking to their peoples in their own terms.

An audience that hears a warning in its own language has no excuse available on the grounds of comprehension, and the sūrah's arguments have been conducted entirely within the vocabulary of Makkan life: trade, harvests, oaths, ancestry, and the standing of a man in his clan. What the message asked of them was not information they could not process. It was a decision about who owned what they had.

A second reading of the verse, reported by the commentators, takes the reminder to be the person of the Prophet ﷺ himself rather than the text he recites. The two readings agree on the substance: either the recitation is a warning offered to all peoples, or the man delivering it is, and the audience's mockery is directed at a benefit intended for them.

The chapter's contents make the closing description of the message precise. It has reminded its hearers that a record is being written and that a day of reckoning is coming; that the man delivering the message is of outstanding character; that a garden's owners lost everything by shutting the poor out; that a series of claims about the Hereafter had no document behind them; that the wicked will be unable to prostrate; and that a prophet who ran away was rescued and restored. Each of those is a reminder in the strict sense of the word: something known, neglected, and brought back into view.

The commentators note a second possible reading of the verse, according to which the reminder being described is not the message but the man who delivers it, since the Prophet ﷺ is himself called a reminder in other passages. Both readings agree on the substance. Either the recitation is a reminder for all the worlds, or the messenger is one, and the sūrah's audience is left with the same fact: what they were mocking was a benefit offered to them and to everyone else.

The contents of the chapter make the closing description exact. A record is being written; a judgement is coming; the man delivering the message is of outstanding character; a garden's owners lost everything by shutting the poor out; the audience's claims about the Hereafter had no document behind them; the wicked will be unable to prostrate; and a prophet who ran away was rescued and restored. A reminder in the strict sense is exactly what those items amount to: things their hearers already knew, had stopped attending to, and were being brought back to face. That is also the reason the sūrah can close without a threat in its final sentence, since the offer it makes is already the whole content of the message.

**The Contrast With the Charge of Madness**

The final clause is placed directly against the mockery in the previous verse, and the opposition is deliberate. The men who said that the reciter was mad were describing a man who was reminding them of what they already knew about their own creation, their own sustenance, and their own death. The sūrah lets the two descriptions stand side by side and leaves the reader to decide which account of the recitation is accurate.

**Why the Chapter Ends Here**

The ending returns to the audience rather than to the messenger. After the warnings, the interrogation, the description of the bared shin, and the story of Jonah, the sūrah's last sentence is an offer stated without conditions. The reminder is for the whole world, which includes the men who had wanted to cut the reciter down with their eyes. The chapter closes with the invitation still open, and that is the note on which the Book leaves the people it has spent fifty verses rebuking.

The sentence is not confined to this closing position. It stands in Sūrah Ṣād as the answer to the arguments about the messenger (38:87), and it appears in al-Takwīr alongside the description of the revelation's delivery (81:27). In each placement the same words settle the same suspicion about who the Qurʾān is for, and here they are the last thing the mockers hear before the sūrah ends.
