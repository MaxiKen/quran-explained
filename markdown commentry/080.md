# Sūrah ʿAbasa (Chapter 80) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Blind Seeker and the Chieftains of Quraysh**

The historical occasion of Sūrah ʿAbasa is documented in authentic traditions in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī (3331) and classical exegesis by ʿĀ'ishah and Ibn ʿAbbās. The Prophet ﷺ was deeply engaged in an intense, high-stakes dialogue with the top leaders of the Qurayshi aristocracy—including ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah, Shaybah, Abū Jahl, and Umayyah ibn Khalaf—desperately striving to convince them of Islam, knowing that their conversion would protect the vulnerable Muslims from persecution and transform Arabian society. In the midst of this delicate conversation, a poor, blind early Muslim named ʿAbdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm stumbled into the gathering. Unaware of who the Prophet was addressing, the blind man called out eagerly: *“O Messenger of Allah, teach me from that which Allah has taught you!”* Annoyed at the interruption and fearing the aristocratic chieftains would be disgusted by the presence of a blind beggar, the Prophet's brow furrowed in a brief, silent frown (*ʿabasa*) and he turned his shoulder away to continue his appeal to the elites. Revelation descended instantly from above the heavens: *“He frowned and turned away, because there came to him the blind man! But what would make you perceive, [O Muḥammad], that perhaps he might be purified, or be reminded and the reminder benefit him? As for he who considers himself free of need, to him you give attention—and not upon you is any blame if he does not purify himself! But as for he who came to you striving, while he fears [Allah], from him you are distracted! No! Indeed, these verses are a reminder!”* (80:1–11). The Prophet ﷺ internalized this divine correction so deeply that for the rest of his life, whenever he saw Ibn Umm Maktūm, he would spread his cloak on the ground for him, embrace him warmly, and say: *“Welcome to the one on whose account my Lord rebuked me!”*—and twice appointed the blind man as the acting governor of Madinah during military campaigns.

**The Structural Architecture and the Deafening Blast**

The architecture of Sūrah ʿAbasa unfolds across three distinct movements:

1. The Divine Reprimand and the Sacred Scrolls (1–16): Corrects the Prophet's priorities, establishes that divine revelation is recorded on honored, exalted, purified sheets (*ṣuḥufin mukarramah, marfūʿatin muṭahharah*), carried by the hands of noble, virtuous angelic scribes (*safarah, kirāmin bararah*).

2. The Ingratitude of Man and the Agricultural Canopy (17–32): Issues an anguished lament over human spiritual blindness: *“Destroyed is man; how ungrateful is he! From what thing did He create him? From a drop of semen He created him and proportioned him; then He made the path easy for him; then He causes him to die and provides a grave for him; then when He wills, He will resurrect him. No! Man has not yet accomplished what He commanded him!”* (80:17–23). The Deafening Blast and the Severing of All Ties (33–42): Stages the arrival of the *Deafening Blast* (*al-Ṣākhkhah*): the Day when every social and biological bond of human kinship is violently severed: - *“The Day a man will flee from his brother,”* - *“And from his mother and his father,”* - *“And from his wife and his children!”* Verse 37 reveals the terrifying psychological reason for this abandonment: *“For every person among them, that Day, will be an affair sufficient to preoccupy him (*li-kulli imri'in minhum yawma'idhin sha'nun yughnīh*)!”* The chapter concludes by portraying the two eternal faces of humanity: radiant, laughing, joyful faces illuminated by divine grace (*wujūhun yawma'idhin musfirah, ḍāḥikatun mustabshirah*); and faces covered in dust, overtaken by black gloom (*tarhaquhā qatarah*)—the wicked, unrepentant deniers.

It is universally famous for its dramatic, intimate opening: *“He frowned and turned away, because there came to him the blind man (*An jā'ahu al-aʿmā*)!”* (80:1–2).

## Verse 80:1

> He frowned and turned ˹his attention˺ away

**The Report Behind the Opening Verb**

ʿĀʾishah's account supplies the scene, and Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī carries it at 3331, graded ṣaḥīḥ in the Dār al-Salām edition. ʿAbdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm, the blind man, came to the Messenger of God saying, "O Messenger of God, guide me," while a prominent man of the idolaters sat with him. The Prophet turned toward that man and asked whether he saw anything objectionable in what was being said to him, and the answer was no. Then these verses came down. Commentators differ about which notable occupied the room — ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah, Umayyah ibn Khalaf, Abū Jahl and al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah are each named in the reports — and the difference does not touch the meaning. What matters is the asymmetry. One man had come asking, and could not see the expression on the face turned away from him. The other had come asking for nothing, and could see all of it.

**The Standing Rule for Whoever Teaches**

This verse belongs to a cluster that regulates the Prophet's attention. *“˹O Prophet!˺ Do not dismiss those ˹poor believers˺ who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure”* (6:52) is a prohibition pointing the same way. *“And patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. Do not let your eyes look beyond them, desiring the luxuries of this worldly life”* (18:28) names the motive to be resisted. *“You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills”* (28:56) removes the calculation that makes the preference look reasonable. Together these passages and the opening verse fix one principle: the worth of a listener in the economy of teaching is not his social weight. Delivery is what the teacher owes, and delivery belongs first to whoever asks.

## Verse 80:2

> ˹simply˺ because the blind man came to him ˹interrupting˺

**Identified by His Blindness Rather Than by His Name**

The Qur'an calls him *al-aʿmā*, the blind man, and never gives his name in this sūrah, though the reports preserve it as ʿAbdullāh ibn Umm Maktūm. The choice is meaningful, and it runs against the practice elsewhere in the Book, where an individual is sometimes named — Zayd in the passage on adoption (33:37) is the one Companion mentioned by name in the Qur'an. Naming fixes an event to a biography. Describing by condition generalizes it. Any teacher in any century has met this man: the one whose need is real, whose appearance is inconvenient, and whose arrival cannot be scheduled.

**The Blindness That Matters Is the Other Kind**

Makkan revelation keeps drawing a line between two blindnesses. The one that disqualifies a person from nothing is physical; the one that disqualifies a person from everything is perceptual. *“Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind”* (22:46) states the contrast in its clearest form, and it belongs to the same Makkan idiom as this sūrah. The man standing in front of the Prophet had functioning eyes removed from his use and a heart fully at work. The men seated with the Prophet had functioning eyes and, on the reports of their own conduct, hearts that were shutting.

**An Act Judged With No Human Witness**

The blind man did not know he had been frowned at. He could not see it, and no one in the room appears to have told him; the reports record no confrontation, no apology delivered to him on the spot, no complaint from him afterwards. The act was, in every human sense, undetected. The verse still places it under judgement, and the judgement arrives in a book that will be recited by millions who never knew his name. That is the point of the scene's invisibility: the moral quality of an act does not depend on the recipient's awareness of it. The same principle is stated elsewhere in explicit terms — *“Or do they think that We do not hear their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks? Yes ˹We do˺! And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording ˹it all˺”* (43:80). Here the recording is not of words but of a face, which is a harder case, since a face is not chosen in the way a sentence is.

## Verse 80:3

> You never know ˹O Prophet˺, perhaps he may be purified

**Purification Is Growth Before It Is Cleaning**

The word is from *zakāh*, and the root carries two senses that English splits apart: to be pure and to grow. A crop that *yazkū* increases; a person who is *zakī* is clean of defect. The Qur'an uses the doubled form here, *yazzakkā*, which makes the action reflexive and intensive — he purifies himself, he becomes purified in himself. Purification in this sense is not the removal of a stain so much as the development of a capacity. That is why the verse attaches it to teaching rather than to ritual: what the blind man might receive is not a washing but a growth, and the growth is his own even though the seed is someone else's words. The same word family carries the obligatory alms, which is called *zakāh* because it purifies the wealth and increases it at once.

**The Same Formula in Other Passages**

*Mā yudrīka* and its close relative *mā adrāka* form a recognizable Qur'anic device for marking the limit of human knowledge. *“It is Allah Who has revealed the Book with the truth and the balance ˹of justice˺. You never know, perhaps the Hour is near”* (42:17) uses the identical construction to place the Hour outside anyone's calculation. The interrogative form appears in the descriptions of the last things: *“And what will make you realize what the Night of Glory is?”* (97:2), and *“And what will make you realize what the Striking Disaster is?”* (101:3). In each case the question is rhetorical and the answer that follows is not an explanation but an intensification — the thing is greater than knowing. The device does the same work here, on a much smaller scale and about a much smaller event. A blind man's soul is placed in the category of things whose outcome no observer can compute, which is an extraordinary elevation of an ordinary person, achieved completely by grammar.

**Why the Hope Is Attached to This Man**

The verses that follow show what purification is worth: *“Successful indeed is the one who purifies their soul”* (91:9), and *“Successful indeed are those who purify themselves”* (87:14), both Makkan and both using the same root in the same reflexive sense. Success is defined by the act of self-purification, not by social position, wealth, or the approval of notables. Against that definition the calculation in the room collapses. The Prophet was weighing a blind beggar against a chief of Quraysh, and the Qur'an's own vocabulary weighs only purification. The man with the highest value in the scene was the one being overlooked, and the value was invisible to every method of assessment available in Mecca. This is not a sentimental point about the poor. It is a claim about the units in which the Qur'an measures persons, and the units are internal: what a person becomes, not what a person commands.

**What the Teacher Can and Cannot Produce**

The possibility in this verse belongs to the seeker, and the delivery belongs to the teacher. The Qur'an keeps the two roles strictly separate. *“Invite ˹all˺ to the Way of your Lord with wisdom and kind advice, and only debate with them in the best manner. Surely your Lord ˹alone˺ knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided”* (16:125) assigns the method to the human being and the knowledge of outcome to God. *“You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills”* (28:56) removes the power to produce the result. Neither verse permits the teacher to stop teaching; both forbid him from managing the results by selecting his audience according to likely yield. That is exactly the error of this scene. The teacher began to allocate attention on the basis of predicted outcomes, and the predicted outcomes are not his to predict. The verse restores the proper order by naming purification as a possibility that no one controls, including the one who delivers the words.

## Verse 80:4

> or he may be mindful, benefitting from the reminder

**Remembrance as Recovery, Not Information**

The verb is *yadhdhakkar*, from *dh-k-r*, a doubled reflexive form of "to remember." The Qur'an's consistent usage treats the reminder as recovering something the hearer already has rather than depositing something new. A *dhikr* is not a lecture; it is a call back. This is why the Qur'an names itself a Reminder so often — *“This ˹Book˺ is only a Reminder and a clear Quran”* (36:69) — and why the act it asks of its hearers is *tadhakkur*, calling to mind. The theological ground is the covenant and the innate disposition: human beings already know that they did not make themselves, and revelation restores a knowledge that has been covered over rather than supplying a fact never held. On this model the blind man's benefit is not the acquisition of content he lacked. He had accepted Islam already, according to the reports. What he stood to gain was the deepening of a possession, and that is precisely the kind of gain a busy teacher dismisses as unimportant, because it produces no visible result and converts no influential person.

**A Reminder Only Works on the Living**

The Qur'an is candid about the limits of its own address. It was sent down *“to warn whoever is ˹truly˺ alive and fulfil the decree ˹of torment˺ against the disbelievers”* (36:70), and the condition of being alive is a condition of the heart. The reminder profits the one who receives it as a reminder; against a sealed heart it produces nothing, and the text says so without embarrassment. *“But ˹continue to˺ remind. For certainly reminders benefit the believers”* (51:55) ties benefit to belief as its precondition. This is the reason the scene is arranged as it is. The blind man was alive in the required sense: he had believed, he had come, he had asked. The chief, by contrast, had not been moved to ask anything. The available probability of benefit was overwhelmingly on the side of the man who was ignored, and the calculation that ran the other way was based on social importance rather than on receptivity. The verse exposes the substitution without naming it.

## Verse 80:5

> As for the one who was indifferent

**A Word That Names a Posture, Not a Balance**

*Man istaghnā* is built on *gh-n-y*, the root of sufficiency and wealth, and the tenth form used here means to consider oneself enough, to deem oneself beyond need. The translation renders it as indifference, which captures the effect rather than the letter. The literal sense is closer to "the one who has made himself self-sufficient" in his own estimation, and the estimation is the point: the form is reflexive, so the sufficiency is claimed by the subject rather than granted by his circumstances. A rich man who knows his dependence is not *istaghnā*; a poor man who believes he needs nothing from God is.

**The Same Word in the Clot**

The Makkan sūrahs return to this root with a regularity that marks it as a diagnostic term. *“Most certainly, one exceeds all bounds once they think they are selfsufficient”* (96:6–7) states the causal chain in its barest form: the sense of sufficiency produces transgression. The verb there, *yaṭghā*, is the same one used of Pharaoh and of the nations destroyed for their overreach. Sufficiency, in this idiom, is not a state of comfort but a state of moral exposure, because the person who believes he needs nothing has removed the only ground on which a command could reach him. That is why the notable in this scene is not described as hostile. Hostility is a response; it presupposes that something is being asked of you. The condition of *istaghnā* is quieter and worse: nothing is being asked, because the person has already answered the question in his own favour.

**Wealth in the Vocabulary of the Makkan Sūrahs**

The short Makkan chapters circle this figure constantly, which suggests the social pressure the early community was under. *“Woe to every backbiter, slanderer, who amasses wealth ˹greedily˺ and counts it ˹repeatedly˺, thinking that their wealth will make them immortal!”* (104:1–3) connects the accumulation of property to a belief in permanence. *“Competition for more ˹gains˺ diverts you ˹from Allah˺, until you end up in ˹your˺ graves”* (102:1–2) describes the same sufficiency as a distraction that ends only at death. In both cases the diagnosis is internal: the problem is not the wealth but the sense of completeness it produces. That is the condition named in this verse, and it explains why the man is not accused of any act. He did nothing to the blind man. He simply existed in a state that made the blind man easy to overlook, and the sūrah treats the state as the real subject. Indifference is not a neutral background; it is an achievement, built by counting.

## Verse 80:6

> you gave him your ˹undivided˺ attention

**A Verb of Courtship**

*Taṣaddā* comes from the root *ṣ-d-w*, whose basic sense is to incline toward, to attend, to turn one's face and interest in a direction. The fifth form used here intensifies and prolongs the movement: not a glance but a sustained orientation, the kind of attention one gives when one is trying to win someone. The translation's "undivided" is a good rendering for exactly that reason, since the verb implies a turning of the whole person rather than a partial courtesy. In classical usage the same root is employed for one who courts favour or seeks the goodwill of a superior. The word choice therefore carries a social charge that a neutral verb would lack. This was not the Prophet noticing an important man in the room. It was the Prophet directing himself toward him, and the direction had a purpose: to hold his interest, to keep him engaged, to bring him closer to the message. The verb names the effort, and the effort is what the verse examines.

**Attention Is the Currency of Teaching**

Everything a teacher has is attention, and the Qur'an regulates its distribution in explicit terms. *“And patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. Do not let your eyes look beyond them, desiring the luxuries of this worldly life”* (18:28) forbids the eyes from travelling past the sincere toward the splendid, and it names the desire that causes the travel. *“˹O Prophet!˺ Do not dismiss those ˹poor believers˺ who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure”* (6:52) makes the same prohibition with the added weight of a warning: dismiss them and you join the wrongdoers. Both passages address the same situation this verse addresses — the pressure to prefer the influential — and both treat the preference as a matter of the eyes and the face rather than of policy. Attention is not an administrative resource that can be allocated by expected return. It is the substance of the teaching itself, and when it goes to the wrong person the teaching has already been misdelivered, however accurate the words.

## Verse 80:7

> even though you are not to blame if he would not be purified

**A Release Placed Inside a Reproach**

The verse does something unusual: it withdraws the very ground on which the reproach seemed to rest. You attended to the notable — and you are not answerable if he does not purify himself. At first reading the two halves pull against each other. If the outcome was never in the Prophet's hands, why is the attention criticized? The answer is that the release and the reproach address different things. The reproach concerns the displacement of the seeker. The release concerns the burden of results. Placing them side by side removes the excuse that makes the displacement look reasonable, since the excuse was always an appeal to consequences: I am doing this for the good that may come of it. The verse says the good that may come of it was never yours to produce, so the appeal fails. What remains is the conduct itself, judged on its own terms, with no consequentialist defence available. Very few moral systems are willing to strip a decision of its outcomes and then judge what is left.

**Delivery, Not Results**

The principle is stated elsewhere in plain terms. *“The Messenger's duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah ˹fully˺ knows what you reveal and what you conceal”* (5:99) fixes the boundary of the office. *“But if they turn away, then your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants”* (3:20) repeats it in a context of argument with the People of the Scripture. The Makkan formulation is blunter still: *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺”* (88:21–22). Across these passages the office is defined by an act the messenger can complete — delivery — and not by an effect he cannot. The definition is protective in both directions. It prevents despair when the message is refused, and it prevents manipulation when the message is received, because in neither case is the messenger the author of the outcome. This verse applies the same definition to a single afternoon: you are not to blame if he does not purify himself.

## Verse 80:8

> But as for the one who came to you, eager ˹to learn˺

**Striving Added to Arrival**

The blind man's coming was described in verse 2 with a single verb: he came. Here the same event is described with two: *jāʾaka yasʿā* — he came to you striving. *Saʿā* means to move with effort, to hasten, to work at a purpose, and it is used elsewhere in the Qur'an for the exertion that qualifies an act as worship. The addition changes the character of the arrival. In verse 2 the coming functions as the cause of the frown; here it functions as a claim. A man who comes has interrupted. A man who comes striving has made an effort that the teacher did not make for him, and the effort establishes a priority.

**Striving in the Qur'an's Moral Ledger**

*Saʿy* is one of the Qur'an's central terms for human action, and its range is instructive. *“But whoever desires the Hereafter and strives for it accordingly, and is a ˹true˺ believer, it is they whose striving will be appreciated”* (17:19) makes striving the content of a life and promises that it will be recognized. *“and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards, and that ˹the outcome of˺ their efforts will be seen ˹in their record˺”* (53:39–40) ties reward to effort rather than to result. In both cases the weight falls on the movement and not on the achievement, which is exactly the logic of this scene. The blind man could not produce an outcome; he could only come, and come with effort. On the Qur'an's own accounting that is a substantial thing, and it is the thing that was overlooked. A community that measures its members by outcomes will always undervalue the ones who can only strive, and the sūrah is correcting that measurement at its point of origin.

## Verse 80:9

> being in awe ˹of Allah˺

**A Continuing State Rather Than a Passing Fright**

The Arabic is *wa-huwa yakhshā* — and he is in awe. Two features of the construction matter. The sentence is nominal, with the pronoun *huwa* stated explicitly before the verb, which in Arabic gives duration and stability rather than a single event. The verb itself is in the imperfect, which describes an ongoing or repeated action. Put together, the phrase does not say that the man was frightened at that moment. It says that awe is his condition, that he stands in it, and that the standing is what qualifies him. The distinction is important because the scene could easily have been read as a moment of anxiety — a blind man interrupting a great conversation, nervous about his reception.

**Awe Carries Knowledge Inside It**

*Khashyah* is not the generic word for fear. Arabic has *khawf* for the fear of a danger, and *khashyah* for the fear that comes from knowing the greatness of the one feared — reverence with recognition in it. The Qur'an states the relation explicitly: *“Of all of Allah's servants, only the knowledgeable ˹of His might˺ are ˹truly˺ in awe of Him”* (35:28). On that definition, awe is an achievement of understanding rather than a temperament, and the blind man's condition is the fruit of something he had learned. This raises the estimate of the whole scene. The man who came asking was not a simpleton needing elementary instruction; he was someone in whom knowledge had already produced reverence, and his request was for more of what had produced it. The one who deemed himself beyond need had knowledge of the world and no reverence in it, which is the precise inversion the sūrah is drawing.

**Self-Sufficiency and Awe Cannot Occupy One Heart**

The counterpart to this verse is the figure introduced two verses earlier. *Istighnā* is the conviction that one needs nothing, and it is the direct negation of *khashyah*, which is the conviction that one is held by Someone. The Qur'an ties the conviction of sufficiency to transgression in a single sentence: *“Most certainly, one exceeds all bounds once they think they are selfsufficient”* (96:6–7). The mechanism is clear enough.

**The Ones the Warner Is Sent To**

Makkan revelation keeps narrowing the address to those who stand in awe. *“Your duty is only to warn whoever is in awe of it”* (79:45) — the verse immediately preceding this sūrah in the received order — defines the audience of warning by the presence of *khashyah* for the Hour. The sūrah that follows it dramatizes the definition in a single afternoon.

## Verse 80:10

> you were inattentive to him

**A Word That Means Being Held by Something Else**

*Talahhā* is from *l-h-w*, the root of diversion, amusement, and the absorption that makes a person forget what matters. The fifth form means to be occupied or diverted by a thing, and the thing doing the occupying is not necessarily frivolous: a serious conversation can hold a man as completely as a game. The translation's "inattentive" is accurate but flattens the word, since *talahhā* implies a positive engagement elsewhere rather than a mere absence of attention. The Prophet was not bored or wandering. He was absorbed — absorbed in a matter he judged important, and the absorption is what removed the blind man from view. The choice of this root rather than a plain verb of turning away is deliberate. Turning away is a decision about a person; being absorbed is a condition produced by a thing, and the condition is more dangerous because the person who is in it does not notice what he is doing.

**Absorption Is the Qur'an's Diagnosis of This Life**

The root *lahw* carries a heavy theological load elsewhere. *“Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children”* (57:20) lists diversion first among the constituents of worldly existence. *“This worldly life is no more than play and amusement. But the Hereafter is indeed the real life, if only they knew”* (29:64) makes the same claim in shorter form. The word therefore belongs to a vocabulary of misplacement: the thing that absorbs a person is not the thing that deserves him. Applied to this scene, the vocabulary produces an uncomfortable reading. The conversation with the notable was a serious matter by every worldly measure, and it functioned here as *lahw* — the absorbing thing that displaced the real. The sūrah does not call the conversation worthless; it calls the absorption misdirected, which is a subtler and more transferable charge.

## Verse 80:11

> But no! This ˹revelation˺ is truly a reminder

**A Particle That Refuses a Whole Train of Thought**

*Kallā* is one of the Qur'an's most emphatic particles, and its function is to reject what has just been said or thought — "no indeed," "far from it," "certainly not." It appears only in the Makkan revelations, never in the Madinan ones, and it is almost always followed by a positive statement that replaces what it refused. Here the refusal is directed at the reasoning the previous ten verses exposed: the calculation that attention should follow importance. *Kallā* does not argue with the calculation. It declines it and starts again. The particle gives the sūrah its hinge. Everything before it is a particular event in a particular room; everything after it is a description of the revelation and of human beings. The transition is made in one word, and the word is a refusal rather than a conjunction, which tells the reader that the new material is not a continuation of the old argument but a correction of the frame in which the old argument was conducted.

**The Qur'an's Own Name for Itself**

*Dhikr* is the term the Book prefers for itself in the Makkan period. *“Enough! Surely this ˹Quran˺ is a reminder”* (74:54) is the nearest formal parallel and belongs to the same phase. *“And this ˹Quran˺ is a blessed reminder which We have revealed. Will you ˹pagans˺ then deny it?”* (21:50) adds the blessing and the challenge. *“This ˹Book˺ is only a Reminder and a clear Quran”* (36:69) separates the Qur'an from poetry in the same breath. The choice of *dhikr* is not modest. A reminder is addressed to someone who already knows and has forgotten, which means the Qur'an presents itself not as news but as recovery. That self-description governs the scene it appears in: the blind man needed no new doctrine, and the notable was not being offered a proposition he had never heard. Both were being reminded, and the difference between them was that one of them wanted to remember.

**A Reminder Presumes a Free Receiver**

The word *dhikr* carries a structural consequence, and the next verse states it: whoever wills, let him be mindful of it. A reminder cannot be forced, because forcing produces compliance rather than recollection. The Qur'an is explicit about the limit this places on the Messenger: *“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺”* (88:21–22), and *“Had your Lord so willed ˹O Prophet˺, all ˹people˺ on earth would have certainly believed, every single one of them! Would you then force people to become believers?”* (10:99). This is why the scene's calculation was doubly mistaken. The Prophet was trying to secure an outcome that no delivery can secure, and he was paying for the try with the one thing he did control. The nature of a reminder fixes both the teacher's duty and the teacher's incapacity, and verse 11 states the nature before verse 12 states the freedom that follows from it.

## Verse 80:12

> So let whoever wills be mindful of it

**The Same Sentence in the Clothed One**

The formula is not unique to this sūrah. Sūrat al-Muddaththir carries the identical verse: *“So let whoever wills be mindful of it”* (74:55), following the same declaration that the Qur'an is a reminder in 74:54. The two passages are separated by years of revelation and arrive at the same wording, which marks the formula as a fixed piece of Makkan address. Al-Muddaththir then adds what this sūrah leaves implicit: *“But they cannot do so unless Allah wills. He ˹alone˺ is worthy to be feared and entitled to forgive”* (74:56). The addition closes the circle that human freedom opens, and its absence here is significant. Sūrat ʿAbasa stops at the freedom of the hearer, because the subject of these ten verses has been the teacher's mistaken try to manage outcomes. To add the divine will at this point would reintroduce a calculation, and the passage has just dismantled the calculation.

**Two Readings of Whoever Wills**

The commentators note that *man shāʾa* can be taken in two ways. On the obvious reading the willing is the hearer's: any person who chooses may take the reminder. On the second reading, reported from al-Qurṭubī's sources, the willing is God's — whoever God wills is the one who remembers — and the sentence describes an outcome rather than issuing an invitation. The first reading fits the immediate context better, since the verse is the consequence of the Qur'an being a reminder and the answer to the teacher's anxiety about results. The second reading is not excluded by it; it simply locates the same event at a different level.

**Freedom Is the Condition of Remembrance**

The Qur'an insists on the absence of coercion wherever it speaks of belief. *“Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood”* (2:256) states the principle in the Madinan period, and the Makkan passages state its consequence for the Messenger: *“And say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve’”* (18:29). The threat attached there belongs to the outcome, not to the choice; the choice is left standing in its full weight. Sūrat al-Insān makes the same move in two verses: *“Surely this is a reminder. So let whoever wills take the ˹Right˺ Way to their Lord. But you cannot will ˹to do so˺ unless Allah wills”* (76:29–30). The pattern is consistent: freedom is asserted first, and the divine will is named second, never as a cancellation of the first but as its ground.

---

## Verse 80:13

> It is ˹written˺ on pages held in honour—

**Sheets Rather Than a Bound Book**

*Ṣuḥuf* is the plural of *ṣaḥīfah*, a sheet or a scroll — a written surface, not a codex. The Qur'an uses the word of earlier revelations with a specificity that suggests deliberate choice: *“Or has he not been informed of what is in the Scripture of Moses”* (53:36), and *“This is certainly ˹mentioned˺ in the earlier Scriptures—the Scriptures of Abraham and Moses”* (87:18–19). Sūrat al-Bayyinah describes the Messenger as *“reciting scrolls of ˹utmost˺ purity”* (98:2). The image is of loose sheets held together by their content rather than by a binding, and it carries an association with antiquity and with the earliest form in which a revelation exists. Applied here, the word elevates the Qur'an into the company of the revelations that preceded it and locates it at the level of the original document rather than the circulating copy. The honour described in the same verse belongs to that level.

**Which Pages Are Meant**

Three identifications are offered. The pages may be those of the Qur'an itself, which is the plainest reading and the one most commentators adopt. They may be the books of all the prophets, so that the reminder of verse 11 is placed inside a single tradition of written revelation. Or they may be the Preserved Tablet, the heavenly original from which every revelation is taken down — *“In fact, this is a glorious Quran, ˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet”* (85:21–22). The third reading has the strongest textual support in the verses that follow, since a book carried by angel-scribes and untouched except by the purified fits a heavenly register better than an earthly manuscript. The first reading is not displaced by it: the same passage in Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah describes the Qur'an in the terms usually reserved for the Tablet. The disagreement is best resolved by holding that the Qur'an is in the Tablet and comes from it, which is what both readings assert from different ends.

**The Insistence That This Speech Is Written**

Makkan revelation repeatedly describes a spoken thing as a written one, and the repetition is a theological claim. The Qur'an is recited aloud, memorized, and delivered by a Messenger who did not read; yet the text insists on pages, records, tablets, pens, and scribes. *“that this is truly a noble Quran, in a well-preserved Record”* (56:77–78). The insistence answers an implicit objection: a speech that exists only in a man's mouth could be altered, forgotten, or invented. A speech that also exists in a written heavenly register cannot. The written form is the guarantee of the spoken one, and the guarantee is offered in the very sūrahs where the speaking was most contested. This verse is part of that pattern, and it places the reminder of verse 11 inside the guaranteed order rather than the improvised one.

**Honour in the Qur'an's Scale**

The root *k-r-m* does its work on human beings too. *“Indeed, We have dignified the children of Adam, carried them on land and sea, granted them good and lawful provisions, and privileged them far above many of Our creatures”* (17:70). Honour in the Qur'an is thus a conferred rank, available to persons and to pages, and in both cases unearned by the recipient. The parallel is instructive for this sūrah. The man who was overlooked had no worldly honour and held a rank conferred by God; the pages described here hold the same kind of rank. Verse 13 overturns it again in the register of revelation: the honour of a book is not its reception.

*“So ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺. For you, by the grace of your Lord, are not a fortune-teller or a madman. Or do they say, ‘˹He is˺ a poet, for whom we ˹eagerly˺ await an ill-fate!’?”* (52:29–30).

## Verse 80:14

> highly esteemed, purified—

**The Verse That Governs the Discussion**

Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah states the same idea in a form that has generated centuries of jurisprudence: *“that this is truly a noble Quran, in a well-preserved Record, touched by none except the purified ˹angels˺”* (56:77–79). The identification of the purified as angels is given in the translation and is the majority exegetical reading, since the record in question is the heavenly one. From that reading the jurists drew an inference by analogy: if the heavenly original is touched only by the pure, the earthly copy should be handled only in a state of ritual purity. The inference is not stated in the text, and a minority of scholars have resisted it on the ground that the verse describes angels rather than prescribing for human beings. Both sides agree on the description; they differ on whether a norm follows from it. The stronger position, in practice and in the settled tradition of the schools, is that the norm does follow, because the Qur'an's descriptions of its own sanctity have consistently been read as instructions.

**Purity as an Answer to a Charge**

The Meccan accusation against the Prophet included forgery: that he was composing or adapting, that he was taught by a human being, that he could produce something else if asked. The claim that these pages are purified answers all three at the root. A text into which nothing foreign has entered cannot have been composed by borrowing, and a text from which nothing has been lost cannot have been tampered with. The answer is stated as description rather than as argument, and its force lies in that: the verse does not contest the charge on the accuser's ground, it describes a condition the charge cannot reach. The same method appears in the insistence on the Preserved Tablet in Sūrat al-Burūj (85:22), where preservation is asserted rather than showed. The Qur'an's self-description functions as a standing rebuttal, and the rebuttal has been recited in every generation since, including by readers who had no idea what was being answered.

## Verse 80:15

> by the hands of angel-scribes

**A Word With Three Possible Roots**

*Safarah* is the plural of *sāfir*, and the commentators trace it to three related meanings, each supported by usage. The first is writing: *safara* gives *asfār*, books, and the scribes are those who inscribe. The second is mediation: *sufarāʾ* are envoys who carry a message between two parties, and the angels are the envoys between God and His Messengers. The third is unveiling, since *safara* also means to make plain or to remove a covering, and the scribes are those through whom the revelation is made clear. All three fit the context, and the Qur'anic use of the root elsewhere supports the first and third: the recording angels of 50:17–18 inscribe deeds, and the description of the Qur'an as a reminder implies making plain. The ambiguity is productive rather than defective, because the office described combines all three functions — writing, carrying, and disclosing — and no single English word covers them.

**The Ḥadīth That Fixed the Meaning for Muslims**

One report has done more to shape how this verse is read than any commentary. From ʿĀʾishah, in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4937 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 798, with the authenticity agreed upon by both compilers: the one who is proficient in the Qur'an and recites it well will be with the noble and righteous scribes, and the one who recites it with difficulty, stammering and finding it hard, will have two rewards. The ḥadīth quotes these two verses by describing them rather than citing them, and it does three things at once. It identifies the scribes as angels. It places the reciter in their company, which is an astonishing rank for a human being. And it removes the rank from proficiency alone, since the struggling reciter is promised a double reward rather than exclusion. The second half has been the consolation of generations of non-Arab Muslims learning to read the text, and it is attached directly to this verse in the received tradition.

**Which Angels Are Meant**

Two identifications compete. The first takes the scribes as the angels appointed to record human deeds, on the ground that the same root is used of writing and that the Qur'an describes such angels explicitly: *“while you are certainly observed by vigilant, honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺”* (82:10–11) and *“As the two recording-angels— ˹one˺ sitting to the right, and ˹the other to˺ the left—note ˹everything˺”* (50:17). The second takes them as the mediators of revelation, with Gabriel at their head, since the context is the transmission of a book rather than the auditing of a life. The second reading fits the surrounding verses better: the subject from verse 11 onward is the reminder and its written form, not the deeds of human beings. The first reading survives because the word is the same and because the association was already fixed in Muslim usage by the ḥadīth, which speaks of the scribes without specifying their assignment.

## Verse 80:16

> honourable and virtuous

**The Same Pairing Elsewhere**

The Qur'an describes the recording angels in almost identical terms: *“while you are certainly observed by vigilant, honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺”* (82:10–11). The word *kirām* appears there with *kātibīn*, the writers, and the combination is close enough to this passage that the two have been read together since the earliest commentaries. Both describe a class of angels characterized by honour and by a function connected with writing, and both place them in a position of observation over human affairs. The parallel strengthens the identification of the scribes in verse 15 as a specific order of angels rather than a general reference, and it also transfers the moral weight: the beings who record human deeds are described as noble, which is a guarantee about the record. A dishonest scribe would produce a dishonest book, and the Qur'an forecloses the possibility by describing the character of the carriers before it invokes their testimony.

**Birr in Qur'anic Usage**

The root *b-r-r* is one of the Qur'an's terms for active goodness, and its range is instructive. It describes filial devotion: John the Baptist was *“kind to his parents”* and *“neither arrogant nor disobedient”* (19:14), and Jesus says of himself, *“and to be kind to my mother. He has not made me arrogant or defiant”* (19:32). It describes the substance of righteousness itself: *“You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish”* (3:92), where *birr* is defined by a costly act rather than a state of mind. In each case the word denotes goodness that costs something and is directed toward another. Applied to angels, the same word carries the same sense: their righteousness is not a passive property but a constant expenditure of obedience. The choice of *bararah* over a more abstract term for purity is therefore meaningful, since it ties the carriers to the vocabulary of duty and devotion that human beings are also addressed in.

**Why Angels Need a Character Reference**

Angels in the Qur'an are defined by obedience: they *“never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded”* (66:6). Given that definition, the adjectives here might seem redundant — of course the carriers of revelation are noble. The redundancy is not idle. The passage is answering a human situation, and in that situation the character of the messengers matters to the reception of the message. Mecca had accused the Prophet of receiving his speech from a human teacher, from jinn, from poets of the past. The answer in these verses is a description of the actual carriers, and the description emphasizes precisely the qualities whose absence would make transmission unreliable: nobility, which excludes deception for gain, and righteousness, which excludes deviation from command. The character reference is an argument in the form of an adjective, and it is aimed at the same objection the previous verses addressed.

**The Qualities Transfer to the Reciter**

The ḥadīth of Bukhārī 4937 and Muslim 798 places the proficient reciter *with* the noble and righteous scribes, and the preposition makes the association the substance of the reward. What the reciter joins is a company defined by these two adjectives, and joining a company of that kind is not a matter of location. The tradition understood the promise as an assimilation: the one who carries the Book is expected to resemble its carriers. That expectation generates the whole ethical literature on the reciter's conduct — the prohibitions against reciting for payment, for display, or for argument, and the insistence that the Qur'an be carried with humility.

## Verse 80:17

> Condemned are ˹disbelieving˺ humans! How ungrateful they are ˹to Allah˺

**An Imprecation in the Passive Voice**

*Qutila al-insān* is literally "the human being was killed," and the form is an idiom of imprecation rather than a report of an event: may he be destroyed, may he perish, cursed is he. Arabic uses the passive of killing to express a wish for ruin without naming who brings it, which gives the phrase a weight that an active curse lacks — the destruction is presented as already decreed and merely awaited. The Qur'an deploys the same construction as a formal opening: *“Condemned are the liars—”* (51:10), *“Condemned are the makers of the ditch—”* (85:4), *“May the hands of Abu Lahab perish, and he ˹himself˺ perish!”* (111:1). In each case the imprecation introduces a judgement rather than a narrative, and the passive voice marks the judgement as settled. Here the formula is turned on the human being as such, immediately after six verses describing the revelation as honoured, raised, purified, and carried by noble hands.

**The Sūrah Changes Its Addressee**

Up to this point the passage has concerned the Prophet, the blind man, the notable, and the nature of the reminder. Verse 17 turns outward to humanity at large, and the turn is unannounced. No connective links it to what precedes, and the change of subject is total: from a room in Mecca to the human being as such. This is the characteristic movement of the short Makkan sūrahs, which use a particular occasion to open onto an universal subject and then leave the occasion behind without marking the departure. What the reader is left with is a sequence that will occupy the rest of the chapter: the human being condemned, his origin described, his death and burial recorded, his resurrection deferred to a will with no date, his food itemized, and his family scattered on the day of the Blast.

## Verse 80:18

> From what substance did He create them

**A Question That Is Answered Immediately**

*Mimma khalaqah* — from what did He create him? The interrogative is rhetorical, and the answer arrives in the next verse without delay. The device is one of the Qur'an's most frequent argumentative forms: pose a question the hearer cannot answer, then supply the answer, and let the gap between the two establish the point. The gap here is not informational. Every Meccan knew what human beings are made of in the ordinary sense; nobody needed a revelation to tell him. What the question does is arrest the hearer and force him to consider the material rather than the man. The imprecation of the previous verse condemned ingratitude in general terms, and a general condemnation invites defence.

**The Same Question Put Elsewhere**

The formula recurs with a consistency that marks it as a set argument. *“Let people then consider what they were created from!”* (86:5) is the nearest parallel. It uses the same verb of consideration that appears in this sūrah's later verse about food. *“Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop, then— behold!—they openly challenge ˹Us˺?”* (36:77) adds the consequence of the origin: the dispute. *“Did We not create you from a humble fluid, placing it in a secure place”* (77:20–21) sets the origin against the security of the womb. Each passage makes the same move from the lowliness of the beginning to the arrogance of the present, and each treats the beginning as a matter of common knowledge that has been forgotten rather than as a discovery. The argument's force depends on that: it is not new information but recovered information, which is what a reminder is.

**An Argument About Power, Not Biology**

The purpose of naming the drop is not embryology but demonstration, and the demonstration is directed at resurrection. If a being was produced from a fluid with no shape and no capacity, then producing him again from dust and bone is not a greater task. The Qur'an states the inference explicitly: *“And they argue with Us— forgetting they were created—saying, ‘Who will give life to decayed bones?’ Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being’”* (36:78–79). The same inference closes Sūrat al-Qiyāmah: *“Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life?”* (75:40). Origin is thus an argument from the lesser to the greater, and the verse that raises the question is the first step of that argument. The human being who denies the second creation is described as having forgotten the first, and the forgetting is the ingratitude named in the previous verse.

## Verse 80:19

> He created them from a sperm-drop, and ordained their development

**The Drop**

*Nuṭfah* is a small quantity of fluid, and in Qur'anic usage it denotes the seminal drop from which a human being begins. The word appears in a set of passages that describe the same origin: *“Were they not ˹once˺ a sperm-drop emitted?”* (75:37), *“˹For˹ indeed, We ˹alone˺ created humans from a drop of mixed fluids, ˹in order˺ to test them, so We made them hear and see”* (76:2), *“Did We not create you from a humble fluid”* (77:20). The consistency is remarkable given that the passages belong to different sūrahs and different moments of revelation, and it suggests a deliberate vocabulary rather than incidental description. The emphasis is on the quantity and the lowliness: a drop, emitted, humble, mixed. Nothing about it predicts a person. The Qur'an's use of the term is not a try at biology; it is a way of stating that the beginning of a human being contains no trace of what a human being becomes, so that everything in between must be accounted for by something other than the material.

**Measured Before Made**

The second verb is *qaddarahū*, from the root *q-d-r*, which carries measurement, apportionment, and decree in a single family of meanings. The same root gives *qadar*, destiny, and *qudrah*, power. To say that God *qaddara* the human being is to say that he was measured out, apportioned, and determined — that a specification preceded the making. The Qur'an uses the verb in this sense as a divine attribute: *“Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High, Who created and ˹perfectly˺ fashioned ˹all˺, and Who ordained precisely and inspired accordingly”* (87:1–3), and *“Who has perfected everything He created. And He originated the creation of humankind from clay”* (32:7). The pairing of creation with ordainment is standard, and it excludes the possibility that the development of a human being is a sequence of accidents. The drop does not become a person by luck. It becomes a person according to a measure that was set, and the setting of the measure is itself an act of the same power that made the material.

**The Stages Listed Elsewhere**

The ordainment named here is itemized in other passages, and the itemization shows what the verb covers. *“And indeed, We created humankind from an extract of clay, then placed each ˹human˺ as a sperm-drop in a secure place, then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation. So Blessed is Allah, the Best of Creators”* (23:12–14). Sūrat al-Ḥajj gives the same stages in the service of an argument about resurrection, and adds that the term in the womb is appointed (22:5).

**From a Drop to a Disputant**

The Qur'an keeps returning to the disproportion between the two ends of this process. *“Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop, then— behold!—they openly challenge ˹Us˺?”* (36:77). The particle rendered "behold" marks the suddenness, and the suddenness is the point: the same being who began as an emitted drop stands up and argues with the One who made him. Nothing in the drop predicted the argument, and nothing in the argument changes the drop. The contrast is the Qur'an's standard way of handling human arrogance, and it works by chronology rather than by moralizing. A person who reads his own history in order — drop, clot, lump, bones, flesh, speech, objection — encounters his objection at the end of a sequence he did not author. The objection loses its footing, not because it is refuted, but because its position in the sequence becomes visible.

The same logic appears at the close of Sūrat al-Qiyāmah, where the stages from drop to clot to formed flesh end in the question about bringing the dead back to life (75:37–40).

## Verse 80:20

> Then He makes the way easy for them

**Three Readings and the Grounds for Each**

The first reading rests on the immediate context. The verse sits between creation from a drop and death with burial, so a description of birth fits the sequence exactly, and the exegetes who hold it note that the birth is the one stage of the sequence not yet mentioned. The second rests on the moral theme of the sūrah: the human being condemned for ingratitude in verse 17 has been given more than a body, and the easing would then be the provision of guidance, as in *“We already showed them the Way, whether they ˹choose to˺ be grateful or ungrateful”* (76:3). The third rests on a report from the Prophet: work, for everyone is made easy for that for which he was created — transmitted in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6596 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2649, with authenticity agreed upon. The ḥadīth is used by the commentators to gloss this verse, and it supports the generic reading by making the easing a principle of creation rather than an event in gestation.

**Easing as a Feature of Divine Government**

*Taysīr* — making easy — is not incidental to the Qur'an's account of how God deals with creatures; it is a stated policy. Sūrat al-Layl sets out the two directions it can take: *“As for the one who is charitable, mindful ˹of Allah˺, and ˹firmly˺ believes in the finest reward, We will help for them the Way of Ease. And as for the one who is stingy, indifferent ˹to Allah˺, and ˹staunchly˺ denies the finest reward, We will help for them the path of hardship”* (92:5–10). Facilitation is granted in both cases, and the direction differs. The verse in this sūrah belongs to the same economy: the way was made easy, and the ease is a gift that precedes any merit. Nothing in the human being's history explains it. The drop did not earn the passage, the child did not choose the birth, and the adult did not procure the guidance. Easing is what the Creator does, and the doing is before the receiving.

## Verse 80:21

> then causes them to die and be buried

**A Verb That Grants a Grave**

The second verb is the fourth form of *q-b-r*, and the fourth form is causative: not "he buried him" but "he caused him to be buried," or more idiomatically, "he granted him burial." The distinction is worth pressing, because the Qur'an could have used the simple form and did not. What is conferred is not the act of interment but the state of having a grave. A body left to scavengers, or lost at sea, or exposed on a battlefield, has died without being granted this. The causative verb names the granting, and the granting is a dignity. It belongs to the same register as the verse that declares the elevation of the human species: *“Indeed, We have dignified the children of Adam, carried them on land and sea, granted them good and lawful provisions, and privileged them far above many of Our creatures”* (17:70). Burial is one of the dignities, and it is listed here among the acts for which the human being is expected to be grateful.

**Burial Taught by a Bird**

The Qur'an locates the origin of burial in a story rather than in a law. After Cain killed his brother and did not know what to do with the body, *“Then Allah sent a crow digging ˹a grave˺ in the ground ˹for a dead crow˺, to show him how to bury the corpse of his brother. He cried, ‘Alas! Have I ˹even˺ failed to be like this crow and bury the corpse of my brother?’ So he became regretful”* (5:31). The detail matters for this verse because it shows burial as something taught, not invented — a practice delivered to humanity from outside, at the moment of its first murder, by a creature acting on instruction.

**The Grave as the Human Distinction**

No other creature in the Qur'an is described as buried. Animals die; the earth receives them; nothing is said of a grave. The human being alone is granted one, and the granting produces the vocabulary of the afterlife that runs through the Book: the people of the graves, the day they are brought out, the questioning in the grave. *“And certainly the Hour is coming, there is no doubt about it. And Allah will surely resurrect those in the graves”* (22:7).

**Death as an Act Rather Than an Event**

The Qur'an consistently attributes dying to God rather than to causes. *“No soul can ever die without Allah's Will at the destined time”* (3:145) places every death inside a permission and a schedule. *“Every being on earth is bound to perish. Only your Lord Himself, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain ˹forever˺”* (55:26–27) states the universality of the perishing and the exception of the One who does not perish. In this verse the attribution is grammatical: He caused him to die. The passive of the human being and the active of God are fixed, and the fixing removes death from the category of accidents. What is caused by a will is not random, and what is not random is answerable. The sequence in this sūrah depends on that: the same will that caused the death will cause the raising, and the raising is described in the next verse with the same verb structure and the same subject.

## Verse 80:22

> Then when He wills, He will resurrect them

**A Will With No Date Attached**

*Idhā shāʾa ansharahū* — then, when He wills, He will raise him. The condition is stated without a term. No time is given, no sign is named, no interval is measured, and the absence is the substance of the verse. Every other act in the chain has a determinate character: creation from a drop, ordainment of development, easing of the way, causing death, causing burial. The raising alone is attached to an unspecified willing, and the attachment does two things at once. It confirms that the raising will occur, since the condition is God's own will and nothing can prevent it.

**To Unfold What Was Gathered**

The verb *anshara* is the fourth form of *n-sh-r*, whose basic sense is to spread out, to unfold, to scatter what was folded or gathered, and to publish what was hidden. Arabic uses the same root for spreading a garment, for the wind scattering rain, and for the resurrection of the dead. The image is of something compressed being opened out. A body that has been folded into a grave and reduced to dust is spread again into a living form. The choice of verb is more concrete than the usual English rendering suggests, and it carries the sense of restoration rather than of new manufacture: what is unfolded is what was folded. That is the answer to the Meccan objection about decayed bones, since unfolding presupposes an original that persists in the knowledge of the One who folded it. The Qur'an makes the presupposition explicit in its reply: they will be revived by the One who produced them the first time, and He has perfect knowledge of every created being (36:79).

**Resurrection Argued From the Beginning**

The verse is the conclusion of an argument begun four verses earlier, and the argument runs from origin to return. *“Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life?”* (75:40) is the same inference in interrogative form, placed at the end of a description of the stages from drop to formed flesh. *“And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him”* (30:27) states the inference directly and adds the comparison: the second making is easier than the first. The comparison is not a claim about effort, since nothing costs God effort; it is a claim about the logic of the case. Whatever produced a person from a drop can produce him from a grave. Sūrat al-Baqarah puts the whole sequence to the disbeliever as a single question: how can you deny God when you were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will all be returned (2:28).

## Verse 80:23

> But no! They have failed to comply with what He ordered

**The Second Refusal in the Sūrah**

*Kallā* appears for the second time in this chapter, and the two occurrences mark its hinges. The first, at verse 11, refused the reasoning that had produced the frown and turned the sūrah from an incident to a doctrine. This one refuses something the reader may have drawn from the preceding six verses. The chain of acts — created, ordained, eased, killed, buried, to be raised — is a description of complete provision, and a description of complete provision invites a comfortable conclusion: the human being is well looked after, and the looking after is automatic. *Kallā* stops that conclusion and replaces it with a failure.

**Two Objects for the Rebuke**

The exegetes identify the target in two ways. The first takes it as the disbeliever who, told of the Resurrection, assumes he will fare well there — the attitude recorded in Sūrat Fuṣṣilat, where the one who is given a taste of mercy after adversity says that the finest reward with God will definitely be his (41:50). On this reading the refusal answers a presumption: you have not complied, so the confidence is misplaced. The second takes it as anyone who believes he has discharged what was required of him, and reads the verse as a check on self-satisfaction. Both readings are supported by the wording, since *mā amarahū* — what He commanded him — is unspecified and covers the whole of obligation. The first reading fits the Makkan context of argument about resurrection; the second fits the moral movement of the sūrah, which has just described a frown as a matter for correction from heaven. The second is the broader and the more useful, and nothing excludes the first.

## Verse 80:24

> Let people then consider their food

**The Ordinary as the Locus of Evidence**

The method belongs to a family of passages. Sūrat al-Ghāshiyah asks the same question of larger objects: *“Do they not ever reflect on camels—how they were ˹masterfully˺ created; and the sky—how it was raised ˹high˺; and the mountains—how they were firmly set up; and the earth—how it was levelled out?”* (88:17–20). Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah narrows to agriculture: *“Have you considered what you sow? Is it you who cause it to grow, or is it We Who do so?”* (56:63–64). Sūrat al-Ṭāriq narrows to the body: *“Let people then consider what they were created from!”* (86:5). Across all of them the object is chosen for its familiarity rather than its grandeur, and the question posed is always about agency: who did this? The familiarity removes the excuse of distance. A man can claim he has never considered the heavens, but he cannot claim he has never eaten, and the eating is the entry point this verse selects.

**The Same Formula Turned on Origin**

The parallel in Sūrat al-Ṭāriq is close enough to be read as a companion verse: *“Let people then consider what they were created from!”* (86:5) uses the same verb, the same subject, and the same structure, with origin replacing food as the object. The two passages divide the human being's dependence in half. His beginning was not his doing, and his continuation is not his doing either. Between the two lies the whole of a life, and both ends are outside it. The pairing also shows why this verse appears where it does. The chain of acts in verses 18 to 22 covered creation and death; the provision of food covers the interval, and the interval is where ingratitude actually occurs. A human being is not ungrateful at his conception or at his burial. He is ungrateful at lunch, and the verse addresses him there.

**Food as the Site of Ingratitude**

The Qur'an ties blessing and ingratitude together in a formula that appears twice: *“If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never be able to number them. Indeed humankind is truly unfair, ˹totally˺ ungrateful”* (14:34), and *“If you tried to count Allah's blessings, you would never be able to number them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”* (16:18). Both begin with the impossibility of enumeration, which is the same point this verse makes by a different route: the food in front of you cannot be traced to its end, because the tracing leads out of your hands at every step. The first formulation pairs the impossibility with the charge of injustice and ingratitude; the second pairs it with forgiveness and mercy. This sūrah has already pronounced the charge in verse 17, and it now supplies the material for it. The counting is what the human being does not do, and the not-doing is the covering that the word for disbelief denotes.

## Verse 80:25

> how We pour down rain in abundance

**The Machinery Behind the Pouring**

Elsewhere the Qur'an describes the process that precedes the release: *“It is Allah Who sends the winds, which then stir up ˹vapour, forming˺ clouds, which He then spreads out in the sky or piles up into masses as He wills, from which you see rain come forth”* (30:48). Sūrat al-Aʿrāf gives the same sequence with the rain as a sign of resurrection: *“He is the One Who sends the winds ushering in His mercy. When they bear heavy clouds, We drive them to a lifeless land and then cause rain to fall, producing every type of fruit. Similarly, We will bring the dead to life, so perhaps you will be mindful”* (7:57). Both passages insert stages — wind, vapour, cloud, driving — between the will and the falling. This verse compresses all of them into a single act of pouring, which is a legitimate compression because the stages are themselves acts of the same agent. The compression also serves the argument: the eater is asked to consider his food, and the food begins with a pouring he did not perform and cannot perform.

**The Same Pouring in the News**

Sūrat al-Nabaʾ describes the identical sequence in nearly the identical words: *“and sent down from rainclouds pouring water, producing by it grain and ˹various˺ plants, and dense orchards?”* (78:14–16). The three elements — poured water, grain, dense gardens — are the same three that occupy verses 25 to 30 of this sūrah, and both passages use the verb of pouring in an emphatic form. The two sūrahs are neighbours in the received order and belong to the same Makkan phase, and the correspondence suggests a fixed pattern of argument rather than two coincidental descriptions. Al-Nabaʾ frames the list as a question about what people already know; this sūrah frames it as an instruction to look at food. The material is shared, and the framing differs, which is characteristic of a revelation that returns to the same evidence from different angles until the evidence is unavoidable.

**Water as the Beginning of Everything Living**

The Qur'an states the priority of water in terms that are close to a principle: *“And We created from water every living thing. Will they not then believe?”* (21:30). *“And Allah has created from water every living creature. Some of them crawl on their bellies, some walk on two legs, and some walk on four”* (24:45). *“And He is the One Who creates human beings from a ˹humble˺ liquid, then establishes for them bonds of kinship and marriage”* (25:54) uses the same imagery at the scale of the individual. Water in these verses is not one provision among many; it is the medium of life itself, and the pouring described here is the arrival of that medium on land. The list of foods that follows — grain, grapes, greens, olives, dates, orchards, fodder — is a list of forms water has taken. That is why the list begins with the pouring rather than with the sowing: nothing in it exists before the water does.

## Verse 80:26

> and meticulously split the earth open ˹for sprouts˺

**The Earth Is Split, Not Softened**

*Shaqiqnā* — We split — is a verb of cleaving, and it describes the earth as something that had to be opened by force. The image is not of soil yielding gently to a seed but of a surface being broken, and the breaking is real: a shoot pushes through crusted ground, and the ground resists. The Qur'an chooses the violent verb rather than a verb of softening or moistening, and the choice matters for the argument that follows in the sūrah, because splitting is what a grave does not do on its own. The same root is used for the splitting of the sky at the end of the world and for the cleaving of the earth in Sūrat al-Ṭāriq, where the earth is described as one that cracks open. A verb that belongs to the vocabulary of rupture is being applied to agriculture, and the application prepares the reader for the rupture of resurrection, which the passage will reach at verse 33.

**Who Performs the Splitting**

The shoot visibly does the work, and the verse attributes it to God. This is not a denial of the plant's action but a statement about agency at a different level, and the Qur'an handles the same question explicitly elsewhere: *“Have you considered what you sow? Is it you who cause it to grow, or is it We Who do so?”* (56:63–64). The farmer sows; the growing is not his.

**Splitting as the Revival of the Dead**

The Qur'an uses the revival of dead land as its standard illustration of resurrection, and the illustration depends on exactly the image in this verse. *“And among His signs is that you see the earth devoid of life, but as soon as We send down rain upon it, it begins to stir ˹to life˺ and swell. Indeed, the One Who revives it can easily revive the dead. He is certainly Most Capable of everything”* (41:39). The stirring and swelling are the earth cracking as the shoots push through. Sūrat Qāf states the inference in a single sentence after a similar list: *“˹as˺ a provision for ˹Our˺ servants. And with this ˹rain˺ We revive a lifeless land. Similar is the emergence ˹from the graves˺”* (50:11). The parallel is exact and it is not decorative: the emergence from a grave is described as similar to the emergence of a shoot from split earth. This verse supplies the mechanism of that similarity, and it does so inside a list of foods.

## Verse 80:27

> causing grain to grow in it

**Growth Attributed to the One Who Split the Earth**

*Anbatnā* — We caused to grow — is the causative of the verb for sprouting, and it keeps the agency where the previous verse put it. The earth was split by God and the grain is grown by God; the seed is not mentioned at all. The omission matters, because a farmer would naturally place the seed at the centre of the account. The verse leaves it out and describes only the pouring, the splitting, and the growing. The result is a picture of provision in which human contribution is not only small but absent from the description, and the absence is a deliberate narrowing of attention. The farmer sows, and Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah asks him directly whether he causes the growth (56:64). Here the question is not asked because the answer has already been given by the verbs: We poured, We split, We grew.

**Grain in the Qur'an's Imagery**

The word recurs in contexts that show its weight. Sūrat al-Raḥmān lists the contents of the earth and names *“and grain with husks, and aromatic plants”* (55:12) — grain described in its harvested form, with the chaff still on it. Sūrat Qāf pairs it with the garden: *“bringing forth gardens and grains for harvest”* (50:9). The parable of spending in the way of God is built completely on a grain: *“The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains”* (2:261). The multiplication in that parable depends on the reader knowing what a single grain does — that it goes into the ground and returns as a plant carrying hundreds of its kind. The Qur'an's use of the seed as its image for charity assumes an agricultural literacy in its audience, and this verse is part of the material that literacy rests on.

**The Base of Every Settlement**

Grain is what allows a place to be inhabited continuously. Meat can be hunted, dates can be gathered, water can be carried, but only grain can be stored through a year and measured against a population. The Qur'an's own narrative of Joseph turns completely on this fact — the interpretation of a dream about years of plenty and years of famine, and the storage of grain through the good years to survive the bad (12:47–49). The narrative is the longest sustained treatment of food in the Book, and its subject is exactly the item named first in this list. Read against that narrative, the verse acquires an economic dimension: the grain is not only food but security, and security is a provision of the same kind as the rain that produced it. Nobody in Mecca or Medina could have survived a bad year on grapes.

## Verse 80:28

> as well as grapes and greens

**The Vine as the Sign of a Settled Land**

*ʿInab* is the grape, and the vine is the plant the Qur'an consistently associates with established abundance. A grain field can be sown in a bad year and lost; a vineyard takes years to establish and bears for decades, which means the presence of vines in a landscape is evidence that the landscape has been at peace. In the Ḥijāz the great vineyard district was al-Ṭāʾif, a day's journey from Mecca and a place the Quraysh knew well, and the mention of grapes in a Makkan sūrah would have been heard as a reference to a real economy rather than a poetic image.

**Grapes Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The vine appears in a set of passages that show how the Qur'an uses it. *“And on the earth there are ˹different˺ neighbouring tracts, gardens of grapevines, ˹various˺ crops, palm trees—some stemming from the same root, others standing alone. They are all irrigated with the same water, yet We make some taste better than others. Surely in this are signs for those who understand”* (13:4). The argument there is about diversity produced from a single input, and the vine is the first item. *“And We have placed in it gardens of palm trees and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth in it”* (36:34) pairs the two plants as the standard couple of Arabian abundance. Sūrat al-Raḥmān lists *“In it are fruit, palm trees with date stalks”* (55:11) and elsewhere names the vine among the produce of Paradise. Across all of these the grape functions as a marker of cultivated plenty, never as a wild food.

**The Vine and What Came From It**

Grapes occupy a particular place in Qur'anic legislation because of what they become. *“And from the fruits of palm trees and grapevines you derive intoxicants as well as wholesome provision. Surely in this is a sign for those who understand”* (16:67). This Makkan verse acknowledges both products of the same plant without yet prohibiting the first, and the prohibition came later in Medina. The mention of grapes in Sūrat ʿAbasa carries no such reference — it is a pure listing of provision — but the reader who knows the other verse reads this one with the knowledge that the same fruit supports both nourishment and intoxication, and that the difference lies in what human beings do with it. The provision is described here in its unprocessed state, which is how every item in the list appears: rain, split earth, grain, grapes, greens, olives, palms. Nothing cooked, nothing fermented, nothing traded.

## Verse 80:29

> and olives and palm trees

**The Blessed Olive**

The olive carries a weight in the Qur'an that no other plant approaches. The verse of light builds its entire image on olive oil: *“lit from ˹the oil of˺ a blessed olive tree, ˹located˺ neither to the east nor the west, whose oil would almost glow, even without being touched by fire”* (24:35). The tree is described as blessed, and its oil as almost luminous on its own. Sūrat al-Tīn opens with an oath by it: *“By the fig and the olive ˹of Jerusalem˺”* (95:1). Sūrat al-Muʾminūn names it among the provisions sent down: *“as well as ˹olive˺ trees which grow at Mount Sinai, providing oil and a condiment to eat”* (23:20). Three different kinds of reference — parable, oath, provision — and in all three the olive is marked out. This verse places it in a list of foods without any of that marking, which is a reminder that the ordinary agricultural context of the olive is before its symbolic one.

**The Palm's Ubiquity**

No tree appears in the Qur'an as often as the palm. It is named in descriptions of Paradise, in descriptions of the earth's produce, in the story of Maryam, who is told to shake the trunk and receive fresh dates (19:25), and in the oaths and parables of the Makkan sūrahs. Sūrat Qāf describes *“and towering palm trees ˹loaded˺ with clustered fruit”* (50:10), and Sūrat al-Raḥmān lists *“In it are fruit, palm trees with date stalks”* (55:11). The tree's importance is material as well as symbolic: the date is the concentrated food of the desert, capable of storage for years, high in sugar, and portable; the palm supplies shade, timber, fibre, and fodder from its trimmings. A settlement with palms can survive a drought that would destroy a settlement with only grain. The verse names the tree rather than the fruit, which points at the standing source rather than the seasonal yield.

**Both Trees in Every List**

The recurrence of the pair across unrelated passages is one of the Qur'an's most consistent descriptive habits. *“He is the One Who produces gardens—both cultivated and wild — and palm trees, crops of different flavours, olives, and pomegranates— similar ˹in shape˺, but dissimilar ˹in taste˺. Eat from the fruit they bear and pay the dues at harvest, but do not waste. Surely He does not like the wasteful”* (6:141). Sūrat al-Anʿām also describes *“producing green stalks from which We bring forth clustered grain. And from palm trees come clusters of dates hanging within reach. ˹There are˺ also gardens of grapevines, olives, and pomegranates”* (6:99). Sūrat Yāsīn pairs palms and vines with springs (36:34). The same items, in the same order, with the same conclusion: a sign for people who understand. This verse is one instance of a fixed pattern, and the fixity is itself evidence that the description is drawn from a single consistent account of provision rather than assembled for the occasion.

## Verse 80:30

> and dense orchards

**The Same Phrase in the News**

Sūrat al-Nabaʾ lists the provisions in almost the same words and closes with this item: *“and sent down from rainclouds pouring water, producing by it grain and ˹various˺ plants, and dense orchards?”* (78:14–16). The identical phrase *ḥadāʾiq ghulbā* appears in both sūrahs, and in both it closes the agricultural list. The correspondence is exact enough to indicate a fixed formula rather than a coincidence of vocabulary, and the formula serves the same function in both chapters: the demonstration of provision as evidence for the Day that both sūrahs go on to describe. Al-Nabaʾ asks the question about the Day first and answers it with the list; Sūrat ʿAbasa gives the list first and reaches the Day at verse 33. The shared wording is the shared argument, and the argument is that the One who produced dense orchards from poured water will produce the events that follow.

**A Garden With Walls**

*Ḥadīqah* is not the general word for garden. Arabic has *jannah* for a garden of any kind, including the gardens of Paradise, and *bustān* for an orchard or planted plot. *Ḥadīqah* is etymologically connected to enclosure — the same root gives the word for a barrier or a surrounding wall — and it denotes a garden that is bounded, protected, and delimited. The distinction matters because an enclosed garden is a made thing: somebody marked the boundary, cleared the ground, planted the trees, and kept animals out. The verse names the enclosure rather than the plants inside it, and the naming shifts the description from produce to place.

**Enclosure Implies Cultivation**

The Qur'an distinguishes cultivated from wild growth: *“He is the One Who produces gardens—both cultivated and wild”* (6:141). The enclosed garden of this verse belongs to the cultivated side, and its cultivation is a human act performed on a divine provision. The farmer does not make the rain, the splitting, or the growing; he makes the boundary, the planting, and the care.

## Verse 80:31

> and fruit and fodder—

**A Word That Puzzled the Early Readers**

*Abb* is the difficult term in this verse, and it appears nowhere else in the Qur'an. The standard explanation is fodder — the grass and pasture cut or grazed by animals — which fits the mention of livestock in the next verse. The early community's own uncertainty about the word is recorded: it is reported that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, reciting this sūrah from the pulpit, reached the verse and asked what the *abb* was, then reproached himself for the question as an affectation of knowledge he did not have. [UNVERIFIED: the report is transmitted through the tafsīr tradition, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, without a chain and grade I was able to confirm in a ḥadīth collection] A similar statement is attributed to Abū Bakr about speaking on a word of the Book without knowledge.

**The Human and the Animal in One Verse**

Fruit is for the reader; fodder is for his livestock. Nothing in the verse subordinates the fodder to the fruit, and the next verse names both recipients together. The arrangement reflects an economy in which the animal's feed is a direct part of the household's provision, since the animal supplies milk, meat, transport, wool, and labour. Sūrat Ṭā Hā states the same pairing as an instruction: *“˹so˺ eat and graze your cattle. Surely in this are signs for people of sound judgment”* (20:54).

**Provision for the Provision**

The fodder occupies a second-order position in the economy of the passage. Grain feeds the person directly; fodder feeds the animal that feeds the person. The chain has an extra link, and the extra link is provided in the same act as the rest. Nothing in the verse marks the difference between first-order and second-order provision, and the absence of marking is the point: the provision extends through the whole system rather than stopping at the human mouth. Sūrat al-Naḥl describes the same extension in detail: *“And He created the cattle for you as a source of warmth, food, and ˹many other˺ benefits”* (16:5), followed by the benefits of riding, carrying loads, and the pleasure of the animals coming home (16:6–7). The cattle are themselves a provision, and their food is a provision for the provision. The verse here names the food without naming the animal, and the next verse names both.

**The Same Pair in the Reward**

The vocabulary of this verse reappears in descriptions of Paradise, where the enclosed gardens and vines of the earthly list become the reward: *“Indeed, the righteous will have salvation— Gardens, vineyards”* (78:31–32). The correspondence between the provision and the reward is one of the Qur'an's persistent techniques, and it works by recognizability. The reader is promised what he already knows, in a form that will not fail. Sūrat al-Raḥmān lists *“In it are fruit, palm trees with date stalks”* (55:11) among the favours recounted before the mention of the two gardens. In each case the term *fākihah* carries its sense of chosen enjoyment into the next world, and the enjoyment there is described as uninterrupted. The list in this sūrah is therefore not closed by the fodder; it is closed by a category that continues past the end of the passage.

## Verse 80:32

> all as ˹a means of˺ sustenance for you and your animals

**The Identical Phrase in the Ones Who Tear Out**

Sūrat al-Nāziʿāt closes its own list of provisions with the same sentence: *“all as ˹a means of˺ sustenance for you and your animals”* (79:33). The wording is identical, the position is identical — the end of an agricultural list — and the two sūrahs are neighbours in the received order and belong to the same Makkan phase. Al-Nāziʿāt's list is shorter and runs from the fixing of the mountains to the bringing forth of water and pasture (79:31–33), while this sūrah itemizes the crops in detail. Both close with the same purpose statement, and both go on immediately to the Day of Judgement. The shared formula indicates a settled pattern of argument in the early revelation: enumerate the provision, state its purpose in one line, and turn to the Blast. The pattern appears here in verses 24 to 33 without a single connective marking the turn, which is how strongly the two halves belong together.

**The Animals Inside the Purpose**

The provision is for you *and for your livestock*, and the inclusion is not an afterthought. Sūrat al-Naḥl devotes its opening verses to the cattle and their benefits: warmth, food, beauty, carrying loads to places unreachable without hardship (16:5–7). The animals are a provision in themselves and a consumer of provision, and this verse places them inside the purpose of the whole agricultural order. The pasture was grown for them, and they were created for the household. The arrangement answers an objection that a modern reader may bring to the list — that the fodder is not a human benefit — by stating that the benefit is mediated and that the mediation was intended. A household with animals has milk, meat, wool, transport, and labour, and every one of those comes through the fodder named in the previous verse. The purpose statement covers the chain, not just the last link.

**Temporariness Built Into the Word**

The Qur'an uses this root repeatedly to mark the brevity of worldly benefit: *“˹There is only˺ brief enjoyment in this worldly life, then to Us is your return, and then We will inform you of what you used to do”* (10:23). The same idea governs the descriptions of respite granted to those who reject the message, where enjoyment is given for a term and then ends. Within this sūrah the term has a specific function. The provision is temporary, and the One who supplied it has already been described as the One who kills, buries, and raises. A temporary benefit supplied by a permanent will is the definition of a test, and the test is whether the recipient acknowledges the supplier while the benefit is running. Verse 17 answered that question for the human being as a species — he is ungrateful — and verse 32 supplies the material against which the answer is measured.

## Verse 80:33

> Then, when the Deafening Blast comes to pass—

**A Name Taken From a Sound**

*Al-ṣākhkhah* is an active participle built from *ṣ-kh-kh*, a root denoting a blow or a cry that strikes the ear — the noise so loud that it deafens. The word is usually explained as the piercing cry, the one that reaches the hearing with the force of a blow, and the early commentators add that it will very nearly deafen whoever hears it. The naming is characteristic of Makkan the doctrine of the Last Day, which prefers sounds and physical events to abstractions: the striking disaster, the overwhelming calamity, the inevitable reality, the deafening blast. Each name is a sensation rather than a concept, and the sensation is chosen so that the Day arrives in the body before it arrives in the mind. This sūrah took one of those names as an alternate title, and the tradition records it as al-Ṣākhkhah after this verse, which is a measure of how completely the word came to stand for the whole chapter.

**The Trumpet and Its Two Blasts**

The blast belongs to a fixed Last-Day sequence. *“The Trumpet will be blown and all those in the heavens and all those on the earth will fall dead, except those Allah wills ˹to spare˺. Then it will be blown again and they will rise up at once, looking on ˹in anticipation˺”* (39:68). Two blows: the first kills, the second raises. Sūrat al-Ḥāqqah describes the accompanying upheaval: *“At last, when the Trumpet will be blown with one blast, and the earth and mountains will be lifted up and crushed with one blow”* (69:13–14). The Qur'an's trumpet is blown by an angel, and the tradition names Isrāfīl, though the name does not appear in the Qur'an itself. What the text supplies is the sound and its effects, and both are described in physical terms: a blowing, a falling, a rising, a crushing. This verse adds the sound's quality — that it strikes the ear — to the sequence.

## Verse 80:34

> on that Day every person will flee from their own siblings

**The Same List in the Ways of Ascent**

Sūrat al-Maʿārij describes the same scene and adds what the fleeing person would give to escape: *“˹although˺ they will be made to see each other. The wicked will wish to ransom themselves from the punishment of that Day by their children, their spouses, their siblings, their clan that sheltered them, and everyone on earth altogether, just to save themselves”* (70:11–14). The list there runs in the opposite direction — children, spouse, siblings, clan, everyone — and it discloses the motive this sūrah leaves implicit. The flight is not from the relative but from the cost of the relative: a person would hand over his whole network to save himself. The two passages complement each other exactly. Sūrat ʿAbasa gives the movement, al-Maʿārij gives the reason, and together they describe a Day in which the social self is liquidated by the person who owns it.

**Why a Man Runs From His Brother**

The exegetes supply an answer from the general teaching of the Qur'an about that Day: every person will be asked about what he owes to others, and the brother is a claimant. Rights between people are not dissolved by death, and on the Day they are settled. A man who wronged his brother in this life — over an inheritance, a debt, a slander, a refusal — meets him there as a creditor. The flight is therefore a try to avoid a settlement, and it is futile, because the settlement does not depend on proximity. The Qur'an states the principle without the story: *“˹They state˺ that no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another, and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards”* (53:38–39). No transfer is possible, and a claim that cannot be transferred cannot be avoided by running.

**No Friend Asks About His Friend**

Al-Maʿārij adds a detail that deepens this verse: *“And no close friend will ask ˹about˺ their friends”* (70:10). The close friend — *ḥamīm*, the one whose friendship is warm and intimate — does not inquire. The absence of inquiry is a stronger statement than the absence of help, since help might be impossible while inquiry costs nothing. On that Day even the question is not asked. Sūrat al-Muʾminūn puts it in the same terms: *“Then, when the Trumpet will be blown, there will be no kinship between them on that Day, nor will they ˹even care to˺ ask about one another”* (23:101). Kinship exists and is not only suspended; it is not asked about. This verse states the same fact through the body rather than through the will: the person does not fail to inquire, he runs. The physical description carries what the abstract statement asserts.

**The Flight Achieves Nothing**

The picture is completed by the silence that the Qur'an places over the same gathering. Sūrat al-Nabaʾ describes the Day as one in which the spirit and the angels stand in ranks and nobody speaks except one granted permission by the Most Compassionate, whose words are true (78:38). A crowd running from its own kin and saying nothing is the opposite of every crowd the reader has known.

## Verse 80:35

> and ˹even˺ their mother and father

**The Strongest Command in the Qur'an's Social Law**

Nowhere is a human duty stated more forcefully. *“For your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him. And honour your parents. If one or both of them reach old age in your care, never say to them ˹even˺ ‘ugh,’ nor yell at them. Rather, address them respectfully. And be humble with them out of mercy, and pray, ‘My Lord! Be merciful to them as they raised me when I was young’”* (17:23–24). The command is attached directly to the command of worship, which places it at the head of the social law. *“And We have commanded people to ˹honour˺ their parents. Their mothers bore them through hardship upon hardship, and their weaning takes two years. So be grateful to Me and your parents. To Me is the final return”* (31:14) joins gratitude to parents with gratitude to God in a single sentence. The verse in this sūrah reverses both. The mother and father a person was commanded to honour are the ones he runs from, and the final return named in Luqmān has arrived.

**Kinship Ends at the Trumpet**

The Qur'an states the ending of relations in terms as flat as this verse's flight: *“Then, when the Trumpet will be blown, there will be no kinship between them on that Day, nor will they ˹even care to˺ ask about one another”* (23:101). The word used for kinship there, *ansāb*, is the same word that denotes the genealogies on which Arabian social identity rested. Lineage, the thing a man recited to establish who he was, is named as absent on that Day. This verse makes the same statement physically. The person does not merely lose his genealogy; he moves away from the people who constituted it. The two descriptions are complementary, and together they remove both the legal and the personal dimensions of the bond. What remains is an individual standing in a crowd, which is the condition the Qur'an describes as the condition of the reckoning.

**No Ransom, No Intercession, No Help**

The reason the flight is futile is stated elsewhere in explicit terms: *“Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another. No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given”* (2:48). Three instruments of rescue are named and refused. Help from a stronger party, intercession from a favoured one, and ransom paid from wealth — the three ways a person in danger is ordinarily saved, and all three are unavailable. The parent is precisely the party from whom a child expects the first and the third. A man who has been wronged looks to his family for defence and for payment. This verse places that expectation inside the flight: he runs from the very people who would once have supplied both, because on that Day they can supply neither and their presence would only add a claim to his account. Luqmān's command was to be grateful to God and to the parents together (31:14), and the joining is remarkable: gratitude to a creature is placed inside gratitude to the Creator, and the reason given is that the parents were the means of the life for which the gratitude is owed. The parents belong to that account, since they are part of the ordainment described in verse 19.

The mother's bond is the one the Qur'an stresses most heavily, because it is the one that involved the body: she bore him in hardship upon hardship, she carried him and weaned him over two years, and her bearing and weaning together are counted at thirty months (46:15).

## Verse 80:36

> and ˹even˺ their spouse and children

**Children as the Adornment of This Life**

*Banūn* is the plural of son, and the Qur'an uses it, together with wealth, as the standard pair for what makes a life look successful. *“Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope”* (18:46). *“Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children”* (57:20). The two verses treat children as an adornment and as an object of competition, and both contrast them with what lasts. This verse places them inside the flight, and the placement is the practical form of the same contrast. A man runs from the thing he counted as his adornment, and the running is the demonstration that the counting was mistaken. Nothing in the verse says so; it lists the children after the spouse and lets the list carry the meaning.

**The Word for the Companion**

*Ṣāḥibah* is the feminine of *ṣāḥib*, one who accompanies, and it is used in the Qur'an for a wife with a consistency that makes it a technical term. The word appears in the jinn's declaration about God: *“˹Now, we believe that˺ our Lord—Exalted is His Majesty—has neither taken a mate nor offspring”* (72:3), where the pair *ṣāḥibah* and *walad* is used to deny any companion or child of the divine. The same pair recurs in the Makkan sūrahs as a formula of denial. Its use here is different but relies on the same sense: the companion is the one who shares a life. The choice of a word built on companionship rather than on contract or possession is significant in a verse about the Day, because companionship is the aspect of marriage that the Day ends. The contract is dissolved by death; the companionship is dissolved by the flight.

**The Same Order in the Other Account**

Sūrat al-Maʿārij gives the ransom list in the reverse order: *“The wicked will wish to ransom themselves from the punishment of that Day by their children, their spouses, their siblings, their clan that sheltered them”* (70:11–13). Children come first there and siblings third, while this sūrah runs sibling, parents, spouse, children. The two orders are not contradictory; they reflect different emphases. Al-Maʿārij lists what a person would give up, and the willingness begins with what he holds most expensive, which is why the children come first. Sūrat ʿAbasa lists what he runs from, and the running begins with the nearest relation available and works toward the closest, which is why the children come last. Both orders end on the same point: nothing is held back, and the network a person built over a lifetime is offered or abandoned in full.

Al-Maʿārij's list ends by widening past the family altogether: the clan that sheltered him, and everyone on earth together, given up so that he might be saved (70:14).

## Verse 80:37

> For then everyone will have enough concern of their own

**What the Verse Removes From the Day**

The translation renders *yughnīh* as "will have enough concern of their own," which chooses the sense of preoccupation over the sense of sufficiency. Both are inside the verb, and the choice is defensible: a man whose affair fills him has no room for another's, so the sufficiency and the preoccupation are the same fact described from two sides. The alternative rendering — his affair suffices him — keeps the echo with verse 5 audible to an English reader, at the cost of sounding like comfort. The preoccupation reading avoids that false comfort, and it is the reading most English translations follow. Neither translation alters the point, which is that the capacity for attention has been fully consumed and nothing is left over to be given away.

**The Ḥadīth That Quotes This Verse**

The verse is the answer given by the Prophet to a question from ʿĀʾishah, in a report recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6527 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 5107 with authenticity agreed upon. He said that the people will be assembled barefoot, naked, and uncircumcised. She asked whether men and women would then look at one another. His answer was that the matter would be too serious for them to notice. The report supplies the context in which the early community understood these verses: the Day as a gathering in which the ordinary occasions of shame, modesty, and attention have been removed, because every person's own case has taken the whole of his awareness. The ḥadīth does not quote the verse word for word, but the commentaries connect them, and the connection is exact in substance — a person too occupied to notice what would otherwise be unavoidable.

**Every Account Stands Alone**

The principle is stated as a doctrine elsewhere and is one of the Qur'an's most repeated Last-Day claims: *“˹They state˺ that no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another, and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards”* (53:38–39). No bearing of another's load, no receiving of another's earning. Sūrat al-Baqarah states the same with the instruments of rescue refused: no soul helps another, no intercession accepted, no ransom taken (2:48). This verse supplies the experiential ground of the doctrine: the reason the transfer does not happen is not only that it is forbidden but that nobody is in a position to try it. A person with an affair that fills him has no capacity left for a transaction. The doctrine and the description are two statements of the same fact, one in the language of law and one in the language of attention.

## Verse 80:38

> On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright

**A Face That Has Dawned**

*Musfirah* is the active participle of *asfara*, to become bright, and the verb is used of the dawn — *isfār* is the moment when light spreads and the horizon clears. A face described as *musfirah* has therefore dawned: it has become luminous from within, in the way a sky becomes luminous before the sun appears. The same root gives *safara*, to uncover or unveil, and *sāfir*, which is why the word for the angel-scribes in verse 15 comes from a related form.

**The Same Picture in the Resurrection Sūrah**

Sūrat al-Qiyāmah describes the two groups in nearly identical terms: *“On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, looking at their Lord. And ˹other˺ faces will be gloomy, anticipating something devastating to befall them”* (75:22–25). The correspondence is close enough to read the two passages together, and the difference is instructive. Al-Qiyāmah adds the object of the bright faces' gaze — they look at their Lord — and the object of the gloomy faces' expectation. This sūrah adds the affect: laughing and rejoicing, dusty and cast in gloom. Between them the two passages give the complete picture, and neither is redundant. The shared opening phrase, *wujūhun yawmaʾidhin*, marks the formula as a settled piece of Makkan the doctrine of the Last Day, used whenever the Day is described in the two outcomes.

**Radiance and Joy Granted Together**

Sūrat al-Insān names the two in a single promise: *“So Allah will deliver them from the horror of that Day, and grant them radiance and joy”* (76:11). The pairing there is the same as the pairing in this sūrah's two verses — a bright face and a rejoicing heart — and both are given as gifts rather than achievements.

**The Face That Was Overlooked in Mecca**

The face carries this weight because the Qur'an treats it as the part of a person that endures and the part that perishes. Sūrat al-Raḥmān sets the two against each other in a single breath: *“Every being on earth is bound to perish. Only your Lord Himself, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain ˹forever˺”* (55:26–27). Everything with a face passes, and the passage is stated as a law rather than a lament. On the Day described here the faces have returned, and they have returned as the visible record of what each person did with the time in which his face was perishing. The brightness is not a reward attached to a face from outside; it is the face that a life produced, shown at last without the means of concealment.

## Verse 80:39

> laughing and rejoicing

**The Good News Arrives Before the Joy**

The Qur'an describes the announcement that produces this state: *“Surely those who say, ‘Our Lord is Allah,’ and then remain steadfast, the angels descend upon them, ˹saying,˺ ‘Do not fear, nor grieve. Rather, rejoice in the good news of Paradise, which you have been promised’”* (41:30). The command to rejoice is attached to a removal — do not fear, do not grieve — and then to a gift. Sūrat Āl ʿImrān describes those killed in God's cause as *“rejoicing in Allah's bounties and being delighted for those yet to join them. There will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve”* (3:170), and adds that they are joyful for receiving grace and for the confirmation that God does not deny the reward of the believers (3:171). The rejoicing of this verse is of the same kind: it is a response to something told, not a temperament. The faces laugh because they have been addressed.

**The Laughter Reversed From This World**

Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn records the other direction of the same act: *“Indeed, the wicked used to laugh at the believers, wink to one another whenever they passed by”* (83:29–30), and closes the account with the reversal — *“But on that Day the believers will be laughing at the disbelievers”* (83:34). Laughter in the Qur'an is therefore not a single thing. It is an expression of contempt in this world and an expression of deliverance in the next, and the same muscle performs both. This verse belongs to the second category, and the category is defined by what has been survived. The believers of Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn were laughed at while they were weak and unable to answer; their laughter on the Day is not a retaliation but a release, and it is described in the same physical terms as the laughter here.

**Laughter in the Qur'an Is Rare and Occasioned**

The Book does not use this verb often, and each use is tied to a specific event. Sarah laughs when she is given the news of a son in her old age, and the laugh is followed immediately by the gift: *“And his wife was standing by, so she laughed, then We gave her good news of ˹the birth of˺ Isaac, and, after him, Jacob”* (11:71). Solomon does not laugh but smiles at the ant's speech, and his smile produces a prayer of gratitude: *“So Solomon smiled in amusement at her words, and prayed, ‘My Lord! Inspire me to ˹always˺ be thankful for Your favours which You have blessed me and my parents with’”* (27:19). In both cases the laughter or smiling is a response to something unheard-of that has been granted. This verse's laughing faces belong to the same pattern, and the pattern identifies the cause: something has been given that was not expected.

**Rejoicing as the Opposite of the Frown**

The same form is used of the believers elsewhere in the Qur'an, where the rejoicing is described as their standing characteristic — rejoicing in God's bounties, joyful for the grace received, certain that the reward of the believers will not be denied (3:170–171). The participle fixes the state as permanent, which is the difference between this laughter and the laughter of Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn, where the wicked laughed at the believers in the past tense, in a world that ended.

## Verse 80:40

> while ˹other˺ faces will be dusty

**The Same Pair in the Resurrection Sūrah**

Sūrat al-Qiyāmah sets the two groups in the same order and adds what the dusty faces expect: *“And ˹other˺ faces will be gloomy, anticipating something devastating to befall them”* (75:24–25). The addition supplies the interior state that this sūrah leaves to the following verse, where the dust is described as cast in gloom. Between the two passages the description is complete: a bright face that laughs at news received, and a dusty face that darkens in anticipation of a calamity seen coming. The shared formula — faces on that Day, some such and some otherwise — is one of the fixed patterns of Makkan the doctrine of the Last Day, and its recurrence across neighbouring sūrahs indicates a settled way of describing the outcome rather than a poetic choice made fresh each time.

**Both Groups in the Same Crowd**

The dust and the brightness are visible to each other, and the visibility is part of the description. Sūrat al-Maʿārij states that the people will be made to see one another even while each is fleeing and each is occupied by his own affair (70:11). The Day is not a set of private judgements delivered separately; it is a gathering in which the outcome of every person is public. This verse's dusty faces are dusty in front of the bright ones, and the bright ones are bright in front of them. Nothing in the verse says so explicitly, and nothing needs to, because the whole passage is set in a single place on a single day. The public character of the two conditions is what makes the face the chosen index: a private state would not require a face.

**The Substance They Were Made From**

The Qur'an describes the human being's origin as dust and clay with a consistency that gives this image a second layer. He was created from dust, then from a drop, then formed into a man (18:37); the earth was made a place of return. A face covered in dust on the Day of gathering is therefore a face returned visibly to the material of its beginning, and the return happens while the person is alive and aware. The bright faces have been changed; the dusty faces have been restored to what they were made of. Nothing in the verse states this, and it should not be pressed as the primary sense, which is plainly humiliation.

**A Humiliation That Is Public and Undeniable**

There is also a literal ground for the dust. The Day arrives with the earth itself destroyed: *“At last, when the Trumpet will be blown with one blast, and the earth and mountains will be lifted up and crushed with one blow”* (69:13–14). A world in which the ground and the mountains have been pulverized is a world full of dust, and the gathering takes place in it.

## Verse 80:41

> cast in gloom—

**The Face That Sees What Is Coming**

Sūrat al-Qiyāmah attaches an expectation to the gloomy faces: they are *“anticipating something devastating to befall them”* (75:25). The anticipation explains the darkening better than anything else in the description, since a face that has understood what is about to happen to it changes. The gloom here is therefore not only a mark imposed but a state endured — the person knows, and the knowing is on him. This sūrah leaves the expectation unstated and describes only the result, which is a harder picture: the reader sees the darkness without seeing what produced it. The unstated cause is supplied by everything the chapter has already described, and the supply is immediate, because the blast and the flight and the isolation have been narrated in the preceding eight verses.

**Darkness as the Qur'an's Image of Loss**

Light and its absence are the Qur'an's governing pair of images for guidance and its refusal. The verse of light describes guidance as a lamp lit from a blessed tree, light upon light (24:35), and the passage that follows describes the opposite as a darkness that cannot be seen through: *“Or ˹their deeds are˺ like the darkness in a deep sea, covered by waves upon waves, topped by ˹dark˺ clouds. Darkness upon darkness! If one stretches out their hand, they can hardly see it. And whoever Allah does not bless with light will have no light!”* (24:40). The gloom on these faces belongs to that order. It is not a shadow but the absence of the blessing described in the other passage, and its presence on a face is the visible form of a refusal that was made long before the Day.

**Dust and Gloom in Sequence**

Both descriptions are nominal sentences with no verb, which in Arabic gives them permanence: dust upon them, gloom cast over them. Nothing happens in either verse; a condition simply holds. The absence of a verb removes any sense of process or development, and the removal is what distinguishes these faces from the bright ones, whose brightness is described with a participle that implies a dawning. One group has become something; the other has settled into something. The two grammars correspond to the two states, and the correspondence is achieved without a word of explanation.

The gifts promised to the other group are named elsewhere as radiance and joy (76:11), and the two things missing here are exactly those two. Where radiance should be there is dust; where joy should be there is gloom. The subtraction is complete, and it is stated by description rather than by contrast, so the reader performs it himself.

## Verse 80:42

> those are the disbelievers, the ˹wicked˺ sinners

**Belief and Conduct Named Together**

*Kufr* covers what a person holds, and *fujūr* covers what he does. The exegetes draw the distinction explicitly: disbelief defines the thoughts and wickedness the actions, and the two charges correspond to the two marks on the faces described in the previous verses. The pairing is not redundant. A person may disbelieve without acting wickedly, and the Qur'an acknowledges such people; he may act wickedly while professing belief, and the Qur'an is full of them. The verse describes those in whom both are present, and the completeness is what justifies the intensive plurals. The same pairing appears throughout the Qur'an's descriptions of the two outcomes: *“Indeed, the virtuous will be in bliss, and the wicked will be in Hell”* (82:13–14), where the division is drawn on conduct and belief together rather than on either alone.

**Fujūr in the Qur'an's Moral Vocabulary**

The root *f-j-r* means to break open, to cleave, to burst forth, and it is used of water breaking out of the ground and of dawn breaking — *fajr* is the daybreak, the hour at which the night is split. Applied to a person it denotes a breaking out of the bounds that were set for him, and the Qur'an states that the capacity for it was installed at the beginning: *“And by the soul and ˹the One˺ Who fashioned it, then with ˹the knowledge of˺ right and wrong inspired it!”* (91:7–8). The two possibilities are inspired into the same soul, and the verse that follows makes the outcome depend on what the person does with them. *Fujūr* is therefore not an alien condition imposed from outside but one of two directions built into the creature. That is what makes the charge in this verse so complete: the wicked were made capable of the opposite and chose this.

**An Ending With No Consolation Attached**

Many Makkan sūrahs close with a turn toward the reader or the Prophet: take the path back to your Lord (78:39), the tranquil soul called to return well-pleased (89:27–28), a command to prostrate and draw near. This one does not. It ends on the class of the disbelievers and the wicked, and the last word is the second of the two charges. The absence of consolation is a choice, and it suits a sūrah that began with a rebuke. The chapter is addressed throughout to a person who needs correcting — a Messenger about attention, a reader about ingratitude — and it ends by showing the outcome of the uncorrected state without softening it. What remains after the last word is the picture the sūrah has been building since the first verse: a face that contracted against a blind man who came asking, and the faces that contract and the faces that shine on the Day when every person's affair suffices him.
