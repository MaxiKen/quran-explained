# Sūrah al-Layl (Chapter 92) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Enshrouding Night and the Divergent Paths**

Al-Layl, "The Night," is the ninety-second chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 21 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with three cosmic and biological oaths: *“By the night when it covers, and by the day when it shines, and by the creation of male and female—indeed, your efforts are diverse / divergent (*inna saʿyakum la-shattā*)!”* (92:1–4). In classical exegesis, Sūrah al-Layl is universally celebrated for verses 17–21, which immortalize the peerless spiritual character of Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq: purchasing and emancipating tortured Muslim slaves (such as Bilāl and ʿĀmir ibn Fuhayrah) out of pure, unadulterated love for God, crowned with the divine guarantee: *“And he will surely be well-pleased (*wa-la-sawfa yarḍā*)!”* In twenty-one balanced, rhythmic verses, Sūrah al-Layl constructs an unshakeable moral binary: contrasting the path of generosity, piety, and ease against the path of miserliness, false self-sufficiency, and ruin.

**The Slave-Market of Makkah and the Mercy of Abū Bakr**

The historical occasion of Sūrah al-Layl is recorded in authentic traditions by Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿUrwah ibn al-Zubayr, and al-Qurṭubī. In the rocky alleys of Makkah, wealthy pagan masters were torturing their enslaved converts to force their apostasy. Abū Bakr used his vast mercantile wealth to purchase these helpless slaves—weak men, elderly women, and vulnerable girls—freeing them immediately for the sake of Allah. His father, Abū Quḥāfah (then still an idolater), chided him: *“O my son, I see you freeing weak, helpless people! Why do you not buy strong, virile young men who could stand with you, defend you, and protect you?”* Abū Bakr replied with breathtaking spiritual purity: *“O my father, I only seek what is with Allah!”* Verses 17–21 descended as an eternal celestial tribute to his sincerity: *“And the most God-fearing will be spared it [the Fire]—he who gives his wealth to purify himself, and not for any favor owed to him for which he seeks recompense, but only seeking the Face of his Lord, the Most High; and he will surely be well-pleased!”*

**The Structural Symmetry of the Two Paths**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Layl is an astonishing masterwork of parallel moral symmetry across three movements:
1. The Two Engines of Human Destiny (1–11): Opens with the oaths of night, day, and the male and female pairs, establishing that human efforts are radically divergent (*la-shattā*). It outlines the two psychological trajectories:
   - *The Path of Ease* (5–7): As for he who gives in charity (*aʿṭā*), maintains God-consciousness (*wattaqā*), and confirms the ultimate good (*wa-ṣaddaqa bi-l-ḥusnā*)—*“We will ease him toward the ease (*fa-sa-nuyassiruhū li-l-yusrā*)!”*
   - *The Path of Difficulty* (8–11): But as for he who is a tight-fisted miser (*bakhila*), considers himself self-sufficient (*wastaghnā*), and denies the ultimate good (*wa-kadhdhaba bi-l-ḥusnā*)—*“We will ease him toward hardship (*fa-sa-nuyassiruhū li-l-ʿusrā*)!”* And what will his wealth avail him when he falls into the grave?
2. The Divine Guidance and the Blazing Fire (12–16): Affirms that guidance is upon God, and that to Him belongs the Hereafter and the first life. It issues the urgent warning: *“So I have warned you of a Fire which blazes (*nāran talaẓẓā*)!”* None will burn therein except the most wretched who denied and turned away.
3. The Archetype of Sincere Charity (17–21): The noble portrait of the God-fearing servant (*al-atqā*) who spends his wealth to purify his soul, seeking neither worldly gratitude nor repaying a social debt, but desiring only the Countenance of his Lord, the Most High (*wajhi Rabbihi al-Aʿlā*), concluding with the divine pledge of absolute satisfaction: *“Wa-la-sawfa yarḍā”* (And he will surely be well-pleased!).

**The Psychology of Facilitation: Yusrā and ʿUsrā**

The profound philosophical insight of Sūrah al-Layl is the concept of *Taysīr*—divine facilitation. Human beings often wonder why doing good feels easy and joyful for some, while for others, prayer and charity feel like an agonizing impossibility. Sūrah al-Layl reveals the secret: when a person takes the first step of generosity (*aʿṭā*), reverence (*ittaqa*), and faith (*ṣaddaqa*), God miraculously softens his inner nature, opening his spiritual arteries and making righteousness easy and delightful for him (*al-Yusrā*). But when a person hoards his wealth, puffs himself up with self-sufficiency (*istaghnā*), and denies truth, God leaves him to his own arrogance, making every evil easy for him and every virtue impossibly heavy (*al-ʿUsrā*). Sūrah al-Layl calls the soul to step onto the path of generous surrender, trusting that the Lord of the worlds will ease its journey into eternal light.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:1

> By the night when it covers

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath That Opens in Darkness**

The chapter begins with the night, and the choice sets the tone for everything that follows. Most of the Qur'an's oath lists begin with the greater term — the sun before the moon, the day before the night — and this one reverses the order. The night is named first, with the verb of covering attached to it, and the day follows as the term that discloses. The reversal is not arbitrary. A chapter about the diversity of human striving opens with the state in which striving stops, and the contrast between the two terms is what the chapter's argument runs on. The reader is placed first in the dark, and then shown the light, and the movement from one to the other is the pattern the rest of the sūrah will apply to human conduct.

The reversal also does something to the reader's expectation. A person who opens a chapter expecting light and is given darkness is put in the position of someone waiting, and waiting is the state the chapter's second case will be contrasted against. The first word therefore sets a posture before it sets a subject.

**The Object Left Unstated**

The verb of covering has no expressed object, and the omission has been read in several ways. The night may cover the world, the earth, the day itself, or all of creation. The omission is not a gap but a device. An unspecified object makes the covering total: whatever exists is inside it. The Qur'an uses a comparable construction elsewhere in its oath pairs, and the effect is the same — the night is presented not as a period but as an act performed on everything. The reader is inside the object of the verb. He cannot stand outside the covering to observe it, and the impossibility is part of what the oath invokes. A statement guaranteed by the night is guaranteed by something the hearer is enclosed in.

Grammatically the omission is common in Arabic oath constructions, where the verb of covering is left to supply its own object from context. What matters here is the effect: the covering is presented as unbounded. Nothing is exempted from it, and the reader cannot locate himself outside the scope of the verse.

**Covering as a Provision**

The night's covering is a mercy in the Qur'an's account, and the chapter relies on that fact. God "has made the night for rest" (6:96), and the alternation of night and day is presented as a design for the creatures living inside it. A world without covering would allow no sleep, no privacy and no end to exposure. The verse therefore swears on a kindness, and the kindness is the ground of the demand that follows. A person addressed by a God who arranged his rest can be asked about his striving, because the asking comes from a source that has already provided for him. The oath is not a threat dressed as a solemnity. It is a reminder of provision, placed at the head of a chapter that will ask what the provision was for.

That provision has a cost attached to it, and the chapter will name the cost. Rest is given so that striving can continue; a person who has been rested has no ground for idleness. The oath on the covering night is therefore not only a comfort but the first item in an account the reader will be asked to settle.

**The Oath Pair and Its Companions**

Swearing by the alternation of night and day is a recurring form in the Qur'an. The chapter that precedes this one opens with the sun and its brightness, the moon following it, the day disclosing and the night covering (91:1–4). The chapter of the Morning Light swears by the forenoon and the night when it falls still (93:1–2). The sūrah of the Dawn opens with the dawn and nights numbered. The repetition establishes a habit of thought: the alternation is a sign, and the sign is available to everyone. The verse in this chapter belongs to that family, and its position at the head of the list gives it a specific job. It supplies the first half of the contrast on which the chapter's central claim — that human striving diverges — will be built.

What distinguishes this chapter's pair is the order and the brevity. Two verbs, two states, and no elaboration. The reader is expected to supply the rest from experience, and the expectation is safe, because every hearer has watched the alternation for as long as he has been alive.

**Night as the Hour of What Is Hidden**

The covering has a moral dimension that the chapter exploits later. The night is when acts are performed without witnesses, and the Qur'an's ethics rank the hidden deed above the visible one: "To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you of your sins" (2:271). The ranking depends on the existence of a night in which hiding is possible. A world that never covered its inhabitants would make display unavoidable, and sincerity would be impossible to distinguish from performance. The oath on the covering night therefore invokes the condition that makes the chapter's later praise of unobtrusive charity intelligible. The giver described at the end of this sūrah gives without seeking a return from anyone, and the night is part of why such giving can exist.

The chapter's later praise of the giver who seeks no return belongs to this register. A gift performed where nobody sees it can only be motivated by something other than opinion, and the night is the condition in which such a gift is possible. The oath has already honoured that condition before the giver is described.

**What the Oath Prepares**

Three oaths will be taken before the chapter states what it is about, and the first of them is the least obvious. A reader approaching the verse for the first time may wonder what the night has to do with generosity and stinginess. The connection is established by the end of the chapter, and it is not allegorical. The night covers, the day discloses, and human beings strive in both. What a person does when he is covered and what he does when he is disclosed are the two measures of his conduct, and the chapter will describe both kinds of person. The oath is therefore a preparation rather than a decoration, and its darkness is the first term in a contrast that the reader will be asked to apply to himself.

The frame is fixed and the frame is not the reader's doing. He did not arrange the alternation, and he cannot suspend it. Whatever he strives for, he strives inside an order set for him, and the chapter's fourth verse will state that the striving diverges. The oath has established the setting in which the divergence matters.

**A Chapter Named After the Dark**

The sūrah takes its title from this verse, and the naming is worth noticing. Most chapters are named after something distinctive in their text — a mountain, an animal, a figure, a word — and this one is named after the night. The title is therefore the first thing a reader meets, and it is the darker of the two terms the chapter swears by. The naming is consistent with the chapter's method, which begins in concealment and moves toward disclosure. A reader who recites the sūrah by name is naming the night, and the naming prepares the movement the chapter performs. Nothing in the title promises light, and the light arrives as the second term rather than the first.

The title therefore works on a reader before he reads a word. He names the chapter, and the name he uses is the name of the state in which the chapter's first case is tested. Nothing about the naming is decorative, and the reader who recites the sūrah regularly is rehearsing the movement it performs.

**Darkness First, in a Dark Period**

The chapter belongs to the early Meccan period, and the ordering of its oath matches its occasion. The Muslims at that time were few, unprotected and exposed to a city that intended to stop them. A chapter that opens with the covering night is speaking to people inside a covering. The verse is therefore not a neutral cosmological remark. It names the state its audience was living in, and it names it before it offers anything else. The reader who meets the verse in comfort reads a description of the sky; the reader who meets it under pressure reads a description of his own situation, and the description is the beginning of the consolation rather than its opposite.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:2

> and the day when it shines

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Term Completes the Contrast**

The day arrives in the second verse with a verb of brightness, and the pair is now complete. The night covers; the day shines. The two verbs are opposites, and the chapter's argument is built on the opposition. Nothing between them is neutral. A world that alternates between concealment and disclosure produces a life in which conduct is alternately hidden and exposed, and the chapter will ask what a person does in each state. The verse is short and its function is structural, but the structure it serves is the whole of the sūrah's moral reasoning.

The brevity of the verse is itself a device. Two terms, two verbs, and no comment attached. The chapter states the contrast and moves on, trusting that the reader will supply the significance from his own experience of the two states. What is not explained cannot be argued with.

**Tajallā: Brightness as Unveiling**

The verb *tajallā* describes a brightness that reveals — the clearing of a sky, the full appearance of something previously hidden. It is the same family of meaning as the verb used in the preceding chapter for the day's disclosure (91:3), and the two chapters are close enough in date that the shared vocabulary is significant. The day in this verse is not merely a period of light but an act of unveiling, and the object is the world that the night had covered. The alternation is therefore described as a pair of actions performed on the same object, and the actions are performed in turn without failure. The reader is watching a system, and the system is the guarantee behind the statement that follows.

The verb's sense of clearing is important for the moral reading that follows. What is unveiled is not created by the unveiling; it was there, hidden. The daylight does not produce a person's conduct, it displays it, and the display is what makes conduct a matter of record rather than of intention alone.

**Daylight as the Time of Earning**

The day's brightness has a practical consequence. It is when work is done: fields are tended, markets are open, goods are moved, and the household's provision is earned. The Qur'an assigns the two states their functions plainly — God "has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision" (6:96). The verse in this chapter swears on the earning half of that arrangement, and the swearing carries a claim. The same daylight that enables a person to feed his family exposes what he does while earning. The chapter's later description of the generous and the stingy is a description of conduct performed in daylight, in public, where other people can see it and respond to it.

The exposure is not limited to other people, and the chapter assumes the fact without stating it. A deed performed in daylight has witnesses, and the chief witness is not human. The verse swears on the hour in which that witnessing operates, and the swearing is part of the chapter's method of establishing accountability.

**The Alternation as a Sign**

The Qur'an treats the alternation of night and day as evidence rather than as background. "Among His signs are the day and the night, the sun and the moon" (41:37). "He wraps the night around the day, and wraps the day around the night" (39:5). The verse in this chapter is one instance of a pattern, and the pattern is doing argumentative work. A system that alternates with perfect reliability implies a disposer who maintains it, and the disposer is named in the very next verse. The reader is led from the reliability of the alternation to the authority of the One who arranged it, and the lead is accomplished in two short verses. Nothing in the movement requires a premise the hearer does not already accept from observation.

The movement from reliability to authority is accomplished without a stated premise. A reader who notices that the alternation has never failed has already granted that something maintains it, and the next verse names the maintainer. The oath list is therefore an argument conducted entirely in observations the reader cannot dispute.

**Brightness and Moral Visibility**

The chapter's moral vocabulary draws on the physical terms it has sworn by. What is done in the light is visible; what is done in the dark is not. The Qur'an's ethics use both states, and it does not treat visibility as the measure of worth. The hidden gift ranks above the displayed one, and the display of generosity is criticised where it is performed for an audience: those "who donate their wealth just to show off" are compared to a barren rock whose film of soil is washed away by rain (2:264). The day's brightness therefore makes conduct visible without making it good. The chapter will describe a person who gives in a way that seeks no return from anyone, and the description belongs to the night's register as much as to the day's.

The distinction between visibility and worth is one the chapter insists on. Brightness makes an act knowable; it does not make it good. The reader who performs his generosity in the disclosed hours and nowhere else has used the day for what it can give him, and the chapter will describe a person who does not.

**What the Two Verses Together Establish**

Read as a unit, the first two verses establish the frame in which human striving occurs. There is a state in which a person is hidden and a state in which he is exposed, and both are arranged by a power he does not control. The chapter's central claim — that human ends diverge — will be made inside that frame. A reader who accepts the frame has accepted the premise the claim needs: that his conduct occurs in conditions set for him, and that the conditions include both concealment and exposure. What he does in each is his own, and the divergence the chapter describes is a divergence of choices made inside a fixed arrangement. The oath has supplied the arrangement; the following verses supply the choices.

The frame includes both concealment and exposure, and the reader is inside both. What he does in each state is his own contribution, and the contribution is what the chapter measures. Nothing about the frame excuses the conduct performed inside it, and nothing about the frame determines that conduct either.

**Nothing Is Lost in the Covering**

The two verses describe a covering and a disclosure of the same object, and the sequence implies that what is covered remains. The night does not destroy the world; it hides it, and the day returns it. The alternation is therefore a guarantee rather than a loss. A reader who applies the image to his own condition finds the point useful: a state of concealment is not a state of destruction, and what is hidden is not gone. The chapter's argument about striving operates inside that guarantee. A person whose direction is not visible to others has not lost it, and the day that discloses will disclose it. The alternation the chapter swears by is the frame in which hidden conduct is preserved rather than erased.

**The Ordinary Day as the Measure of a Life**

The alternation is not an occasional event but the rhythm of every life. A person passes through concealment and disclosure daily, and the chapter swears on that rhythm rather than on a rare phenomenon. The choice makes the oath applicable to everyone at every moment. A reader cannot say that the sign is remote, since he passed through it last night and will pass through it again tonight. The measure of his conduct is therefore set by the most familiar feature of his existence, and the familiarity is what makes the measure inescapable. Whatever he does in the covered hours and whatever he does in the disclosed hours together make up the striving the chapter is about to describe.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:3

> And by ˹the One˺ Who created male and female

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Creator Enters the Oath**

Two oaths on created states, and then a third on the One who creates. The shift is the same one the preceding chapter makes when it moves from the sun and the night to the Builder of the heaven. A reader who has granted the reliability of the alternation has been prepared for the naming of the disposer, and the naming arrives in the third verse. The oath is no longer on a feature of the world but on the source of a feature — specifically, the source of the most basic distinction in the created order. The chapter's argument about human striving will rest on this verse, because the authority to judge striving belongs to the One who made the strivers.

The naming of the disposer is the point at which the oath list acquires an authority. Before this verse the reader has been shown features of the world; after it he has been introduced to the One responsible for the feature that matters most to the argument — the existence of the strivers themselves.

**The Grammatical Reading and Its Alternative**

The Arabic word here is *mā*, which can function as "the One who" or as "that which". On the second reading the verse would swear by the creation of the male and the female rather than by their Creator, and some exegetes preferred that reading on the ground that God does not swear by Himself. The same question arises in the three parallel verses of the preceding chapter, and the same answer applies. The personal reading is the more natural in Arabic, and the theological difficulty is not serious: God swears by what He wills, and swearing by His own act of creation is a way of magnifying it. The consistency of the construction across both chapters supports the personal reading in all of them.

The alternative reading survives in the tradition for a reason worth stating: it protects the principle that creatures swear by their Creator and not the reverse. The principle is sound, and the personal reading does not violate it, since God's swearing by His own act magnifies the act rather than subjecting the swearer.

**The Pair as the Basic Form of Creation**

The male and the female are named as the fundamental pairing of the created order. The Qur'an states the same fact elsewhere: "O humanity! Indeed, We created you from a male and a female, and made you into peoples and tribes so that you may ˹get to˺ know one another" (49:13), and again "O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women" (4:1). The pairing is not presented as a hierarchy in these verses. It is presented as the mechanism by which the population exists at all. The oath in this verse swears on that mechanism, and the swearing honours both terms equally.

The mechanism is stated in these verses without hierarchy, and the absence of hierarchy is what the chapter's argument requires. A reader who arrives at the description of two kinds of person must not be able to sort them by birth, and the oath has already removed birth from the available categories.

**What the Oath Excludes**

Placing the creation of male and female inside an oath removes both terms from the category of achievements. Neither sex is earned, chosen or attained, and neither can be cited as a ground of superiority. The verse that follows the oath states the principle explicitly in the sūrah of the Rooms: "Surely the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous among you" (49:13). The chapter in front of us makes the same point by its structure. It swears by the creation of the pair and then describes two kinds of person — the one who gives and the one who withholds — without reference to sex, lineage or status. The categories that matter in the chapter's argument are entirely behavioural, and the oath has already excluded the others.

The exclusion operates silently, which is what makes it effective. Nothing in the verse argues against claims of superiority; it simply swears by the creation of both terms, and the swearing places them on the same footing before the argument begins. A reader cannot rank what has been sworn by as one act.

**Outward Polarity and Inward Polarity**

The exegetes note a pattern in this chapter's oaths. The outward polarity of creation — night and day, male and female — is followed by an inward polarity: the one who gives and fears, and the one who withholds and denies. The pattern is deliberate. A world built on pairs is a world in which distinction is fundamental, and the chapter transfers the logic of distinction from the body to the soul. The reader who accepts that the created order is arranged in complementary pairs is being prepared to accept that human beings are arranged in two directions of striving. The transfer is not an argument but a habit of thought, and the oath list establishes the habit before the claim is made.

The transfer from outward pairs to inward directions is the chapter's central move, and it is prepared rather than performed here. Once a reader accepts that distinction is built into the created order, the claim that striving is distinguished no longer sounds like a moralising assertion. It sounds like a description.

**Why This Oath, in This Chapter**

The chapter's subject is generosity and stinginess, and the oath on the creation of the pair is not obviously connected to it. The connection is one of dependence. Both terms of the pair are created, and neither is self-sufficient. A person who has been made by another, in a form he did not choose, and who depends on others of his kind for his own existence, is not in a position to claim independence from the One who made him. The chapter's description of the stingy person includes exactly that claim — he deems himself beyond need — and the oath has already answered it. Before the argument begins, the reader has been told that he was created, paired and dependent, and the information is placed in the form of a solemnity so that it cannot be skipped.

Dependence is the fact the oath establishes, and dependence is what the second case denies. A person who deems himself beyond need has contradicted the third verse as well as the fifth, and the contradiction is visible to anyone who reads the two together. The oath has answered the claim before it is made.

**The Pair as the Mechanism of the Population**

The oath names the pair rather than the individual, and the choice is deliberate. No human being exists except through the pair, and the fact is stated elsewhere as the ground of a common obligation: "O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women" (4:1). The verse in this chapter swears on that mechanism, and the swearing honours both terms equally. Neither is the origin of the other in the sense that would justify a hierarchy, and the chapter's later argument makes no use of sex as a category. The reader who arrives at the description of the giver and the withholder is reading about two dispositions, not about two kinds of people by birth.

**An Oath on Creating Rather Than on a Creature**

The third oath differs from the first two in kind. The night and the day are created states; the third is the One who creates. The shift raises the register of the whole list, and it does so at the point where the chapter is about to make its central claim. A statement about the diversity of human striving requires an authority competent to judge striving, and the authority is named here. The reader who accepts the first two oaths has accepted that a disposer arranges the alternation; the third oath names the disposer and attaches him to the creation of the strivers themselves. The claim that follows is therefore made under the strongest guarantee the chapter can supply.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:4

> Surely the ends you strive for are diverse

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Statement the Oaths Were Taken For**

Three oaths, and then the claim they guarantee. The verse is the answer to the swearing, and it is stated in four words: your striving is diverse. The emphatic particle marks the statement as settled, and the word for striving — *saʿy* — denotes effort with a direction, not movement in general. The claim is not that people differ in temperament or taste. It is that they are working toward different ends, and that the difference is fundamental enough to divide them into two groups. The verse supplies the thesis of the chapter, and the ten verses that follow are its exposition.

The four words carry the whole thesis, and their brevity means the reader meets the claim before he has formed a resistance to it. The oaths have done the preparatory work; the claim arrives as a plain statement of fact, and the elaboration follows over the next ten verses rather than preceding it.

**Saʿy: Effort With a Destination**

The word chosen is significant. *Saʿy* is used in the Qur'an for purposeful exertion, and it appears in the description of the rite performed between the two hills during pilgrimage — a walking that is neither a stroll nor a race but a directed effort with a fixed end. Applied to a life, the word implies that every person is moving toward something, and that the movement has a terminus. The chapter's claim is that the termini differ. Nobody in the verse is described as standing still. The division is between directions, not between activity and idleness, and the reader who assumes that the inactive person belongs to neither group has misread the word.

The word's religious use reinforces the point. A rite performed between two fixed points is a directed effort with a terminus, and the same word applied to a life implies that the terminus exists whether or not the person has identified it. The chapter's claim is that people differ about where they are going.

**The Two Ends, and Nothing Between Them**

The exegetes read the diversity as a division into two rather than a spectrum. People work either toward redemption or toward ruin, and there is no intermediate destination. The reading is supported by the structure of the chapter, which describes exactly two cases in symmetrical terms and assigns each a facilitated path. The claim is stark, and its starkness is the point. A person who believes that most lives end in a middle state has no reason to examine his direction; a person who believes there are two ends has a reason to check. The verse does not argue for the division. It states it, and the stating is what gives the following ten verses their urgency.

The absence of an intermediate destination is what gives the descriptions their force. A reader who believes in a middle outcome can defer the question indefinitely; a reader who believes there are two ends has to answer it now. The verse supplies the second belief, and it supplies it without argument.

**Why the Claim Needs an Oath**

A statement about the diversity of human ends is not self-evident. From the outside, most lives look similar: people work, accumulate, raise families and die. The claim that these similar lives are directed toward different ends is a claim about the invisible, and it requires a guarantee. The three oaths supply it, and they supply it in a particular way. The alternation of night and day is a distinction maintained without visible effort; the creation of male and female is a distinction built into the origin of the population. Both are pairs that everybody accepts. The oath list therefore establishes that distinction is a feature of the created order before it claims that human striving is distinguished. The reader who grants the first has no consistent ground for refusing the second.

The guarantee is built from pairs the reader already accepts, and the method is why the claim can be so short. A long argument would invite counter-argument; a claim resting on accepted distinctions does not. The reader who grants night and day, male and female, has granted the logic the claim uses.

**The Verse Against the Illusion of a Common Destination**

The claim contradicts an assumption that most people hold without examining it: that everyone is ultimately going to the same place by different routes. The verse denies the assumption, and the denial is the chapter's most uncomfortable statement. It implies that the neighbour who appears to be living the same life is not living it toward the same end, and that the difference is not a matter of opinion. The chapter does not soften the claim with a discussion of cases or exceptions. It states the diversity and then describes the two directions in enough detail for a reader to locate himself. The locating is the purpose of the statement, and it is why the verse is placed immediately after the oaths rather than later in the argument.

The assumption is comfortable and the verse removes the comfort. Similar lives are not similar destinations, and the difference is not visible from outside. The chapter does not soften this by adding exceptions or by describing cases; it states the diversity and expects the reader to test himself against it.

**What the Reader Is Asked to Do With the Claim**

The verse is addressed to a plural — your striving — and the plural includes the reader. The claim is not about other people's ends but about the diversity of ends in which the reader's own is included. The chapter's method is to describe both cases in enough detail that self-location becomes possible, and the description begins in the next verse. A reader who treats the verse as a sociological observation about humanity has taken nothing from it. The verse asks a question that can only be answered personally: which end is your striving directed toward? The answer is not given by profession or by feeling. It is given by the two descriptions that follow, and the reader is expected to test himself against them.

The question is unavoidable once the reader accepts the frame. His striving has a direction, and the direction is discoverable from his conduct rather than from his intentions. The two descriptions that follow supply the means of discovery, and the chapter expects him to use them on himself rather than on his neighbours.

**Diversity Stated Without Approval or Complaint**

The verse records a fact and attaches no evaluation to it. Human striving is diverse; the verse does not say that the diversity is unfortunate, nor that one of the directions is more natural than the other. The neutrality is a rhetorical device. A reader who meets a claim stated without emotion is more likely to examine it, because he is not being asked to agree with a judgement. The evaluation arrives afterwards, in the description of the two cases, and by then the reader has already accepted the fact of divergence. The chapter's method throughout is to establish a premise quietly and then draw the consequence, and this verse is the quietest premise in the sūrah.

**No Middle Destination**

The exegetes read the diversity as a division into two ends, and the reading is supported by the structure of the chapter. Ten verses follow, and they describe exactly two cases in symmetrical terms, each with a facilitated path and each with a final state. There is no third case, and no verse in the chapter describes a person who is neither. The claim is severe, and it is stated without qualification. A reader who assumes that most lives end somewhere in the middle is holding a view the chapter does not share, and the chapter expects him to test the assumption against the two descriptions rather than against his feelings. The absence of a middle term is what gives the descriptions their urgency.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:5

> As for the one who is charitable, mindful ˹of Allah˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Case Begins With Giving**

The chapter divides humanity into two, and the first case it describes begins with an act of expenditure. Not belief, not prayer, not knowledge — giving. The ordering is a judgement about where a person's direction becomes visible. Belief is interior and can be claimed; prayer is regular and can be performed out of habit; giving costs something and cannot be faked for long. The verse therefore identifies the first group by the act that most reliably reveals a direction. The reader who asks how to tell which of the two ends he is moving toward is directed to his expenditure before he is directed to his convictions.

The ordering is a claim about evidence. Convictions are invisible and can be professed by anyone; expenditure is visible and cannot be sustained by a person who does not mean it. The chapter therefore begins its positive case with the test that is hardest to fake, and the reader is directed there first.

**Aṭā: Giving Without Specification**

The verb is general. It does not specify what is given, to whom, or in what amount, and the generality is deliberate. Giving covers charity in wealth, help in kind, time spent on another's need, and — as some exegetes note — the giving to God of what God is owed. The absence of specification widens the category so that no reader can exclude himself on grounds of poverty. A person with nothing to give can still give attention, effort or protection, and the verse's first term is available to him. The chapter's later verses will describe the manner of the giving in detail; this verse establishes the disposition, and the disposition is not a function of wealth.

The generality is deliberate and it widens the category beyond wealth. Attention given to a person in distress, effort spent on another's need, protection offered to someone who cannot protect himself — all of these are forms of giving, and the verse's first term covers them without qualification.

**Ittaqā: Mindfulness as the Second Term**

The second quality is *taqwā*, and the word is difficult to render because it combines fear, care and consciousness. A person with *taqwā* is one who guards himself against what would harm him in God's reckoning, and the guarding shapes his conduct continuously rather than at intervals. The pairing of giving with mindfulness is significant. Giving without mindfulness can be performance or impulse; mindfulness without giving can be a private state with no effect on anyone. The verse joins them, and the joining is the description of the first group. A reader who has one of the two and not the other has not been described by this verse, and the chapter expects him to notice which one is missing.

The guarding is continuous rather than occasional, and that continuity is what distinguishes it from remorse. A person with mindfulness does not feel badly at intervals; he arranges his conduct around a reckoning he expects. The arrangement shapes small decisions, and the small decisions are where the chapter's division is decided.

**The Order of the Two Qualities**

Giving is named first, and the order has been read as an indication that the outward act is the visible form of the inward state. The exegetes also note that the sequence matches the ordinary path: a person begins by giving because he has been told to, and the giving produces the mindfulness that sustains it. The reverse sequence — a person who becomes mindful and then begins to give — is equally possible, and the verse does not decide between them. What it insists on is that both are present. A reader who waits for a state of mindfulness before he begins to give may wait indefinitely, and the verse's ordering suggests that the giving is where the state is formed.

Neither sequence is privileged by the verse, and the openness is practical. A reader who waits for the inward state before acting may never act; a reader who acts may find the state forming inside the action. The chapter describes the configuration, not the route by which a person arrives at it.

**The Report Connected With These Verses**

The tradition links this passage to a discussion about destiny. A report agreed upon by al-Bukhārī and Muslim records the companions asking the Prophet ﷺ whether deeds matter if the places of the people of Paradise and the Fire are already registered, and his answer: work, for everyone will be facilitated toward that for which he was created. The exegetes connect that answer to these very verses, since the chapter goes on to describe facilitation in both directions. The connection is important for reading the verse correctly. The description of the giver is not a description of a person who was made to give. It is a description of a person who gives, and the facilitation promised in the seventh verse is the consequence of the giving rather than its substitute.

The link matters because it prevents a fatalistic reading of the chapter. The facilitation promised two verses later is a response to conduct, and the report connects the two without collapsing them. A person is eased toward what he was created for, and what he was created for is expressed in what he does.

**A Case Described, Not a Person Named**

The verse uses an indefinite — the one who gives — and the indefiniteness keeps the description open. No name is attached, no tribe is identified, and no historical occasion is required. The chapter is describing a type, and the type is defined by conduct that any reader can check in himself. The openness is what makes the verse usable. A reader who looks for a named exemplar can always conclude that the description applies to someone else; a reader confronted with a type has nowhere to put the description except on himself. The chapter's method throughout is to describe rather than to name, and the method reaches its fullest form at the end, where the giver is described without any reference to who he was.

The openness is what makes the description usable. A named exemplar can be admired from a distance; a type has to be matched against one's own conduct. The chapter's method throughout is to describe rather than to name, and the method leaves the reader with nowhere to put the description but on himself.

**Three Qualities That Form One Disposition**

The verse lists three items, and the list is not a set of separate achievements. Giving, mindfulness and belief describe a single orientation from three angles: what the person does with his property, how he stands in relation to God, and what he expects. Remove any one and the other two change character. A person who gives without mindfulness is impulsive; a person who is mindful without giving is inert; a person who believes without either has a conviction with no effect. The chapter describes the configuration, and the configuration is what identifies the group. The reader who checks himself against the list is checking a pattern rather than a score.

**The Description Is Open to the Poor**

Nothing in the verse requires wealth. The verb of giving is general, and the object is unspecified, which means the category includes a person who gives his time, his effort or his protection. The chapter's later verse describes the giving of wealth, and the description is illustrative rather than restrictive. A reader who concludes that the first case belongs to the rich has misread the verb. The man who buys a weak slave in order to free him is the example the tradition supplies, and the purchase required means; the man who shields a neighbour from harm requires none. Both are described by this verse, and the inclusiveness is what makes the chapter's division applicable to everyone who reads it.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:6

> and ˹firmly˺ believes in the finest reward

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Ḥusnā: The Finest Thing**

The third quality of the first group is belief in *al-ḥusnā*, the feminine superlative of good — the finest, the most excellent. The word is definite, which means it refers to something specific rather than to goodness in general, and the exegetes have identified it in several ways. Most take it as the testimony that there is no god but God, or belief in God simply. Others take it as Paradise, since the Qur'an calls the reward of the Hereafter "the finest" in several places. A third reading takes it as God Himself, on the ground that the most beautiful names belong to Him. The readings converge more than they diverge: the finest thing is the ultimate object of belief, and believing in it is what orients a life.

The definiteness of the word excludes a general optimism about goodness. The finest is a specific object, and the readings differ about its identity rather than about its ultimacy. A reader who believes in goodness in the abstract has not been described by this verse; the verse requires an object.

**Belief as the Third Term, Not the First**

The verse places belief after giving and mindfulness, and the placement is instructive. A reader might expect the chapter to begin with faith, since faith is the ground of acceptance in the Qur'an's theology. Instead the first case is described by what a person does, and belief is named third. The order does not demote belief. It locates it. Belief in the finest reward is the conviction that gives the other two qualities their direction: a person gives because he believes there is a return, and he guards himself because he believes the return is real. The verse describes the three as a single configuration, and the configuration is what identifies the group.

The placement locates belief inside a life rather than ahead of it. A person does not first acquire a complete conviction and then begin to act; he acts, and the acting clarifies the conviction. The verse describes the configuration as it exists in a person who is already giving, and the description is therefore realistic.

**Why the Reward Rather Than the Punishment**

The belief specified here is in the finest reward, not in the punishment. The chapter mentions the Fire later, and its description of the second group includes denial of the same reward. The emphasis on reward in the positive case is a feature of the Qur'an's method in the short Meccan chapters: the good is described by what it leads to, and the leading is attractive rather than frightening. A person who gives because he fears is performing a calculation under threat; a person who gives because he believes in the finest thing is acting out of desire. The verse describes the second, and the difference between the two motives is the difference between compliance and devotion.

The emphasis on reward shapes the character of the good the chapter describes. Fear produces compliance and compliance has a ceiling; desire produces effort and effort does not. A person who gives because the finest thing is real gives more freely than one who gives to avoid a consequence.

**Certainty as the Qualifier**

The translation renders the verb with an emphasis — firmly believes — and the emphasis is warranted. The Arabic construction denotes a settled conviction rather than an opinion held provisionally. A person who entertains the possibility of the finest reward while reserving judgement has not been described by this verse. The certainty matters for the argument of the chapter, since the facilitation promised in the next verse is a response to a settled state. The Qur'an describes the same firmness elsewhere in the language of the heart at rest, and it treats uncertainty about the Hereafter as the root of the behaviour it condemns. The reader who gives occasionally and doubts regularly is in a different position from the one described here.

The settled conviction is what distinguishes this case from ordinary religious sentiment. Most people hold beliefs provisionally and act accordingly — partially, intermittently, with reservations. The verse describes a person whose belief has stopped being provisional, and the difference shows in the consistency of his giving.

**The Finest Reward and the Two Worlds**

The belief in the finest reward does not require a person to despise this world. The Qur'an states the relation plainly: "But whoever desires the Hereafter and strives for it accordingly, and is a ˹true˺ believer, it is they whose striving will be appreciated" (17:19), and the case it condemns is the one who desires this fleeting world alone (17:18). The distinction is between ranking and rejection. A believer in the finest reward may still work, trade and provide; what he does not do is treat this world as the final term of his striving. The verse describes a person whose expenditure is shaped by a conviction about a return, and the shaping is visible in the way he gives — freely, and without needing to be thanked.

The shaping is visible in the manner of the gift rather than in its size. A person who believes in a return gives without needing to be thanked, because the thanks were never the payment he expected. The chapter will describe that manner explicitly, and the description follows from the belief named here.

**The Symmetry With the Verse That Denies It**

The chapter's second case is described in terms that mirror these exactly. The one who is stingy and deems himself beyond need denies the finest reward. The symmetry is deliberate and complete: the same object is believed by one group and denied by the other, and the belief or denial is what produces the opposite behaviours. The verse therefore does more than describe the first group. It identifies the hinge on which the chapter's division turns. A reader who wants to know which of the two cases he belongs to is directed to a single question, and the question is about the finest thing: does he believe in it? The answer determines the direction of his striving, and the direction determines the path that will be made easy for him.

The question is the hinge, and the chapter knows it. Everything else — the giving, the guarding, the withholding, the claim of independence — follows from the answer. A reader who wants to change his direction is directed to the conviction rather than to the behaviour, because the behaviour is downstream of it.

**Belief in the Unseen as the Ground of Giving**

Every act of giving is a transfer from the visible to the invisible. A person parts with something he can see in exchange for something he cannot, and the exchange is rational only if the invisible term is real. The verse therefore names the belief that makes the giving coherent. A man who does not believe in the finest reward is not being irrational when he withholds; he is being consistent with his disbelief. The chapter's two cases are therefore not two levels of virtue but two convictions, and the convictions produce the conduct. The reader who finds himself unable to give has been given a diagnosis rather than a rebuke: the belief is missing, and the belief is what needs attention.

**What Denial Does to a Life**

The opposite of this verse is described later in the chapter, and its effects are worth stating here. A person who denies the finest reward organises his life around what he can secure in this world, and the organising produces a specific character: cautious, acquisitive, unwilling to transfer anything, and suspicious of anyone who asks. The character is not chosen; it follows from the denial. The chapter describes the outcome of that character in a single verse about wealth that will not benefit its owner. The reader who observes the denial operating in himself will recognise the caution and the acquisitiveness as its symptoms, and the recognition is the first step toward the belief this verse describes.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:7

> We will facilitate for them the Way of Ease

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Promise Attached to the Three Qualities**

Three qualities have been described, and the verse states what follows from them. God will make the way of ease easy. The construction is emphatic: the ease is doubled in the Arabic, and the doubling is not decorative. It says that the path itself is easy and that the making of it easy is also performed. A reader who has given, guarded himself and believed is promised not a reward at the end but a condition along the way, and the condition is that his path becomes practicable. The promise is therefore about the present rather than only about the future, which is what makes it a consolation in the middle of difficulty.

The doubling in the Arabic is emphatic rather than redundant, and the emphasis answers a doubt the reader is likely to have. A person who has begun to give and finds it hard will wonder whether the promise applies to him yet. The doubled ease says that the making-easy is itself supplied.

**Taysīr: Making Easy as a Divine Act**

The verb is *yassara*, to make easy, and the Qur'an uses it for God's action on a person's path rather than for the person's own competence. The ease is supplied, not achieved. The same assurance appears in the legislation on fasting, where the reason given for the concessions is that "Allah intends ease for you, not hardship" (2:185), and in the sūrah of Divorce, where the promise is attached to mindfulness: after hardship, Allah will bring about ease (65:7). The verse in this chapter states the general rule in its strongest form. The ease is not a consequence that follows mechanically from good conduct; it is an act performed by God on the path of a person who has been described in the previous two verses.

The attribution matters for the reader's expectation. Ease that depends on his own competence would fail whenever his competence failed. Ease supplied by God does not depend on the person's condition, which is why the promise can be relied on by someone who is tired, poor or opposed.

**Why Ease Is Promised to the Giver**

The connection between giving and ease is not obvious, and it is worth stating. A person who gives has removed an obstacle from his own path, and the obstacle is attachment. Wealth is the most common form of security in the world, and a person who holds it tightly has built his sense of safety on something he cannot keep. Giving dismantles that construction, and the dismantling is painful at the moment and liberating afterwards. The ease promised in this verse includes the ease of a person who no longer needs his wealth to feel safe. The Qur'an describes the same liberation elsewhere: "And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful" (64:16). The saved person is the one whose path has been made easy, and the saving is from himself.

The liberation is the practical content of the promise, and it arrives before any external change. A person who has given has already begun to loosen the grip that wealth had on his sense of safety, and the loosening is felt as relief. The verse names that relief and attributes it correctly.

**The Reciprocity of Turning Toward God**

The verse describes a reciprocity that the Qur'an states as a rule: "Indeed, Allah would never change a people's state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺" (13:11). The movement begins with the person and is completed by God. The giver has taken a step, and the step is answered by a making-easy that he could not perform himself. The reciprocity is not symmetrical — the human act is small and the divine act is large — but it is real, and it is the mechanism by which a religious life becomes sustainable. A person who had to generate his own ease would exhaust himself; a person whose effort is met by facilitation can continue. The verse supplies the second half of that arrangement.

The asymmetry of the reciprocity is what makes a religious life possible at all. If the response were proportionate to the human act, nobody could sustain it. The small step is met by a large one, and the meeting is what allows a person to continue after failures that would otherwise end the effort.

**The Path of Ease as a Direction, Not a Comfort**

The ease promised is the ease of a path, and a path has a destination. The verse does not promise a comfortable life, and it does not promise the removal of difficulty. It promises that the way toward the finest reward will be made easy for the person who is moving along it. The distinction matters for anyone who reads the verse as a guarantee of prosperity. The giver described in the previous verses may still be poor, sick or opposed; what is promised is that his direction will not be blocked. The Qur'an's use of the same language elsewhere confirms the reading: guidance itself is described as an opening of the heart, and the opening is what makes the path practicable.

The destination gives the ease its meaning. A path made easy toward ruin would be a calamity, and the chapter describes exactly that in the verse that mirrors this one. The reader who wants ease must therefore ask where the path leads before he celebrates how smooth it has become.

**The Verse as an Answer to the Question of Destiny**

The report connected with these verses — in which the companions ask whether deeds matter if the ends are already registered, and the Prophet answers that everyone is facilitated toward what he was created for — is answered here in the text itself. The facilitation described in this verse is attached to the conduct described in the two before it. A person who gives, guards himself and believes is facilitated toward ease; the verse does not describe a person facilitated toward ease who then gives. The order is fixed, and the order is the answer. What a person does determines what is made easy for him, and the determination is stated without qualification. The reader who asks whether his deeds matter is answered by the sequence of the verses.

The sequence answers the question without stating an argument. Conduct first, facilitation second, and the connection between them is fixed by the order of the verses. A reader who asks whether his deeds matter is answered by the arrangement of the text rather than by a claim inside it.

**Ease in the Path and Ease in the Heart**

The facilitation promised here has two aspects, and both are experienced. The path becomes practicable: obstacles that would have stopped the person are removed or reduced, and the direction he has chosen becomes the one he can actually walk. The heart becomes settled: the person no longer fights himself at every step, and the act that once required an argument now requires only a decision. The Qur'an describes the second aspect in the language of an opened heart, and the opening is what makes obedience easy rather than costly. The reader who has found a good act difficult and then, after repeating it, easy has experienced the facilitation the verse describes. The verse names the experience and attributes it.

**Recognising the Promise in One's Own Life**

The promise is testable, and the test is available to any reader. A person who has given, guarded himself and believed can ask what has become easier since he began. If giving has become less costly in feeling, if the thought of the finest reward has become more settled, and if the path he chose requires less argument than it did, the facilitation is operating. The test is not a measure of prosperity. A person may be poor, sick and opposed while his path is being made easy in the sense the verse describes. The distinction between external circumstance and internal facilitation is the distinction the chapter relies on, and the reader who confuses them will misread both this verse and the one that mirrors it.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:8

> And as for the one who is stingy, indifferent ˹to Allah˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Case Begins With Withholding**

The mirror image of the first case opens with the opposite of giving. The verb *bakhila* denotes stinginess — the refusal to give what one has and could give. It is not a description of poverty. A poor person cannot be stingy in the sense this verse requires, since stinginess presupposes something to withhold. The verse therefore identifies the second group by the same test as the first: what a person does with what he has. The reader who has nothing is not described here, and the exclusion is a comfort rather than a slight. The chapter's division is between those who give and those who withhold, and a person with nothing to withhold belongs to neither by that measure. The presupposition matters for the reader's self-examination. A person who has nothing is not described here, and the exclusion is not a mercy for the stingy but a clarification of who the chapter is addressing. The test is what a person does with what is in his hand at the moment a need appears.

**Istaghnā: Deeming Himself Beyond Need**

The second quality is *istaghnā*, and the word carries a claim. It denotes considering oneself self-sufficient, beyond need, independent. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the human being's error at its root: a person transgresses because he considers himself self-sufficient. The claim is directed upward. A person who deems himself beyond need has removed his dependence on God from his own self-description, and the removal is what makes the stinginess coherent. A man who believes he needs nothing from God has no reason to give anything for God. The two qualities in this verse are not separate faults; the second explains the first. The claim is rarely stated aloud, which is why the verse names it. Nobody announces that he needs nothing from God; he simply stops asking, stops accounting for his conduct, and stops expecting a return. The verse gives the silent claim a name, and naming it is what makes it examinable.

**An Inversion of the Proper Order**

The exegetes describe the combination in this verse as an inversion. The proper relation of a human being is dependence above and generosity below: obedient to God, giving to people. The person described here reverses both terms. He is stingy toward those below him and independent of the One above him. The inversion is the definition of the second case, and it is why the chapter treats the two qualities as a single configuration. A reader who is generous to people while treating God as unnecessary has half of the inversion; a reader who is devout while withholding from people has the other half. The verse describes the person who has both, and both are required for the description to fit. The inversion explains why the two faults appear together so reliably. Generosity toward people and dependence on God are the same posture seen from two sides, and the refusal of one produces the refusal of the other. The reader who notices either feature in himself should look for the second, because it will be there.

**The Psychological Sequence**

Stinginess and the sense of self-sufficiency reinforce each other in a sequence worth tracing. A man who accumulates begins to feel secure; the security produces the impression of independence; the independence removes the motive for giving; and the absence of giving increases the accumulation. The cycle is self-confirming, and it is why the chapter treats the state as a direction rather than a mood. Nobody becomes stingy in a day. The verse describes the endpoint of a process in which each step made the next step easier, and the next verse will describe what God does in response. The reader who is inside the cycle will not feel it as a cycle; he will feel it as prudence. The cycle also explains why the state is hard to see from inside. Each step looks like good sense at the time: keeping is prudent, independence is dignified, requests are interruptions. The verse describes the endpoint of a chain of reasonable decisions, and the reasonableness is what delays the recognition.

**The Contrast With the First Case**

The three qualities of the first case have exact counterparts here. Giving is answered by withholding; mindfulness by the claim of independence; belief in the finest reward by its denial. The symmetry is complete, and the completeness is the chapter's argument. The two cases are not two temperaments but two directions, and each direction is marked by a coherent set of dispositions rather than by isolated acts. A reader who wants to locate himself is given a checklist of three items in each column, and the columns are mutually exclusive. There is no configuration in which a person is stingy, self-sufficient and a denier of the reward while also giving, guarding himself and believing. The verse makes the choice explicit by making the descriptions exhaustive. The exhaustiveness of the two descriptions removes the middle ground a reader might hope to occupy. Nobody is partly generous and partly independent in the sense the chapter means; the configuration is one or the other, and it is discoverable from conduct. The verse makes the discovery possible by making the descriptions complete.

**What the Verse Is Not Saying**

The verse does not condemn wealth, and it does not condemn the enjoyment of what a person has lawfully acquired. The Qur'an repeatedly describes provision as a favour and instructs its recipients to spend from it. What the verse condemns is the disposition of a person who holds his wealth without giving and explains the holding to himself as independence. The distinction is between possession and posture. A rich man who gives is described in the first case; a poor man who withholds what little he has and considers himself beyond need is described in the second. The chapter's categories are moral rather than economic, and the reader who assumes that the verse is about money has missed the quality that makes the money significant. The posture is what the verse identifies, and the posture is independent of the amount held. A poor man who guards what little he has and explains the guarding to himself as independence is inside the description; a rich man who transfers freely is outside it. The chapter measures direction rather than volume.

**Two Directions, One Inversion**

The verse describes a person whose relations are reversed in both directions at once. Toward those below him he is closed; toward the One above him he is independent. The two reversals are not independent faults but a single posture seen from two sides. A person who believes he needs nothing from God has no reason to give to anyone, and a person who gives to no one reinforces his sense that he needs nothing. The cycle is self-sealing, and the chapter describes it as a direction rather than as a set of mistakes. The reader who examines himself will find the two features together or not at all, and finding them together is what places him in the second case. Neither feature is sufficient alone to identify the case, and the pairing is what the verse insists on. A man who is closed to people but acknowledges his dependence has a defect rather than a direction; a man who claims independence while giving has a confusion rather than a settled refusal. The inversion requires both.

**The Wealth That Will Not Save**

The verse is followed shortly by a statement about wealth that will not benefit its owner at the moment he falls. The connection between the two is the argument of the chapter. The person described here has built his sense of security on what he holds, and the holding is exactly what will fail him. The stinginess is therefore not only a moral fault but a strategic error. He has refused to transfer wealth into a form that would survive, and the refusal was based on a belief about his own independence that the verse identifies as false. The reader who is accumulating in order to be safe is being told that the accumulation is the least safe thing he could be doing. The refusal is therefore compounded by the outcome. He kept the wealth to be safe and lost the safety, and the losing was not a matter of bad luck but of a miscalculation about what wealth can do. The verse states the miscalculation in advance, and stating it in advance is the whole of the warning.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:9

> and ˹staunchly˺ denies the finest reward

**Expanded Commentary**

**Denial of the Same Object**

The object denied in this verse is identical to the object believed in the verse describing the first case: *al-ḥusnā*, the finest. The identity is the hinge of the chapter. The two groups are not distinguished by what they know but by what they affirm or refuse about the same thing. The exegetes identify the finest variously as the testimony of divine oneness, as Paradise, or as God Himself, and the readings agree on the point that matters: the object is ultimate, and the response to it divides humanity. A reader who thinks the chapter's two cases are distinguished by behaviour alone has not read this verse. The behaviour follows from the affirmation or the denial. The identity of the object is what makes the chapter's division sharp. Two groups are not responding to different claims but to the same one, and the responses are opposite. A reader cannot explain the difference by saying that he was shown something else, because the object is fixed and named in both descriptions.

**Kadhdhaba: An Active Denial**

The verb is intensive. It does not describe a person who fails to believe but one who declares something false. The denial is an act, and the act has a direction — it is performed against a claim that has been made to him. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the peoples who rejected their messengers, and its use here places the stingy person in that category. The comparison is severe, and it is not accidental. A person who withholds from the needy and declares the finest reward to be false is performing, in the register of his own life, the same act performed by the peoples who were destroyed. The chapter's later reference to the most wretched confirms the comparison. The intensity of the verb excludes a passive reading. A person who has never considered the claim cannot declare it false, and a person who declares it false has engaged with it and rejected it. The verse therefore describes someone who was addressed and answered, and the answer was a refusal.

**Why Denial Produces Stinginess**

The connection between the two qualities in this pair is causal, and the causality runs from belief to conduct. A person who does not believe in a return has no reason to transfer wealth now for a benefit later. His calculation is rational on his premises: the wealth is real, the reward is not, and the transfer is a loss. The verse therefore identifies the root of the behaviour rather than the behaviour itself. The Qur'an states the same connection where it describes those whose spending is not accepted: "And what prevented their donations from being accepted is that they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger, they never come to prayer except half-heartedly, and they never donate except resentfully" (9:54). The resentment in that verse has the same source as the stinginess in this one. The rationality is internal to the disbelief, and that is why argument rarely changes it. The man is not making an arithmetic error; he is being consistent with a premise the chapter rejects. The correction therefore has to reach the premise, and the chapter reaches it by describing the conduct the premise produces.

**The Denial as a Choice, Not a Conclusion**

Nothing in the verse suggests that the denial is compelled by evidence. The Qur'an consistently describes its signs as clear and its warnings as delivered, and it treats rejection as a decision. The person described here has been told about the finest reward, and he has declared it false. The declaration is therefore a moral act with an epistemic appearance. The chapter expects the reader to see through the appearance. A man who has never been told about a reward cannot deny it; a man who denies it has performed an act of refusal, and the refusal has consequences that the next verse states. The decision has an appearance of intellectual honesty, and the appearance is what makes it durable. A person who thinks he has examined a claim and found it wanting will not revisit it. The verse treats the examination as a form of the refusal rather than as an alternative to it.

**Symmetry Completed**

Verses 5 to 9 form two triads in exact parallel: gives, is mindful, believes — withholds, deems himself beyond need, denies. Each triad describes a coherent person rather than a list of faults, and the parallelism removes any suggestion that the two cases are unevenly described. The chapter gives the same amount of space to both, uses the same structure for both, and assigns each a facilitation in the following verse. The fairness of the description is what makes the outcome acceptable. A reader who reaches the end of the chapter and objects that the second group was described harshly has not noticed that the description was symmetrical, and that the harshness belongs to the acts themselves. The fairness of the description is what allows the chapter to end without apology. Both cases are given the same space, the same structure and the same precision, and neither is caricatured. The reader who reaches the ending has been shown two coherent ways of living and has been asked to choose between them.

**The Reader's Own Denial**

The verse is phrased in the third person, but its application is personal. Most readers do not consider themselves deniers of the finest reward, and the verse asks them to examine the claim. Denial in the sense described here is not usually a public announcement. It appears in conduct: a person who says he believes in a return and never transfers anything for it is performing the denial while professing the belief. The chapter's method is to test belief by expenditure, and the test is available to any reader at any time. What a person gives is the measure of what he actually believes about the finest thing, and the measure does not depend on his self-description. The test is behavioural and therefore honest. A person's statements about the finest reward are shaped by what he wants them to be; his handling of his property is not. The chapter trusts the ledger over the profession, and the reader who wants an accurate answer about himself should do the same.

**Denial as the Root of the Whole Case**

The three qualities of the second case are not equally weighted, and the denial is the root. Withholding follows from a conviction that there is no return, and the claim of independence follows from a conviction that nothing is owed. Remove the denial and the other two collapse: a man who believes in the finest reward has a reason to give, and a man who has a reason to give discovers that he is not independent. The chapter therefore identifies the denial as the point at which the second case begins, and it is why the verse is placed last in the triad. The reader who wants to change his direction is directed to the conviction rather than to the behaviour, because the behaviour is downstream. The diagnosis is practical rather than theological. A reader who finds himself unable to give has been told where the blockage is, and the blockage is not in his will but in his conviction. Working on the willingness without working on the belief is why most resolutions about generosity fail within a month.

**Denial Performed Without Words**

Most readers do not announce a denial, and the verse does not require an announcement. The denial is performed whenever a person acts as though the finest reward were not real. He keeps what he could give, because keeping it is the rational move on his premises. He defers the transfer, because the transfer has no return he believes in. He explains the deferral to himself in the language of prudence. The chapter treats this pattern as the denial the verse names, and the treatment is severe because the pattern is common. A reader who wants to know whether he denies the finest reward should not examine his statements. He should examine his ledger, because the ledger is where the belief is recorded. The pattern is common enough that the chapter treats it as the ordinary form of the denial rather than an extreme one. Prudence, deferral and the language of responsibility are the vocabulary in which the denial is usually expressed, and the vocabulary is respectable. The verse asks the reader to look past it.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:10

> We will facilitate for them the path of hardship

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Verb, the Opposite Path**

The verse uses the same verb of facilitation that appeared in the promise to the giver, and the symmetry is exact. God makes easy — and what He makes easy here is the path of hardship. The construction is deliberate and paradoxical. A path of hardship is not made comfortable; it is made easy to walk, which means the person on it finds each successive step less resisted than the last. The facilitation is therefore not a mercy in this instance. It is the removal of obstacles from a direction that leads somewhere the person will not want to go. The verse states one of the Qur'an's most severe ideas: that a person can be helped along a path, and the help can be his ruin. The paradox is the most severe statement in the chapter, and it is stated without emphasis or comment. A person is not punished with difficulty here; he is accommodated in a direction. The accommodation feels like success, and the feeling is the mechanism by which the direction becomes permanent.

**The Reciprocity of Turning Away**

The mechanism is the mirror of the one described in the seventh verse. There, a step toward God is met by a making-easy. Here, a step away is met by the same. The Qur'an states the general rule: "Indeed, Allah would never change a people's state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺" (13:11). The reciprocity operates in both directions, and the verse describes the second. A person who withholds, claims independence and denies the reward finds each of those positions easier to hold than the last. The hardening is not imposed from outside; it is the accumulated effect of choices, each of which was facilitated by the one before. The rule operates the same way in both directions, and the symmetry is what makes it intelligible. A step toward God is met by facilitation; a step away is met by facilitation. Neither response is arbitrary, and neither is imposed before the step is taken. The chapter describes a system rather than a mood.

**Hardship as the Name of a Direction**

The word for hardship here is the opposite of the ease promised to the giver, and the exegetes gloss it with evil. The identification is important. The path made easy for the second group is not a path of external difficulty — poverty, illness, opposition — but a path of moral deterioration. A person on it finds wrongdoing progressively easier, and the ease is the sign that he is further along than he was. The verse therefore reverses the ordinary way of reading difficulty. External hardship is not evidence of God's displeasure, and internal ease in wrongdoing is not evidence of His approval. The reader who measures his standing by how comfortable his sins have become is measuring with the wrong instrument. The identification of hardship with evil is the key to reading the verse correctly. What is being made easy is not a difficult circumstance but a wrong direction, and the ease is the sign of progress along it. The reader who measures his state by external comfort is using a measure the verse rejects.

**Why This Is Not Coercion**

The facilitation described in this verse has been read by some as evidence that God determines a person's end irrespective of his choices, and the reading does not hold. The facilitation is attached to conduct described in the two preceding verses. A person is not facilitated toward hardship before he has withheld, claimed independence and denied; the sequence in the text is fixed, and the sequence is the answer. The verse describes a response to a direction already chosen, and the response makes the chosen direction easier to continue. What is removed is not the possibility of turning back but the felt difficulty of continuing forward. The distinction preserves both the seriousness of the state and the reality of the choice that produced it. The sequence preserves the responsibility of the person on the path. He chose the direction, and the facilitation followed the choice rather than preceding it. What is removed is the friction that might have stopped him, and the removal is a response to his persistence rather than its cause.

**The Warning in the Facilitation**

The verse functions as a warning precisely because it is not a threat of external punishment. A person threatened with pain can hope to endure it; a person told that his path will become easy has been given a worse piece of news. The absence of resistance is what makes a direction irreversible. The Qur'an describes the same state where it speaks of hearts that are sealed and of a drawing-on that feels like blessing: the person is led further while believing he is being favoured. The verse in this chapter states the mechanism in a single phrase, and the phrase is the reason the chapter's two cases are described with such care. A reader who is inside the second case will not feel like a person being punished. He will feel like a person getting on with his life. The warning is more effective than a threat of pain because it cannot be braced against. Pain can be endured; the absence of resistance cannot be noticed. The verse tells the reader that the danger signal he is waiting for will not arrive, and that its absence is itself the signal.

**The Reader's Check**

The verse supplies a test, and the test is uncomfortable. A person who wants to know which path is being made easy for him can ask what has become effortless. If giving has become easier than withholding, and mindfulness easier than indifference, the facilitation described in the seventh verse is operating. If the opposite is true — if refusing a request costs nothing and the thought of a return seems remote — the facilitation described here is operating. The test is available at any moment and requires no interpretation. The chapter has described both directions in enough detail that a reader can locate himself, and the locating is the purpose of the symmetry. The check is available at any moment and requires no information the reader lacks. He knows what has become effortless and what still costs him something. The chapter supplies the interpretation of that knowledge, and the interpretation is the opposite of the one most people apply to it.

**A Path That Becomes Easier to Walk**

The paradox in this verse deserves restating plainly. Hardship is not made pleasant; it is made easy to enter and to continue along. A person on this path finds each successive step less resisted than the one before. The first refusal costs something; the fifth costs nothing. The first denial requires an argument; the tenth requires only a habit. The facilitation is therefore a description of deterioration that feels like progress, and the feeling is the danger. The verse does not describe a person being punished with difficulty. It describes a person being allowed to continue in a direction he has chosen, and the allowing is what makes the direction final. The progression is cumulative and largely unnoticed. Each refusal lowers the cost of the next, and each denial shortens the argument required for the following one. The verse describes the mechanism by which a person arrives at a position he could not have adopted at the start, and arrives there comfortably.

**Signs That the Facilitation Is Operating**

The verse supplies a test, and the test is uncomfortable because it runs in the opposite direction from the one people expect. Ask what has become effortless. If refusing a request costs nothing, if the thought of a return has become remote, and if the arguments for withholding now come to mind without effort, the facilitation described here is operating. If the opposite is true — if giving has become easier than keeping, and if the finest reward has become more real rather than less — the facilitation described in the seventh verse is operating. The two states are distinguishable at any moment, and neither requires an external measure. The reader who performs the test honestly will know which verse describes him, and the knowing is the purpose of the symmetry. The test is deliberately uncomfortable because comfort is what the second path produces. A reader who finds that his refusals cost him nothing and his denials require no argument has been given a diagnosis rather than a reassurance. The diagnosis is still useful, because it arrives while the direction can be changed.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:11

> And their wealth will be of no benefit to them when they tumble ˹into Hell˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Ends the Calculation**

Every act of withholding is based on a calculation: the wealth kept is worth more than the benefit forgone. The verse cancels the calculation by naming a moment at which the kept wealth has no function. It does not argue that giving is more profitable in this world, and it does not promise that the generous man will be rich. It states that there is a point past which wealth cannot operate, and that the point is reached by everyone. The reader who has been weighing the cost of generosity against its return is told that one term in his equation has an expiry date, and that the date is fixed. The cancellation is total and it arrives without warning. Nothing in the calculation indicates where the calculation stops working, and the person performing it has no way of knowing how much time remains. The verse therefore interrupts a process that feels continuous and safe, and the interruption is the point of the verse.

**Taraddā: Falling Headlong**

The verb describes a fall, and the form denotes a person tumbling downward rather than being placed somewhere. The image is of a descent that the person cannot arrest, and the translation's rendering — when they tumble — preserves it. The choice of verb matters for the argument. A person who is carried somewhere may protest; a person who is falling has already lost control. The wealth mentioned in the first half of the verse is useless precisely because its owner is in that state. The verse joins the two facts — the possession and the fall — and the joining is the whole of its warning. What he accumulated is intact and irrelevant at the same time. The image of the fall removes any suggestion of a controlled transition. A person who is placed somewhere can prepare; a person who is falling cannot. The wealth mentioned in the same verse is intact during the fall and useless in it, and the juxtaposition is what makes the warning concrete.

**What Wealth Cannot Purchase**

The Qur'an states the limitation of wealth in its most explicit form in the legislation on charity: "O believers! Donate from what We have provided for you before the arrival of a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession" (2:254). Three mechanisms by which a person ordinarily escapes consequences are removed — the deal, the connection and the advocate — and wealth operates through all three in this world. The verse in this chapter states the same fact from the other side. The wealth itself will not benefit its owner, because the mechanisms through which wealth works have been closed. The reader who understands how his money protects him here is being told that the protection is a feature of this world and not of the next. The three mechanisms are how wealth operates in this world, and their closure is what the verse announces. A reader who understands his own security will recognise the three and will recognise that all of them depend on a world that continues. The verse states the moment at which it does not.

**The Same Statement Made Throughout the Revelation**

The claim is not local to this chapter. Worldly wealth is described as adornment, and the lasting deeds as "far better with your Lord in reward and in hope" (18:46). The longer treatment likens the whole of worldly life to rain whose plants delight the sowers before they wither and are reduced to chaff (57:20). The verse in this chapter is the compressed form of a claim made repeatedly, and its compression gives it force. A reader who has heard the claim in a paragraph can set the paragraph aside; a reader who meets it in a single sentence, immediately after a description of the path of hardship, cannot. The repetition across the revelation is evidence that the claim is central rather than incidental. A theme stated once can be treated as a rhetorical flourish; a theme stated in every register — legal, narrative, eschatological — is a doctrine. The verse is the compressed form of that doctrine.

**Why the Verse Follows the Facilitation**

The sequence of the chapter matters. The path of hardship has just been made easy for the person who withholds, and the verse states what the path leads to. The placement answers an objection. A person inside that facilitation feels no difficulty, and the absence of difficulty can be mistaken for evidence that the direction is safe. The verse removes the mistake. The ease is real, and it is the ease of a descent. The wealth that made the withholding possible is still in his hands when the descent begins, and its presence changes nothing. The reader who measures his direction by how easy his life feels has been answered in advance. The placement answers the objection before it is raised. Ease in a wrong direction feels like confirmation, and the verse removes the confirmation by naming the destination. The reader who feels his life going smoothly has been told that smoothness is not evidence about direction, and the telling comes at the point of maximum plausibility.

**The Boaster of the Other Chapter**

The type described here appears in the sūrah of the City, where a man announces that he has destroyed enormous wealth and asks whether anyone sees him (90:5–6). The two chapters describe the same figure from different angles. There the complaint is about expenditure; here the withholding is about refusal. Both figures treat wealth as the measure of their standing, and both are answered with the same fact: the wealth will be present and useless at the moment it is needed. The reader who meets the type in one chapter will recognise him in the other, and the recognition is the point of the repetition. The two chapters describe one figure from two angles, and the reader who meets him in either will recognise him in the other. Both figures treat property as the measure of standing, and both are answered with the same fact about the moment at which property stops functioning.

**What the Verse Asks the Reader to Do**

The verse does not ask the reader to despise his wealth. It asks him to use it while it still operates. The Qur'an's consistent instruction is to spend from what has been provided, and the spending is described as a transfer rather than a loss: whatever is donated is paid back in full (2:272). The verse supplies the reason for the urgency. Wealth has a period of usefulness, and the period ends at a moment the owner does not control. A person who waits for a convenient time to give has misunderstood the verse, because the inconvenience he is waiting to avoid is the only condition in which giving means anything. The urgency is the practical content of the verse. Wealth has a period of usefulness, and the period is not extended by accumulating more of it. A reader who is waiting for a better moment to transfer has misunderstood the verse, because the waiting is what the verse identifies as the error.

**The Moment the Ledger Closes**

The verse names a moment, and the moment is the same for everyone. There is a point at which a person's wealth stops functioning — not because it has been taken but because the mechanisms through which it works have closed. The verse describes the point as a fall, and the fall is not something a person can prepare for by accumulating more. The reader who is planning for the future with his wealth is planning inside a period that has an end, and the end is not determined by the size of the plan. The verse therefore interrupts the ordinary arithmetic of a life. It does not forbid planning; it states that the planning has a horizon, and that the horizon arrives without consultation. The horizon is the fact that reorganises the arithmetic. A person who plans inside a period with no end plans one way; a person who knows the period ends plans another. The verse does not forbid planning, and it does not require asceticism. It states the horizon, and the statement changes the plan.

**What Should Be Done Before the Moment**

The verse implies an instruction without stating it. Wealth that will not benefit its owner afterwards can benefit him beforehand, and the benefit is obtained by transferring it. The Qur'an states the instruction plainly: donate from what has been provided before the arrival of a day on which there is no bargaining, friendship or intercession (2:254). The transfer converts property that is about to become useless into something that is not. The reader who reads this verse and does nothing has understood it and declined it, which is the position the chapter describes as the second case. The verse is short because the instruction is simple, and the simplicity is what makes the refusal culpable. The instruction is simple enough that refusing it cannot be explained by complexity. Transfer what will become useless into what will not, and do it while the transfer is still possible. The chapter describes the person who performed the transfer at the end of the sūrah, and the description is the instruction made visible.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:12

> It is certainly upon Us ˹alone˺ to show ˹the way to˺ guidance

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Obligation God Places on Himself**

The verse is phrased as a commitment, and the commitment is stated in the first person plural with an emphatic particle: upon Us is the guidance. Nothing in the Qur'an obliges God from outside; whatever obligation exists here is one He has taken on. The form of the statement is therefore significant. It is not a description of what God happens to do but a declaration of what He has undertaken, and the declaration is made in a chapter that has just described two paths. The reader is told, before the warning arrives, that the showing of the way is not something left to chance or to human initiative. It has been guaranteed. The undertaking removes the question from the list of things a person must secure for himself. Guidance is not a resource to be competed for, hoarded or earned by influence, and the verse says so by making its provision God's own commitment rather than an achievement of the seeker.

**What the Undertaking Covers**

The exegetes read the undertaking in two ways, and both are supported. The first takes it as the explanation of what is lawful and what is prohibited — the delivery of the guidance in a form a person can act on. The second takes it as the guarantee that the one who travels the path of guidance will reach God. The first reading concerns the giving of the guidance; the second concerns its completion. The chapter's structure favours the first, since the warning that follows presupposes that the guidance has been delivered and can be refused. The second reading is not excluded, and it supplies the assurance the first lacks: the path shown is a path that arrives. The scope is comprehensive in a way that anticipates the reader's objections. Whatever he thinks he needs in order to begin has been covered by the undertaking, and whatever he fears has been covered as well. The verse is written so that no gap remains for a person to say that his case was not provided for.

**Guidance Is Not the Messenger's Task**

The Qur'an states the limit of the Prophet's office repeatedly, and the statements are emphatic. "You are not responsible for people's guidance ˹O Prophet˺—it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills" (2:272). "You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills, and He knows best who are ˹fit to be˺ guided" (28:56). The verse in this chapter belongs to that group, and its placement is significant. It arrives in a chapter that has described a person who denies and turns away, and it relieves the messenger of responsibility for the denial. The reader who concludes that a refusal reflects badly on the one who conveyed the message has been answered. The distinction is what protects both the truth and the messenger. A claim that cannot be compelled is not a weak claim; it is a claim addressed to a person who is able to refuse it. The verse keeps the offer genuine by keeping the acceptance voluntary.

**A Comfort Addressed to a Man Under Pressure**

The chapter was revealed to a man whose message was being rejected by the city he lived in, and the verse is a comfort to him. The undertaking removes the burden of outcome from his shoulders. He is responsible for the conveying, and the conveying has been done; the guidance itself is upon God. The distinction is not a technicality for a person watching his community refuse what he has brought. It is the difference between a man who has failed and a man who has been refused. The verse supplies the second description, and the chapter that contains it is one of several revealed in the same period for the same purpose. The timing of the reassurance is what makes it operative. A statement of relief given at a moment of ease would be received as information; given under pressure, it is received as support. The verse is placed where a man who has been carrying more than was asked of him can set the burden down.

**Two Senses of Guidance**

The Qur'an uses the language of guidance in two senses, and the distinction is necessary for reading this verse. There is the showing of the way, which is given to everyone; and there is the guidance of the heart, which follows the response. The first sense is what this verse undertakes. The second is described elsewhere as an opening: "Whoever Allah wills to guide, He opens their heart to Islam" (6:125). The chapter's two cases correspond to the two responses. Both groups have been shown the way, and the divergence described in the fourth verse is a divergence of response rather than of information. The reader who claims that he was never shown has been answered by the undertaking in this verse. The two senses are not in tension, and the verse depends on both. Without delivery there is nothing to accept, and without acceptance the delivery has not reached its end. The undertaking covers the first completely and offers the second genuinely, and the reader's part lies entirely inside the second.

**Why the Verse Is Placed Here**

The verse stands between the description of the two paths and the warning about the Fire, and the placement is deliberate. A warning issued without a prior assurance of guidance would be a threat against people who may not have been told. The verse supplies the assurance, and the warning that follows is therefore just. The reader arrives at the description of the Fire knowing that the way was shown, that the showing was guaranteed by God, and that the refusal described in the previous verses was a refusal of something delivered. The sequence is the chapter's jurisprudence, and it is complete before the warning begins. The placement is deliberate and structural. Two paths have been described and a destination has been named; the verse then states that the way back has been provided. The reader who has been following the argument is told at exactly this point that the situation is not a trap.

**The Reader's Position**

The undertaking relieves the messenger but not the hearer. The guidance is upon God; the response is upon the reader. The chapter has described the two responses in detail, and the description is available to anyone who reads it. A person who concludes from this verse that the outcome is not in his hands has read it against the ten verses that precede it, in which the facilitation of each path is attached to the conduct of the person on it. What is guaranteed is the showing. What remains is the turning, and the turning is what the following verses describe. The reader's position is therefore one of receipt rather than of acquisition. He is not required to construct the way, to negotiate access to it, or to prove himself worthy of it in advance. He is required to recognise that it has been given and to walk on it, and the requirement is within his power.

**The Undertaking and the Messenger's Relief**

For the man to whom this chapter was revealed, the verse removed a burden he was carrying. He had conveyed a message and watched his city refuse it, and the refusal could be read as a failure of the conveying. The undertaking answers the reading. The guidance is upon God, and the conveying was the messenger's task alone. The distinction is not a technicality for a person whose life is devoted to a message that is being rejected. It is the difference between carrying a responsibility and carrying a disappointment. The verse supplies the second, and it does so in a form that cannot be argued with, since the undertaking is stated by the One who took it on. The relief is real but it is not a reduction of effort. The messenger is freed from an impossible responsibility, not from the work itself, and the distinction is the one a reader under pressure needs. Nobody is obliged to produce results that depend on another person's heart.

**Guidance Delivered, Response Required**

The undertaking relieves the conveyor but not the hearer. The guidance has been shown, and the showing is guaranteed; what remains is the response, and the response is not covered by any undertaking. The chapter's two cases are descriptions of responses, and the facilitation attached to each is a consequence of the response rather than a substitute for it. A reader who concludes from this verse that his direction is not in his hands has read it against the ten verses that precede it. What is guaranteed is the delivery. What is required is the turning, and the turning is what the following verses describe in enough detail for a reader to perform it. The division of labour is the practical content of the verse. Guidance is delivered, the response is owed, and each party has a task that cannot be transferred. A reader who confuses the two either abandons the response or attempts the delivery, and the verse separates them so that neither error is necessary.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:13

> And surely to Us ˹alone˺ belong this life and the next

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ownership of Both Terms**

The verse states an ownership that covers the two realms in which a person can want anything. This life and the next are both God's, and the statement leaves no third term in which an independent claim could be made. The formulation is echoed elsewhere in the revelation: to God belong the Hereafter and this world. The claim is not about possession in the sense of control alone; it is about the source of whatever is desired in either realm. A person who wants something in this life is asking from the Owner of this life, and a person who wants something in the next is asking from the same Owner. The verse removes the possibility of playing one realm against the other. The pairing of the two terms is what makes the claim difficult to evade. A person might dispute a statement about this world alone or about the next alone; a statement covering both leaves him no territory. The verse is constructed so that the ownership it asserts has no exception and no rival.

**Why Both Realms Are Named**

The pairing answers a specific error. A person who believes that this world is his to take and the next is God's to give has divided the ownership, and the division licenses the behaviour the chapter condemns. The person described in the eighth verse deems himself beyond need, and the self-sufficiency he claims is a claim about this world in particular. The verse answers the claim by naming both realms in a single statement of ownership. Nothing is outside it, and the reader who has been accumulating in this world has been accumulating inside a property that belongs to someone else. The statement is not a threat. It is a correction of a description. Both realms are named because both are involved in the reader's conduct. The wealth he withholds belongs to the first, and the outcome of withholding belongs to the second. A description that named only one of them would allow a person to think that his conduct in this world has no bearing on the other.

**The Refutation of the Self-Sufficient Man**

The connection between this verse and the second case described earlier is direct. A man who withholds from the needy and considers himself independent has made a claim about his own standing. The verse answers it with the simplest possible fact: everything he holds is held inside an ownership he did not establish and cannot terminate. The self-sufficiency he claims is a description of his feelings rather than of his position. The Qur'an makes the same point where it describes wealth and children as a test rather than a possession, and where it instructs the believers to donate from what God has provided — a formulation that attributes the provision even as it commands the giving. The refutation is decisive because it addresses the claim at its root. A man who deems himself beyond need has to suppose that he is self-sufficient in some realm, and the verse closes both realms against that supposition. What remains is not independence but a claim to it.

**Reward and Punishment in Both Realms**

The exegetes note that the ownership of both realms carries a consequence: the Owner can reward and punish in either. The Qur'an states the principle where it answers those who desire the reward of this world alone, pointing out that with God is the reward of both. The verse in this chapter supplies the ground of that answer. A person who seeks a return in this life is seeking from the same source as a person who seeks a return in the next, and the seeking does not exhaust what is available. The chapter's two cases differ in what they seek, and the difference is one of horizon rather than of source. The distribution between the realms is what makes the chapter's warnings intelligible. Reward and punishment are not confined to a future they cannot be checked against; both operate in this world as well, in the facilitation and the hardship already described. The reader can therefore observe part of the outcome while he is still able to change it.

**The Ground of the Warning That Follows**

The verse is placed immediately before the warning about the Fire, and the placement is the argument. A warning is only meaningful if the one who issues it has the power to carry it out, and the power is established here. The Owner of both realms can act in either, and the Fire described in the next verse is part of what He owns. The reader who reaches the warning has already been told that the issuer is not a claimant but an owner. The sequence — undertaking to show the way, ownership of both realms, warning of the Fire — is a complete statement of the position: guidance has been given by the One who has the power to enforce its consequences. The warning is grounded rather than asserted, and the grounding is the ownership. A fire that belongs to someone who owns both realms cannot be escaped by moving between them, and the reader who hopes to avoid the warning by leaving one realm for the other has been told that there is no such move.

**Dependence as the Reader's Actual Position**

The verse describes a fact about the reader rather than a doctrine about God. Everything he has is held inside an ownership that is not his, and the holding is temporary. The Qur'an's instruction follows from the fact: spend from what has been provided, because the provision was never owned absolutely. The reader who accepts the verse has accepted the premise of the chapter's whole argument about giving. A person who gives from what he believes he owns absolutely is performing a sacrifice; a person who gives from what he holds on trust is returning part of a trust. The second description is the one this verse supplies, and it is what makes the giving described later in the chapter intelligible. The reader's actual position is the one the verse states, and it is not diminished by the statement. To be owned is to be provided for, sustained and addressed, and the person who denies the ownership does not thereby escape it. He only ceases to notice that he is being sustained.

**Ownership and the Wealth in the Reader's Hand**

The verse has an immediate practical consequence. What a person holds is held inside an ownership that is not his, and the holding is temporary and conditional. The Qur'an's instruction follows directly: donate from what God has provided, a formulation that attributes the provision at the moment it commands the giving. The reader who accepts the verse has accepted the premise of the chapter's whole argument about expenditure. A gift given from property believed to be owned absolutely is a sacrifice, and a sacrifice is measured by its cost. A gift given from property held on trust is a return, and a return is measured by its faithfulness. The second description changes the feeling of the act, and the change is what makes giving sustainable over a lifetime. The ownership extends to the wealth the reader holds, and the extension is the practical point. What is in his hand is not his in the sense of independent possession, and the chapter's instruction about giving assumes exactly that. A person who grasps that his property is held on terms finds transfer easier, because he is not being asked to surrender what was entirely his.

**One Owner of Two Realms, and No Escape Between Them**

The pairing of the two realms closes a route of evasion. A person who is refused something in this world may hope for it in the next, and a person who is offered the next may try to keep the first. The verse states that both belong to the same Owner, which means there is no second party to appeal to and no gap between the two jurisdictions. The reader who wants something is asking from a single source, and the source decides in which realm the request is answered. The chapter's two cases differ in what they asked for, and the difference is one of horizon. The verse supplies the fact that makes the choice meaningful: there is nowhere else to ask, and nothing else to ask for. The completeness of the ownership is what gives the chapter its force at this point. There is no third realm to flee to and no gap between the two, and the reader who has followed the argument has nowhere to place himself except in the position the verse assigns to him.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:14

> And so I have warned you of a raging Fire

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warning Issued in the First Person**

The chapter shifts register at this verse. The preceding statements have been in the third person or in the general form of an oath and its answer; this one is addressed directly, in the first person, to a plural audience. The shift is a rhetorical decision, and it marks the moment at which the chapter stops describing and starts speaking. The reader is no longer overhearing an account of two types of person. He is being addressed by the One who owns both realms, and the address is a warning. The directness is what gives the following verses their force, since a warning delivered in the first person cannot be attributed to a reporter. The person who issues the warning has the standing to issue it, and the standing is what makes the verse effective. A warning from a person with no authority over the outcome is advice; a warning from the owner of both realms is information about a fact he controls. The verse gives the reader both the content and the credential.

**Talā: A Fire That Blazes**

The word used for the Fire is *talā*, from a root denoting blazing and fierce heat. It is not the general term for fire but a specific one, describing a fire in its active state rather than a fire that exists. The Qur'an names the Fire with a series of such terms, each carrying a different image: the Crusher, the abyss, the scorching Fire. The verse in this chapter uses the term of blazing, and the choice fits the warning's function. A reader is not being told that a place exists; he is being told that something is burning, and that the burning has been announced to him in advance. The announcement is the mercy inside the warning. The imagery is chosen for its visibility rather than its novelty. A fire that blazes is something every reader has seen, and the verse uses the familiarity to convey the intensity. What distinguishes this fire is not that it burns but that it is being pointed out to him in advance by the one who can prevent his approach to it.

**Warning as a Mercy**

The verse is severe, and its severity is a kindness. A person who has not been warned cannot be blamed for entering a danger; a person who has been warned and enters it has chosen. The Qur'an's consistent practice is to warn before punishing, and the warning is recorded so that the reader can see that it was given. The undertaking in the previous verse — upon Us is the guidance — is completed here. The guidance has been shown, the ownership has been stated, and the consequence has been announced. Nothing further is required for the warning to be just, and nothing in the chapter suggests that it was delivered reluctantly. The mercy is in the timing rather than in the tone. A warning delivered before the danger can be avoided is an act of care even when its language is severe, and the reader who finds the verse harsh is responding to the wording rather than to the intention behind it. The verse could have been left unstated.

**The Fire Described Elsewhere**

The Qur'an's descriptions of the Fire are varied, and the variety serves the warnings in which they appear. The sūrah of the Slanderer names it the Crusher and asks what will make the reader realise what the Crusher is, answering that it is the Fire of Allah, sealed over its inhabitants (104:4–9). The sūrah of the Calamity describes the abyss as the home of those whose scale is light (101:8–11). This chapter's term is the blazing, and the term is chosen for a chapter about conduct performed in the ordinary day. The reader who is warned of a blazing fire while going about his business is being addressed in the register of his own life, and the register is what makes the warning reach him. The descriptions elsewhere accumulate into a picture that no single verse provides. Heat, confinement, fuel and duration are each stated somewhere, and the reader who gathers them is not being given a metaphor but a description of a place. The verse names the place, and the rest of the revelation describes it.

**The Sequence From Ownership to Warning**

The verse follows immediately on the statement that both realms belong to God, and the sequence is the argument. A warning issued by a claimant can be resisted; a warning issued by an owner cannot. The reader who has accepted the previous verse has accepted that the one warning him has authority over the place he is warning him about. The warning is therefore not a prediction offered by an observer but a notice given by the proprietor. The chapter's structure makes the point without stating it: undertaking, ownership, warning, and then the description of who will enter. The movement from ownership to warning is what makes the chapter's logic complete. Having established that both realms are his, the one who speaks now states what he has prepared in one of them. The warning is not an interruption of the argument; it is the argument's consequence.

**Addressed to Everyone Within Earshot**

The warning is plural, and the plural includes every hearer. No group is exempted, no condition is attached, and no distinction is drawn between the righteous and the rest at this point. The distinction comes in the next verse, where the category of those who will enter is defined. The verse therefore addresses everyone first and narrows afterwards, and the order is deliberate. A reader who is told that a danger exists is placed inside the audience of the warning before he is told whether he is in the category of those affected. The placement produces attention, and attention is what the following verses require. The audience is deliberately unrestricted. Nobody is addressed by name, and nobody is excluded by condition, so every person who hears the verse is inside its scope. The generality is what makes it a warning rather than a condemnation of a particular group.

**The Warning as the Completion of Justice**

A warning completes a sequence, and the sequence in this chapter is exact. The guidance was undertaken by God; the ownership of both realms was stated; the warning is now issued. Nothing in that order is arbitrary, and the order is what makes the warning just. A person threatened without having been shown the way is a victim; a person threatened after the way has been shown and by the One who owns the destination is a person being informed. The verse therefore belongs to the chapter's jurisprudence rather than to its rhetoric. It is the last step before the description of who is affected, and it is issued in the first person so that no intermediary can be blamed for it. The completion of justice is what the warning supplies. A world in which the generous and the miser receive the same end would make the chapter's distinctions meaningless, and the reader who accepts the distinctions has to accept their outcome. The verse states the outcome plainly so that the distinctions are not merely rhetorical.

**A Warning Heard in the Middle of Ordinary Life**

The verse is addressed to people going about their day, and the address is the point. The reader is not in a courtroom or at the end of his life. He is in the middle of an ordinary week, and the warning arrives there. The Qur'an's warnings are consistently placed in that register, because a warning that arrives only at the end cannot be acted on. The reader who finds the verse abrupt has noticed something true about it: it interrupts. The interruption is the mercy inside the warning, since a person interrupted can still change direction. The chapter that contains the warning goes on to describe the person who is removed from the Fire, and the description is available to whoever was interrupted in time. The ordinary setting of the hearing is what makes the warning land. A person is not being addressed at a moment of crisis but in the middle of a week, with his affairs running normally, and the verse assumes that this is the moment at which it will be useful. Heard later it would be too late, and the verse knows it.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:15

> in which none will burn except the most wretched—

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Exclusivity That Narrows the Category**

The verse restricts the warning it follows. Not everyone who hears it will enter the Fire; only the most wretched will. The restriction is expressed in the strongest available form — none except — and the form is significant. A warning that applied to everyone would be a general threat; a warning that applies to a defined extreme is a diagnostic. The reader is given a category and a test, and the test is supplied in the verse that follows. The narrowing is not a softening of the warning. It is a specification of who it concerns, and the specification is what makes it possible for a reader to locate himself relative to it. The narrowing is what gives the verse its severity. Many people will face the fire, and the verse is not about them; it is about a category so restricted that the reader is entitled to ask who is in it. The restriction is the point, and the question is the one the following verses answer.

**Al-Ashqā: The Superlative of Wretchedness**

The word is the superlative of wretched, and the definite article makes it a title rather than a description. The most wretched is not a person having a bad day or a sinner who has slipped. He is the one at the extreme of the category, and the chapter defines him in the next verse by two acts: denial and turning away. The use of a superlative is consistent with the Qur'an's practice elsewhere, where the same word is applied to the man who hamstrung the she-camel of Thamūd (91:12). The parallel is instructive. The most wretched of a people is the one whose act expressed a settled condition, and the condition had been formed long before the act. The superlative is doing precise work. The verse does not say that this person suffers more than others in degree but that he occupies a position no one else occupies. The exclusivity is grammatical as well as semantic, and the grammar is what makes the category closed rather than comparative.

**The Parallel With the Tribe Destroyed**

The chapter of the Sun describes the most wretched of Thamūd as a man roused by his people to kill a sign he had been warned about (91:12–14). The parallels between the two chapters are close: both use the superlative, both describe a refusal after a warning, and both end with a statement about consequences. The reader who has read the earlier chapter recognises the category here, and the recognition supplies the content that this verse leaves unstated. The most wretched is not a person who committed a single bad act. He is a person who destroyed a sign after being told what it was, and the destruction was the expression of a condition already complete. The parallel supplies the historical content the verse itself withholds. A tribe destroyed for killing the she-camel is a documented event, and the reader who knows the story knows what the superlative implies. The verse relies on that knowledge rather than restating it, which is why the identification matters.

**The Exclusivity as a Comfort**

The narrowing has a second function, and it is a consoling one. A reader who fears that his ordinary failings place him in the category is told that the category is defined by extremes. The person who sins and repents, who withholds and then gives, who doubts and then believes is not described by this verse. The verse describes the one who denies and turns away, and both verbs denote settled positions rather than lapses. The comfort is not a licence. It is a correction of a fear that would paralyse rather than motivate, and the correction is made by the grammar of the verse itself. The comfort is real and it belongs to the reader who is worried about himself. A person who is checking his conduct, giving from his property and asking about his obligations is not inside the category, and the verse's narrowness is what makes the exclusion certain. The category has a definition, and he does not meet it.

**The Test the Verse Supplies**

The category is defined by conduct that a reader can check. Denial is a position taken toward a claim that has been made; turning away is a movement performed in response to a summons. Neither is a state of feeling, and neither is hidden. A person who has been told about the finest reward and declares it false has performed the first; a person who has been summoned to give and has turned his back has performed the second. The verse that follows names both, and the naming is the test. The reader who wants to know his distance from the category is given two questions, and both can be answered honestly without interpretation. The test is supplied so that the reader can settle the question himself. He is not asked to accept an assessment of his standing from anyone else; he is given a criterion he can apply to his own conduct. The criterion is behavioural, which means it can be answered honestly.

**Why the Verse Comes Before the Definition**

The order of the two verses is deliberate. The category is named first, in a superlative, and the definition follows. The arrangement produces a moment of uncertainty in the reader, and the uncertainty is the point. A person who reads "none will burn except the most wretched" will ask who the most wretched is, and the question is answered in the next verse. The arrangement is more effective than the reverse. A definition followed by a name would allow the reader to skip the name; a name followed by a definition makes him wait for it, and the waiting is what fixes the definition in his mind. The placement prepares the definition that follows. Naming the category before describing it means the reader hears the description as an answer to a question already in his mind. The order is deliberate, and reversing it would leave the description without a frame.

**The Narrowness of the Category Is Not Leniency**

The restriction in this verse could be read as a softening, and it is not. Naming a narrow category makes the category precise, and precision increases rather than reduces the seriousness of the description. A general threat can be dismissed as exaggeration; a specific description can be checked. The reader who reads "none except the most wretched" is being given a definition he can test himself against, and the test is supplied in the next verse. The narrowing therefore transfers the burden to the reader. He cannot rely on the vagueness of the warning, because the warning has been made exact, and the exactness is directed at him. The narrowness is not a relaxation of the standard. It identifies the extreme case so that the reader can measure his distance from it, and the measuring is the purpose of the naming. A person who concludes that he is outside the category has not thereby concluded that he is safe; he has concluded that he is not at the furthest point.

**How a Person Becomes the Most Wretched**

The superlative describes an endpoint, and endpoints are reached by degrees. A person does not become the most wretched of his generation in a single act. He denies a claim, and the denial becomes easier. He turns away once, and the turning becomes a habit. The habit hardens until the summons no longer registers, and at that point the act that expresses the condition costs nothing. The chapter of the Sun describes the same process in a single man: the most wretched of a tribe, roused by his people, who killed a sign he had been warned about (91:12). The reader who wants to avoid the category is directed not to the final act but to the first denial, because the first is the one that can still be reversed. The process is the practical content of the verse. Nobody is placed in the category by a single act; he arrives there through a sequence of refusals, each of which was individually survivable. The sequence is described in the verses that follow, and the reader who reads them is being shown the steps rather than the destination alone.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:16

> who deny and turn away

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs, Two Movements**

The definition of the most wretched is given in two verbs, and the two describe different acts. The first is denial — declaring a claim to be false. The second is turning away — withdrawing from a person or a summons. Denial is directed at a statement; turning away is directed at a presence. The pairing covers both ways a person can refuse a messenger: he can reject the message, and he can leave the room. The verse names both, and the naming is exhaustive. There is no third form of refusal that escapes the definition, and there is no form of refusal that does not involve one of the two. The two movements describe one refusal from two angles, and the reader needs both because either alone is ambiguous. Denial can be mistaken for doubt and aversion for busyness. Taken together they identify a settled position rather than a passing state, and the identification is what the category requires.

**Denial and Aversion Together**

The combination is what constitutes the extreme. A person who denies but remains may be argued with; a person who turns away but does not deny may be reached. The person described here has done both, and the combination is a settled state rather than a moment. The Qur'an describes the same combination in its accounts of the peoples who were destroyed, and it treats the turning as the decisive act. A people who argue are still engaged; a people who have turned their backs have ended the engagement. The verse in this chapter identifies the extreme case by the presence of both features, and the identification is why the category is narrow. The conjunction is what closes the possibility of a partial case. A person who denies without turning away has not committed himself, and a person who turns away without denying may be avoiding something he has not judged. The verse requires both, and the requirement makes the category narrow and testable.

**The Order of the Verbs**

Denial is named first, and the order reflects the ordinary sequence. A person hears a claim, declares it false, and then withdraws. The turning follows the denial rather than preceding it, which means the refusal is a conclusion the person has reached rather than an aversion he has felt. The sequence matters for the moral assessment. A person who turns away because he cannot bear to hear has a different position from one who turns away because he has decided the message is false. The verse describes the second, and the second involves a judgement made and acted on. The reader who examines his own refusals will find both kinds, and the verse is addressed to the kind that involves a decision. The order is diagnostic rather than accidental. Belief produces conduct and its refusal produces withdrawal, so the inner position is stated before the outer movement. The reader who reverses the order will look for a reason behind his own withdrawal and find one he invented afterwards.

**Tawallā: The Turning of the Back**

The verb denotes a physical turning, and the Qur'an uses it repeatedly for the act of leaving a messenger. The image is concrete: a body that was facing a person and is now facing away. The choice of a physical verb for a spiritual act is characteristic of the Qur'an's method, and it makes the act visible. A reader can recognise the movement in himself, because he has performed it — in conversation, in a gathering, in the moment when a request was made and he removed himself from the room. The verse describes the movement and leaves the reader to supply the occasions. The turning is a public act with a bodily form, which is why the verse chooses it. Nobody turns his back privately on something he is considering, and the gesture marks the end of deliberation. The verse is describing a person who has finished thinking about the matter.

**The Definition Completes the Category**

Verses 15 and 16 together form a single statement, and neither is complete without the other. The first names the extreme; the second defines it. A reader who reads only the first is left with an undefined fear; a reader who reads only the second has a description without a consequence. The pairing supplies both, and the pairing is the chapter's most precise piece of moral definition. The most wretched is not the sinner, the doubter or the negligent. He is the person who has declared the finest reward false and has turned away from the one who told him about it, and the two acts together place him in the category the Fire is promised to. The definition supplies what the earlier naming withheld. The reader who reached the superlative without knowing who it covers has been given the covering conditions, and both are within his knowledge. The definition is behavioural, so the question it answers can be answered honestly.

**The Contrast That Follows**

The definition is immediately followed by its opposite, and the contrast is the chapter's final movement. The most wretched will burn; the most mindful will be removed from the Fire. The symmetry is not exact, and the asymmetry is significant. The wretched are defined by what they refused; the mindful are defined by what they gave. The chapter's two cases end where they began — in withholding and in giving — and the ending is described in terms of the Fire and of removal from it. The reader who has followed the chapter arrives at the contrast with both descriptions in view, and the choice between them is the choice the chapter has been asking him to make since the fourth verse. The contrast is what makes the two categories mutually exclusive rather than merely different. The description that follows is the mirror image of this one, and the reader who reads them side by side sees that there is no third position between them. The choice is therefore real and not a matter of degree.

**Turning Away as the Decisive Act**

Of the two verbs, the turning is the one that ends the relationship. Denial can be answered; an argument can be reopened; a claim declared false can be argued again. A person who has turned his back cannot be addressed at all, because he is no longer facing the one who would address him. The Qur'an's accounts of destroyed peoples consistently place the turning at the point of no return, and the turning is described in physical terms for that reason. The verse in this chapter names it second, and the placement identifies it as the completion of the refusal. The reader who is still arguing has not turned; the reader who has stopped listening has. The decisiveness lies in the combination, and the reader can check it without reference to anyone else's assessment. A settled denial accompanied by a settled withdrawal is a direction, and a direction has an outcome the chapter has already named. The verse is the diagnosis that makes the outcome foreseeable.

**The Two Verbs in the Reader's Own Conduct**

The definition is testable, and the test requires no interpretation. Has the reader declared the finest reward false — not in words but in the way he handles his property? Has he turned away from a summons — not once in anger but as a settled posture? The two questions cover the whole of the definition, and both can be answered honestly by anyone. The chapter's method throughout has been to describe conduct rather than feeling, and the description of the most wretched follows the method. A reader who answers no to both questions is not in the category, and a reader who answers yes to either has been given the information while it is still useful. The verse is the last point at which the description can be read as a warning rather than as a verdict. The reader's own conduct is the place the definition does its work. He is not asked to identify such a person among his acquaintances but to check whether either movement is present in himself, and the check is simple: what has he stopped believing, and what has he stopped doing as a result.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:17

> But the righteous will be spared from it—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Second Superlative**

The verse answers the one before it with a matching extreme. Where the previous verse named the most wretched, this one names *al-atqā* — the most mindful, the one furthest along the path of guarding himself against what would harm him in God's reckoning. The pairing of two superlatives is the chapter's structural centre. Humanity has been described as striving toward diverse ends, and the two ends are now personified in two figures, each at the limit of his kind. The reader is not being offered a spectrum. He is being shown two poles, and the poles are defined by the verses that follow each of them. The second superlative balances the first and completes the chapter's structure. One category has an extreme case, and so does the other, and the two are stated in the same terms. The reader who has followed the argument can now see the whole shape rather than two unrelated descriptions.

**Removal Rather Than Escape**

The verb used here denotes being kept at a distance from something, not merely avoiding it. The person described is not a man who slips past the Fire; he is a man who is placed away from it. The difference matters. Escape implies proximity and effort; removal implies an act performed on his behalf by someone else. The chapter's positive case has been described throughout as a person whose path is facilitated by God, and the removal is the final instance of the same facilitation. The reader who imagines that he will have to negotiate his way past the Fire at the last moment has misread the verb. The distance is arranged, and it is arranged for the person described in the verses that follow. The word describes an act performed on the person rather than an achievement of his. He is removed from the fire, and the removal is not something he negotiated, purchased or earned by calculation. The verse states the distance as a fact about him, and the fact is not his own doing.

**The Symmetry With the Most Wretched**

The two figures are defined in opposite terms, and the opposition is exact. The most wretched denies and turns away; the most mindful gives, seeks no return and aims at the Face of his Lord. Neither figure is described by his feelings, his lineage or his standing. Both are described by acts, and the acts are within the reach of any reader. The symmetry is what makes the chapter's division fair. A reader who objects that the description of the wretched is severe has to reckon with the fact that the description of the mindful is equally specific, and equally available. The chapter gives the same attention to both, and it expects the reader to supply the same honesty in reading both. The symmetry is exact in form and opposite in content. Both categories have a superlative, and each superlative is defined by the conduct already described. The reader who has understood one description understands the other, because the two are constructed by the same method and inverted.

**The Reports About the Occasion of These Verses**

The tradition connects this passage with Abū Bakr, who used to buy weak and disabled slaves in order to free them. His father is reported to have asked why he did not buy strong men who could defend him, and the answer was that he was not seeking his own defence. The verses that praise the one who gives his wealth to purify himself were read as a description of that conduct. The connection is supported by an authentic report in which ʿUmar is recorded as saying, "Abū Bakr is our master and he emancipated our master," meaning Bilāl (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3754). The chapter's positive case was recognised by the first generation in a specific person, and the recognition tells the reader what the description looks like in practice: the purchase of people who could do nothing for the purchaser. The reports give the description a named example, and the naming is what allows the reader to test his understanding. A man who freed a man is not an abstraction; the identification places the category inside the community the reader belongs to, and it shows the scale on which the conduct was performed.

**A Reward That Was Not Bargained For**

The removal from the Fire is described as a consequence of the giving, and the giving was performed without a promise attached. The person described in the following verses gives to purify himself and seeks the Face of his Lord; nothing in the description suggests that he gave in order to be removed from the Fire. The reward is therefore not the price of a transaction but the response to a disposition. The distinction is important for the reader's own practice. A person who gives in order to obtain a specific reward is performing a purchase, and the purchase is reflected in the manner of his giving. The chapter describes a person who gave without an invoice, and the invoice was drawn up afterwards by someone else. The absence of bargaining is what distinguishes the reward from a transaction. A payment received in exchange for services rendered settles an account; a reward given to a person who expected nothing closes nothing and leaves him in surplus. The verse describes surplus rather than settlement.

**A Category That Is Still Open**

The verse is written in the future tense and describes a type rather than a named individual. The category of the most mindful is therefore unoccupied in the reader's own case until he acts. The chapter supplies the definition in the three verses that follow, and the definition is made of acts that remain available: giving from one's wealth, seeking no return from anyone, aiming at God. A reader who concludes that the description belongs to the first generation has not read the verses that define it. The definition is behavioural, and behaviour can begin today. The removal promised at the end is attached to the description, and the description is open to anyone who fits it. The category remains open, and the openness is the practical content of the verse. Nobody is told that the example given is the only instance, and the reader who gives from what he holds has been shown that the description can apply to him. The condition is the motive, not the amount.

**Removal as an Act Done to Him**

The verb in this verse is passive, and the passivity is significant. The person described does not remove himself from the Fire; he is removed. The distinction corrects a common misunderstanding of the religious life, in which a person is imagined to be negotiating his way past a danger by his own effort. The chapter's account is different. The person gave, sought no return and aimed at God, and the removal is the response to that disposition rather than the reward for a performance. The reader who has been trying to secure himself by accumulation or by calculation is being shown a different mechanism, and the mechanism begins with giving rather than with securing. The passive form is the theological point. Removal is an act done to a person by the one who owns the fire, and the person's own conduct is the occasion rather than the cause. The verse keeps the distinction so that the reward cannot be read as a wage.

**The Distance From the Fire as the Measure of the Giving**

The chapter places the removal immediately before the description of the giver, and the placement invites a correlation. The person who is kept far from the Fire is the person who gave his wealth to purify himself, who owed nobody anything and who aimed at the Most High. The three clauses describe a direction, and the removal describes the distance that direction produces. A reader who wants to know how far he is from the Fire has been given a way to measure it, and the measure is his giving rather than his feeling. The chapter does not offer any other measure, and the absence of alternatives is what makes the measure reliable. The measure is the distance, and the distance is what the giving produced. The verse does not quantify the giving and it does not rank the giver; it states how far he ends up from what he was warned about. The reader can therefore judge the worth of his own giving by its direction rather than by its size.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:18

> who donate ˹some of˺ their wealth only to purify themselves

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Act of Giving Comes First in the Definition**

The most mindful is defined first by what he does with his wealth, and the definition is precise. He gives his wealth, and the giving has a stated purpose: purification. The verse does not describe a feeling of generosity or an intention to help. It describes a transfer of property with a motive attached, and both elements are required for the description to fit. A reader who feels generous and gives nothing has not been described here, and a reader who gives for another reason has been described in a different place. The chapter's positive case begins, like its negative case, with money. The definition begins with an act because the chapter has been describing conduct throughout. A person is not identified by what he believes alone but by what he does with what he holds, and the giving is placed first so that the motive which follows is understood as the reason for a real act rather than a feeling.

**Yatazakkā: The Same Root as the Alms**

The verb is from the root of *zakāh*, and the root carries both purification and growth. A thing that is purified by this verb is also increased, and the two senses are inseparable in Arabic usage. The connection to the obligatory alms is therefore not accidental: the alms are called by a name derived from the same root because the payment both cleanses the wealth and increases it. The chapter of the Sun states the same principle in a single line — successful is the one who purifies his soul (91:9) — and this verse applies it to property. A reader who gives has performed the act described in both chapters at once, and the act is the same act. The shared root connects the giving to the wider practice of alms, and the connection is deliberate. The same term that describes the obligatory transfer describes this one, and the reader is meant to see that the motive being defined here is the motive required there. The practice and its inner condition are not separable.

**Purification as the Stated Motive**

The verse specifies the purpose of the giving, and the specification excludes other motives. The man described does not give to be seen, to be thanked, to discharge a social obligation or to obtain influence. He gives in order to be purified. The motive is internal and it is directed at the giver's own state rather than at anyone's opinion of him. The Qur'an describes the same motive where it instructs the Prophet to take charity from the community: "Take from their wealth ˹O Prophet˺ charity to purify and bless them" (9:103). The purification is the point of the transaction, and the transaction is with God rather than with the recipient. Stating the motive makes the definition falsifiable. A gift given for purification looks identical from outside to a gift given for return or display, and the verse insists that the difference is not merely invisible but decisive. The reader who cannot identify his own motive has been given something to work on.

**The Conduct the Tradition Recognised**

The reports about Abū Bakr supply the concrete form of this verse. He bought slaves who were weak and unlikely to be of use to him, and he bought them in order to free them. When his father asked why he did not buy strong men who could defend him, he answered that he was not seeking his own defence. The answer is the verse in narrative form. The purchase had no utility for the purchaser, and its only return was the purification described here. The reader who asks what the verse looks like in practice is given an example in which the giver obtained nothing except the state the verse names. The recognition by the tradition is what makes the identification concrete. A man known for freeing people is not a hypothetical case, and the reader who knows the example can see what the description means at full scale. The scale is not the requirement, but it shows that the description admits of a maximum.

**What the Purification Removes**

The verse implies that wealth carries something that needs removing, and the implication is consistent with the Qur'an's treatment of property elsewhere. Wealth is a test: "Your wealth and children are only a test, but Allah ˹alone˺ has a great reward" (64:15). It is also an attachment, and attachment distorts judgement. The giving described here removes the attachment by transferring the property, and the removal is what the verse calls purification. A reader who gives while retaining the sense that the wealth was his to keep has performed the transfer without the purification. The chapter's insistence on the motive is what distinguishes the two cases. What is removed is the residue the wealth carries. Property acquired, held and guarded leaves a mark on the person holding it, and the transfer is the means by which the mark is taken off. The verse is not describing a payment for past faults but a cleaning of the holder himself.

**Contrast With the Displayed Gift**

The verse's motive is the opposite of the one the Qur'an condemns. Charity followed by reminders and hurtful words is likened to a barren rock whose film of soil is washed away by rain, leaving nothing behind (2:264). The image describes a gift that looked fertile and was not. The gift described in this verse is the reverse: it looks unremarkable, since nobody is being thanked and nobody is being told, and it produces the state the giver sought. The ranking is stated explicitly elsewhere: to give publicly is good, but to give privately is better (2:271). The person described here belongs to the second category, and the category is defined by the absence of an audience. The contrast is the sharpest in the chapter. A gift given to be seen has an audience and a return, and the audience is the point of it. A gift given to purify has no audience at all, and the absence is not a loss but the condition of its value.

**The Verse as the Chapter's Positive Definition**

This verse and the two that follow form a single description, and the description is the chapter's answer to the question raised in the fourth verse. Human striving is diverse; here is one of the directions, described in three clauses. The reader who wants to know what the path of ease looks like from the inside is given the answer: a person who gives from his wealth to purify himself, who owes nobody and expects nothing, and who is aiming at God. Nothing in the description requires wealth beyond what a person has, and nothing in it requires an audience. The description is available, and the chapter ends by stating what the person who fits it will receive. The positive definition is the answer to the negative descriptions that precede it. The reader who has been told what the wretched person does and what he denies is finally told what the other person does, and the telling is complete enough that he can act on it today.

**Wealth as the Thing Most in Need of Purification**

The verse attaches purification to property rather than to the body or the ritual, and the attachment is deliberate. Wealth is the form of security human beings most rely on, and reliance distorts the one who relies. A person who holds wealth tightly begins to measure himself by it, to defer obligations because of it, and to regard requests as threats. The giving described in this verse removes the distortion by transferring the object of it. The purification is therefore not a ceremonial cleansing but a correction of a relationship. The reader who gives and feels lighter has experienced what the verse names, and the lightness is the evidence that the purification occurred. Wealth needs purification because of what holding it does to a person. The verse treats the transfer as a benefit to the giver rather than a cost, and the treatment reverses the ordinary assumption. The reader who gives in order to keep himself clean has understood the verse.

**Benefit That Belongs to the Giver**

The purpose clause in this verse is reflexive: he gives to purify himself. The recipient benefits, and the benefit to the recipient is real, but the stated purpose concerns the giver's own state. The formulation is consistent with the Qur'an's account of charity elsewhere, where the giving is described as good for the giver and the reward as returning to him. The reader who gives in order to improve someone else's condition has performed a good act; the reader who gives in order to be purified has performed the act this verse describes. The difference is not in the transfer but in the aim, and the aim is what the following verses examine. The chapter is precise about motives because motives are what it is measuring. The benefit belonging to the giver is the practical reason for the instruction. God does not need the transfer, and the person receiving it is helped, but the verse locates the deepest benefit in the one who makes it. A reader who has been reluctant to give has been told that the reluctance is costing him something.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:19

> not in return for someone’s favours

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Negation That Clears the Ledger**

The verse states what the giving is not, and the negation is exact. The literal construction is that no one has a favour with him that is to be repaid. The giver is not settling an account. Nobody stands before him with a credit he is discharging, and nobody will stand after him with a claim he has created. The negation removes the gift from the system of exchange entirely, and the removal is what distinguishes it from every ordinary act of generosity. Most gifts in human life are transactions in one direction or another; this one is not, and the verse says so in a single clause. The negation clears the ledger before anything is stated about the reward. Nothing is owed to this giver and nothing is owed by him, and the clearing is what makes the promise that follows unmixed. The reader who expects an accounting has been told there is none to make.

**Two Directions Excluded**

The exegetes read the negation as covering both directions of reciprocity. The giver is not repaying a favour done to him in the past, and he is not creating an obligation that will be repaid to him in the future. The two exclusions together close every route by which a gift can become a payment. A reader who gives to someone who once helped him has performed the first; a reader who gives in order to be helped later has performed the second. The verse excludes both, and the exclusion is what makes the following verse necessary. Once reciprocity is removed, a motive has to be supplied, and the next verse supplies it. Both directions are excluded because both are ways of turning a gift into an exchange. A favour expected from the recipient and a favour owed to a benefactor are the same transaction seen from two ends, and the verse closes both ends at once. What remains is the act alone.

**The Social Economy the Verse Leaves Behind**

Every society runs on reciprocity. Favors create debts, debts create obligations, and obligations bind people into networks of mutual dependence. The system is not wicked; it is the ordinary mechanism by which communities hold together. The verse describes a person who operates outside it, and the description is a judgement on the system rather than a condemnation of it. A gift given inside the network produces a debtor; a gift given outside it produces nothing except the state of the giver. The chapter ranks the second above the first, and the ranking is what the following verses confirm. The economy the verse leaves behind is the one the reader lives in. Favour, obligation and return are how most giving is organised, and the verse does not condemn that organisation; it describes a gift made outside it. The reader who has never made such a gift has been shown what one would look like.

**The Report That Explains the Verse**

The tradition supplies an occasion for this verse and the one that follows. When Bilāl was being tortured for his testimony of God's oneness, Abū Bakr bought him and freed him, and his opponents said that he had done it only because he was obliged to Bilāl for a favour. The verses that follow describe a person with no such obligation, and the description answers the accusation directly. Whether or not the occasion is relied on historically, the reading it preserves is the plain sense of the text. A gift attributed to an existing debt is a payment, and the verse exists to distinguish the giving of the most mindful from payments of that kind. The report identifies the verse's first instance and gives the exclusion a face. A man who freed people without expecting anything in return is the concrete form of the description, and the reader can measure his own conduct against that example rather than against an abstraction.

**Why Reciprocity Diminishes a Gift**

A gift that repays a debt discharges an obligation, and the discharging is its purpose. Once discharged, nothing remains. A gift that creates an obligation binds the recipient, and the binding is its purpose. In both cases the gift is an instrument, and the recipient is the means by which the instrument works. The verse describes a gift that is neither. Its purpose is internal to the giver — purification — and its direction is toward God rather than toward the recipient. The recipient benefits, but he is not the reason. The distinction is subtle and it is decisive, because it determines whether the act is a moral act or a transaction with a moral appearance. Reciprocity diminishes a gift because it converts it into a purchase. A person who gives in order to receive has bought something, and the price he paid was the gift. The verse removes the purchase from the category altogether, which is why the reward is stated as satisfaction rather than as payment.

**The Test the Verse Applies to the Reader**

The verse supplies a check that any giver can perform. When you give, ask whether anyone's name is in the ledger. If you are repaying someone, the gift is a payment. If you are creating a debtor, the gift is an investment. If neither is present, the gift belongs to the category this verse describes. The test is not a demand for perfection; it is a way of seeing what one is actually doing. The chapter of the City describes the opposite case in a single quotation — a man announcing how much wealth he has destroyed (90:6) — and the announcement is the sound of a ledger being read aloud. The verse here describes a person who keeps no such ledger, and the absence of the ledger is what makes his giving count. The test is available to the reader at the moment of giving rather than afterwards. He can ask, before the transfer, whether he would still make it if no one ever knew and nothing ever came of it. The question is short, and the answer is usually immediate.

**A Gift With Nobody in View**

The negation in this verse produces a strange result: a gift in which the recipient is almost incidental. The giver is not repaying him, not obliging him and not seeking his opinion. The recipient receives the benefit, and the benefit is real, but the transaction is not between the two of them. The verse describes a gift whose accounting is closed on one side only, and the closure is what makes it a moral act rather than a social one. The reader who has given and then watched for a response has not performed this act. The watching is the sign that the ledger is still open, and the open ledger is what the verse closes. A gift with nobody in view is the definition of the category. Not the recipient, not the witnesses, not the giver's own reputation: the verse excludes all three, and the exclusion is what makes the reward unmixed. The reader who satisfies the condition has satisfied the whole of the chapter's positive requirement.

**The Freedom of the Unobligated Giver**

A person who gives without creating or discharging an obligation is free in a way the ordinary giver is not. He is not waiting to be thanked, so ingratitude does not wound him. He is not owed anything, so he cannot feel cheated. He can give to a person who dislikes him and to a person who will never know, and the giving costs him the same in both cases. The freedom is a practical benefit, and the chapter states it as part of the description rather than as a promise. The reader who finds his own generosity fragile — quick to appear and quick to withdraw — is being shown the reason. The fragility belongs to the ledger, and the verse describes a person who keeps none. The freedom is the reward's precondition. A person who is unobligated cannot be paid, and a person who cannot be paid can be given to without limit. The verse is not describing a loss of recompense but a change of register, and the change is why satisfaction is the term the chapter uses.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:20

> but seeking the pleasure of their Lord, the Most High

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Face as the Person Sought**

The verse supplies the motive the previous verse cleared space for. The Arabic speaks of seeking the Face of his Lord, and the idiom denotes the person rather than a direction. To seek someone's face is to seek the person himself, without intermediary and without ulterior aim. The verse therefore names the highest possible motive in the shortest possible form. Nothing stands between the giver and God: not the recipient, not the community, not the giver's own reputation, and not even the reward. The seeking is directed at the One sought, and the direction is exclusive. The face stands for the person sought, and the phrasing identifies the direction of the act. The giver is not distributing benefit into a general pool; he is addressing someone. The verse names that someone by a name reserved for God, and the naming is the whole of the definition.

**Al-Aʿlā: The Most High**

The name attached to the Lord in this verse is the superlative of high. Its function here is not decorative. A person who seeks the Most High has set his aim above every competing object, and the name states the position of that object in relation to everything else. The Qur'an uses the same name at the opening of the sūrah that commands the glorification of the Lord's name, and its use in a verse about motive is a reminder that the aim of an act determines its worth. A gift aimed at a low object is low; a gift aimed at the Most High takes its character from the aim. The reader who wants to know why the same amount given by two people can differ in value is answered by the name. The superlative is what makes the motive exclusive. A gift given for the Most High cannot simultaneously be given for an audience, a return or a reputation, because those are lesser objects and the verse admits no partnership. The exclusivity is grammatical, and it is what the reader is being asked to meet.

**The Same Motive Described Elsewhere**

The Qur'an records the motive in the words of the givers themselves. The righteous "give food—despite their desire for it—to the poor, the orphan, and the captive, ˹saying to themselves,˺ ‘We feed you only for the sake of Allah, seeking neither reward nor thanks from you’" (76:8–9). The statement in that passage is the interior speech of the giver, and it matches this verse clause for clause: the giving is for God, and neither reward nor thanks is sought from anyone. The two passages describe the same person from different angles. The reader who wants a model of the motive is given one in which the model speaks, and the speech is exactly what this verse summarises. The same motive is described elsewhere in the revelation, and the repetition is what establishes it as a requirement rather than an ideal. The reader who encounters it in more than one place cannot treat it as an exception made for an unusual person; it is the standard the practice is measured against.

**A Single Motive, Stated Exclusively**

The construction of the verse allows no addition. It does not say that the giver seeks the pleasure of his Lord among other things, or primarily, or after other considerations. It states the seeking and stops. The exclusivity is the standard, and the standard is high. A reader who gives partly for God and partly for the approval of his community has not been described by this verse, and the chapter does not offer a partial version of the description. The severity is not accidental. The chapter has spent twenty verses distinguishing two directions of striving, and the distinction depends on the purity of the aim. A mixed aim belongs to neither pole, and the chapter is describing the poles. Stating the motive exclusively removes the reader's room for manoeuvre. A mixed motive is not a partial fulfilment; it is a different act. The verse is therefore more demanding than it sounds, and the reader who has been giving with an audience in mind has been told that the audience changes what the act is.

**What the Motive Produces in Practice**

A giver whose aim is the Face of God is unaffected by the recipient's response. If he is thanked, the thanks are not what he sought; if he is insulted, the insult does not undo the act. The stability of the motive produces a stability in the conduct, and the stability is what makes the giving sustainable. A person who gives for gratitude stops giving when the gratitude stops. A person who gives for reputation stops when the audience leaves. The person described in this verse continues, because the object of his seeking does not change and does not withdraw. The reader who has found his own generosity to be intermittent is being shown the reason: the aim was set at something that varies. In practice the motive produces conduct that is quiet, steady and unselective. A person seeking an audience chooses recipients who will be seen and occasions that will be noticed; a person seeking the Most High gives where the need is, including where nobody will know. The difference is visible in the pattern, not in the amount.

**The Summit of the Chapter's Description**

The three verses that define the most mindful ascend. The first names the act — giving wealth to purify. The second removes the human ledger — no favour to be repaid. The third names the aim — the Face of the Most High. The ascent is from an outward act to an inward direction, and the direction is the last thing the chapter says about the person before it states his reward. The placement is a judgement. What ultimately describes a person is not what he did but what he was aiming at when he did it, and the aiming is what God sees. The verse is therefore the summit of the chapter's moral description, and the reward that follows is attached to it rather than to the act alone. This is the highest point of the chapter's positive description. The earlier verses defined the act and its inner condition; this verse names the object of the condition, and the naming completes the description. The reader who reaches it has been given the whole of what the chapter asks.

**Seeking as an Activity**

The verb of seeking in this verse denotes an active pursuit rather than a wish. A person who seeks something is moving toward it, and the movement has a direction and a cost. The verse therefore describes the giver's aim as an activity that organises his conduct, not as a sentiment he holds while doing something else. The distinction matters for the reader's practice. A person who says that he gives for God's pleasure while arranging his giving around what will be noticed has a wish rather than a pursuit. The pursuit is visible in the choices it produces: the gift given where nobody sees, the amount given that nobody would guess, the recipient chosen because of need rather than because of usefulness. Seeking is an activity, and the verse describes a direction of attention rather than a feeling. A person who seeks the face of the Most High is arranging his conduct around that end, and the arrangement is checkable. The reader who has never arranged anything around it has been told what such an arrangement looks like.

**The Aim Determines the Weight of the Act**

Two people can give the same amount to the same person, and the two acts can differ completely in weight. The difference is the aim, and the aim is not visible to any observer. The verse names the highest possible aim, and the naming explains why the chapter places it last in its description of the most mindful. The act has already been described; the ledger has already been closed; what remains is the direction of the person's wanting, and the direction is what God sees. The reader who wants his giving to count is directed here rather than to the amount. A small transfer aimed at the Most High outweighs a large one aimed at an audience, and the weighing is not performed by anyone in this world. The aim determines the weight of the act, and this is why the chapter can promise satisfaction rather than measurement. Two gifts identical in size differ entirely in worth if their aims differ, and the verse settles the question by making the aim the criterion. The reader is therefore working on the right thing when he works on his intention.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:21

> They will certainly be pleased

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Promise Measured by Satisfaction**

The chapter ends with three words, and the words describe a state rather than an object. The giver will be pleased. No garden is described, no river is named, and no quantity is stated. The promise is framed in terms of the receiver's satisfaction, which is the only measure that cannot fall short. A reader who is promised a specified reward can compare it with what others received; a reader who is promised that he will be pleased has been promised something that admits no comparison. The verse is the most economical form of the promise available, and its economy is what makes it complete. The promise is measured by the recipient rather than by the amount, and the measurement is the point. A sum can be counted and found insufficient; satisfaction cannot be insufficient, because it is defined as the end of wanting. The verse therefore promises something that cannot disappoint the person who receives it.

**The Echo of the Chapter of the Morning Light**

The same promise appears in the chapter revealed shortly before this one, and the wording is almost identical: "And ˹surely˺ your Lord will give so much to you that you will be pleased" (93:5). The repetition across two adjacent chapters is significant. In the earlier chapter the promise is addressed to the Prophet in the second person, at a moment when he had been accused of abandonment. In this chapter it is addressed to a type — the one who gives his wealth to purify himself — in the third person. The transfer of the promise from the messenger to the giver is the chapter's final move. What was assured to him has been made available to anyone who fits the description. The echo connects the ending to the chapter of the morning light, and the connection is deliberate. Both chapters close with satisfaction after a description of giving, and the reader who notices the echo understands that the pattern is not accidental. Two short chapters reach the same destination by the same route.

**Why Satisfaction Is the Right Term**

The giver described in the preceding verses sought nothing from anyone. He did not want thanks, he did not want repayment, and he did not want an audience. A reward framed as public honour would not suit him, and a reward framed as material increase would not answer his motive. The verse supplies the reward that fits: satisfaction. A person whose aim was the Face of God is satisfied by the attainment of that aim, and the attainment is what the promise describes. The reader who notices that the reward matches the motive has understood the chapter's method. The end of a striving is determined by what the striving was aimed at, and the aim in this case was not a thing. Satisfaction is the right term because the giver expected nothing. A person who expected a return can be underpaid, and the verse would have to specify an amount; a person who expected nothing cannot be, and no amount needs specifying. The term fits the motive described in the previous verses exactly.

**The Structure of the Ending**

The chapter's last five verses describe a removal, a giving, an absence of obligation, an aim, and a satisfaction. The sequence is deliberate and it ends inwardly. Nothing in the final verse refers to the Fire, to the wretched, or to the wealth that will not benefit its owner. The chapter that opened with an oath on the night closes with the contentment of a person who gave in secret. The contrast between the opening and the ending is the whole of the chapter's argument compressed into its frame: darkness and light, withholding and giving, ruin and pleasure. The reader who has followed the sequence arrives at the last word already persuaded. The structure of the ending closes the chapter without a summary. The warnings have been issued, the categories defined and the conduct described; the final verse states the outcome for the person who met the description. The reader is left with a promise rather than a restatement, and the promise is the appropriate close.

**What the Pleasure Includes**

The verse does not itemise the satisfaction, and the omission allows it to include everything the chapter has promised. The removal from the Fire is part of it. The acceptance of the gift is part of it, and the acceptance matters to a person who gave without knowing whether the giving counted. The meeting with the One he was aiming at is the largest part, and it is the part the verse leaves unstated. The Qur'an describes the same double satisfaction elsewhere: "Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him" (98:8). The mutuality of that description is what the three words of this verse point toward, and the pointing is enough. What the pleasure includes is not itemised, and the absence of an itemisation is what the term means. It covers the acceptance of the act, the removal of what was feared and the arrival at what was hoped for, and the reader is not required to know the details in order to want it.

**The Last Word of the Chapter**

A chapter that began with the diversity of human striving ends with the pleasure of one of the two groups. The choice of ending is a rhetorical decision, and it is a generous one. The chapter could have closed on the Fire, as the chapter of the Sun closes on a Maker who fears no consequence. It closes instead on satisfaction, and the closing leaves the reader with the positive term in view. The warning has been delivered and the category of the wretched has been defined; what remains in the reader's mind at the end is the state of the person who gave. The verse is therefore an invitation as much as a promise, and it is offered to whoever is still holding the text. The last word of the chapter is a promise addressed to a person who gave without expecting anything, and the placement is the reward's final argument. Everything before it described conduct; this verse describes what the conduct is worth. Anyone who has read the chapter knows both, and the knowing is the point of the order.

**The Pleasure of a Person Who Expected Nothing**

The giver described in the preceding verses sought no return from anyone, and the verse promises him satisfaction anyway. The sequence is instructive. A person who does not expect a reward is not calculating, and a person who is not calculating cannot be disappointed. The satisfaction promised to him is therefore of a kind he could not have arranged for himself, because arrangement requires calculation and calculation would have spoiled the giving. The reader who has given without expecting anything and found himself unexpectedly content has experienced the promise in a small form. The verse names the experience and places it at the end of a life rather than at the end of an afternoon. A person who expected nothing receives satisfaction as an unearned surplus, and the surplus is the reason the promise can be absolute. Nothing he did created an entitlement, so nothing he failed to do reduces it. The verse is describing generosity on the part of the one who promised.

**An Invitation in the Form of a Promise**

The chapter ends by describing a state rather than by issuing a command, and the description functions as an invitation. The reader is shown a person who gave, sought nothing, aimed at God and was satisfied, and the description is left open. Nobody is named in it, and no condition excludes the reader from it. The chapter that opened with an oath on the night closes with the pleasure of a giver, and the closing is the argument. The warning has been delivered and the category of the wretched has been defined with precision; what remains in front of the reader is the other description, and the description can still be fitted. The last word of the chapter is satisfaction, and it is offered to whoever is willing to begin with the giving. The invitation is carried by the form of the promise. Nobody is commanded to give in this verse; a person is shown what the giving is worth and left to decide. A person who has followed the chapter has been given both the description and the reward, and the decision remaining is entirely his.
