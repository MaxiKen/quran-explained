# Sūrah al-Layl (Chapter 92) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Enshrouding Night and the Divergent Paths**

Al-Layl, "The Night," is the ninety-second chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 21 verses of early Makkan revelation. It opens with three cosmic and biological oaths: *“By the night when it covers, and by the day when it shines, and by the creation of male and female—indeed, your efforts are diverse / divergent (*inna saʿyakum la-shattā*)!”* (92:1–4). In classical exegesis, Sūrah al-Layl is universally celebrated for verses 17–21, which immortalize the peerless spiritual character of Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq: purchasing and emancipating tortured Muslim slaves (such as Bilāl and ʿĀmir ibn Fuhayrah) out of pure, unadulterated love for God, crowned with the divine guarantee: *“And he will surely be well-pleased (*wa-la-sawfa yarḍā*)!”* In twenty-one balanced, rhythmic verses, Sūrah al-Layl constructs an unshakeable moral binary: contrasting the path of generosity, piety, and ease against the path of miserliness, false self-sufficiency, and ruin.

**The Slave-Market of Makkah and the Mercy of Abū Bakr**

The historical occasion of Sūrah al-Layl is recorded in authentic traditions by Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿUrwah ibn al-Zubayr, and al-Qurṭubī. In the rocky alleys of Makkah, wealthy pagan masters were torturing their enslaved converts to force their apostasy. Abū Bakr used his vast mercantile wealth to purchase these helpless slaves—weak men, elderly women, and vulnerable girls—freeing them immediately for the sake of Allah. His father, Abū Quḥāfah (then still an idolater), chided him: *“O my son, I see you freeing weak, helpless people! Why do you not buy strong, virile young men who could stand with you, defend you, and protect you?”* Abū Bakr replied with breathtaking spiritual purity: *“O my father, I only seek what is with Allah!”* Verses 17–21 descended as an eternal celestial tribute to his sincerity: *“And the most God-fearing will be spared it [the Fire]—he who gives his wealth to purify himself, and not for any favor owed to him for which he seeks recompense, but only seeking the Face of his Lord, the Most High; and he will surely be well-pleased!”*

**The Structural Symmetry of the Two Paths**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Layl is an astonishing masterwork of parallel moral symmetry across three movements:
1. The Two Engines of Human Destiny (1–11): Opens with the oaths of night, day, and the male and female pairs, establishing that human efforts are radically divergent (*la-shattā*). It outlines the two psychological trajectories: - *The Path of Ease* (5–7): As for he who gives in charity (*aʿṭā*), maintains God-consciousness (*wattaqā*), and confirms the ultimate good (*wa-ṣaddaqa bi-l-ḥusnā*)—*“We will ease him toward the ease (*fa-sa-nuyassiruhū li-l-yusrā*)!”* - *The Path of Difficulty* (8–11): But as for he who is a tight-fisted miser (*bakhila*), considers himself self-sufficient (*wastaghnā*), and denies the ultimate good (*wa-kadhdhaba bi-l-ḥusnā*)—*“We will ease him toward hardship (*fa-sa-nuyassiruhū li-l-ʿusrā*)!”* And what will his wealth avail him when he falls into the grave?
2. The Divine Guidance and the Blazing Fire (12–16): Affirms that guidance is upon God, and that to Him belongs the Hereafter and the first life. It issues the urgent warning: *“So I have warned you of a Fire which blazes (*nāran talaẓẓā*)!”* None will burn therein except the most wretched who denied and turned away.
3. The Archetype of Sincere Charity (17–21): The noble portrait of the God-fearing servant (*al-atqā*) who spends his wealth to purify his soul, seeking neither worldly gratitude nor repaying a social debt, but desiring only the Countenance of his Lord, the Most High (*wajhi Rabbihi al-Aʿlā*), concluding with the divine pledge of absolute satisfaction: *“Wa-la-sawfa yarḍā”* (And he will surely be well-pleased!).

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:1

> By the night when it covers

**Covering as a Provision**

The night's covering is a mercy in the Qur'an's account, and the chapter relies on that fact. God "has made the night for rest" (6:96), and the alternation of night and day is presented as a design for the creatures living inside it. A world without covering would allow no sleep, no privacy and no end to exposure. The verse therefore swears on a kindness, and the kindness is the ground of the demand that follows. A person addressed by a God who arranged his rest can be asked about his striving, because the asking comes from a source that has already provided for him. The oath is not a threat dressed as a solemnity. It is a reminder of provision, placed at the head of a chapter that will ask what the provision was for.

That provision has a cost attached to it, and the chapter will name the cost. Rest is given so that striving can continue; a person who has been rested has no ground for idleness. The oath on the covering night is therefore not only a comfort but the first item in an account the reader will be asked to settle.

**The Oath Pair and Its Companions**

Swearing by the alternation of night and day is a recurring form in the Qur'an. The chapter that precedes this one opens with the sun and its brightness, the moon following it, the day disclosing and the night covering (91:1–4). The chapter of the Morning Light swears by the forenoon and the night when it falls still (93:1–2). The sūrah of the Dawn opens with the dawn and nights numbered. The repetition establishes a habit of thought: the alternation is a sign, and the sign is available to everyone. The verse in this chapter belongs to that family, and its position at the head of the list gives it a specific job. It supplies the first half of the contrast on which the chapter's central claim — that human striving diverges — will be built.

What distinguishes this chapter's pair is the order and the brevity. Two verbs, two states, and no elaboration. The reader is expected to supply the rest from experience, and the expectation is safe, because every hearer has watched the alternation for as long as he has been alive.

**Night as the Hour of What Is Hidden**

The covering has a moral dimension that the chapter exploits later. The night is when acts are performed without witnesses, and the Qur'an's ethics rank the hidden deed above the visible one: "To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you of your sins" (2:271). The ranking depends on the existence of a night in which hiding is possible. A world that never covered its inhabitants would make display unavoidable, and sincerity would be impossible to distinguish from performance. The oath on the covering night therefore invokes the condition that makes the chapter's later praise of unobtrusive charity intelligible. The giver described at the end of this sūrah gives without seeking a return from anyone, and the night is part of why such giving can exist.

The chapter's later praise of the giver who seeks no return belongs to this register. A gift performed where nobody sees it can only be motivated by something other than opinion, and the night is the condition in which such a gift is possible. The oath has already honoured that condition before the giver is described.

**What the Oath Prepares**

Three oaths will be taken before the chapter states what it is about, and the first of them is the least obvious. A reader approaching the verse for the first time may wonder what the night has to do with generosity and stinginess. The connection is established by the end of the chapter, and it is not allegorical. The night covers, the day discloses, and human beings strive in both. What a person does when he is covered and what he does when he is disclosed are the two measures of his conduct, and the chapter will describe both kinds of person. The oath is therefore a preparation rather than a decoration, and its darkness is the first term in a contrast that the reader will be asked to apply to himself.

The frame is fixed and the frame is not the reader's doing. He did not arrange the alternation, and he cannot suspend it. Whatever he strives for, he strives inside an order set for him, and the chapter's fourth verse will state that the striving diverges. The oath has established the setting in which the divergence matters.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:2

> and the day when it shines

**Tajallā: Brightness as Unveiling**

The verb *tajallā* describes a brightness that reveals — the clearing of a sky, the full appearance of something previously hidden. It is the same family of meaning as the verb used in the preceding chapter for the day's disclosure (91:3), and the two chapters are close enough in date that the shared vocabulary is significant. The day in this verse is not only a period of light but an act of unveiling, and the object is the world that the night had covered. The alternation is therefore described as a pair of actions performed on the same object, and the actions are performed in turn without failure. The reader is watching a system, and the system is the guarantee behind the statement that follows.

The verb's sense of clearing is important for the moral reading that follows. What is unveiled is not created by the unveiling; it was there, hidden. The daylight does not produce a person's conduct, it displays it, and the display is what makes conduct a matter of record rather than of intention alone.

**Daylight as the Time of Earning**

The day's brightness has a practical consequence. It is when work is done: fields are tended, markets are open, goods are moved, and the household's provision is earned. The Qur'an assigns the two states their functions plainly — God "has made the night for rest and ˹made˺ the sun and the moon ˹to travel˺ with precision" (6:96). The verse in this chapter swears on the earning half of that arrangement, and the swearing carries a claim. The same daylight that enables a person to feed his family exposes what he does while earning. The chapter's later description of the generous and the stingy is a description of conduct performed in daylight, in public, where other people can see it and respond to it.

The exposure is not limited to other people, and the chapter assumes the fact without stating it. A deed performed in daylight has witnesses, and the chief witness is not human. The verse swears on the hour in which that witnessing operates, and the swearing is part of the chapter's method of establishing accountability.

**The Alternation as a Sign**

The Qur'an treats the alternation of night and day as evidence rather than as background. "Among His signs are the day and the night, the sun and the moon" (41:37). "He wraps the night around the day, and wraps the day around the night" (39:5). The verse in this chapter is one instance of a pattern, and the pattern is doing argumentative work. A system that alternates with perfect reliability implies a disposer who maintains it, and the disposer is named in the very next verse. The reader is led from the reliability of the alternation to the authority of the One who arranged it, and the lead is accomplished in two short verses. Nothing in the movement requires a premise the hearer does not already accept from observation.

The movement from reliability to authority is accomplished without a stated premise. A reader who notices that the alternation has never failed has already granted that something maintains it, and the next verse names the maintainer. The oath list is therefore an argument conducted entirely in observations the reader cannot dispute.

**Brightness and Moral Visibility**

The chapter's moral vocabulary draws on the physical terms it has sworn by. What is done in the light is visible; what is done in the dark is not. The Qur'an's ethics use both states, and it does not treat visibility as the measure of worth. The hidden gift ranks above the displayed one, and the display of generosity is criticised where it is performed for an audience: those "who donate their wealth just to show off" are compared to a barren rock whose film of soil is washed away by rain (2:264). The day's brightness therefore makes conduct visible without making it good. The chapter will describe a person who gives in a way that seeks no return from anyone, and the description belongs to the night's register as much as to the day's.

The distinction between visibility and worth is one the chapter insists on. Brightness makes an act knowable; it does not make it good. The reader who performs his generosity in the disclosed hours and nowhere else has used the day for what it can give him, and the chapter will describe a person who does not.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:3

> And by ˹the One˺ Who created male and female

**The Creator Enters the Oath**

Two oaths on created states, and then a third on the One who creates. The shift is the same one the preceding chapter makes when it moves from the sun and the night to the Builder of the heaven. The oath is no longer on a feature of the world but on the source of a feature — specifically, the source of the most basic distinction in the created order. The chapter's argument about human striving will rest on this verse, because the authority to judge striving belongs to the One who made the strivers.

The naming of the disposer is the point at which the oath list acquires an authority.

**The Grammatical Reading and Its Alternative**

The Arabic word here is *mā*, which can function as "the One who" or as "that which". On the second reading the verse would swear by the creation of the male and the female rather than by their Creator, and some exegetes preferred that reading on the ground that God does not swear by Himself. The same question arises in the three parallel verses of the preceding chapter, and the same answer applies. The personal reading is the more natural in Arabic, and the theological difficulty is not serious: God swears by what He wills, and swearing by His own act of creation is a way of magnifying it. The consistency of the construction across both chapters supports the personal reading in all of them.

The alternative reading survives in the tradition for a reason worth stating: it protects the principle that creatures swear by their Creator and not the reverse. The principle is sound, and the personal reading does not violate it, since God's swearing by His own act magnifies the act rather than subjecting the swearer.

**The Pair as the Basic Form of Creation**

The male and the female are named as the fundamental pairing of the created order. The Qur'an states the same fact elsewhere: "O humanity! Indeed, We created you from a male and a female, and made you into peoples and tribes so that you may ˹get to˺ know one another" (49:13), and again "O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women" (4:1). The pairing is not presented as a hierarchy in these verses. It is presented as the mechanism by which the population exists at all. The oath in this verse swears on that mechanism, and the swearing honours both terms equally.

The mechanism is stated in these verses without hierarchy, and the absence of hierarchy is what the chapter's argument requires. A reader who arrives at the description of two kinds of person must not be able to sort them by birth, and the oath has already removed birth from the available categories.

**What the Oath Excludes**

Placing the creation of male and female inside an oath removes both terms from the category of achievements. Neither sex is earned, chosen or attained, and neither can be cited as a ground of superiority. The verse that follows the oath states the principle explicitly in the sūrah of the Rooms: "Surely the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous among you" (49:13). The chapter in front of us makes the same point by its structure. It swears by the creation of the pair and then describes two kinds of person — the one who gives and the one who withholds — without reference to sex, lineage or status. The categories that matter in the chapter's argument are entirely behavioural, and the oath has already excluded the others.

The exclusion operates silently, which is what makes it effective. Nothing in the verse argues against claims of superiority; it simply swears by the creation of both terms, and the swearing places them on the same footing before the argument begins. A reader cannot rank what has been sworn by as one act.

**The Pair as the Mechanism of the Population**

The oath names the pair rather than the individual, and the choice is deliberate. No human being exists except through the pair, and the fact is stated elsewhere as the ground of a common obligation: "O humanity! Neither is the origin of the other in the sense that would justify a hierarchy, and the chapter's later argument makes no use of sex as a category. The reader who arrives at the description of the giver and the withholder is reading about two dispositions, not about two kinds of people by birth.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:4

> Surely the ends you strive for are diverse

**The Statement the Oaths Were Taken For**

Three oaths, and then the claim they guarantee. The verse is the answer to the swearing, and it is stated in four words: your striving is diverse. The emphatic particle marks the statement as settled, and the word for striving — *saʿy* — denotes effort with a direction, not movement in general. The claim is not that people differ in temperament or taste. It is that they are working toward different ends, and that the difference is fundamental enough to divide them into two groups. The verse supplies the thesis of the chapter, and the ten verses that follow are its exposition.

The four words carry the whole thesis, and their brevity means the reader meets the claim before he has formed a resistance to it. The oaths have done the preparatory work; the claim arrives as a plain statement of fact, and the elaboration follows over the next ten verses rather than preceding it.

**Saʿy: Effort With a Destination**

The word chosen is significant. *Saʿy* is used in the Qur'an for purposeful exertion, and it appears in the description of the rite performed between the two hills during pilgrimage — a walking that is neither a stroll nor a race but a directed effort with a fixed end. Applied to a life, the word implies that every person is moving toward something, and that the movement has a terminus. The chapter's claim is that the termini differ. Nobody in the verse is described as standing still. The division is between directions, not between activity and idleness, and the reader who assumes that the inactive person belongs to neither group has misread the word.

The word's religious use reinforces the point. A rite performed between two fixed points is a directed effort with a terminus, and the same word applied to a life implies that the terminus exists whether or not the person has identified it. The chapter's claim is that people differ about where they are going.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:5

> As for the one who is charitable, mindful ˹of Allah˺

**Ittaqā: Mindfulness as the Second Term**

The second quality is *taqwā*, and the word is difficult to render because it combines fear, care and consciousness. A person with *taqwā* is one who guards himself against what would harm him in God's reckoning, and the guarding shapes his conduct continuously rather than at intervals. The pairing of giving with mindfulness is significant. Giving without mindfulness can be performance or impulse; mindfulness without giving can be a private state with no effect on anyone. The verse joins them, and the joining is the description of the first group. A reader who has one of the two and not the other has not been described by this verse, and the chapter expects him to notice which one is missing.

The guarding is continuous rather than occasional, and that continuity is what distinguishes it from remorse. A person with mindfulness does not feel badly at intervals; he arranges his conduct around a reckoning he expects. The arrangement shapes small decisions, and the small decisions are where the chapter's division is decided.

**The Report Connected With These Verses**

The tradition links this passage to a discussion about destiny. A report agreed upon by al-Bukhārī and Muslim records the companions asking the Prophet ﷺ whether deeds matter if the places of the people of Paradise and the Fire are already registered, and his answer: work, for everyone will be facilitated toward that for which he was created. The exegetes connect that answer to these very verses, since the chapter goes on to describe facilitation in both directions. The connection is important for reading the verse correctly. The description of the giver is not a description of a person who was made to give. It is a description of a person who gives, and the facilitation promised in the seventh verse is the consequence of the giving rather than its substitute.

The link matters because it prevents a fatalistic reading of the chapter. The facilitation promised two verses later is a response to conduct, and the report connects the two without collapsing them. A person is eased toward what he was created for, and what he was created for is expressed in what he does.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:6

> and ˹firmly˺ believes in the finest reward

**Al-Ḥusnā: The Finest Thing**

The third quality of the first group is belief in *al-ḥusnā*, the feminine superlative of good — the finest, the most excellent. The word is definite, which means it refers to something specific rather than to goodness in general, and the exegetes have identified it in several ways. Most take it as the testimony that there is no god but God, or belief in God simply. Others take it as Paradise, since the Qur'an calls the reward of the Hereafter "the finest" in several places. A third reading takes it as God Himself, on the ground that the most beautiful names belong to Him. The readings converge more than they diverge: the finest thing is the ultimate object of belief, and believing in it is what orients a life.

The definiteness of the word excludes a general optimism about goodness. The finest is a specific object, and the readings differ about its identity rather than about its ultimacy. A reader who believes in goodness in the abstract has not been described by this verse; the verse requires an object.

**Belief as the Third Term, Not the First**

The verse places belief after giving and mindfulness, and the placement is instructive. A reader might expect the chapter to begin with faith, since faith is the ground of acceptance in the Qur'an's theology. Instead the first case is described by what a person does, and belief is named third. The order does not demote belief. It locates it. Belief in the finest reward is the conviction that gives the other two qualities their direction: a person gives because he believes there is a return, and he guards himself because he believes the return is real. The verse describes the three as a single configuration, and the configuration is what identifies the group.

The placement locates belief inside a life rather than ahead of it. A person does not first acquire a complete conviction and then begin to act; he acts, and the acting clarifies the conviction. The verse describes the configuration as it exists in a person who is already giving, and the description is therefore realistic.

**The Finest Reward and the Two Worlds**

The belief in the finest reward does not require a person to despise this world. The Qur'an states the relation plainly: "But whoever desires the Hereafter and strives for it accordingly, and is a ˹true˺ believer, it is they whose striving will be appreciated" (17:19), and the case it condemns is the one who desires this fleeting world alone (17:18). The distinction is between ranking and rejection. A believer in the finest reward may still work, trade and provide; what he does not do is treat this world as the final term of his striving. The verse describes a person whose expenditure is shaped by a conviction about a return, and the shaping is visible in the way he gives — freely, and without needing to be thanked.

The shaping is visible in the manner of the gift rather than in its size. A person who believes in a return gives without needing to be thanked, because the thanks were never the payment he expected. The chapter will describe that manner explicitly, and the description follows from the belief named here.

**Belief in the Unseen as the Ground of Giving**

Every act of giving is a transfer from the visible to the invisible. A person parts with something he can see in exchange for something he cannot, and the exchange is rational only if the invisible term is real. The verse therefore names the belief that makes the giving coherent. A man who does not believe in the finest reward is not being irrational when he withholds; he is being consistent with his disbelief. The chapter's two cases are therefore not two levels of virtue but two convictions, and the convictions produce the conduct. The reader who finds himself unable to give has been given a diagnosis rather than a rebuke: the belief is missing, and the belief is what needs attention.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:7

> We will facilitate for them the Way of Ease

**Taysīr: Making Easy as a Divine Act**

The verb is *yassara*, to make easy, and the Qur'an uses it for God's action on a person's path rather than for the person's own competence. The ease is supplied, not achieved. The same assurance appears in the legislation on fasting, where the reason given for the concessions is that "Allah intends ease for you, not hardship" (2:185), and in the sūrah of Divorce, where the promise is attached to mindfulness: after hardship, Allah will bring about ease (65:7). The verse in this chapter states the general rule in its strongest form. The ease is not a consequence that follows mechanically from good conduct; it is an act performed by God on the path of a person who has been described in the previous two verses.

The attribution matters for the reader's expectation. Ease that depends on his own competence would fail whenever his competence failed. Ease supplied by God does not depend on the person's condition, which is why the promise can be relied on by someone who is tired, poor or opposed.

**Why Ease Is Promised to the Giver**

The connection between giving and ease is not obvious, and it is worth stating. A person who gives has removed an obstacle from his own path, and the obstacle is attachment. Wealth is the most common form of security in the world, and a person who holds it tightly has built his sense of safety on something he cannot keep. Giving dismantles that construction, and the dismantling is painful at the moment and liberating afterwards. The ease promised in this verse includes the ease of a person who no longer needs his wealth to feel safe. The Qur'an describes the same liberation elsewhere: "And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are ˹truly˺ successful" (64:16). The saved person is the one whose path has been made easy, and the saving is from himself.

The liberation is the practical content of the promise, and it arrives before any external change. A person who has given has already begun to loosen the grip that wealth had on his sense of safety, and the loosening is felt as relief. The verse names that relief and attributes it correctly.

**The Reciprocity of Turning Toward God**

The verse describes a reciprocity that the Qur'an states as a rule: "Indeed, Allah would never change a people's state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺" (13:11). The movement begins with the person and is completed by God. The giver has taken a step, and the step is answered by a making-easy that he could not perform himself. The reciprocity is not symmetrical — the human act is small and the divine act is large — but it is real, and it is the mechanism by which a religious life becomes sustainable. A person who had to generate his own ease would exhaust himself; a person whose effort is met by facilitation can continue. The verse supplies the second half of that arrangement.

The asymmetry of the reciprocity is what makes a religious life possible at all. If the response were proportionate to the human act, nobody could sustain it. The small step is met by a large one, and the meeting is what allows a person to continue after failures that would otherwise end the effort.

**The Path of Ease as a Direction, Not a Comfort**

The ease promised is the ease of a path, and a path has a destination. The verse does not promise a comfortable life, and it does not promise the removal of difficulty. It promises that the way toward the finest reward will be made easy for the person who is moving along it. The distinction matters for anyone who reads the verse as a guarantee of prosperity. The giver described in the previous verses may still be poor, sick or opposed; what is promised is that his direction will not be blocked. The Qur'an's use of the same language elsewhere confirms the reading: guidance itself is described as an opening of the heart, and the opening is what makes the path practicable.

The destination gives the ease its meaning. A path made easy toward ruin would be a calamity, and the chapter describes exactly that in the verse that mirrors this one. The reader who wants ease must therefore ask where the path leads before he celebrates how smooth it has become.

**Recognising the Promise in One's Own Life**

The promise is testable, and the test is available to any reader. A person who has given, guarded himself and believed can ask what has become easier since he began. If giving has become less costly in feeling, if the thought of the finest reward has become more settled, and if the path he chose requires less argument than it did, the facilitation is operating. The test is not a measure of prosperity. A person may be poor, sick and opposed while his path is being made easy in the sense the verse describes. The distinction between external circumstance and internal facilitation is the distinction the chapter relies on, and the reader who confuses them will misread both this verse and the one that mirrors it.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:8

> And as for the one who is stingy, indifferent ˹to Allah˺

**The Second Case Begins With Withholding**

The mirror image of the first case opens with the opposite of giving. The verb *bakhila* denotes stinginess — the refusal to give what one has and could give. It is not a description of poverty. A poor person cannot be stingy in the sense this verse requires, since stinginess presupposes something to withhold. The verse therefore identifies the second group by the same test as the first: what a person does with what he has. The reader who has nothing is not described here, and the exclusion is a comfort rather than a slight. The chapter's division is between those who give and those who withhold, and a person with nothing to withhold belongs to neither by that measure. The presupposition matters for the reader's self-examination. A person who has nothing is not described here, and the exclusion is not a mercy for the stingy but a clarification of who the chapter is addressing. The test is what a person does with what is in his hand at the moment a need appears.

**Istaghnā: Deeming Himself Beyond Need**

The second quality is *istaghnā*, and the word carries a claim. It denotes considering oneself self-sufficient, beyond need, independent. The Qur'an uses the same verb for the human being's error at its root: a person transgresses because he considers himself self-sufficient. The claim is directed upward. A person who deems himself beyond need has removed his dependence on God from his own self-description, and the removal is what makes the stinginess coherent. A man who believes he needs nothing from God has no reason to give anything for God. The two qualities in this verse are not separate faults; the second explains the first. The claim is rarely stated aloud, which is why the verse names it. Nobody announces that he needs nothing from God; he simply stops asking, stops accounting for his conduct, and stops expecting a return. The verse gives the silent claim a name, and naming it is what makes it examinable.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:9

> and ˹staunchly˺ denies the finest reward

**Denial of the Same Object**

The object denied in this verse is identical to the object believed in the verse describing the first case: *al-ḥusnā*, the finest. The identity is the hinge of the chapter. The two groups are not distinguished by what they know but by what they affirm or refuse about the same thing. The exegetes identify the finest variously as the testimony of divine oneness, as Paradise, or as God Himself, and the readings agree on the point that matters: the object is ultimate, and the response to it divides humanity. A reader who thinks the chapter's two cases are distinguished by behaviour alone has not read this verse. The behaviour follows from the affirmation or the denial. The identity of the object is what makes the chapter's division sharp. Two groups are not responding to different claims but to the same one, and the responses are opposite. A reader cannot explain the difference by saying that he was shown something else, because the object is fixed and named in both descriptions.

**Why Denial Produces Stinginess**

The connection between the two qualities in this pair is causal, and the causality runs from belief to conduct. A person who does not believe in a return has no reason to transfer wealth now for a benefit later. His calculation is rational on his premises: the wealth is real, the reward is not, and the transfer is a loss. The verse therefore identifies the root of the behaviour rather than the behaviour itself. The Qur'an states the same connection where it describes those whose spending is not accepted: "And what prevented their donations from being accepted is that they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger, they never come to prayer except half-heartedly, and they never donate except resentfully" (9:54). The resentment in that verse has the same source as the stinginess in this one. The rationality is internal to the disbelief, and that is why argument rarely changes it. The man is not making an arithmetic error; he is being consistent with a premise the chapter rejects. The correction therefore has to reach the premise, and the chapter reaches it by describing the conduct the premise produces.

**The Reader's Own Denial**

The verse is phrased in the third person, but its application is personal. Most readers do not consider themselves deniers of the finest reward, and the verse asks them to examine the claim. Denial in the sense described here is not usually a public announcement. It appears in conduct: a person who says he believes in a return and never transfers anything for it is performing the denial while professing the belief. The chapter's method is to test belief by expenditure, and the test is available to any reader at any time. What a person gives is the measure of what he actually believes about the finest thing, and the measure does not depend on his self-description. The test is behavioural and therefore honest. A person's statements about the finest reward are shaped by what he wants them to be; his handling of his property is not. The chapter trusts the ledger over the profession, and the reader who wants an accurate answer about himself should do the same.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:10

> We will facilitate for them the path of hardship

**The Same Verb, the Opposite Path**

The verse uses the same verb of facilitation that appeared in the promise to the giver, and the symmetry is exact. God makes easy — and what He makes easy here is the path of hardship. The construction is deliberate and paradoxical. A path of hardship is not made comfortable; it is made easy to walk, which means the person on it finds each successive step less resisted than the last. The facilitation is therefore not a mercy in this instance. It is the removal of obstacles from a direction that leads somewhere the person will not want to go. The verse states one of the Qur'an's most severe ideas: that a person can be helped along a path, and the help can be his ruin. The paradox is the most severe statement in the chapter, and it is stated without emphasis or comment. A person is not punished with difficulty here; he is accommodated in a direction. The accommodation feels like success, and the feeling is the mechanism by which the direction becomes permanent.

**The Reciprocity of Turning Away**

The mechanism is the mirror of the one described in the seventh verse. There, a step toward God is met by a making-easy. Here, a step away is met by the same. The Qur'an states the general rule: "Indeed, Allah would never change a people's state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺" (13:11). The reciprocity operates in both directions, and the verse describes the second. A person who withholds, claims independence and denies the reward finds each of those positions easier to hold than the last. The hardening is not imposed from outside; it is the accumulated effect of choices, each of which was facilitated by the one before. The rule operates the same way in both directions, and the symmetry is what makes it intelligible. Neither response is arbitrary, and neither is imposed before the step is taken. The chapter describes a system rather than a mood.

**Why This Is Not Coercion**

The facilitation described in this verse has been read by some as evidence that God determines a person's end irrespective of his choices, and the reading does not hold. The facilitation is attached to conduct described in the two preceding verses. A person is not facilitated toward hardship before he has withheld, claimed independence and denied; the sequence in the text is fixed, and the sequence is the answer. The verse describes a response to a direction already chosen, and the response makes the chosen direction easier to continue. What is removed is not the possibility of turning back but the felt difficulty of continuing forward. The distinction preserves both the seriousness of the state and the reality of the choice that produced it. The sequence preserves the responsibility of the person on the path. He chose the direction, and the facilitation followed the choice rather than preceding it. What is removed is the friction that might have stopped him, and the removal is a response to his persistence rather than its cause.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:11

> And their wealth will be of no benefit to them when they tumble ˹into Hell˺

**Taraddā: Falling Headlong**

The verb describes a fall, and the form denotes a person tumbling downward rather than being placed somewhere. The image is of a descent that the person cannot arrest, and the translation's rendering — when they tumble — preserves it. The choice of verb matters for the argument. The wealth mentioned in the first half of the verse is useless precisely because its owner is in that state. The verse joins the two facts — the possession and the fall — and the joining is the whole of its warning. What he accumulated is intact and irrelevant at the same time.

**What Wealth Cannot Purchase**

The Qur'an states the limitation of wealth in its most explicit form in the legislation on charity: "O believers! Donate from what We have provided for you before the arrival of a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession" (2:254). Three mechanisms by which a person ordinarily escapes consequences are removed — the deal, the connection and the advocate — and wealth operates through all three in this world. The verse in this chapter states the same fact from the other side. The wealth itself will not benefit its owner, because the mechanisms through which wealth works have been closed. The reader who understands how his money protects him here is being told that the protection is a feature of this world and not of the next. The three mechanisms are how wealth operates in this world, and their closure is what the verse announces. A reader who understands his own security will recognise the three and will recognise that all of them depend on a world that continues. The verse states the moment at which it does not.

**The Same Statement Made Throughout the Revelation**

The claim is not local to this chapter. Worldly wealth is described as adornment, and the lasting deeds as "far better with your Lord in reward and in hope" (18:46). The longer treatment likens the whole of worldly life to rain whose plants delight the sowers before they wither and are reduced to chaff (57:20). The verse in this chapter is the compressed form of a claim made repeatedly, and its compression gives it force. A reader who has heard the claim in a paragraph can set the paragraph aside; a reader who meets it in a single sentence, immediately after a description of the path of hardship, cannot. The repetition across the revelation is evidence that the claim is central rather than incidental. A theme stated once can be treated as a rhetorical flourish; a theme stated in every register — legal, narrative, eschatological — is a doctrine. The verse is the compressed form of that doctrine.

**The Boaster of the Other Chapter**

The type described here appears in the sūrah of the City, where a man announces that he has destroyed enormous wealth and asks whether anyone sees him (90:5–6). The two chapters describe the same figure from different angles. There the complaint is about expenditure; here the withholding is about refusal. Both figures treat wealth as the measure of their standing, and both are answered with the same fact: the wealth will be present and useless at the moment it is needed. The reader who meets the type in one chapter will recognise him in the other, and the recognition is the point of the repetition. The two chapters describe one figure from two angles, and the reader who meets him in either will recognise him in the other. Both figures treat property as the measure of standing, and both are answered with the same fact about the moment at which property stops functioning.

**What the Verse Asks the Reader to Do**

The verse does not ask the reader to despise his wealth. It asks him to use it while it still operates. The Qur'an's consistent instruction is to spend from what has been provided, and the spending is described as a transfer rather than a loss: whatever is donated is paid back in full (2:272). The verse supplies the reason for the urgency. Wealth has a period of usefulness, and the period ends at a moment the owner does not control. A person who waits for a convenient time to give has misunderstood the verse, because the inconvenience he is waiting to avoid is the only condition in which giving means anything. The urgency is the practical content of the verse. A reader who is waiting for a better moment to transfer has misunderstood the verse, because the waiting is what the verse identifies as the error.

**What Should Be Done Before the Moment**

The verse implies an instruction without stating it. Wealth that will not benefit its owner afterwards can benefit him beforehand, and the benefit is obtained by transferring it. The transfer converts property that is about to become useless into something that is not. The reader who reads this verse and does nothing has understood it and declined it, which is the position the chapter describes as the second case. The verse is short because the instruction is simple, and the simplicity is what makes the refusal culpable. The instruction is simple enough that refusing it cannot be explained by complexity. Transfer what will become useless into what will not, and do it while the transfer is still possible. The chapter describes the person who performed the transfer at the end of the sūrah, and the description is the instruction made visible.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:12

> It is certainly upon Us ˹alone˺ to show ˹the way to˺ guidance

**What the Undertaking Covers**

The exegetes read the undertaking in two ways, and both are supported. The first takes it as the explanation of what is lawful and what is prohibited — the delivery of the guidance in a form a person can act on. The second takes it as the guarantee that the one who travels the path of guidance will reach God. The first reading concerns the giving of the guidance; the second concerns its completion. The chapter's structure favours the first, since the warning that follows presupposes that the guidance has been delivered and can be refused. The second reading is not excluded, and it supplies the assurance the first lacks: the path shown is a path that arrives. The scope is comprehensive in a way that anticipates the reader's objections. Whatever he thinks he needs to begin has been covered by the undertaking, and whatever he fears has been covered as well. The verse is written so that no gap remains for a person to say that his case was not provided for.

**Guidance Is Not the Messenger's Task**

The Qur'an states the limit of the Prophet's office repeatedly, and the statements are emphatic. "You are not responsible for people's guidance ˹O Prophet˺—it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills" (2:272). "You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills, and He knows best who are ˹fit to be˺ guided" (28:56). The verse in this chapter belongs to that group, and its placement is significant. It arrives in a chapter that has described a person who denies and turns away, and it relieves the messenger of responsibility for the denial. The reader who concludes that a refusal reflects badly on the one who conveyed the message has been answered. The distinction is what protects both the truth and the messenger. A claim that cannot be compelled is not a weak claim; it is a claim addressed to a person who is able to refuse it. The verse keeps the offer genuine by keeping the acceptance voluntary.

**A Comfort Addressed to a Man Under Pressure**

The chapter was revealed to a man whose message was being rejected by the city he lived in, and the verse is a comfort to him. The undertaking removes the burden of outcome from his shoulders. He is responsible for the conveying, and the conveying has been done; the guidance itself is upon God. The distinction is not a technicality for a person watching his community refuse what he has brought. It is the difference between a man who has failed and a man who has been refused. The verse supplies the second description, and the chapter that contains it is one of several revealed in the same period for the same purpose. The timing of the reassurance is what makes it operative. A statement of relief given at a moment of ease would be received as information; given under pressure, it is received as support. The verse is placed where a man who has been carrying more than was asked of him can set the burden down.

**Two Senses of Guidance**

The Qur'an uses the language of guidance in two senses, and the distinction is necessary for reading this verse. There is the showing of the way, which is given to everyone; and there is the guidance of the heart, which follows the response. The first sense is what this verse undertakes. The second is described elsewhere as an opening: "Whoever Allah wills to guide, He opens their heart to Islam" (6:125). The chapter's two cases correspond to the two responses. Both groups have been shown the way, and the divergence described in the fourth verse is a divergence of response rather than of information. The reader who claims that he was never shown has been answered by the undertaking in this verse. The two senses are not in tension, and the verse depends on both. Without delivery there is nothing to accept, and without acceptance the delivery has not reached its end. The undertaking covers the first completely and offers the second genuinely, and the reader's part lies entirely inside the second.

**The Undertaking and the Messenger's Relief**

For the man to whom this chapter was revealed, the verse removed a burden he was carrying. He had conveyed a message and watched his city refuse it, and the refusal could be read as a failure of the conveying. The undertaking answers the reading. The guidance is upon God, and the conveying was the messenger's task alone. The distinction is not a technicality for a person whose life is devoted to a message that is being rejected. It is the difference between carrying a responsibility and carrying a disappointment. The verse supplies the second, and it does so in a form that cannot be argued with, since the undertaking is stated by the One who took it on. The relief is real but it is not a reduction of effort. The messenger is freed from an impossible responsibility, not from the work itself, and the distinction is the one a reader under pressure needs. Nobody is obliged to produce results that depend on another person's heart.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:13

> And surely to Us ˹alone˺ belong this life and the next

**The Refutation of the Self-Sufficient Man**

The connection between this verse and the second case described earlier is direct. A man who withholds from the needy and considers himself independent has made a claim about his own standing. The verse answers it with the simplest possible fact: everything he holds is held inside an ownership he did not establish and cannot terminate. The self-sufficiency he claims is a description of his feelings rather than of his position. The Qur'an makes the same point where it describes wealth and children as a test rather than a possession, and where it instructs the believers to donate from what God has provided — a formulation that attributes the provision even as it commands the giving. The refutation is decisive because it addresses the claim at its root. A man who deems himself beyond need has to suppose that he is self-sufficient in some realm, and the verse closes both realms against that supposition. What remains is not independence but a claim to it.

**The Ground of the Warning That Follows**

The verse is placed immediately before the warning about the Fire, and the placement is the argument. A warning is only meaningful if the one who issues it has the power to carry it out, and the power is established here. The Owner of both realms can act in either, and the Fire described in the next verse is part of what He owns. The reader who reaches the warning has already been told that the issuer is not a claimant but an owner. The sequence — undertaking to show the way, ownership of both realms, warning of the Fire — is a complete statement of the position: guidance has been given by the One who has the power to enforce its consequences. The warning is grounded rather than asserted, and the grounding is the ownership. A fire that belongs to someone who owns both realms cannot be escaped by moving between them, and the reader who hopes to avoid the warning by leaving one realm for the other has been told that there is no such move.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:14

> And so I have warned you of a raging Fire

**The Warning Issued in the First Person**

The chapter shifts register at this verse. The preceding statements have been in the third person or in the general form of an oath and its answer; this one is addressed directly, in the first person, to a plural audience. The shift is a rhetorical decision, and it marks the moment at which the chapter stops describing and starts speaking. The reader is no longer overhearing an account of two types of person. He is being addressed by the One who owns both realms, and the address is a warning. The directness is what gives the following verses their force, since a warning delivered in the first person cannot be attributed to a reporter. The person who issues the warning has the standing to issue it, and the standing is what makes the verse effective. A warning from a person with no authority over the outcome is advice; a warning from the owner of both realms is information about a fact he controls. The verse gives the reader both the content and the credential.

**Talā: A Fire That Blazes**

The word used for the Fire is *talā*, from a root denoting blazing and fierce heat. It is not the general term for fire but a specific one, describing a fire in its active state rather than a fire that exists. The Qur'an names the Fire with a series of such terms, each carrying a different image: the Crusher, the abyss, the scorching Fire. The verse in this chapter uses the term of blazing, and the choice fits the warning's function. A reader is not being told that a place exists; he is being told that something is burning, and that the burning has been announced to him in advance. The announcement is the mercy inside the warning. The imagery is chosen for its visibility rather than its novelty. A fire that blazes is something every reader has seen, and the verse uses the familiarity to convey the intensity. What distinguishes this fire is not that it burns but that it is being pointed out to him in advance by the one who can prevent his approach to it.

**The Fire Described Elsewhere**

The Qur'an's descriptions of the Fire are varied, and the variety serves the warnings in which they appear. The sūrah of the Slanderer names it the Crusher and asks what will make the reader realise what the Crusher is, answering that it is the Fire of Allah, sealed over its inhabitants (104:4–9). The sūrah of the Calamity describes the abyss as the home of those whose scale is light (101:8–11). This chapter's term is the blazing, and the term is chosen for a chapter about conduct performed in the ordinary day. The reader who is warned of a blazing fire while going about his business is being addressed in the register of his own life, and the register is what makes the warning reach him. The descriptions elsewhere accumulate into a picture that no single verse provides. Heat, confinement, fuel and duration are each stated somewhere, and the reader who gathers them is not being given a metaphor but a description of a place. The verse names the place, and the rest of the revelation describes it.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:15

> in which none will burn except the most wretched—

**Al-Ashqā: The Superlative of Wretchedness**

The word is the superlative of wretched, and the definite article makes it a title rather than a description. The most wretched is not a person having a bad day or a sinner who has slipped. He is the one at the extreme of the category, and the chapter defines him in the next verse by two acts: denial and turning away. The use of a superlative is consistent with the Qur'an's practice elsewhere, where the same word is applied to the man who hamstrung the she-camel of Thamūd (91:12). The parallel is instructive. The most wretched of a people is the one whose act expressed a settled condition, and the condition had been formed long before the act. The superlative is doing precise work. The verse does not say that this person suffers more than others in degree but that he occupies a position no one else occupies. The exclusivity is grammatical as well as semantic, and the grammar is what makes the category closed rather than comparative.

**The Parallel With the Tribe Destroyed**

The chapter of the Sun describes the most wretched of Thamūd as a man roused by his people to kill a sign he had been warned about (91:12–14). The parallels between the two chapters are close: both use the superlative, both describe a refusal after a warning, and both end with a statement about consequences. The reader who has read the earlier chapter recognises the category here, and the recognition supplies the content that this verse leaves unstated. The most wretched is not a person who committed a single bad act. He is a person who destroyed a sign after being told what it was, and the destruction was the expression of a condition already complete. The parallel supplies the historical content the verse itself withholds. A tribe destroyed for killing the she-camel is a documented event, and the reader who knows the story knows what the superlative implies. The verse relies on that knowledge rather than restating it, which is why the identification matters.

**Why the Verse Comes Before the Definition**

The order of the two verses is deliberate. The category is named first, in a superlative, and the definition follows. The arrangement produces a moment of uncertainty in the reader, and the uncertainty is the point. A person who reads "none will burn except the most wretched" will ask who the most wretched is, and the question is answered in the next verse. The arrangement is more effective than the reverse. A definition followed by a name would allow the reader to skip the name; a name followed by a definition makes him wait for it, and the waiting is what fixes the definition in his mind. The placement prepares the definition that follows. Naming the category before describing it means the reader hears the description as an answer to a question already in his mind. The order is deliberate, and reversing it would leave the description without a frame.

**How a Person Becomes the Most Wretched**

The superlative describes an endpoint, and endpoints are reached by degrees. A person does not become the most wretched of his generation in a single act. He denies a claim, and the denial becomes easier. He turns away once, and the turning becomes a habit. The habit hardens until the summons no longer registers, and at that point the act that expresses the condition costs nothing. The process is the practical content of the verse. Nobody is placed in the category by a single act; he arrives there through a sequence of refusals, each of which was individually survivable.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:16

> who deny and turn away

**Two Verbs, Two Movements**

The definition of the most wretched is given in two verbs, and the two describe different acts. The first is denial — declaring a claim to be false. The second is turning away — withdrawing from a person or a summons. Denial is directed at a statement; turning away is directed at a presence. The pairing covers both ways a person can refuse a messenger: he can reject the message, and he can leave the room. The verse names both, and the naming is exhaustive. There is no third form of refusal that escapes the definition, and there is no form of refusal that does not involve one of the two. The two movements describe one refusal from two angles, and the reader needs both because either alone is ambiguous. Denial can be mistaken for doubt and aversion for busyness. Taken together they identify a settled position rather than a passing state, and the identification is what the category requires.

**The Definition Completes the Category**

Verses 15 and 16 together form a single statement, and neither is complete without the other. The first names the extreme; the second defines it. A reader who reads only the first is left with an undefined fear; a reader who reads only the second has a description without a consequence. The pairing supplies both, and the pairing is the chapter's most precise piece of moral definition. The most wretched is not the sinner, the doubter or the negligent. He is the person who has declared the finest reward false and has turned away from the one who told him about it, and the two acts together place him in the category the Fire is promised to. The definition supplies what the earlier naming withheld. The reader who reached the superlative without knowing who it covers has been given the covering conditions, and both are within his knowledge. The definition is behavioural, so the question it answers can be answered honestly.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:17

> But the righteous will be spared from it—

**The Second Superlative**

The verse answers the one before it with a matching extreme. Where the previous verse named the most wretched, this one names *al-atqā* — the most mindful, the one furthest along the path of guarding himself against what would harm him in God's reckoning. The pairing of two superlatives is the chapter's structural centre. Humanity has been described as striving toward diverse ends, and the two ends are now personified in two figures, each at the limit of his kind. The reader is not being offered a spectrum. He is being shown two poles, and the poles are defined by the verses that follow each of them. The second superlative balances the first and completes the chapter's structure. One category has an extreme case, and so does the other, and the two are stated in the same terms. The reader who has followed the argument can now see the whole shape rather than two unrelated descriptions.

**The Reports About the Occasion of These Verses**

The tradition connects this passage with Abū Bakr, who used to buy weak and disabled slaves to free them. His father is reported to have asked why he did not buy strong men who could defend him, and the answer was that he was not seeking his own defence. The verses that praise the one who gives his wealth to purify himself were read as a description of that conduct. The connection is supported by an authentic report in which ʿUmar is recorded as saying, "Abū Bakr is our master and he emancipated our master," meaning Bilāl (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3754). The chapter's positive case was recognised by the first generation in a specific person, and the recognition tells the reader what the description looks like in practice: the purchase of people who could do nothing for the purchaser. The reports give the description a named example, and the naming is what allows the reader to test his understanding. A man who freed a man is not an abstraction; the identification places the category inside the community the reader belongs to, and it shows the scale on which the conduct was performed.

**A Category That Is Still Open**

The verse is written in the future tense and describes a type rather than a named individual. The category of the most mindful is therefore unoccupied in the reader's own case until he acts. The chapter supplies the definition in the three verses that follow, and the definition is made of acts that remain available: giving from one's wealth, seeking no return from anyone, aiming at God. A reader who concludes that the description belongs to the first generation has not read the verses that define it. The definition is behavioural, and behaviour can begin today. The removal promised at the end is attached to the description, and the description is open to anyone who fits it. The category remains open, and the openness is the practical content of the verse. Nobody is told that the example given is the only instance, and the reader who gives from what he holds has been shown that the description can apply to him. The condition is the motive, not the amount.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:18

> who donate ˹some of˺ their wealth only to purify themselves

**An Act of Giving Comes First in the Definition**

The most mindful is defined first by what he does with his wealth, and the definition is precise. He gives his wealth, and the giving has a stated purpose: purification. The verse does not describe a feeling of generosity or an intention to help. It describes a transfer of property with a motive attached, and both elements are required for the description to fit. The chapter's positive case begins, like its negative case, with money. The definition begins with an act because the chapter has been describing conduct throughout. A person is not identified by what he believes alone but by what he does with what he holds, and the giving is placed first so that the motive which follows is understood as the reason for a real act rather than a feeling.

**Yatazakkā: The Same Root as the Alms**

The verb is from the root of *zakāh*, and the root carries both purification and growth. A thing that is purified by this verb is also increased, and the two senses are inseparable in Arabic usage. The connection to the obligatory alms is therefore not accidental: the alms are called by a name derived from the same root because the payment both cleanses the wealth and increases it. The chapter of the Sun states the same principle in a single line — successful is the one who purifies his soul (91:9) — and this verse applies it to property. A reader who gives has performed the act described in both chapters at once, and the act is the same act. The shared root connects the giving to the wider practice of alms, and the connection is deliberate. The same term that describes the obligatory transfer describes this one, and the reader is meant to see that the motive being defined here is the motive required there. The practice and its inner condition are not separable.

**Purification as the Stated Motive**

The verse specifies the purpose of the giving, and the specification excludes other motives. The man described does not give to be seen, to be thanked, to discharge a social obligation or to get influence. He gives to be purified. The motive is internal and it is directed at the giver's own state rather than at anyone's opinion of him. The Qur'an describes the same motive where it instructs the Prophet to take charity from the community: "Take from their wealth ˹O Prophet˺ charity to purify and bless them" (9:103). The purification is the point of the transaction, and the transaction is with God rather than with the recipient. Stating the motive makes the definition falsifiable. A gift given for purification looks identical from outside to a gift given for return or display, and the verse insists that the difference is not only invisible but decisive. The reader who cannot identify his own motive has been given something to work on.

**The Conduct the Tradition Recognised**

The reports about Abū Bakr supply the concrete form of this verse. He bought slaves who were weak and unlikely to be of use to him, and he bought them to free them. When his father asked why he did not buy strong men who could defend him, he answered that he was not seeking his own defence. The answer is the verse in narrative form. The purchase had no utility for the purchaser, and its only return was the purification described here. The reader who asks what the verse looks like in practice is given an example in which the giver obtained nothing except the state the verse names. The recognition by the tradition is what makes the identification concrete. A man known for freeing people is not a hypothetical case, and the reader who knows the example can see what the description means at full scale. The scale is not the requirement, but it shows that the description admits of a maximum.

**What the Purification Removes**

The verse implies that wealth carries something that needs removing, and the implication is consistent with the Qur'an's treatment of property elsewhere. Wealth is a test: "Your wealth and children are only a test, but Allah ˹alone˺ has a great reward" (64:15). It is also an attachment, and attachment distorts judgement. The giving described here removes the attachment by transferring the property, and the removal is what the verse calls purification. A reader who gives while retaining the sense that the wealth was his to keep has performed the transfer without the purification. The chapter's insistence on the motive is what distinguishes the two cases. What is removed is the residue the wealth carries. Property acquired, held and guarded leaves a mark on the person holding it, and the transfer is the means by which the mark is taken off. The verse is not describing a payment for past faults but a cleaning of the holder himself.

**Contrast With the Displayed Gift**

The verse's motive is the opposite of the one the Qur'an condemns. Charity followed by reminders and hurtful words is likened to a barren rock whose film of soil is washed away by rain, leaving nothing behind (2:264). The image describes a gift that looked fertile and was not. The gift described in this verse is the reverse: it looks unremarkable, since nobody is being thanked and nobody is being told, and it produces the state the giver sought. The ranking is stated explicitly elsewhere: to give publicly is good, but to give privately is better (2:271). The person described here belongs to the second category, and the category is defined by the absence of an audience. The contrast is the sharpest in the chapter. A gift given to be seen has an audience and a return, and the audience is the point of it. A gift given to purify has no audience at all, and the absence is not a loss but the condition of its value.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:19

> not in return for someone’s favours

**The Report That Explains the Verse**

The tradition supplies an occasion for this verse and the one that follows. When Bilāl was being tortured for his testimony of God's oneness, Abū Bakr bought him and freed him, and his opponents said that he had done it only because he was obliged to Bilāl for a favour. The verses that follow describe a person with no such obligation, and the description answers the accusation directly. Whether or not the occasion is relied on historically, the reading it preserves is the plain sense of the text. A gift attributed to an existing debt is a payment, and the verse exists to distinguish the giving of the most mindful from payments of that kind. The report identifies the verse's first instance and gives the exclusion a face. A man who freed people without expecting anything in return is the concrete form of the description, and the reader can measure his own conduct against that example rather than against an abstraction.

**The Test the Verse Applies to the Reader**

The verse supplies a check that any giver can perform. When you give, ask whether anyone's name is in the ledger. If you are repaying someone, the gift is a payment. If you are creating a debtor, the gift is an investment. The test is not a demand for perfection; it is a way of seeing what one is actually doing. The chapter of the City describes the opposite case in a single quotation — a man announcing how much wealth he has destroyed (90:6) — and the announcement is the sound of a ledger being read aloud. The verse here describes a person who keeps no such ledger, and the absence of the ledger is what makes his giving count. The test is available to the reader at the moment of giving rather than afterwards. He can ask, before the transfer, whether he would still make it if no one ever knew and nothing ever came of it. The question is short, and the answer is usually immediate.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:20

> but seeking the pleasure of their Lord, the Most High

**The Face as the Person Sought**

The verse supplies the motive the previous verse cleared space for. The Arabic speaks of seeking the Face of his Lord, and the idiom denotes the person rather than a direction. To seek someone's face is to seek the person himself, without intermediary and without ulterior aim. The verse therefore names the highest possible motive in the shortest possible form. Nothing stands between the giver and God: not the recipient, not the community, not the giver's own reputation, and not even the reward. The seeking is directed at the One sought, and the direction is exclusive. The face stands for the person sought, and the phrasing identifies the direction of the act. The giver is not distributing benefit into a general pool; he is addressing someone. The verse names that someone by a name reserved for God, and the naming is the whole of the definition.

**The Same Motive Described Elsewhere**

The Qur'an records the motive in the words of the givers themselves. The righteous "give food—despite their desire for it—to the poor, the orphan, and the captive, ˹saying to themselves,˺ ‘We feed you only for the sake of Allah, seeking neither reward nor thanks from you’" (76:8–9). The statement in that passage is the interior speech of the giver, and it matches this verse clause for clause: the giving is for God, and neither reward nor thanks is sought from anyone. The two passages describe the same person from different angles. The reader who wants a model of the motive is given one in which the model speaks, and the speech is exactly what this verse summarises. The same motive is described elsewhere in the revelation, and the repetition is what establishes it as a requirement rather than an ideal. The reader who encounters it in more than one place cannot treat it as an exception made for an unusual person; it is the standard the practice is measured against.

**The Summit of the Chapter's Description**

The three verses that define the most mindful ascend. The first names the act — giving wealth to purify. The second removes the human ledger — no favour to be repaid. The third names the aim — the Face of the Most High. The ascent is from an outward act to an inward direction, and the direction is the last thing the chapter says about the person before it states his reward. The placement is a judgement. What ultimately describes a person is not what he did but what he was aiming at when he did it, and the aiming is what God sees. The verse is therefore the summit of the chapter's moral description, and the reward that follows is attached to it rather than to the act alone. This is the highest point of the chapter's positive description. The earlier verses defined the act and its inner condition; this verse names the object of the condition, and the naming completes the description. The reader who reaches it has been given the whole of what the chapter asks.

## Sūrah al-Layl 92:21

> They will certainly be pleased

**The Echo of the Chapter of the Morning Light**

The same promise appears in the chapter revealed shortly before this one, and the wording is almost identical: "And ˹surely˺ your Lord will give so much to you that you will be pleased" (93:5). The repetition across two adjacent chapters is significant. In the earlier chapter the promise is addressed to the Prophet in the second person, at a moment when he had been accused of abandonment. In this chapter it is addressed to a type — the one who gives his wealth to purify himself — in the third person. The transfer of the promise from the messenger to the giver is the chapter's final move. What was assured to him has been made available to anyone who fits the description. The echo connects the ending to the chapter of the morning light, and the connection is deliberate. Both chapters close with satisfaction after a description of giving, and the reader who notices the echo understands that the pattern is not accidental. Two short chapters reach the same destination by the same route.

**The Structure of the Ending**

The chapter's last five verses describe a removal, a giving, an absence of obligation, an aim, and a satisfaction. The sequence is deliberate and it ends inwardly. Nothing in the final verse refers to the Fire, to the wretched, or to the wealth that will not benefit its owner. The chapter that opened with an oath on the night closes with the contentment of a person who gave in secret. The contrast between the opening and the ending is the whole of the chapter's argument compressed into its frame: darkness and light, withholding and giving, ruin and pleasure. The reader who has followed the sequence arrives at the last word already persuaded. The structure of the ending closes the chapter without a summary. The warnings have been issued, the categories defined and the conduct described; the final verse states the outcome for the person who met the description. The reader is left with a promise rather than a restatement, and the promise is the appropriate close.

**What the Pleasure Includes**

The verse does not itemise the satisfaction, and the omission allows it to include everything the chapter has promised. The removal from the Fire is part of it. The acceptance of the gift is part of it, and the acceptance matters to a person who gave without knowing whether the giving counted. The meeting with the One he was aiming at is the largest part, and it is the part the verse leaves unstated. The Qur'an describes the same double satisfaction elsewhere: "Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him" (98:8). The mutuality of that description is what the three words of this verse point toward, and the pointing is enough. What the pleasure includes is not itemised, and the absence of an itemisation is what the term means. It covers the acceptance of the act, the removal of what was feared and the arrival at what was hoped for, and the reader is not required to know the details to want it.

**The Last Word of the Chapter**

A chapter that began with the diversity of human striving ends with the pleasure of one of the two groups. The choice of ending is a rhetorical decision, and it is a generous one. The chapter could have closed on the Fire, as the chapter of the Sun closes on a Maker who fears no consequence. It closes instead on satisfaction, and the closing leaves the reader with the positive term in view. The warning has been delivered and the category of the wretched has been defined; what remains in the reader's mind at the end is the state of the person who gave. The verse is therefore an invitation as much as a promise, and it is offered to whoever is still holding the text. The last word of the chapter is a promise addressed to a person who gave without expecting anything, and the placement is the reward's final argument. Everything before it described conduct; this verse describes what the conduct is worth. Anyone who has read the chapter knows both, and the knowing is the point of the order.
