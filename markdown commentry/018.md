# Sūrah al-Kahf (Chapter 18) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Sanctuary of the Cave and the Antidotes to Tribulation**

Al-Kahf, "The Cave," is the eighteenth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 110 verses of mature Makkan revelation. Positioned precisely at the mathematical center of the thirty parts (*juz'*) of the sacred text, Sūrah al-Kahf is universally celebrated for its profound Last-Day and spiritual virtues. In authentic prophetic traditions, the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ strongly urged its regular recitation every Friday, promising in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim that *“Whoever memorizes ten verses from the beginning of Sūrah al-Kahf will be protected from the trial of the Anti-Christ (*al-Dajjāl*).”* It takes its name from the miraculous sanctuary of the cave (*al-kahf*) in which a small band of monotheistic youths took refuge to escape the lethal religious tyranny of a Roman emperor, where God caused them to sleep for over three centuries. Within its luminous tapestry, the sūrah presents four legendary narrative suites, each diagnosing and providing an invincible spiritual antidote to one of the four foundational temptations (*fitnah*) that threaten human civilization: the trial of faith (*dīn*), the trial of wealth (*māl*), the trial of knowledge (*ʿilm*), and the trial of power (*sulṭān*).

**The Four Parables and the Grand Synthesis**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Kahf is an astonishing four-part symphonic masterpiece, structured around four primary stories interspersed with religious commentaries. The first narrative (9–26) recounts the Sleepers of the Cave (*Aṣḥāb al-Kahf*): young men who abandoned aristocratic families and social status to preserve their pure monotheism, sheltering in a mountain cave with their faithful dog, where God preserved their bodies for 309 solar/lunar years, demonstrating the ultimate victory of faith over political persecution. The second narrative (32–44) delivers the parable of the Two Gardens: an affluent agriculturalist who boasts of his orchards, wealth, and lineage, denying the resurrection, whose companion warns him to say *Mā shā' Allāh* ("What Allah wills; there is no power except in Allah"), before a cosmic whirlwind strikes his paradise, leaving him wringing his hands in regret over ruined vines—the archetype of the trial of wealth. The third narrative (60–82) stages the unforgettable odyssey of Moses and the mysterious, enlightened sage Khidr: Moses seeks spiritual knowledge and is tested by three perplexing, seemingly unethical actions—the scuttling of a poor fisherman's boat, the slaying of a young boy, and the rebuilding of a crumbling wall in a hostile town. The fourth narrative (83–98) presents Dhū al-Qarnayn: the righteous global ruler endowed with supreme technology and military resources, who travels to the far west where the sun sets in a murky spring, the far east. The mountainous barrier between two peaks, where he builds a massive iron and copper rampart to wall off the chaotic forces of Gog and Magog (*Ya'jūj wa-Ma'jūj*), attributing his monumental technological achievement not to personal genius, but to divine grace: *“This is a mercy from my Lord”* (18:98)—the antidote to the trial of power. The epilogue (102–110) warns against the ultimate losers in deeds: those whose effort is lost in worldly life while they imagine they are doing good, concluding with the cosmic metaphor of the ocean as ink: *“Say, 'If the sea were ink for the words of my Lord, the sea would be exhausted before the words of my Lord were exhausted'”* (18:109), and sealing the discourse with the call to pure, uncorrupted monotheism.

## Verse 18:1

> All praise is for Allah Who has revealed the Book to His servant, allowing no crookedness in it

**A Crookedness That Nobody Could See**

The word rendered here as crookedness is *ʿiwaj*, and Arabic keeps it apart from a look-alike. *ʿAwj*, with an open vowel on the first letter, names a bend you can see: a leaning post, a road that veers, a warped plank. *ʿIwaj*, with a kasrah on that same letter, names deviation that cannot be seen — deviation in meaning, intention, and belief. This verse uses the second form.

The choice narrows the denial. What is excluded from the Book is not a physical flaw but a doctrinal one: no statement that undercuts another statement elsewhere, no principle that collapses when it is applied, no part of the text arguing against a different part. Al-Zamakhsharī reads the negation in exactly that direction, as covering any internal disagreement within the revelation.

The same word turns up again later in the Book, attached there to the clarity of its language: *“˹It is˺ a Quran ˹revealed˺ in Arabic without any crookedness, so perhaps they will be conscious ˹of Allah˺”* (39:28). Both passages put *ʿiwaj* where a reader might expect ambiguity, and both refuse it.

**The Servant Named Before the Book**

The phrase *ʿalā ʿabdihī*, upon His servant, does grammatical work as well. The preposition *ʿalā* marks a descent onto a bearer, not composition by an author. Later Qur'anic language keeps the pattern: *“Blessed is the One Who sent down the Standard to His servant, so that he may be a warner to the whole world”* (25:1).

Each one of them — the youths who hide in the cave, the traveler who joins Mūsā, the ruler who builds a barrier — acts as a servant working under a Lord, and this line establishes that category before any of them appears.

**A Book Meant to Be Voiced**

The earliest reports about this sūrah concern hearing it rather than studying it. Al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib reported that a man was reciting Sūrat al-Kahf with his horse tied beside him. A cloud descended and kept drawing nearer until the horse began to shy, and when the man told the Prophet ﷺ, the answer was that this was tranquillity descending because of the Qur'an (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5011; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 795). In a parallel report, Usayd ibn Ḥuḍayr saw something like a cloud full of lamps above him as he recited, and was told that these were angels drawn near by his voice, who would have stayed visible until morning had he gone on (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5018).

A practical promise is attached to learning its opening by heart. Abū al-Dardāʾ reported the Prophet ﷺ saying that whoever memorises the first ten verses of Sūrat al-Kahf will be protected from the Dajjāl (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 809a), while a second chain in the same collection gives the last ten verses instead (809b).

**Consistency Offered as a Public Test**

The denial of crookedness is unusual in that it invites inspection instead of forbidding it. *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies”* (4:82). Few sacred texts of that period stake their authority on a reader's ability to find fault with their internal logic.

## Verse 18:2

> ˹making it˺ perfectly upright, to warn ˹the disbelievers˺ of a severe torment from Him; to give good news to the believers—who do good—that they will have a fine reward

**One Word Pointing in Two Directions**

*Qayyim* is an intensive form built on the root *q-w-m*, the root of standing. It carries both senses at once: standing firm in itself, and holding something else upright. Arabic reserves a still stronger form of the same root for one of the divine names, *al-Qayyūm*, in the verse of the Throne: *“Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the EverLiving, All-Sustaining”* (2:255). What stands by itself and keeps everything else standing is applied there to God; here the same quality is attributed to a book.

The Qur'an uses the word of guidance in the same double sense. *“Surely this Quran guides to what is most upright, and gives good news to the believers—who do good—that they will have a mighty reward”* (17:9). Guidance and uprightness travel together because the thing that guides must itself be straight; a crooked measure cannot straighten anything measured by it.

Al-Rāzī gives the general reason for the description: the Qur'an is called *qayyim* because it is complete in itself and completes something else. That is the structure the word holds in both its uses.

**A Custodian Over What Came Before**

The word has a third shade of meaning, and several commentators take it here. *Qayyim* can describe a custodian, the one who stands over a thing and keeps it in order. Read that way, the Qur'an is upright in the sense of being set over earlier scriptures: confirming what in them is true, witnessing to their origin, correcting what has been mixed into them, and abrogating some of their rulings.

Elsewhere the same word governs a way of life rather than a text. It describes the religion of Abraham: *“Surely my Lord has guided me to the Straight Path, a perfect way, the faith of Abraham, the upright”* (6:161). It describes the religion commanded to Joseph's household, *“That is the upright faith, but most people do not know”* (12:40), the natural way in Sūrat al-Rūm, *“That is the Straight Way, but most people do not know”* (30:30), and the worship commanded in Sūrat al-Bayyinah, *“That is the upright Way”* (98:5).

When a word that normally describes a way of life is applied to a Book, the Book inherits the job. It becomes the thing that keeps practice straight.

**A Torment Issued From Where God Is**

The first of the two purposes names its object before it names an audience: to warn of a severe torment. The severity is *baʾs*, a word the Qur'an reserves for punishment that falls with weight — military defeat, famine, the seizure of a community — rather than for a general bad outcome.

**The Twin Task Given to Every Messenger**

Warning and good news are not this sūrah's invention; they are the standing commission of the prophets. *“Then Allah raised prophets as deliverers of good news and as warners”* (2:213). The pairing appears across the Book as the definition of the messenger's office, which is why a book that carries out both tasks is described here as upright: it performs the function for which messengers were sent, in a form that does not bend.

## Verse 18:3

> in which they will remain forever

The construction behind the translation is compact: an active participle, *khālidīn*, those remaining, followed by *abadan*, forever. Both elements carry weight.

**Eternity Is What Makes It a Reward**

The vocabulary of the Qur'an makes the point explicit elsewhere. Paradise is called *Jannāt ʿAdn*, Gardens of Eternity, and the phrase *“to stay there for ever and ever”* returns as a refrain: *“Their reward with their Lord will be Gardens of Eternity, under which rivers flow, to stay there for ever and ever”* (98:8). The same formula closes the promise in Sūrat al-Nisāʾ, *“to stay there for ever and ever”* (4:57), and the description of the Day of Gathering, where belief and good action are answered with admission *“to stay there for ever and ever”* (64:9).

The repetition is not decorative. Each time the formula appears it distinguishes the promised outcome from every good thing that has an ending — wealth, health, victory, reputation, life itself. Those goods are real, and the Qur'an does not disparage them, but they are bounded, and a bounded good cannot be the final answer to a whole life.

Eternity is therefore not an extra feature bolted onto the reward. It is the feature that makes the reward what it claims to be.

**Two Words Carrying the Whole Claim**

A participle describes a condition rather than an event. The verse does not say that they will enter and then continue; it names them as inhabitants. Remaining is treated as their settled state, in the same way the Qur'an describes the people of the Garden elsewhere: *“They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever”* (2:25).

*Abadan* closes the door on exceptions. It is the adverb used for time without limit, and its presence rules out the qualifications weaker formulations leave available — a long stay, a stay subject to review, a stay that could be ended by a later decree.

**Measured Against a World That Passes Away**

The force of the word *abadan* depends on what it is set against, and the Qur'an supplies the contrast without being asked. The created order is described as perishing: *“Every being on earth is bound to perish”* (55:26). Not some beings, and not eventually — everything on earth, by the terms of its existence.

Worldly good is described in the same terms. The pleasures people compete for are listed in full — *“women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land”* — and then dismissed as belonging to this life alone, with the finest destination held elsewhere (3:14 — *“These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination”*). The list is long precisely so that the contrast lands: everything on it ends.

**A Promise Completed by Naming Its Length**

Naming eternity ends that watching. The clause is therefore as much about the recipient's present condition as about his future one: someone told that his reward has no end can stop negotiating. The Qur'an describes the outcome in similar terms when it calls the final result of the righteous the ultimate triumph (64:9 — *“That is the ultimate triumph”*) and describes the Garden's contents as lasting: *“eternal is its fruit as well as its shade”* (13:35).

The same verse supplies the contrast in a single stroke: *“But the outcome for the disbelievers is the Fire!”* (13:35). Permanence belongs to both outcomes, which is why the length of a state matters more than the fact of entering it.

*“Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded tenfold”* (6:160).

## Verse 18:4

> and to warn those who claim, “Allah has offspring.”

**The Idiom of Taking a Child**

The Arabic is *ittakhadha waladan*, to take a child, and the phrase is narrower than the English suggests. Taking a child in classical usage means assuming special responsibility for one: recognising a child, adopting one, bringing one into one's own household as one's own. The noun *walad* covers offspring generally, without specifying birth.

The same phrase appears across the Qur'an in the same negative form: *“They say, ‘Allah has offspring.’ Glory be to Him!”* (2:116).

**Whose Claim Is Being Answered**

Three groups in the Prophet's ﷺ world made claims of this shape, and commentators divide over which is in view. The Quraysh attributed daughters to God: *“And they attribute ˹angels as˺ daughters to Allah—glory be to Him!— the opposite of what they desire for themselves”* (16:57). The charge returns in Sūrat al-Zukhruf, *“Still they have labelled the angels, who are servants of the Most Compassionate, as female”* (43:19), and is met with a single question: *“Has He chosen daughters over sons?”* (37:153).

Claims by or about Christians and Jews are recorded in the Qur'an as well, among them the statement at (10:68 — *“They say, “Allah has offspring.” Glory be to Him! He is the Self-Sufficient. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. You have no proof of this! Do you say about Allah what you do not know”*). Because this sūrah is Makkan in origin, the immediate target is most plausibly the idolatrous belief about the angels, which the Prophet's ﷺ own audience actually held.

**The Claim Existed to Secure an Intercessor**

Attributing daughters to God was not idle theology; it had a function. The angels so described were invoked as intermediaries, and the Qur'an exposes the reasoning behind the practice directly: *“Indeed, sincere devotion is due ˹only˺ to Allah. As for those who take other lords besides Him, ˹saying,˺ ‘We worship them only so they may bring us closer to Allah’”* (39:3).

The same logic is turned back on its users in Sūrat Yūnus: *“They worship besides Allah others who can neither harm nor benefit them. Say, ‘These are our intercessors with Allah.’ Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, ‘Are you informing Allah of something He does not know in the heavens or the earth?’”* (10:18).

Seen from this angle, the claim about offspring is a supporting structure. Kinship with God is what would give an intercessor standing, and the warning in this verse attacks the foundation rather than the practice built on it. Remove the kinship and the intermediation has no ground. Sūrat al-Najm puts the double standard plainly: *“Do you ˹prefer to˺ have sons while ˹you attribute˺ to Him daughters?”* (53:21), followed by the verdict, *“Then this is ˹truly˺ a biased distribution!”* (53:22).

**Why This Claim Above Others**

The Book states the refusal in its most compressed form in Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ: *“He has never had offspring, nor was He born”* (112:3). Both halves matter together; denying offspring without denying birth would leave the door open on the other side. Sūrat Maryam puts the same refusal in the language of impropriety: *“It is not for Allah to take a son! Glory be to Him.”* (19:35). Sūrat al-Zumar treats the claim as incoherent on its own terms: *“Had it been Allah’s Will to have offspring, He could have chosen whatever He willed of His creation”* (39:4).

Against these, attributing a child to God is not one error among many. It dismantles the distinction on which every other doctrine depends.

## Verse 18:5

> They have no knowledge of this, nor did their forefathers. What a terrible claim that comes out of their mouths! They say nothing but lies

**The Charge Is Ignorance, Not Malice**

The first answer to the claim about offspring is epistemic. Before the Qur'an calls it false, it calls it ungrounded: they have no knowledge of it. The objection is not that the speakers are wicked or insincere, but that they have nothing to stand on.

This is the Qur'an's habitual form of attack on false belief. It asks for the source, and when no source appears, it treats the question as settled. The same demand is put bluntly elsewhere: *“You have no proof of this! Do you say about Allah what you do not know?”* (10:68). The rebuke there concerns the absence of any route to the conclusion, not the conclusion's content.

**The Forefathers Removed From the Argument**

The second clause widens the charge backwards. The speakers have no knowledge, *nor did their forefathers*. Inheritance of a belief is not evidence for it, and the verse treats the ancestral chain as an extension of the same ignorance rather than as a source of authority.

The point is a standing theme in the Qur'an. Those told to follow revelation reply, *“No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing”* (2:170), and the response asks whether the forefathers had any understanding at all. The Book returns to the argument repeatedly because it identifies imitation, not reasoning, as the engine that keeps a false belief in circulation across generations.

The clause also does practical work in a Makkan setting. Ancestral practice was the strongest justification available in that society; a man's religion was his tribe's religion. To deny that the forefathers knew is to remove the only support the claim actually had.

Sūrat al-Zukhruf notes that such statements will be recorded and their speakers questioned about them (43:19 — *“Their statement will be recorded, and they will be questioned”*).

**A Claim Measured as Monstrous**

The same word appears in an almost identical context: *“Has your Lord favoured you ˹pagans˺ with sons and taken angels as ˹His˺ daughters? You are truly making an outrageous claim”* (17:40). The pairing of the two passages shows that the Qur'an reserves this vocabulary for one specific class of statement — assertions about God's own nature made without knowledge.

## Verse 18:6

> Now, perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial, if they ˹continue to˺ disbelieve in this message

**The Same Sentence Appears Twice**

Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ contains a nearly identical line: *“Perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their disbelief”* (26:3). The near-identity between two Makkan revelations is significant. A repeated address of this kind marks something durable in its addressee, not a single occasion.

**A Word for Grief That Consumes**

The verb behind the translation is *bākhiʿ*, from a root whose basic sense is to slaughter or to destroy. Applied to the self, it describes a person working himself toward ruin. The image is not of sadness but of expenditure: a life being spent on sorrow.

Al-Rāzī offers a softer reading of the same word — to expend oneself in grief, to be utterly worn down by concern. The two readings differ in degree rather than in kind, and both keep the sense of self-destruction that the root carries. The verse is not telling the Prophet ﷺ to feel less; it is telling him that his feeling has reached the point where it damages the one who feels it.

Arabic has ordinary words for sadness — *ḥuzn*, *ghamm*, *asaf*.

Repetition of this sort also fixes the object of the grief. In both places it is disbelief — not persecution, not poverty, not insult. What distressed the Prophet ﷺ was the state his people were in, and the possibility that they would remain in it.

The Qur'an's consoling verses point the same way. *“Do not let their words grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. Surely all honour and power belongs to Allah”* (10:65). The consolation is not that their words are harmless but that honour does not depend on them.

**Consolation Delivered as a Reassignment**

The consolations scattered through the Book do not minimise the rejection; they relocate its meaning. *“We certainly know that what they say grieves you ˹O Prophet˺. It is not your honesty they question—it is Allah’s signs that the wrongdoers deny”* (6:33). The verse separates the messenger from the message: what is being refused is not him.

Sūrat Fāṭir states the same reassignment as a command: *“So do not grieve yourself to death over them ˹O Prophet˺. Surely Allah is All-Knowing of what they do”* (35:8). The knowledge of God is offered as the reason to stop, since nothing is being lost that He has not already recorded.

**The Occasion Reported in the Sīrah**

The sīrah literature preserves a report that fits this verse closely. Transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and recorded by al-Ṭabarī, it describes the Quraysh sending two envoys to the Jewish scholars of Madinah for a test of the Prophet's ﷺ claims. The scholars set three questions: the youths who vanished in an earlier age, the traveller who reached the east and west, and the spirit. The Prophet ﷺ promised an answer the following day, revelation did not come when he expected it, and the delay stretched until the Quraysh began treating his silence as disproof.

The report is a sīrah narrative rather than a graded ḥadīth, and its details vary between transmissions. What is consistent across them — a public challenge, a wait for revelation, and distress at the delay — matches the temper of this verse exactly. A man who had promised an answer and could not yet give it would feel the refusal of his people more sharply than usual.

## Verse 18:7

> We have indeed made whatever is on earth as an adornment for it, in order to test which of them is best in deeds

**What the Adornment Consists Of**

The commentators list the contents without disagreement: plants and animals, metals and minerals, water and cultivated land — everything that makes the earth desirable rather than merely habitable. Al-Rāzī adds a further item and considers it the most significant: human beings themselves, the noblest of what the earth carries.

The Qur'an's own inventory in Sūrat Āl ʿImrān is concrete: *“women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land”* (3:14). The list is deliberately worldly — nothing in it is spiritual, and nothing in it is shameful. These are the things people actually compete for, and they are described as made appealing.

Including human beings in the adornment has a consequence often missed. Other people are part of what is displayed, which means relationships, status, and admiration all fall inside the test. The trial is not confined to wealth.

**Adornment as the Instrument of Examination**

Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazzālī reads the verse in exactly these terms in the *Iḥyāʾ*: the earth's adornment is the means by which God examines human beings on their capacity for detachment. The trial is not whether a person has attractive things but whether he is had by them.

**The Test Is Stated in the First Person**

*Which of them* is left open, and no category — rich, poor, powerful, obscure — is given an advantage in advance. The pronoun *them* refers back to the human beings who inhabit the decorated earth, so the group under examination is everyone alive in it, without exception.

God made the earth's contents an ornament *to test*.

**Sweet and Green: The Prophet's ﷺ Own Description**

The Prophet ﷺ described the same condition in a single image. Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī reported him saying that the world is sweet and green, and that Allah has placed people as successors in it to see how they will act (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2742). The two descriptions agree on both halves: the world is genuinely attractive, and its attractiveness is the setting of an examination.

## Verse 18:8

> And We will certainly reduce whatever is on it to barren ground

**Juruz: Land With Nothing Left on It**

The word translated as barren ground is *ṣaʿīdan juruzā*, and both terms carry weight. *Ṣaʿīd* is bare, level earth — the surface with everything removed from it. *Juruz* comes from a root meaning to cut off or to sever, and it describes land stripped of vegetation, land that yields nothing.

The image completes the reversal. Ornament is what covers a surface; *juruz* is the surface with the covering cut off. The same earth, seen before and after.

**Certainty Marked Twice in One Sentence**

Arabic has several ways to make a statement emphatic, and this sentence uses more than one. The particle *inna* asserts; the active participle *jāʿilūn* presents the act as already underway rather than merely possible. Combined, they describe a future that has been settled.

**What the Bare Ground Argues**

The stripping of the earth is part of a wider Qur'anic picture. The perishability of everything on it is stated flatly: *“Every being on earth is bound to perish”* (55:26). The verse here adds the condition of the place itself, not only of its inhabitants.

Sūrat Ṭāhā describes the same event in surface: God will leave the earth *“leaving the earth level and bare”* (20:106), *“with neither depressions nor elevations to be seen”* (20:107). The flattening removes not just what grows on the ground but the features that make places distinguishable — valleys, hills, landmarks, boundaries.

**The Same Leveling in Sūrah Ṭāhā**

The parallel passage is worth reading beside this verse because it supplies what this one omits: the scale of the change. Mountains are removed, depressions are filled, and the surface becomes uniform (20:106–107 — *“leaving the earth level and bare with neither depressions nor elevations to be seen.””*). The transformation is not only of vegetation but of terrain.

Set against that, the adornment of the previous verse is revealed as temporary covering on a surface that will be exposed. The two verses of Sūrat al-Kahf make the same point in two strokes: first the decoration, then its removal, with the test suspended between them.

The two passages also differ usefully in emphasis. Sūrat Ṭāhā stresses the transformation of the landscape; Sūrat al-Kahf stresses the loss of what the landscape carried. One removes the setting, the other the contents, and between them nothing that a person could call his own survives the change.

## Verse 18:9

> Have you ˹O Prophet˺ thought that the people of the cave and the plaque were ˹the only˺ wonders of Our signs

**Why This Story Arrived at This Moment**

The occasion is preserved in the sīrah. The Quraysh sent envoys to the Jewish scholars of Madinah — the reports name al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith and ʿUqbah ibn Abī Muʿīṭ — and were given three questions as a test of prophethood: the youths of an earlier age, the traveller who reached the east and the west, and the spirit. The Prophet ﷺ expected an answer the next day, and revelation was delayed.

Two of the three questions are answered inside this sūrah. That fact explains the verse's tone. The story was not chosen because it was the most remarkable thing available; it was the answer to a question set by people testing whether the Prophet ﷺ was genuine.

Read beside the previous verse about the Prophet's ﷺ grief, the pairing becomes clear. A man under public examination, waiting for revelation that had not come, is now given the answer — and the first thing the answer does is refuse to be impressed with itself.

**The Same Story in Christian Memory**

The narrative is not unique to the Qur'an. Christian tradition knows it as the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus, and the two accounts share a core: youths fleeing persecution under the emperor Decius, who reigned from 249 to 251, a cave whose entrance was blocked, a long sleep, an awakening, and a journey into the city to buy food.

The earliest Western reference is Gregory of Tours in his *Glory of the Martyrs*, who says he received the story through a Syrian interpreter; in Syriac it appears in a homily by Jacob of Sarug, who died in 521. The number of sleepers varies: most Greek and Latin versions give seven, while many Syriac texts give eight.

**A Miracle Given to People Who Were Not Prophets**

Islamic belief has a term for this distinction. *Muʿjizāt* are the evidentiary miracles given to prophets in support of a claim to prophethood; *karāmāt* are wonders granted to the friends of God without any such claim attached. This story is the classical proof-text for the second category, and al-Rāzī and al-Qushayrī both use it in that way.

**Protection Attached to This Sūrah**

The story carries a practical promise in the ḥadīth literature. Abū al-Dardāʾ reported the Prophet ﷺ saying that whoever memorises the first ten verses of Sūrat al-Kahf will be protected from the Dajjāl (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 809a), with a variant chain giving the last ten verses (809b). A separate report from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī promises light between two Fridays for whoever recites the sūrah on that day, recorded by al-Ḥākim and al-Bayhaqī and graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Jāmiʿ 6470).

Al-Qurṭubī's explanation of the first report connects it directly to this story: a person who has reflected on the wonders of the cave will not be overwhelmed by the wonders the Dajjāl displays.

## Verse 18:10

> ˹Remember˺ when those youths took refuge in the cave, and said, “Our Lord! Grant us mercy from Yourself and guide us rightly through our ordeal.”

**Named Only by Their Age**

The Qur'an gives these people one identifier: they were youths. No names, no family, no city, no trade. The anonymity is deliberate and it is repeated — the same word returns later in the account, where the narrative insists that they were young men who believed in their Lord.

Age is not incidental to the story. The commentators observe that it is usually the young of a community who respond to a prophet, while the elders hold to inherited practice, and the Qur'an makes the same point elsewhere. When the idols of the town are attacked, the report that reaches its leaders is that *“We heard a young man, called Abraham, speaking ˹ill˺ of them”* (21:60).

**What the Prayer Assumes**

Asking for mercy from God's presence assumes that no one else can supply it. Asking for guidance through an ordeal assumes the ordeal will continue. Both assumptions are visible in the wording, and both are remarkable in a group of young men who have just fled for their lives.

A supplication of the Prophet ﷺ runs in the same direction and uses almost the same words. Busr ibn Abī Arṭāt reported hearing him pray: *Allāhumma aḥsin ʿāqibatanā fī al-umūr kullihā*, O Allah, grant a good end to all our affairs and protect us from disgrace in this world and punishment in the next (Musnad Aḥmad 17628, declared authentic by Ibn Ḥibbān and al-Ḥākim). The youths asked for the same thing in fewer words: not a quick exit but a sound ending.

**Mercy Asked For Where It Originates**

The same phrase earlier in this sūrah described a torment issuing from God's presence (18:2 — *“˹making it˺ perfectly upright, to warn ˹the disbelievers˺ of a severe torment from Him; to give good news to the believers—who do good—that they will have a fine reward”*). Placed together, the two uses show a single source with two outputs, and a group of believers addressing that source without mediation.

---

## Verse 18:11

> So We caused them to fall into a dead sleep in the cave for many years

**Sleep and Death Held Apart in Sūrat al-Zumar**

Sleep and death stand on either side of a single decision. The youths of the cave were released rather than kept, and their story is the Qur'an's own illustration of that release on a scale no ordinary sleeper experiences.

**A Sleep Described as a Striking on the Ears**

The Arabic is literal where the translation is smooth: We struck upon their ears. The verb *ḍaraba* with the preposition *ʿalā* describes something laid over a thing and held there — a covering imposed from outside. Applied to the ears, it means that hearing was shut off.

The same verb and preposition appear elsewhere in the Qur'an for an imposed condition. The children of Israel are told that *“They were stricken with disgrace and misery”* (2:61), where the literal sense is humiliation struck upon them and left there. The construction fastens a state to a person by an outside agent, and here the state is sleep.

The duration stated here is *sinīn*, a number of years. The indefinite plural fixes nothing, and the precise figure is supplied only later in the sūrah.

The clearest statement of the distinction this verse depends on appears in Sūrat al-Zumar: *“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ calls back the souls ˹of people˺ upon their death as well as ˹the souls˺ of the living during their sleep. Then He keeps those for whom He has ordained death, and releases the others until ˹their˺ appointed time. Surely in this are signs for people who reflect.”* (39:42).

---

## Verse 18:12

> then We raised them so We may show which of the two groups would make a better estimation of the length of their stay

**A Verb Borrowed From Resurrection**

The word for raising them is *baʿathnāhum*, and *baʿth* is the Qur'an's ordinary term for resurrection. It is used of the dead being raised on the Day of Judgment, and of messengers being sent. Here it describes young men waking up in a cave.

The parallel is not forced on the text by later readers.

**"So That We May Know"**

The purpose clause says that they were raised so that God may know which of the two groups estimated the stay better. The phrasing has been a standing difficulty, since God's knowledge is before every event and does not wait on it.

The Qur'an uses this grammar repeatedly. *“We will certainly test you ˹believers˺ until We prove those of you who ˹truly˺ struggle ˹in Allah’s cause˺ and remain steadfast, and reveal how you conduct yourselves”* (47:31). The testing reveals; it does not discover.

**A Proof-Text for the Wonders of Saints**

Al-Rāzī records that Sufi commentators read this verse as establishing a category of wonder outside prophecy. The youths were not prophets and made no claim to prophethood, yet an event beyond the natural order occurred to them and for them.

Islamic belief names the two categories distinctly. *Muʿjizāt* are evidentiary miracles granted to prophets in support of a claim; *karāmāt* are wonders granted to the friends of God with no claim attached. The distinction protects both ideas: a wonder does not make a prophet, and the absence of a claim does not make a wonder impossible.

**Testing as a Standing Grammar**

The same construction appears where the change of the direction of prayer is explained: *“We assigned your former direction of prayer only to distinguish those who would remain faithful to the Messenger from those who would lose faith”* (2:143). Again in Sūrat Āl ʿImrān: *“Allah would not leave the believers in the condition you were in, until He distinguished the good from the evil ˹among you˺”* (3:179).

---

## Verse 18:13

> We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ their story in truth. They were youths who truly believed in their Lord, and We increased them in guidance

**Youth Stated Twice**

The word for youths appears here and again a few verses later, and the repetition is emphatic. These were *fityah*, young men, and the narrative wants that fact kept in view.

The commentators connect it to a pattern they find across the Qur'an: it is generally the young of a community who respond to a prophet, while its elders hold to inherited practice. When the idols of a town are attacked, the report that reaches its leaders names the age of the suspect: *“We heard a young man, called Abraham, speaking ˹ill˺ of them”* (21:60).

Age is doing religious work here. The young have less invested in the arrangement they are asked to leave, and the Qur'an treats that as a moral advantage rather than an excuse.

**Belief First, Increase Second**

The order of the two clauses matters. They believed in their Lord; then God increased them in guidance. Increase assumes something already present, and the verse is explicit that the faith was theirs before the increase came.

The sequence answers a question about how guidance works. It is not a possession granted once and held statically. It is a condition that grows, and the growth is attributed to God rather than to the believer's effort alone.

The same structure appears wherever the Qur'an discusses increase in guidance: *“As for those who are ˹rightly˺ guided, He increases them in guidance and blesses them with righteousness”* (47:17), and *“And Allah increases in guidance those who are ˹rightly˺ guided”* (19:76). In each case the increase follows a direction already taken.

**Increase as a Law of the Heart**

The Qur'an treats increase as a general law operating in both directions. A heart that turns away has its sickness increased: *“There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase”* (2:10). A heart that turns toward God has its faith increased: *“He is the One Who sent down serenity upon the hearts of the believers so that they may increase even more in their faith”* (48:4).

Neither increase is arbitrary. Both follow the direction the person has already chosen, and both are attributed to God as the one who does the increasing. The verse about the cave youths places them on the second of these trajectories at the moment of greatest pressure.

**The Same Increase in Sūrat al-Fatḥ**

The parallel is worth holding beside this verse: *“He is the One Who sent down serenity upon the hearts of the believers so that they may increase even more in their faith. To Allah ˹alone˺ belong the forces of the heavens and the earth”* (48:4).

Both passages describe faith increasing under pressure, and both attribute the increase to a divine act rather than to human resolve. The cave youths received theirs before standing up to a king; the companions received theirs before facing an army. The mechanism is the same, and in both cases it is described as something done to the heart rather than something the heart manages on its own.

---

## Verse 18:14

> And We strengthened their hearts when they stood up and declared, “Our Lord is the Lord of the heavens and the earth. We will never call upon any god besides Him, or we would truly be uttering an outrageous lie.”

**Hearts Tied Fast Before Any Word Was Spoken**

The verse opens with an act performed on the youths rather than by them: We bound their hearts. The verb *rabaṭa* describes tying something firmly — a rope made fast, a load secured — and applied to the heart it means that the interior was held together under pressure.

The same verb appears in a military context at Badr: *“And He sent down rain from the sky to purify you, free you from Satan’s whispers, strengthen your hearts, and make ˹your˺ steps firm”* (8:11). In both places the strengthening is paired with a situation in which fear would be the reasonable response, and in both it is described as a gift.

*Idh qāmū*, when they stood, admits two readings, and the commentators divide.

**The Alternative Named as a Lie**

The conditional clause is unusually strong. They do not say that calling on another god would be wrong, or mistaken, or forbidden. They say it would be *shataṭan* — an outrageous claim, a statement that has travelled far past the bounds of what can be said.

**The Same Declaration From Abraham**

The parallel is close enough to be exact: *“He replied, ‘In fact, your Lord is the Lord of the heavens and the earth, Who created them ˹both˺. And to that I bear witness.’”* (21:56). A young man, addressing his people's religion, grounds his refusal in the scope of God's creation and adds a personal testimony.

The Qur'an sets the two statements side by side across different sūrahs without comment, and the effect is to place the cave youths inside a lineage. They are not an isolated case of youthful courage but one instance of a declaration that prophets before them had already made — and made, in Abraham's case, in the same sentence about the Lord of the heavens and the earth (6:78 — *“Then when he saw the sun shining, he said, “This must be my Lord—it is the greatest!” But again when it set, he declared, “O my people! I totally reject whatever you associate ˹with Allah in worship˺”*).

---

## Verse 18:15

> ˹Then they said to one another,˺ “These people of ours have taken gods besides Him. Why do they not produce a clear proof of them? Who then does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah

**A Demand for Evidence, Not for Devotion**

The youths do not argue that their people's gods are ugly, cruel, or unworthy. They ask for a proof. The noun is *sulṭān*, an authority or warrant — the thing that would entitle a claim to be believed — qualified by *mubīn*, clear and manifest.

The commentators draw a general principle from the question. Worship must be grounded in intellectual proof and cannot rest on imitation alone.

**Imitation as What the Demand Excludes**

*Taqlīd* — following another without knowing his grounds — is the practice the question is aimed at, and the Qur'an attacks it repeatedly. When people are told to follow what God has revealed, they answer: *“No! We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing”* (2:170), and the reply asks whether the forefathers understood anything at all.

There is a second edge. The question is addressed to the youths' own community — *these people of ours*. They are not interrogating strangers. They are asking their own people for grounds that their people have never been asked to give.

That makes the question harder to dismiss. An outsider's demand for proof can be answered by appeal to belonging; a demand from inside the community removes even that refuge.

**The Rhetorical Question That Settles It**

The final clause is not really a question. *Who does more wrong than the one who fabricates a lie against God?* expects no answer because none exists; it states a maximum.

The formula recurs across the Qur'an with almost identical wording: *“Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His signs?”* (6:21). *“Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His revelations?”* (10:17). *“And who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or reject the truth after it has reached them?”* (29:68); *“Who does more wrong than the one who fabricates lies about Allah when invited to submit ˹to Him˺?”* (61:7).

The repetition marks a category. Fabrication against God is treated as the ceiling of wrongdoing, above murder, above oppression, above denial. The reason is structural: every other wrong distorts something created, while this one distorts the description of the Creator on which every other judgement depends.

**The Same Formula in Sūrat Yūnus**

The parallel verse is worth reading beside this one: *“Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His revelations? Indeed, the wicked will never succeed”* (10:17).

The addition in Sūrat Yūnus supplies what Sūrat al-Kahf leaves implicit — the outcome. Fabrication and denial are named as the two poles of the same wrong, one adding to God and the other subtracting from what He sent, and both are answered with the same verdict: no success. The cave youths reached the same conclusion from inside their own city, before they had left it.

---

## Verse 18:16

> Since you have distanced yourselves from them and what they worship besides Allah, take refuge in the cave. Your Lord will extend His mercy to you and accommodate you in your ordeal.”

**Separation Comes First, Refuge Second**

The sentence is built on a condition already fulfilled: since you have distanced yourselves from them and from what they worship besides Allah. The withdrawal is described as accomplished before the cave is mentioned.

The distancing is attributed to the youths — *since you have distanced yourselves* — and the refuge follows as a consequence.

**Two Things Promised in One Sentence**

The assurance has two parts. God will extend mercy to you, and He will make your affair easy — literally, He will provide for you a *mirfaq*, a place of leaning, from a root meaning to rest the arm on a cushion.

The first promise is general and the second is precise. Mercy covers their condition; accommodation covers their circumstances. Together they answer the prayer recorded earlier in the narrative, which asked for mercy from God's presence and right direction in their affair.

The wording is careful about what is not promised. No deliverance from the ordeal is announced, no defeat of the king, no return home. What is promised is mercy inside the ordeal and ease within it — a difference that anyone in a long trial will recognise.

**The Cave the Qur'an Remembers Best**

The most famous cave in Islamic memory belongs to a later event. During the migration to Madinah, the Prophet ﷺ and Abū Bakr sheltered in a cave while the Quraysh searched for them. The Qur'an records the moment: *“While they both were in the cave, he reassured his companion, ‘Do not worry; Allah is certainly with us.’ So Allah sent down His serenity upon the Prophet”* (9:40).

One difference sharpens the comparison. The Prophet ﷺ and Abū Bakr were in the cave for days and knew it; the youths were in theirs for centuries and did not. Both groups were protected without acting, but only one group was conscious of the protection while it lasted.

## Verse 18:17

> And you would have seen the sun, as it rose, inclining away from their cave to the right, and as it set, declining away from them to the left, while they lay in its open space. That is one of the signs of Allah. Whoever Allah guides is truly guided. But whoever He leaves to stray, you will never find for them a guiding mentor

**A Scene Described to a Witness Who Was Not There**

The verse opens in the second person: you would have seen. The addressee is the Prophet ﷺ according to al-Ṭabarī, and an unspecified observer according to others. Neither reading changes the effect, which is to place the reader at the mouth of the cave.

There is also an irony in the address. Nobody was there to see this. The protection described happened in an empty landscape, unnoticed by anyone, and the verse supplies the only witness it will ever have.

**An Open Space With Air Moving Through It**

The phrase translated as its open space describes a broad area within the cave — a *fahwā*, an open expanse. The commentators read it as indicating a cave with a wide mouth, allowing air and cool breezes to reach the sleepers.

The detail answers an objection a hearer would raise immediately. Bodies sealed in a closed space for centuries would decay from damp and stagnant air. A spacious cave with through-draft addresses the problem, and the verse mentions it in the same breath as the sun.

The Qur'an treats shade as a blessing in its own right, listed among the gifts of the created order: *“And Allah has provided you shade out of what He created, and has given you shelter in the mountains”* (16:81). The youths receive both gifts at once — shade and a shelter in the mountains — without asking for either.

**Guidance and Straying Stated as the Conclusion**

The last two sentences step outside the scene completely. Whoever Allah guides is truly guided, and whoever He leaves to stray will find no guiding mentor.

The connection to the cave is not immediately obvious, and the commentators explain it in two ways. One reads the sentences as applying to the youths: they were guided, and their guidance produced the protection just described. The other reads them as applying to their persecutors, who were left to stray and found no one to redirect them.

In a story about two groups — protected sleepers and a pursuing king — the formula applies to both, and that is precisely why it closes the scene.

**Shade as a Standing Blessing**

The physical detail of this verse belongs to a wider Qur'anic theme. Shade is described as extended by God and guided by the sun: *“Have you not seen how your Lord extends the shade—He could have simply made it ˹remain˺ still if He so willed—then We make the sun its guide”* (25:45), *“causing the shade to retreat gradually?”* (25:46).

In the Garden, shade becomes permanent: *“and We will place them under a vast shade”* (4:57). The cave is the intermediate case — shade arranged for a specific purpose, for a specific group, for a specific number of years — and the verse treats it as an instance of the same generosity.

Both readings are supported by the Qur'an's use of the same formula elsewhere: *“Whoever Allah guides is truly guided. And whoever He leaves to stray, they are the ˹true˺ losers”* (7:178), and *“Whoever Allah guides is truly guided. And whoever He leaves to stray, you will find no guardians for them besides Him”* (17:97).

---

## Verse 18:18

> And you would have thought they were awake, though they were asleep. We turned them over, to the right and left, while their dog stretched his forelegs at the entrance. Had you looked at them, you would have certainly fled away from them, filled with horror

**Turned Over So the Earth Would Not Consume Them**

The turning has a stated agent and an intelligible purpose. God turned them to the right and to the left, and al-Ṭabarī explains the reason: to prevent their bodies from being consumed by contact with the ground.

The detail is medically plausible and theologically pointed. A body left in one position develops pressure damage; the Qur'an attributes the prevention of that damage to a deliberate act. Nothing in the description suggests the youths turned themselves, which would have been impossible in the depth of sleep described earlier.

**The Dog at the Entrance**

One strand of commentary removes the animal altogether. Al-Ṭabarī records the view that *kalb* here was the name of a man — their cook, according to some — and al-Zamakhsharī records the reading *kālib*, a dog-keeper, in which case the limbs stretched at the entrance would be arms.

**Sleep as the Image of the Interval**

The state described in this verse is neither life as it is normally lived nor death, and the Qur'an has a way of speaking about exactly that region. Sleep is called a taking of the soul: *“He is the One Who calls back your souls by night and knows what you do by day, then revives you daily to complete your appointed term”* (6:60).

The same idea appears in Sūrat al-Zumar, where death and sleep are handled as one process with two outcomes (39:42 — *“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ calls back the souls ˹of people˺ upon their death as well as ˹the souls˺ of the living during their sleep.”*). Sleep is therefore a minor death, and waking a minor resurrection — which is why the cave narrative works as an argument about the greater one.

**A Saying About Sleep and Death**

A line often quoted in this connection runs: people are asleep, and when they die they wake. It is often circulated as a prophetic ḥadīth, and that attribution does not hold. The ḥadīth scholars, including al-Sakhāwī in *al-Maqāṣid al-Ḥasanah* and al-Qārī in *al-Asrār al-Marfūʿah*, record it as a saying of ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib rather than of the Prophet ﷺ.

---

## Verse 18:19

> And so We awakened them so that they might question one another. One of them exclaimed, “How long have you remained ˹asleep˺?” Some replied, “Perhaps a day, or part of a day.” They said ˹to one another˺, “Your Lord knows best how long you have remained. So send one of you with these silver coins of yours to the city, and let him find which food is the purest, and then bring you provisions from it. Let him be ˹exceptionally˺ cautious, and do not let him give you away

**Silver Coins and the Purest Food**

Two practical instructions follow, and both are specific. The first concerns money: send one of you with these silver coins of yours. The word *wariq* means coined silver — currency already minted, not bullion — and the demonstrative *these* implies money the youths actually carried when they fled.

The second concerns what to buy: let him see which of them has the purest food. The word *azkā* carries both senses of purity — ritually lawful and physically wholesome — and the commentators read it as covering both. Even in flight, the group's standard for food is not convenience.

There is also a detail about community in the instruction. The food is to be brought back for all of them — *bring you provisions from it* — and one man is sent for the whole group. Nobody disperses into the city separately, which is consistent with the caution that follows.

**Caution Issued as a Command**

The final instruction is emphatic: let him be *extremely* cautious — the verb *yatalaṭṭaf*, from a root meaning subtlety and gentleness, in an intensified form — and let him not make anyone aware of you.

---

The verse closes the sleepers' awakening with an instruction that has a legal edge: they should send one of them with the silver coins to the town to find the purest food, and be careful and let no one notice them. The caution is explained in the next verses by the danger the community was in, and the same care for the market's honesty appears in the sūrah's mention of the coins themselves — the point being that a place where they could buy food at all was a mercy after their long sleep. The sūrah's closing instruction about the number of the sleepers — that its knowledge belongs to God and disputing about it is fruitless (18:22 — *“Some will say, “They were three, their dog was the fourth,” while others will say, “They were five, their dog was the sixth,” ˹only˺ guessing blindly. And others will say, “They were seven and their dog was the eighth.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “My Lord knows best their ˹exact˺ number. Only a few people know as well.” So do not argue about them except with sure knowledge, nor consult any of those ˹who debate˺ about them”*) — is placed within the same passage, so the practical needs of food and safety are set beside the uselessness of speculation.

**The Details of the Errand**

The verse sends one of them into the town with the silver coins they had been carrying, and it tells him to be careful about the food he buys and to be gentle in his dealings so that no one recognises them. The words used for the money and for the food describe ordinary market business: the coins were coined silver, and the instruction about the food was to choose what is good and wholesome. The story is precise about the practical details, and the effect is to make the men in the cave real — they had woken from a long sleep in the clothes and the poverty of another age, and they were trying not to be noticed.
**The Ruling Behind the Caution**

The commentators drew a principle from the caution in the verse: a person should be careful with what he holds in trust for others, and careful about exposing himself to harm where nothing is gained by it. The Book states the same obligation elsewhere in a command to hand back what is held in trust to its owners and to judge with justice (4:58 — *“Indeed, Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners; and when you judge between people, judge with fairness.”*), and the Sūrah of the Cow makes the steward responsible for the property of orphans until they are grown (2:220 — *“upon this world and the Hereafter. And they ask you ˹O Prophet˺ concerning orphans. Say, “Improving their condition is best. And if you partner with them, they are bonded with you ˹in faith˺. And Allah knows who intends harm and who intends good. Had Allah willed, He could have made it difficult for you. Surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.””*). The sleepers' care with the money was not fear of a thief. It was a concern for a community that had spent itself, and the Book records it in a verse that could have been written about a market on any morning.
**The Question Nobody Could Answer**

The verse closes with the sentence that ties the story to the passage around it: God knows best how long they remained. The sūrah has already told the reader not to argue about details that carry no instruction — how many of them there were, and what the dog's colour was (18:22 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “My Lord knows best their ˹exact˺ number.”*). The time of the sleep is the same kind of question, and the verse answers it by leaving it with God and returning the reader to the practical matter in hand, which is a bag of silver coins and a hungry group of men with a town to buy food in.

## Verse 18:20

> For, indeed, if they find out about you, they will stone you ˹to death˺, or force you back into their faith, and then you will never succeed.”

**Never Succeeding as the Final Verdict**

The sentence closes with *lan tufliḥū*, you will never succeed. The negative particle *lan* denies the possibility permanently rather than temporarily, and *falāḥ* is a comprehensive word: success, flourishing, deliverance, the attainment of what one was aiming at.

**Stoning Named Specifically**

The punishment is *rajm*, stoning — a public, communal execution in which the crowd is the instrument. The choice of word is not incidental. Stoning is what a society does to someone it regards as a polluter rather than a criminal; it requires no court and no single executioner.

**What Coercion Excuses and What It Does Not**

The Qur'an addresses the situation of compelled belief elsewhere, and the ruling is nuanced. *“Whoever disbelieves in Allah after their belief—not those who are forced while their hearts are firm in faith, but those who embrace disbelief wholeheartedly—they will be condemned by Allah and suffer a tremendous punishment”* (16:106).

That ruling raises a question about the cave youths. If coercion is excused, why do they treat forced return as ruin? The answer lies in the wording of 16:106 — *“Whoever disbelieves in Allah after their belief—not those who are forced while their hearts are firm in faith, but those who embrace disbelief wholeheartedly—they will be condemned by Allah and suffer a tremendous punishment”* itself, which excuses the coerced but condemns those who open their hearts to disbelief. Compulsion is a real danger precisely because it does not always stop at the tongue. The youths' fear is not of a legal verdict but of themselves.

**The Same Distinction in Sūrat al-Naḥl**

The parallel is exact and worth reading beside this verse: *“not those who are forced while their hearts are firm in faith”* (16:106). Both passages separate the state of the heart from the words extracted from it, and both refuse to treat the two as identical.

The difference between them is instructive. Sūrat al-Naḥl states a ruling for someone already under compulsion; Sūrat al-Kahf records believers arranging not to reach that point. The ruling and the precaution belong together, and the cave narrative supplies the second half.

---

## Verse 18:21

> That is how We caused them to be discovered so that their people might know that Allah’s promise ˹of resurrection˺ is true and that there is no doubt about the Hour. When the people disputed with each other about the case of the youth ˹after their death˺, some proposed, “Build a structure around them. Their Lord knows best about them.” Those who prevailed in the matter said, “We will surely build a place of worship over them.”

**A Structure, or a Place of Worship**

The two proposals are not equally weighted in the verse's grammar. The first is reported as a suggestion — *some proposed* — with the concession *their Lord knows best about them* attached to it. The second is reported as a decision by those who prevailed.

**What Was Dug Up at Ephesus**

The site traditionally identified with the story has been excavated, and the findings are worth recording. On the slope of Mount Pion — Panayır Dağı, above the ruins of Ephesus near modern Selçuk — Austrian excavations in 1926 to 1928 uncovered a church built over a grotto, surrounded by several hundred graves dating to the fifth and sixth centuries.

The graves are the significant find. People wanted to be buried beside the sleepers, and the inscriptions found on the walls and in the graves invoke them by name. Within a few generations of the story's earliest written versions, this hillside had already become the address of the event.

The dispute recorded in the verse is about commemoration. One party proposes a structure built around them; the party that prevails proposes a place of worship. Al-Ṭabarī reports that a building was erected which later became a place of prayer.

**A Reported Speech, Not a Permission**

The words *we will surely build a place of worship over them* are the speech of a group in a story, not an instruction from God. The distinction is essential, because the verse has sometimes been cited as if it authorised building over graves.

Later Islamic practice reads it the other way. ʿĀʾishah reported that during his final illness the Prophet ﷺ said: *“Allah has cursed the Jews and Christians, who have taken the graves of their prophets as places of worship”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1390; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 529), and she added that his own grave was kept from a prominent place for exactly that reason.

The two texts do not conflict once the category of each is fixed. One narrates what people said; the other rules on what believers may do. Reading the first as a ruling requires ignoring that it is quoted speech inside a narrative.

**What the Verse Settles**

The verse reports the dispute that broke out once the sleepers were found. The men who came on the cave proposed building a place of worship over the site, and the verse records their proposal and then moves to the account of the two who argued about the number. The commentators read the verse as a record of two responses to a sign: one group wants to mark the place and one wants to argue about the details. The Book leaves the first aside without endorsing it and turns the reader to the second, where the command is given not to argue about the number at all (18:22 — *“Some will say, “They were three, their dog was the fourth,” while others will say, “They were five, their dog was the sixth,” ˹only˺ guessing blindly. And others will say, “They were seven and their dog was the eighth.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “My Lord knows best their ˹exact˺ number. Only a few people know as well.” So do not argue about them except with sure knowledge, nor consult any of those ˹who debate˺ about them”*).
**The Sleeping and the Waking**

The verse also states the earlier part of the account, where God woke the sleepers so that they might question one another. That detail belongs to the sūrah's method with all its stories: a sign is not left as a private experience, it is turned into a question people ask in the street. The Sūrah of the Cave does the same with the story of Moses and the servant, where the acts are done and then explained, and with the wall and the orphans, where the reason is given after the objection. The Book tells its stories with people in them talking.
**Where It Stands in the Passage**

The verses before this one describe the sleepers waking and sending one of their number to buy food, and the verses after it move to the command not to dispute the count. The Book's purpose in the account is stated in the sentence about the men who argued: the story is told so that people may know that God's promise is true and that there is no doubt about the Hour. The builders and the counters are both beside that purpose, and the sūrah keeps the reader looking at it.

## Verse 18:22

> Some will say, “They were three, their dog was the fourth,” while others will say, “They were five, their dog was the sixth,” ˹only˺ guessing blindly. And others will say, “They were seven and their dog was the eighth.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “My Lord knows best their ˹exact˺ number. Only a few people know as well.” So do not argue about them except with sure knowledge, nor consult any of those ˹who debate˺ about them

**A Referral That Is Not an Answer**

Do not consult any of them about them. The pronouns are deliberately dense: do not ask the people who debate this matter *about* this matter. Al-Ṭabarī reads the referent as the People of the Book, the source to whom the Quraysh had directed their question in the first place.
The instruction completes an arc. The challenge had been to test the Prophet ﷺ against the knowledge of the Jewish scholars of Madinah. The answer arrives, and with it a ruling that those scholars are no longer the reference point. The revealed account has replaced the consulted authority.
The instruction also protects the Prophet ﷺ from a trap. Every additional question referred outward keeps the authority outside the revelation, and each answer becomes one more statement to be tested against another tradition. Closing the referral ends that sequence.
**The Unseen Has a Single Owner**

Underneath the whole verse sits a principle stated elsewhere without qualification: *“With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him. And He knows what is in the land and sea. Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge”* (6:59).
The principle explains why the number is withheld. If knowledge of the unseen belongs to God alone, then a human estimate of it — three, five, seven — has no standing however confidently it is stated. The verse is not being coy about a fact; it is enforcing a boundary.
The verse performs a kind of triage on its own material. What serves the purpose is given at length — the flight, the prayer, the sleep, the sun, the discovery. What serves curiosity is withheld, and the withholding is stated rather than left as a silence.

**The Three Guesses and the Command to Stop**

The verse reports what people were saying about the sleepers of the cave: some said three, with the fourth their dog; some said five, with the sixth their dog; some said seven, and the eighth their dog. Then it gives the instruction: say that my Lord knows best their number, and only a few know them, and do not argue about them except with an obvious proof, and do not ask anyone for an opinion about them. The debate had been going on among the People of the Book before the question ever reached the Prophet, and the sūrah closes it rather than joining it.
**Why the Sūrah Answers This Way**

The answer is the method of the Book in matters that do not carry the message. The number of the sleepers changes nothing about the lesson: God kept them alive as a sign of the raising of the dead (18:21 — *“That is how We caused them to be discovered so that their people might know that Allah’s promise ˹of resurrection˺ is true and that there is no doubt about the Hour. When the people disputed with each other about the case of the youth ˹after their death˺, some proposed, “Build a structure around them. Their Lord knows best about them.” Those who prevailed in the matter said, “We will surely build a place of worship over them.””*). The Book also refuses to let the community build a case on a guess, since an opinion offered with confidence in the place of knowledge is the habit that runs through the sūrah's other stories. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that he was among the few mentioned in the verse, and his view was that they were seven. The report is a scholar's opinion, and the verse's own command stands above it: the matter belongs to God.

## Verse 18:23

> And never say of anything, “I will definitely do this tomorrow,”

**A Prohibition Aimed at Certainty, Not at Planning**

The verse forbids a specific sentence rather than a specific action. What is prohibited is saying *I will definitely do this tomorrow* — a statement about one's own future delivered in the indicative, with the certainty built into the verb.

Planning is not the target. The Qur'an elsewhere describes people arranging journeys, harvests, and battles without criticism. What is criticised here is the grammatical form of self-sufficiency: a future asserted as though the speaker controlled it.

The word *definitely* in the translation carries the weight. Arabic marks the certainty in the verb form itself, and the prohibition attaches to that marker.

The object of the prohibition is equally broad: *of anything*.

**What the Exception Actually Asserts**

The Qur'an states the same principle as a plain fact elsewhere: *“No soul knows what it will earn for tomorrow, and no soul knows in what land it will die”* (31:34). The exception clause is that verse compressed into a phrase.

Notice what the formula does not do. It does not weaken the intention or license inaction. A person who says he will do something tomorrow, God willing, has still committed to doing it; the qualification concerns who makes the commitment effective, not whether it is sincere.

**The Practice the Verse Founded**

Jurists discussed the boundaries. Where the statement is an oath, the exception must generally be attached at once to take legal effect, and a broken oath carries its own expiation. Where the statement is ordinary speech, the formula is a recognition rather than a condition.

**Solomon's Oath in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī**

The clearest illustration of the principle comes from the Prophet ﷺ himself, who told a story about a prophet who forgot the formula. Abū Hurayrah reported that Solomon said he would go around to his wives that night, each of whom would bear a child who would fight in God's cause; he was told to say *if Allah wills*, and he did not (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6720).

The outcome is given in the report: none conceived except one, who bore a half-formed child, and the Prophet ﷺ added that had Solomon said the formula, his purpose would have been achieved.

## Verse 18:24

> without adding, “if Allah so wills!” But if you forget, then remember your Lord, and say, “I trust my Lord will guide me to what is more right than this.”

**The Exception That Completes the Sentence**

The verse supplies the phrase the previous one required, and its position is grammatical rather than incidental. The prohibition was on saying *I will definitely do this tomorrow*; the exception attaches to the sentence itself, converting an assertion into a conditional.

The effect is a change of speaker. A statement qualified by *if Allah so wills* no longer claims that the speaker will bring the event about; it states an intention and places its fulfilment elsewhere. The same words, with four added, mean something different.

**"More Right Than This"**

The phrase the speaker is told to add has been read in two directions.

The first takes it as the content of the remembrance itself: on remembering, one says that God may guide him to more than that correct than the statement he has just made. The second reads it as a prayer of the Prophet ﷺ for a greater sign than the account of the cave youths — a request that the evidence of his prophethood be increased beyond what he had just been given.

**The Same Remedy in Sūrat al-Anʿām**

The parallel is close enough to be instructive: *“Should Satan make you forget, then once you remember, do not ˹continue to˺ sit with the wrongdoing people”* (6:68). A different subject, the same structure — forgetfulness anticipated, remembrance prescribed, no reproach attached.

The consistency across two Makkan sūrahs suggests a settled approach rather than a local accommodation. The believer is expected to fail at attention and to recover from it, repeatedly, and the text is written for someone who will.

## Verse 18:25

> They had remained in their cave for three hundred years, adding nine

**Three Hundred Solar Years Are Three Hundred and Nine Lunar Years**

The classical explanation of the nine is calendrical, and the arithmetic holds. Three hundred solar years of 365.2422 days total 109,572.7 days; divided by the lunar year of 354.367 days, that is 309.2 lunar years. The nine-year difference is not an addition to the period but the same period counted in a different system.

It also explains why the figure is expressed as a sum. A scribe copying "three hundred and nine" would have no reason to split it; a translator reconciling two calendars would.

**A Figure That Does Not Close the Matter**

The next verse instructs the Prophet ﷺ to say that God knows best how long they stayed, and commentators have handled the apparent tension in three ways.

The first treats the figure as the number the People of the Book gave, with God's knowledge left superior to it. The second takes the 309 years as the actual duration of the sleep and reads God's greater knowledge as referring to the time the youths spent in the cave after their death. The third sees the figure as settling an existing dispute, with the following statement confirming the settlement.

**The Same Estimate After a Hundred Years**

The Qur'an offers a precise parallel. A man is caused to die for a century and is then raised: *“Allah asked, ‘How long have you remained ˹in this state˺?’ He replied, ‘Perhaps a day or part of a day.’ Allah said, ‘No! You have remained here for a hundred years!’”* (2:259).

## Verse 18:26

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah knows best how long they stayed. With Him ˹alone˺ is ˹the knowledge of˺ the unseen of the heavens and the earth. How perfectly He hears and sees! They have no guardian besides Him, and He shares His command with none.”

**The Unseen Has a Single Owner**

The second sentence generalises the point. With Him is the unseen of the heavens and the earth — not a portion of it, and not a shared custody.

The Qur'an repeats this assignment constantly: *“With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him”* (6:59). The formula is used to close questions rather than to open speculation, and it appears wherever someone claims knowledge of what has not been disclosed.

The scope of the phrase is also total: the unseen of the heavens *and the earth*. Nothing in either realm is excluded, which rules out the common compromise of assigning God the heavens and leaving human enquiry the earth.

**No Guardian Besides Him**

The pronoun in *they have no guardian besides Him* is read in two ways. Al-Rāzī records both: it may refer to the youths of the cave, or to those who disputed about their affair.

Both readings are supported by the wording, and both are consistent with the Qur'an's use of *walī* elsewhere. The choice between them does not change the verse's force: whatever group is meant, there is no one else to turn to.

**The Correction Moses Received**

The verse comes after the servant tells Moses that the knowledge he has is the knowledge he was given, and it begins with the command to tell them what the reality is: God knows best how long they stayed. The sūrah has handled the same question about the sleepers a few verses earlier, where the answers of the guessers were dismissed and the Prophet was told that only a few know the truth (18:22 — *“Only a few people know as well.””*). The Book's method with questions of this kind is stated twice in the same passage: the number and the duration belong to God, and the discussion is closed so that the reader may turn to what he is being taught.
**The Sentence About Forgiveness**

The same verse tells the Prophet to take a lesson from what he saw and heard, and the commentators read the phrase as an instruction about the patience he had failed to keep with the servant: whoever wishes for the meeting with his Lord should do good work and associate no one in the worship of his Lord. The Sūrah of the Cave then closes its account of the two men of the boats, the boy and the wall with the sentence that the servant's knowledge was the knowledge of God. The passage is one of the sūrah's strongest statements that a person sees a fragment and judges a whole, and the verse puts a command to tolerance in the mouth of the correction.
**The Keys That Belong to God**

The Book repeats the same formula about the unseen in the Sūrah of the Cattle, where the keys of the unseen are with God and no one knows them but He (6:59 — *“With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him.”*), and in the Sūrah of the Ant, where nobody in the heavens or the earth knows the hidden except God (27:65 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “None in the heavens and the earth has knowledge of the unseen except Allah. Nor do they know when they will be resurrected”*). The phrase is used in the Book to close speculation rather than to open it, and it appears wherever a claim to hidden knowledge is being made. The verse in front of us joins the two halves, the unseen and the command to worship, so that the reader is told where the knowledge ends and the duty begins.

## Verse 18:27

> Recite what has been revealed to you from the Book of your Lord. None can change His Words, nor can you find any refuge besides Him

**An Instruction to Recite, Not to Revise**

The command is short and its object is fixed: recite what has been revealed. The Prophet ﷺ is not asked to adapt the message, to soften a passage, or to produce a version suited to his audience.

The occasion explains the instruction. The Makkans had asked for exactly that — *“Bring us a different Quran or make some changes in it”* (10:15) — and the answer given there was that it is not his to change. This verse states the same ruling as a positive command rather than a refusal.

The placement after the cave story is significant. A long narrative has just been delivered, complete with details the audience wanted and details it did not.

*Utlū* means to recite aloud, in sequence, to an audience.

**Words That Cannot Be Changed**

The second clause is absolute. None can change His Words — no person, no community, no generation. The Qur'an makes the same claim elsewhere in almost identical terms: *“The Word of your Lord has been perfected in truth and justice. None can change His Words”* (6:115).

The claim has a precise scope. It does not deny that God alters His own revelation, and the Qur'an says so plainly: *“When We replace a verse with another—and Allah knows best what He reveals—they say, ‘You ˹Muḥammad˺ are just a fabricator’”* (16:101). Replacement is a divine act; alteration by anyone else is excluded.

The distinction matters for how the text has been transmitted. A book whose words cannot be changed by its readers imposes a duty on them: preserve exactly, and argue with the meaning rather than with the wording.

**Recitation as the Standing Commission**

The verse generalises beyond its occasion. The command to recite what has been revealed is the definition of the messenger's task, and the Qur'an states it in the same terms elsewhere: recite what has been revealed to you from the Book (29:45 — *“Recite what has been revealed to you of the Book and establish prayer.”*).

The commission excludes three things at once. It excludes invention, since only what was revealed may be recited. It excludes selection, since the object is the whole of what was revealed. And it excludes editorial judgement about audience, since the reciter's relation to the text is fixed by the verb.

What remains is a task that looks modest and is not: deliver the words, in order, without addition, to people who may not want them.

**The Same Refusal in Sūrat Yūnus**

The parallel passage supplies the reasoning this verse compresses: *“Say ˹to them˺, ‘It is not for me to change it on my own. I only follow what is revealed to me. I fear, if I were to disobey my Lord, the punishment of a tremendous Day.’”* (10:15).

Sūrat Yūnus gives the motive — fear of the Day — where Sūrat al-Kahf gives the command. Together they describe a messenger who cannot change the text even if he wished to, and who does not wish to because he knows what compliance would cost.

## Verse 18:28

> And patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. Do not let your eyes look beyond them, desiring the luxuries of this worldly life. And do not obey those whose hearts We have made heedless of Our remembrance, who follow ˹only˺ their desires and whose state is ˹total˺ loss

**Patience Directed at Company, Not Only at Hardship**

The verb is unusual in its construction: keep *yourself* patient *with* those who call upon their Lord. The object of the patience is the speaker's own soul, and the preposition places that soul in the company of a specific group.

Most Qur'anic commands to patience concern endurance under hardship. This one concerns staying put among people. The difficulty being addressed is not persecution but the temptation to prefer better company.

The reflexive object sharpens it. The soul must be held in place, which implies that it wants to move. Nobody needs to be told to keep himself with people he already prefers.

The command therefore assumes a real temptation. It is addressed to a man whose followers were poor, persecuted, and socially worthless to the men who might otherwise have joined him. The instruction is not a courtesy but a demand that he resist an advantage.

The occasion is recorded in the interpretive tradition. Prominent Makkans indicated that they were willing to sit and listen to the Prophet ﷺ, on one condition: that his poorer followers leave, so that they would not have to share a gathering with people of low standing.

**Morning and Evening**

The group is described by its practice: those who call upon their Lord morning and evening. The two times mark the structure of a devotional day — invocation at the start and at the close, with the hours between governed by them.

The same formula appears as an instruction elsewhere: glorify Him morning and evening (33:42 — *“and glorify Him morning and evening”*). The description here is therefore not a compliment but an identification — these are the people whose lives are organised around remembrance, and that is what makes them worth staying with.

The qualifier is decisive. They call upon their Lord *seeking His pleasure* — literally, desiring His Face. The phrase excludes every other motive: not status, not protection, not the benefits of belonging.

The Qur'an uses the same phrase for giving: *“We feed you only for the sake of Allah, seeking neither reward nor thanks from you”* (76:9). The motive is what distinguishes an act of worship from a social performance, and the verse identifies the group by it.

The mystics read the phrase at its furthest reach, as the desire for the vision of God itself. Whether read minimally or maximally, it fixes the direction of the group's attention — toward God rather than toward the gathering's other members.

**The Same Command in Sūrat al-Anʿām**

The parallel verse is more explicit about the stakes: *“˹O Prophet!˺ Do not dismiss those ˹poor believers˺ who invoke their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. You are not accountable for them whatsoever, nor are they accountable for you. So do not dismiss them, or you will be one of the wrongdoers”* (6:52).

The addition is the warning at the end. Dismissing the poor is not a social misjudgement but an act that makes the dismiss-er a wrongdoer. Sūrat al-Kahf states the command; Sūrat al-Anʿām states the consequence of ignoring it.

A third passage supplies the same lesson from the other direction. The Prophet ﷺ was reproved for frowning and turning away *“˹simply˺ because the blind man came to him ˹interrupting˺”* (80:1–2) — a momentary preference for the notables present, corrected in revelation before it hardened into a practice.

## Verse 18:29

> And say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve.” Surely We have prepared for the wrongdoers a Fire whose walls will ˹completely˺ surround them. When they cry for aid, they will be aided with water like molten metal, which will burn ˹their˺ faces. What a horrible drink! And what a terrible place to rest

The instruction is to make a single declaration: this is the truth from your Lord. No argument accompanies it, no evidence is summarised, no appeal to the audience's interests is attached.

**A Fire With Walls Around It**

The Fire is described as having a *surādiq* — a wall, canopy, or enclosing covering — that surrounds its inhabitants completely. The image is of an enclosure with no gap, not of a pit one might climb out of.

The Qur'an uses the same picture elsewhere: *“on the Day the punishment will overwhelm them from above them and from below their feet”* (29:55), and *“They will have layers of fire above and below them”* (39:16). The consistency of the image suggests it is meant literally rather than as a figure for intensity.

The word itself is telling. A *surādiq* is what surrounds a tent or a pavilion — an enclosure made for those inside it. Applied to the Fire, it turns a place of punishment into a dwelling with no exit, which is why the verse ends by calling it a place to rest.

**Relief That Turns Out to Be Punishment**

The substance is *muhl*, described by the commentators as molten metal, or as a thick liquid like the dregs of oil. It is offered in response to a request for water, and it burns the faces of those who approach it.

**The Drinks of Hell Against the Drinks of the Garden**

The contrast is drawn explicitly elsewhere in the Qur'an, and the two descriptions are meant to be read together. The Garden's provision is listed in full: *“rivers of fresh water, rivers of milk that never changes in taste, rivers of wine delicious to drink. Rivers of pure honey”* (47:15), and the same verse ends by asking whether its inhabitants can be compared to those *“left to drink boiling water that will tear apart their insides.”*

**What the Verse Declares**

The instruction is to say, once and without argument, that this is the truth from your Lord. The Book then sets the two roads in front of the hearer and leaves the choice: whoever wishes may believe, and whoever wishes may disbelieve. The sentence is one of the plainest statements in the Book that faith is not to be produced by force, and the Sūrah of the Cow states the same principle in its own words (2:256 — *“Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood. So whoever renounces false gods and believes in Allah has certainly grasped the firmest, unfailing hand-hold. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing”*). The verse gives the truth and then gives the responsibility, and it does not put a sword behind the invitation.
**The Picture of the Fire**

The verse describes the fire as a tent that closes around the people who are in it, and the drink that is offered to them as water like molten metal that scalds the faces. The Book gives the picture with material details because the audience for it was made up of people who knew what a besieged camp looked like. The Sūrah of the Troops gives the same picture in another form, where the fire closes over the people as a canopy (39:16 — *“They will have layers of fire above and below them. That is what Allah warns His servants with. So fear Me, O My servants”*), and the Sūrah of Muḥammad describes the drink in its own terms (47:15 — *“˹Can they be˺ like those who will stay in the Fire forever, left to drink boiling water that will tear apart their insides”*). The point of the picture is the closing, and the verse states it before the next one names the reward of the other road.
**Where the Passage Stands**

The verse belongs to the parable of the two men in the sūrah, and it follows the wealthy man's boasts and the collapse of his garden. The sūrah places it there so that the reader has just watched a comfortable life end in a night, and it then gives both roads at once. The next verses describe the garden prepared for the mindful, and the Book leaves the two pictures facing each other, which is how it argues about the next life in most of its places.

## Verse 18:30

> As for those who believe and do good, We certainly never deny the reward of those who are best in deeds

**A Negation Reinforced Twice**

The promise is built on a double emphasis. The particle of certainty comes first — We certainly — and the verb that follows is negative: We never deny, or more literally, We do not cause to be lost. Two devices are stacked on a single statement, and both are needed to say what the verse says.

The early believers in Makkah had lost property, family standing, and in some cases their freedom, and none of it had yet produced a visible return.

**Reward Preserved Rather Than Merely Granted**

The verb chosen is significant. *Iḍāʿah* means to waste, to squander, to let something be lost through neglect. Its negation says that God does not mislay what people have done.

The Qur'an uses the same formula repeatedly, and always in contexts where effort has been costly: *“Surely Allah never discounts the reward of the good-doers”* (9:120), spoken of those who suffered thirst and fatigue in God's cause, and *“And be patient! Certainly Allah does not discount the reward of the good-doers”* (11:115).

The pattern identifies the audience of the promise. It is addressed to people whose good deeds have not yet produced any return in this world — which, at the time this sūrah was revealed, described most of its hearers who believed.

The verb's ordinary use reinforces the point. To *aḍāʿa* something is to lose it carelessly, as one loses a possession left unattended. Its negation says that no act of obedience is left unattended by God, however small it was or however long ago it was performed.

**Īmān, Islām, and Iḥsān**

The verse's two terms belong to a three-part scheme the Prophet ﷺ set out himself. In the report known as the ḥadīth of Jibrīl — recorded by Muslim (8) and al-Bukhārī (50) — the angel asks in turn about *islām*, *īmān*, and *iḥsān*, and receives three distinct answers: the practices, the beliefs, and the state of worshipping as though one sees God.

The verse names the second and implies the third. Those who believe and do good are described in its second half as *those who are best in deeds* — the language of *aḥsan*, from which *iḥsān* is formed.

The connection is more than lexical. A deed is *aḥsan* when it is both sincere and correct, and sincerity at its highest is the awareness of being seen. The verse therefore holds all three categories in a single sentence.

The scheme also explains why the Qur'an never treats belief as enough on its own. In the ḥadīth of Jibrīl the three are asked about separately, and the Prophet ﷺ answers each on its own terms — belief without practice is not the second category, and practice without awareness is not the third.

**The Same Pairing in Sūrat al-Bayyinah**

The parallel is short and unequivocal: *“Indeed, those who believe and do good—they are the best of ˹all˺ beings”* (98:7). The same two terms, the same superlative, and no additional condition attached.

Read beside this verse, the parallel confirms that the pairing is not a rhetorical habit but a definition. What makes a human being best is not knowledge alone, lineage, or intensity of feeling, but belief accompanied by action — and the reward attached to that combination is described in both passages as secure.

## Verse 18:31

> It is they who will have the Gardens of Eternity, with rivers flowing under their feet. There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold, and wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade, reclining there on ˹canopied˺ couches. What a marvellous reward! And what a fabulous place to rest

**The Gardens of Eternity Named by Their Title**

The reward is introduced with a proper name: *Jannāt ʿAdn*, the Gardens of Eternity. The Qur'an uses the title repeatedly — in Sūrat al-Tawbah, al-Raʿd, al-Naḥl, Maryam, Ṭāhā, Fāṭir, Ṣād, Ghāfir, and al-Bayyinah — and each use carries the permanence in the name itself.

The title answers the previous verse directly. Those whose reward will not be lost are now told what the reward is called, and its name is the guarantee. No further assurance about duration is needed once the place has been named.

The plural is also worth noting. Gardens, not a garden. The image is of an estate rather than a single enclosed space, which suits a reward described as beyond calculation.

**Bracelets, Silk, and Brocade**

The description turns to adornment, and it is specific: bracelets of gold, garments of fine silk — *sundus* — and rich brocade, *istabrāq*. The two textiles are named separately because they differ: the first is the fine fabric, the second the thick, ornate weave.

The commentators divide over how literally to take the list. Many treat the items as symbols of a state that cannot be described in the language of this world, and al-Rāzī offers a distinction worth keeping: the garments represent what the inhabitants earned by their deeds, while the bracelets represent an addition from God beyond their earning.

One feature of the list would have struck the first audience immediately. Gold and silk were prohibited to the men of that community. Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī reported the Prophet ﷺ saying: *“Allah has permitted silk and gold to the females of my Ummah. Has forbidden them to the males”* (Sunan an-Nasaʾī 5265; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 1720), and ʿAlī reported him holding silk in one hand and gold in the other and declaring both forbidden to the men of his community (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4057).

**The Same Garments in Sūrat al-Insān**

The parallel description supplies a detail this verse omits: *“The virtuous will be ˹dressed˺ in garments of fine green silk and rich brocade, and adorned with bracelets of silver, and their Lord will give them a purifying drink”* (76:21).

Gold in one passage, silver in the other; the same garments in both. The variation has been read as indicating ranks within the Garden, and the difference in metal is the kind of detail the Qur'an varies when it describes states of unequal nearness.

What both passages share is the green. The colour appears here, in Sūrat al-Insān, and in the description of green cushions in Sūrat al-Raḥmān (55:76 — *“All ˹believers˺ will be reclining on green cushions and splendid carpets”*) — a consistent palette for the place this verse has just named.

## Verse 18:32

> Give them ˹O Prophet˺ an example of two men. To ˹the disbelieving˺ one We gave two gardens of grapevines, which We surrounded with palm trees and placed ˹various˺ crops in between

**The Wealth Is Described as Given**

The grammar of the verse settles a question before it can be asked. *To one of them We gave two gardens* — the subject of the giving is God, and the object is the wealth. Nothing in the sentence attributes the gardens to the owner's effort, inheritance, or skill.
The same construction governs the rest of the description: We surrounded them, We placed.
**The Makkans' Own Demand, Granted in Fiction**

The parable describes almost exactly what the Makkans had demanded as a condition of belief. They had said they would not believe *“until you have a garden of palm trees and vineyards, and cause rivers to flow abundantly in it”* (17:91).
The reply is also a refusal to meet the demand on its own terms. No garden was produced for the Makkans to inspect. A garden was described in a story, and the description was turned back on them as a question about what property does to the people who hold it.
**The Owners of the Garden in Sūrat al-Qalam**

The Qur'an offers a second parable of the same shape, and the comparison sharpens this one. *“Indeed, We have tested those ˹Meccans˺ as We tested the owners of the garden—when they swore they would surely harvest ˹all˺ its fruit in the early morning”* (68:17).
In Sūrat al-Qalam the owners are a group who exclude the poor from their harvest and lose the garden overnight. Here the owner is a single man who loses it after a conversation. Both parables attach the loss to a claim of ownership, and both address the same audience.

**The Parable and Its Two Men**

*Wa-ḍrib lahum mathalan* — set out a parable for them. The story runs from this verse to verse forty-four: one man given two vineyards with palm trees and a crop between them, and a neighbour with nothing but his faith. The man of the gardens comes to his companion boasting — I have more wealth than you and more men — and then commits the thing the parable was built to expose: he enters his garden and says this will never end, and does not mention God (18:34-35 — *“And he entered his property, while wronging his soul, saying, “I do not think this will ever perish”*). His companion answers with the words the sūrah wants the reader to hold: why did you not say, when you entered, that this is what God has willed, and that God is my Lord (18:39 — *“If only you had said, upon entering your property, ‘This is what Allah has willed!”*). The story is placed among the sūrah's answers to the wealthy men of Makkah, who were saying the same things about their own gardens and their own standing.
**How the Story Ends**

The ending comes in one night. The garden is surrounded by ruin, the man wrings his hands over what he spent on it, and everything he built is left bare (18:42 — *“And so all his produce was ˹totally˺ ruined, so he started to wring his hands for all he had spent on it, while it had collapsed on its trellises.”*). His regret is not about the money but about the sentence the passage records: I wish I had not associated anyone with my Lord (18:42 — *“He cried, “Alas! I wish I had never associated anyone with my Lord ˹in worship˺!””*). The next verse states the conclusion, that there the authority belongs to God, the truth (18:44 — *“At this time, support comes ˹only˺ from Allah—the True ˹Lord˺. He is best in reward and best in outcome”*), and the sūrah follows it with a picture of this world as rain on a plant that withers (18:45 — *“˹It is˺ like the plants of the earth, thriving when sustained by the rain We send down from the sky.”*). The parable is not against wealth. It is against the assumption that wealth is a verdict, and it puts the counting of the credit where the Book always puts it: with the One who decided to give it and can decide to take it back.

## Verse 18:33

> Each garden yielded ˹all˺ its produce, never falling short. And We caused a river to flow between them

**A Harvest That Never Failed**

The two gardens are described in a single clause: each brought its produce and did not fall short in any respect. The Arabic phrase behind the translation is stronger than the English — literally, the gardens *did him no wrong*.

**The River That Completed the Gift**

The second clause adds the one element an Arabian estate cannot produce for itself: water. We caused a river to flow between them — a perennial stream running through the middle of the two gardens.

It also completes the echo of the Makkans' demand, which had asked for a garden of palms and vines with rivers caused to flow in the midst of it (17:91 — *“or until you have a garden of palm trees and vineyards, and cause rivers to flow abundantly in it”*). Every item on their list is now present, and the man who holds it does not believe.

The river is placed *between* the two gardens rather than beside them, which means both properties share it. Water in that climate is the one asset that cannot be duplicated, and its position makes the estate a single holding rather than two separate ones.

**Adornment That Has an Ending**

The verse leaves its description unqualified, and the qualification comes from elsewhere in the Qur'an. The inventory of worldly good ends with a verdict: *“These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination”* (3:14).

The limitation is temporal rather than moral. The gardens are good, and a reader who wants them has understood the description correctly. What the Qur'an objects to is the word *never* — a term no earthly property can honestly carry.

---

## Verse 18:34

> And he had other resources ˹as well˺. So he boasted to a ˹poor˺ companion of his, while conversing with him, “I am greater than you in wealth and superior in manpower.”

**A Boast Delivered in Conversation**

The setting is specified with care: he boasted to his companion *while conversing with him*. This is not a soliloquy, a proclamation, or a speech to a crowd. It is a remark made in the course of a conversation between two men who know each other.

**The Two Measures of Standing**

The Book warns repeatedly that the pair proves nothing about divine favour. *“Adding, ‘We are far superior ˹to the believers˺ in wealth and children, and we will never be punished’”* (34:35) is answered with the plain statement that wealth and children are not a sign of standing with God.

**Manpower as a Claim to Security**

The second measure deserves its own attention. *Stronger in men* means sons, retainers, servants, and dependants — the human force that in a tribal society stood between a man and his enemies.

There is also a tribal assumption embedded in it. Manpower is counted in sons first and retainers second, and both are understood as continuing after the father.

**The Same Boast in Sūrat Sabaʾ**

The parallel is exact in substance and tone: *“Adding, ‘We are far superior ˹to the believers˺ in wealth and children, and we will never be punished.’”* (34:35).

Sūrat Sabaʾ supplies what Sūrat al-Kahf leaves to the narrative: the inference drawn from the comparison. The men there conclude from their wealth that punishment will not reach them, which is the same movement the garden owner makes two verses later when he says the Hour will not come.

---

## Verse 18:35

> And he entered his property, while wronging his soul, saying, “I do not think this will ever perish

**The Wrong Is Named Before the Words Are Quoted**

The verse arranges its elements in a specific order: he entered his garden, *wronging himself*, and said. The moral verdict is inserted before the speech it judges, so the reader meets the statement already classified.

That arrangement prevents a sympathetic reading. A man entering his property and remarking on its permanence might otherwise sound like any owner taking satisfaction in his work. The phrase *ẓāliman li-nafsih* removes the ambiguity before the quotation begins.

The Qur'an uses the same formula throughout for sin: wronging oneself. The description is precise, since the damage described in the following verses falls on the owner rather than on anyone else.

**"I Do Not Think This Will Ever Perish"**

The statement is a prediction about permanence, and it is stated with the confidence of a man reporting what he has observed. His gardens have never failed; therefore they will not.

The inference is where the wrong lies. Regularity in the past does not establish necessity in the future, and the Qur'an states the alternative in the plainest terms: *“Everything is bound to perish except He Himself”* (28:88).

**The Long Hope**

The commentators name the condition precisely: *ṭūl al-amal*, the lengthening of hope. It is the state in which a person's expectations extend indefinitely forward, and in which death stops functioning as a limit on planning.

The Qur'an identifies the same condition elsewhere and mocks it: *“thinking that their wealth will make them immortal!”* (104:3). The garden owner does not quite say that, but the distance between his statement and that one is small — a property that never perishes implies an owner who does not need to leave it.

**Everything Perishes Except His Face**

The corrective is stated elsewhere without qualification: *“And do not invoke any other god with Allah. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. Everything is bound to perish except He Himself. All authority belongs to Him. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned”* (28:88).

Sūrat al-Raḥmān states the same fact in a single line: *“Every being on earth is bound to perish”* (55:26). Read beside the garden owner's claim, the two verses measure the distance between what a man sees from inside his property and what is true of it.

---

## Verse 18:36

> nor do I think the Hour will ˹ever˺ come. And if in fact I am returned to my Lord, I will definitely get a far better outcome than ˹all˺ this.”

**A Conditional That Conceals a Denial**

The hedge deserves attention: *if in fact I am returned to my Lord*. On the surface this is an admission that the Hour might come. In context it functions as the opposite.

The conditional allows the speaker to deny the premise while arguing about its consequences. He does not believe he will be returned; he is describing what would happen if he were, and the description is a boast rather than a hope.

The Qur'an identifies the move by name in Sūrat Maryam, asking whether the man has looked into the unseen or taken a pledge from God (19:78 — *“Has he looked into the unseen or taken a pledge from the Most Compassionate”*).

**Merit Assumed to Outlive Death**

The reasoning behind the boast is recoverable, and al-Rāzī states it: the man believes his wealth belongs to him on the basis of his own merit, and since merit does not die with the body, he expects to be provided for after death on the same terms.

The inference has a shape worth naming. Present favour is read as a verdict on the person; a verdict on the person survives the person; therefore further favour can be expected. Every step is questionable, and the Qur'an attacks the first step wherever it appears.

The same claim is recorded elsewhere with the same confidence: *“Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ the one who rejects Our revelations yet boasts, ‘I will definitely be granted ˹plenty of˺ wealth and children ˹if there is an afterlife˺.’?”* (19:77), followed immediately by the question that dismantles it: *“Has he looked into the unseen or taken a pledge from the Most Compassionate?”* (19:78).

**The Inference the Qur'an Attacks Everywhere**

Reading prosperity as approval is one of the Book's standing targets. The mechanism has a name in the tradition — *istidrāj*, the gradual drawing-on of a person who mistakes increase for endorsement — and the Qur'an describes it without softening: *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise”* (6:44).

The same theme runs through Sūrat al-Qalam: *“So leave to Me ˹O Prophet˺ those who reject this message. We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot understand. I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless”* (68:44–45).

Qārūn states the premise openly and is answered with a history lesson: *“He replied, ‘I have been granted all this because of some knowledge I have.’ Did he not know that Allah had already destroyed some from the generations before him who were far superior to him in power?”* (28:78). The garden owner makes the same claim in different words.

**Almost the Same Words in Sūrat Fuṣṣilat**

The parallel is so close that it reads as the same statement in another register: *“And if We let them taste a mercy from Us after being touched with adversity, they will certainly say, ‘This is what I deserve. I do not think the Hour will ˹ever˺ come. And if in fact I am returned to my Lord, the finest reward with Him will definitely be mine.’”* (41:50).

Sūrat Fuṣṣilat adds the first clause that Sūrat al-Kahf leaves implicit — *this is what I deserve* — and supplies the answer the parable will deliver narratively: We will inform the disbelievers of what they used to do.

---

## Verse 18:37

> His ˹believing˺ companion replied, while conversing with him, “Do you disbelieve in the One Who created you from dust, then ˹developed you˺ from a sperm-drop, then formed you into a man

**A Reply That Opens as a Question**

The companion's answer does not begin with a denial, a rebuke, or a counter-claim. It begins with a question: do you disbelieve in the One who created you?

The verb also fixes the character of the offence. Disbelief here is not doubt or hesitation but *kufr* — a refusal of something known. The question implies that the matter is not genuinely open, which is why it is posed as a question rather than argued as a case.

**Three Stages, Named in Order**

The question lists an origin in three movements: created from dust, developed from a sperm-drop, formed as a man. The sequence is the Qur'an's standard account of human origin, and it appears in the same order elsewhere.

Sūrat al-Ḥajj sets it out at length: *“We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh”* (22:5). Sūrat al-Muʾminūn continues through the stages to the finished creature (23:14 — *“then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation.”*), and Sūrat al-Qiyāmah compresses the whole account into a single line: *“Were they not ˹once˺ a sperm-drop emitted?”* (75:37).

The two endpoints do the work. Dust is the lowest material available; a formed man is a moral agent. Between them lies nothing the man himself contributed.

**The Question Carries Its Own Answer**

The commentators add a further step. Al-Rāzī notes that being *formed as a man* includes the endowment of intelligence and moral responsibility — *taklīf*. The third stage is therefore not only physical but normative: the same act that made him a man made him accountable.

**The Same Sequence in Sūrat al-Ḥajj**

The parallel argument is set out in full where the Qur'an addresses resurrection directly: *“O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh —fully formed or unformed—to show ˹Our power˺ to you”* (22:5).

Both passages use origin as evidence, and both move from the first stage to the last without intermediary agents. Sūrat al-Ḥajj draws the conclusion the garden owner refused: the one who assembled a person once can assemble him again.

---

## Verse 18:38

> But as for me: He is Allah, my Lord, and I will never associate anyone with my Lord ˹in worship˺

**Lordship as the Ground of Worship**

The word *rabb* carries more than the English "Lord". It means the one who owns, sustains, and brings something to completion — the one responsible for a thing from its beginning to its end.

The word also answers the garden's ownership question in a single term. Whatever a man maintains, the one who sustained him from dust holds the prior claim, and the parable's dispute is settled by the vocabulary its characters use.

**What the Confession Costs the Man Who Makes It**

The declaration is easy to say and expensive to hold. The speaker has no property, no dependants, and no social standing; his refusal to associate partners with God has already cost him the position that the other man is boasting about.

The commentators draw out the consequence. Al-Qushayrī and al-Rāzī both note that a person who knows wealth and poverty come from the same source is grateful in plenty and patient in adversity, because neither state appears to him as a verdict on his worth.

The difference is not one of temperament but of foundation. Two men in the same conversation hold positions of unequal security, and the one with the weaker social standing has the stronger ground. That inversion is what the preceding verses commanded the Prophet ﷺ to respect.

**The Same Confession From the Youths of the Cave**

The parallel inside this sūrah is exact in structure: *“Our Lord is the Lord of the heavens and the earth. We will never call upon any god besides Him, or we would truly be uttering an outrageous lie”* (18:14).

---

## Verse 18:39

> If only you had said, upon entering your property, ‘This is what Allah has willed! There is no power except with Allah!’ Even though you see me inferior to you in wealth and offspring

**The Sentence That Should Have Been Said**

The companion identifies the omission precisely. On entering his garden, the owner should have said: *mā shāʾa Allāh, lā quwwata illā billāh* — this is what Allah has willed, and there is no power except with Allah.

The two clauses do different work. The first attributes what is seen to a will other than the owner's. The second denies that any power operates independently of that will. Together they restate the whole of the parable's theology in eight words.

The timing is specified too: *upon entering*. The formula is not a general creed to be recited sometimes but a response to a particular moment — the moment a person stands inside something impressive that belongs to him.

**No Power Except With God**

The second clause is one of the most repeated phrases in Islamic devotion, known as the *ḥawqalah*. It denies *ḥawl* — movement or change of state — and *quwwah* — the capacity to effect it — to anything apart from God.

The Prophet ﷺ gave the phrase a specific standing. Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī reported that the Prophet ﷺ heard him saying it and asked whether he should be told of a word that is one of the treasures of Paradise, then named this phrase (reported by al-Bukhārī and Muslim).

Its use here is not devotional decoration. The owner has just claimed strength in manpower and permanence in property; the clause denies both, and denies them in the abstract form that covers every future claim of the same kind.

**A Treasure of Paradise in the Ṣaḥīḥ**

The phrase the companion recommends has a documented place in the Prophet's ﷺ teaching. The report from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī is recorded in both Ṣaḥīḥ collections, and the description attached to it — a treasure of Paradise — places the *ḥawqalah* among the shortest and most highly valued of devotional formulas.

Read together, the two texts turn a narrative detail into a discipline: the sentence the rich man should have said is the sentence believers are taught to say.

---

## Verse 18:40

> perhaps my Lord will grant me ˹something˺ better than your garden, and send down upon your garden a thunderbolt from the sky, turning it into a barren waste

**The Word "Perhaps" as Theology**

The particle is *ʿasā* — perhaps, it may be. The companion does not promise himself a garden and does not claim to know what God will do.

The distinction is the sūrah's own. It has just instructed the Prophet ﷺ never to say *I will definitely do this tomorrow* without adding *if Allah so wills*, and the poor companion is the only character in the parable who speaks in that manner. The owner speaks in the forbidden register; the believer speaks in the commanded one.

**"Better Than Your Garden"**

The phrase deserves a moment on its own. The poor man does not ask for the same garden, or for its restoration, or for an equal share. He speaks of something better than it.

The wording shows that he has not accepted the owner's scale of value even while conceding his figures. A better thing need not be a larger garden; the Qur'an's descriptions of what God gives use categories the owner cannot count.

**The Same Reversal in Sūrat al-Qalam**

The parallel parable delivers the same ending by a different route. *“Indeed, We have tested those ˹Meccans˺ as We tested the owners of the garden—when they swore they would surely harvest ˹all˺ its fruit in the early morning”* (68:17).

In Sūrat al-Qalam the loss comes overnight to a group who had excluded the poor from their harvest; here it comes to one man who had excluded God from his accounting. Both gardens are destroyed, and in both cases the destruction follows a statement of certainty about the future.

---

## Verse 18:41

> Or its water may sink ˹into the earth˺, and then you will never be able to seek it out.”

**Water as the Qur'an's Test Case for Dependence**

Sūrat al-Mulk puts it as a question with only one possible answer: *“Say, ‘Consider this: if your water were to sink ˹into the earth˺, then who ˹else˺ could bring you flowing water?’”* (67:30). Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah makes the same move over the water people drink: *“Is it you who bring it down from the clouds, or is it We Who do so?”* (56:69).

**"You Will Never Be Able to Seek It Out"**

The final clause is the severest in the warning. The verb describes active searching, and the negation denies its success.

The word *ghawr* means depth, and the depth is the point. The water has not moved somewhere else; it has gone down, out of reach, into the ground that the gardens were planted in.

Both halves of the warning use the same grammatical form — a particle of possibility followed by a subjunctive. The companion says *perhaps* and *or*, not *will*.

The choice of water is not incidental to this sūrah or to the Qur'an generally. Water is the one provision no society has ever been able to produce, and the Qur'an uses it repeatedly to establish the point.

**The Same Question in Sūrat al-Mulk**

Sūrat al-Mulk poses it as a question addressed to everyone; Sūrat al-Kahf poses it as a warning addressed to one man who owns a river. The content is identical, and so is the implied answer.

Read together, the two passages show that the companion's threat was not a curse but a statement of fact about how gardens — and everything else — continue to exist.

---

## Verse 18:42

> And so all his produce was ˹totally˺ ruined, so he started to wring his hands for all he had spent on it, while it had collapsed on its trellises. He cried, “Alas! I wish I had never associated anyone with my Lord ˹in worship˺!”

**Wringing the Hands Over What Was Spent**

The gesture is physical and specific: he began to wring his hands. The Qur'an rarely records body language, and when it does the detail carries weight.
What he grieves over is stated precisely — *all he had spent on it*. The regret is about expenditure. Years of labour, irrigation work, trellis-building, and maintenance have gone, and the man is calculating the loss in the same currency he used to measure his superiority.
**Regret as the Qur'an's Measure of a Wasted Life**

The exclamation *alas* — *yā ḥasratā* — is the standard Qur'anic cry of someone who has understood too late. It appears at the moment of death, at the moment of judgment, and here at the moment of loss.
**The Same Cry in the Story of Qārūn**

The parallel is exact in structure and in outcome. *“Then We caused the earth to swallow him up, along with his home. There was no one to help him against Allah, nor could he even help himself”* (28:81).
The pairing is not accidental. Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ and Sūrat al-Kahf are treating the same error in two registers, and both close with the same statement about helplessness.

**What the Regret Was Made Of**

The verse is the moment the parable turns, and the words the man uses are worth reading closely. He does not say that he wishes he had saved his money or planted a second garden. He says he wishes he had not associated anyone with his Lord (18:42 — *“He cried, “Alas! I wish I had never associated anyone with my Lord ˹in worship˺!””*). The companion had told him to say that this is what God willed and that God is his Lord (18:39 — *“If only you had said, upon entering your property, ‘This is what Allah has willed! There is no power except with Allah!’ Even though you see me inferior to you in wealth and offspring”*), and the rich man had answered with a boast instead. The Book records his ruin with the words of his own confession, and it gives the reader one line of the conversation he should have had.

## Verse 18:43

> And he had no manpower to help him against Allah, nor could he ˹even˺ help himself

**"Against Allah" Fixes the Direction of the Loss**

The phrase *against Allah* is doing important work. It states that the event he is facing is not a natural accident but an act with an agent, and that no human force operates in the relevant direction.

The confusion is easy to make and the parable shows why. Inside the domain, the evidence for strength is overwhelming: the gardens stood, the harvest came, the household obeyed. Nothing in that experience prepares a person for the discovery that all of it was operating under a permission he had not noticed.

**No Protector Other Than Allah**

The Qur'an states the general rule in the same terms elsewhere: *“They will have no protector or intercessor other than Allah. Even if they were to offer every ˹possible˺ ransom, none will be accepted from them”* (6:70).

Sūrat al-Anʿām adds the element this verse leaves implicit — that the absence of help is not a temporary shortage but a permanent condition of the situation. No quantity of wealth can change it, which is exactly what the garden owner has just discovered at the cost of his estate.

---

## Verse 18:44

> At this time, support comes ˹only˺ from Allah—the True ˹Lord˺. He is best in reward and best in outcome

**Walāyah: Protection That Is Also Closeness**

The word translated as support is *walāyah*, from the same root as *walī* — guardian, protector, close ally. The term carries more than defence. It means nearness, attachment, and the standing of someone who belongs to you.

The Qur'an uses the same word for the believer's position: *“Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light”* (2:257). Protection in that sense is not something acquired but something entered into, and it does not lapse when circumstances change.

**Al-Ḥaqq: The True Set Against the False Claim**

The divine name attached here is *al-Ḥaqq* — the True, the Real. Some read it as an adjective qualifying protection rather than as a name of God, giving the sense *true protection belongs to God*.

**Best in Reward and Best in Outcome**

The double superlative closes the parable, and both terms are chosen against the owner's language. He had spoken of receiving something *better than this*; the verse speaks of God as *best* in reward and in what follows.

**The Guardianship Assigned Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The same assignment is made with an explicit contrast: *“Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light. As for the disbelievers, their guardians are false gods who lead them out of light and into darkness”* (2:257).

Sūrat al-Baqarah adds what Sūrat al-Kahf leaves to the narrative — that the two guardianships lead in opposite directions. The garden owner chose his own; the parable shows where it led.

---

## Verse 18:45

> And give them a parable of this worldly life. ˹It is˺ like the plants of the earth, thriving when sustained by the rain We send down from the sky. Then they ˹soon˺ turn into chaff scattered by the wind. And Allah is fully capable of ˹doing˺ all things

**Three Stages, and the Speed of the Third**

The stages are ordered with care. The rain comes down; the plants thrive, mixed and mingling with one another; then they turn to chaff. The middle stage is described with a word suggesting density and luxuriance, so that the first two movements build before the third.

**Chaff as the Qur'an's Standard Ending**

The same image appears wherever the Qur'an describes the fate of worldly achievement. Sūrat al-Zumar states it as an observation about agriculture: *“Do you not see that Allah sends down rain from the sky—channelling it through streams in the earth—then produces with it crops of various colours, then they dry up and you see them wither, and then He reduces them to chaff?”* (39:21).

Chaff is the right image because it is what remains when the useful part is gone. It is not destruction in the sense of annihilation; the material is still there, blowing across the ground. What has left it is value.

**The Same Image in Sūrat al-Ḥadīd**

The parallel is the fullest statement of the simile anywhere in the Qur'an: *“Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children. This is like rain that causes plants to grow, to the delight of the planters. But later the plants dry up and you see them wither, then they are reduced to chaff”* (57:20).

Sūrat al-Ḥadīd adds two things this verse leaves implicit — the delight of those who planted, and the list of activities that fill a worldly life. Both additions describe the garden owner exactly.

**The Garden Owner Who Forgot the Question**

The parable is placed directly after the story of the man with the two gardens. He had walked into his property in the middle of its fruit and said that he did not think it would ever perish, and that if he were returned to his Lord he would find something better there (18:35-36 — *“And he entered his property, while wronging his soul, saying, “I do not think this will ever perish”*). His companion answered him on the spot: if only, when you entered your garden, you had said, what God wills; there is no power except with God (18:39 — *“If only you had said, upon entering your property, ‘This is what Allah has willed!”*). This verse is the Book's comment on the whole scene: the garden is rain-fed greenery that turns to chaff and blows away. The Book makes the same point in the opening of the sūrah when it says the things of the earth are an adornment placed there for a test, and that they will be made barren dust (18:7-8 — *“We have indeed made whatever is on earth as an adornment for it, in order to test which of them is best in deeds”*). The owner of the gardens is not being told that his property was a crime; he is being told what it is made of.

**The One Who Scatters the Chaff Is the One Who Raises the Dead**

The verse ends with the words that carry the lesson from the garden to the grave: God is capable of all things. The Book uses the same move everywhere it describes the green world dying: the One who brought the dead earth to life with rain is able to bring the dead to life at the summons (36:33-35 — *“There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat”*; 30:50 — *“See then the impact of Allah’s mercy: how He gives life to the earth after its death! Surely That ˹same God˺ can raise the dead. For He is Most Capable of everything”*; 50:9-11 — *“And We send down blessed rain from the sky, bringing forth gardens and grains for harvest”*). Chaff blown by the wind is the Book's own picture of what happens to the pride of a man who forgot his Lord, and the ability that scattered it is the same ability that will gather the scattered bodies on the last day. The reader who takes the parable seriously is left with two facts: the greenery has no power to remain, and the One who ends it has no limit to His power.

---

## Verse 18:46

> Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope

**Why Children Are Counted With Wealth**

The pairing is standard in the Qur'an, and the reason is that both are held for the same purpose. Wealth buys security now; children extend it forward, providing labour, defence, and continuity.

The garden owner's boast made the connection explicit. He was richer in wealth and stronger in men, and the second claim rested on the first — dependants are maintained out of surplus. Verse 43 showed what happens when the surplus goes.

The Qur'an's own description of worldly life names the same pair as its engine: *competition in wealth and children* (57:20 — *“Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children.”*). The verse is not scolding people for wanting them. It is identifying what the competition consists of, and then asking what survives it.

**"The Everlasting Good Deeds"**

The Arabic phrase — *al-bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt* — pairs a plural of permanence with a plural of righteousness: the enduring things, the sound things. Nothing about the phrase specifies which deeds qualify, which is why the commentators have read it broadly.

The word choice also answers the previous verse precisely. The plants became chaff; these deeds do not become anything else.

**The Same Comparison in Sūrat Maryam**

The parallel verse uses nearly identical terms: *“And Allah increases in guidance those who are ˹rightly˺ guided. And the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in outcome”* (19:76).

Sūrat Maryam substitutes outcome for hope, which shifts the emphasis from what a person expects to what a thing ends in. Both formulations answer the same error — the assumption that what is visible now will still be standing later.

## Verse 18:47

> ˹Beware of˺ the Day We will blow the mountains away, and you will see the earth laid bare. And We will gather all ˹humankind˺, leaving none behind

**The Mountains as the Measure of the Permanent**

The Qur'an repeatedly describes mountains as the earth's stabilisers. *“He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you”* (16:15), and Sūrat al-Nabaʾ calls them pegs (78:7 — *“and ˹made˺ the mountains as ˹its˺ pegs”*) — the stakes that hold a tent in place.

That description explains why they are chosen here. In a world without steel or concrete, a mountain is the most permanent object a human being ever sees. Civilisations come and go inside the lifespan of a hill.

To describe the mountains as removed is therefore to say something stronger than that the earth will be destroyed. It is to say that the standard by which permanence is measured will itself be withdrawn.

There is a further irony in the choice. The garden owner measured his position against his companion; societies measure themselves against their monuments and their landscape. The verse takes the largest available measure and removes it, leaving nothing against which anyone can be counted as superior.

**Three Readings of the Removal**

The commentators divide over what the verse describes. Some take it as the mountains being taken off the earth; some as their being rendered nonexistent; some as their being lifted and driven through the air like clouds.

The third reading has textual support. Sūrat al-Naml describes the mountains as travelling like clouds while appearing firmly fixed (27:88 — *“Now you see the mountains, thinking they are firmly fixed, but they are travelling ˹just˺ like clouds.”*), and the image of something vast moving smoothly is difficult to dismiss.

Elsewhere the Qur'an supplies further descriptions that do not need to be harmonised into one event: the mountains crushed in a single blow (69:14 — *“and the earth and mountains will be lifted up and crushed with one blow”*), like carded wool (101:5 — *“and the mountains will be like carded wool”*), blown away (77:10 — *“and the mountains are blown away”*). The variety is a feature of the material rather than a problem in it. Each image conveys the same thing — that what was solid becomes something else — and none of them claims to be a technical account.

**"Laid Bare" and What Emerges**

The word translated as laid bare is *bārizah*, from a root meaning to come into clear view or to emerge. That etymology has produced two readings.

The first is topographical. With the mountains gone and every construction removed, the earth becomes an open plain with nothing obstructing sight. Some commentators extend this to trees and vegetation as well, leaving a bare surface.

The second reads the emergence as that of the dead from their graves. The earth becomes *bārizah* because what was inside it comes into view.

The first reading is the more natural fit for the verse's grammar, which describes what the observer sees of the earth rather than what happens inside it.

**The Same Removal in Sūrat al-Nabaʾ**

The parallel describes the event in a single image: *“and the mountains will be blown away, becoming ˹like˺ a mirage”* (78:20).

Sūrat al-Nabaʾ adds the comparison that Sūrat al-Kahf leaves implicit. A mirage looks like something solid and is nothing, which is the exact description the parable has just given of an estate believed to be permanent.

The two verses together close the sūrah's argument about permanence. The gardens were a mirage in a small field; the mountains are a mirage on the scale of the earth.

## Verse 18:48

> They will be presented before your Lord in rows, ˹and the deniers will be told,˺ “You have surely returned to Us ˹all alone˺ as We created you the first time, although you ˹always˺ claimed that We would never appoint a time for your return.”

**Rows as an Arrangement With No Rank**

The gathering is described with a single spatial term: they will be presented in rows. The commentators liken the formation to the rows of prayer, or to the ranks that form around the Kaʿbah during the pilgrimage, and some hold that each religious community occupies its own row.

Whatever the arrangement is meant to look like, its function is the same. A row is a formation in which everyone faces the same direction and nobody stands above anybody else. The image removes the vertical distinctions the Makkans had been drawing.

There is also a reading that takes the word as a single line rather than multiple rows, so that all are visible to one another.

**"As We Created You the First Time"**

Sūrat al-Isrāʾ puts the argument as a question and answer: *“Then they will ask ˹you˺, ‘Who will bring us back ˹to life˺?’ Say, ‘The One Who created you the first time’”* (17:51). Sūrat Yā-Sīn makes the same move (36:79 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being”*).

The second meaning is descriptive rather than argumentative. They arrive in the condition in which they were first created — alone, unaccompanied, holding nothing. Both senses are present in the wording, and both serve the verse.

The combination is what gives the phrase its weight. The one who made you can remake you, and what arrives at the remaking is you without your possessions. Neither half of that statement is comfortable, and the verse delivers both in six words.

**The Answer to Every Social Distinction**

Sūrat al-Anʿām states the arrival in full: *“˹Today˺ you have come back to Us all alone as We created you the first time —leaving behind everything We have provided you with. We do not see your intercessors with you—those you claimed were Allah’s partners ˹in worship˺. All your ties have been broken and all your claims have let you down”* (6:94).

**The Same Sentence in Sūrat al-Anʿām**

The fullest version of the address appears where the Qur'an describes the same moment: *“˹Today˺ you have come back to Us all alone as We created you the first time —leaving behind everything We have provided you with”* (6:94).

Sūrat al-Anʿām adds the detail this verse leaves to the imagination — that the things left behind were the very provisions given during life, and that the intercessors invoked are nowhere to be seen.

## Verse 18:49

> And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it. They will cry, “Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?” They will find whatever they did present ˹before them˺. And your Lord will never wrong anyone

**A Book That Is Not the Qur'an**

The book named here is not scripture. It is the record of a person's own acts, produced on the Day and given to that person to read.

The distinction is essential to the verse's force. A book of revelation is something a person receives and may accept or reject. A book of deeds is something a person has written without meaning to, and cannot argue with.

The Qur'an describes this record in several places and with consistent detail. It is written by *honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺*, who *know whatever you do* (82:11–12 — *“They know whatever you do”*), and it is bound to the person in advance: *“We have bound every human’s destiny to their neck. And on the Day of Judgment We will bring forth to each ˹person˺ a record which they will find laid open”* (17:13). The verse says the wicked are in fear *of what is in it*, and the commentators specify two objects of that fear: punishment from God, and disgrace before other people.

The second is the more uncomfortable of the two, and it explains why the verse places them in a gathered assembly rather than alone.

Nobody says *woe to us* over a private matter.

**Your Lord Will Never Wrong Anyone**

The closing clause is the verse's religious anchor. It follows directly from what precedes it, and the connection is not a pious appendix.

The judgment described is completely self-sourced. What people find in the record is what they did, and the Qur'an repeats the formula: punishment comes only for what their own hands sent forward (3:182 — *“This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation.””*; 8:51 — *“This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation.””*; 22:10 — *“˹They will be told,˺ “This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation.””*). No external standard is applied to them, and no debt is invented.

The clause therefore answers an objection the verse invites. A record that leaves out nothing could be described as merciless. The verse answers that mercy and justice are not in tension here, because the record contains nothing the person did not put there.

The Qur'an states the same formula to the punished themselves: *“This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation”* (3:182). The address is given to those receiving the penalty, which shows that the claim is meant to be heard by them rather than merely asserted about them.

**The Record Given in the Right Hand**

The same scene is described from the other side: *“As for those given their records in their right hand, they will cry ˹happily˺, ‘Here ˹everyone˺! Read my record!’”* (69:19).

The contrast is exact. The wicked fear what is in the book; the saved invite everyone to read it. Both groups are holding the same kind of document, and the difference between them is completely in its contents.

That pairing completes the verse's argument. The record is not an instrument of condemnation but a mirror, and the fear it produces is a measurement of a life rather than a verdict imposed on it.

## Verse 18:50

> And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who was one of the jinn, but he rebelled against the command of his Lord. Would you then take him and his descendants as patrons instead of Me, although they are your enemy? What an evil alternative for the wrongdoers ˹to choose˺

**A Story the Qur'an Has Already Told Six Times**

The command to prostrate before Adam appears in Sūrat al-Baqarah, al-Aʿrāf, al-Ḥijr, al-Isrāʾ, Ṭā-Hā, and Ṣād. This verse retells it in four lines.

What the summary keeps is the refusal and its characterisation. Everything else — the dialogue, the garden, the expulsion — is dropped, because none of it is needed for the point being made.

**Angel or Jinn: The Dispute and Its Grounds**

One position holds that the identification as a jinn explains the refusal. Angels do not disobey: they *do not speak until He has spoken, ˹only˺ acting at His command* (21:27 — *“who do not speak until He has spoken, ˹only˺ acting at His command”*). If Iblīs had been an angel he could not have refused; his refusal proves he was not one, and the exception in the verse is a grammatical exception rather than a membership of the group.

**Taking Him and His Offspring as Patrons**

The Qur'an explains what such patronage consists of: obedience. *“They have taken devils as their masters instead of Allah— thinking they are ˹rightly˺ guided”* (7:30). To take someone as a *walī* is to follow their direction, and the verse treats the following as the substance of the relationship.

**An Enemy Openly Declared**

The verse states the fact that makes the question rhetorical: they are your enemy. The Qur'an repeats this description of Satan throughout — a clear enemy, stated in al-Baqarah, al-Anʿām, al-Aʿrāf, Yūsuf, al-Isrāʾ, and al-Qaṣaṣ.

**The Same Exchange Described in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf**

Sūrat al-Aʿrāf adds the element that makes the exchange tragic rather than merely foolish — the belief that the choice was sound. Nobody takes an enemy as a protector while knowing him to be an enemy; the enmity has to be misdescribed first.

**What the Verse Adds to the Story It Has Told**

The command to prostrate before Adam appears in six other places, and this verse gives it in the fewest words. The additions here are two: the note that the angels bowed except Iblīs, and the sentence that Iblīs was one of the jinn and left his Lord's command. The commentators discussed the last point at length, since the account had placed him among the angels, and the sentence resolves the question the story raises: the refusal came from a creature who had a choice, and the Book names him by his kind so that the reader does not take him for a different order of being.
**The Warning the Verse Gives**

The sentence continues with a command that has been read as the sūrah's own instruction to its audience: will you then take him and his descendants as allies instead of Me, when they are enemies to you? The Book turns the story into a question about the reader's own life, and it does so in a sūrah that has just told the parable of the man who misled someone with a claim of knowledge (18:42 and the companion's correction — *“And so all his produce was ˹totally˺ ruined, so he started to wring his hands for all he had spent on it, while it had collapsed on its trellises. He cried, “Alas! I wish I had never associated anyone with my Lord ˹in worship˺!””*). The word used for the taking is the same one the Book uses for the patronage that a person gives to a power he trusts.
**The Evil Exchange**

The verse ends by naming the bargain: how evil is the exchange for the wrongdoers. The Book uses the word for a substitution several times, and in every case the thing given up is worth more than the thing taken. The Sūrah of the Cow names the same exchange at the beginning of the sūrah when it describes the people who sold the guidance they had for a price (2:16 — *“They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided”*), and the Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān reports the same word about the bargain the deniers make (3:177 — *“Those who trade belief for disbelief will never harm Allah in the least, and they will suffer a painful punishment”*). The story of Iblīs in this verse is placed so that the reader hears the question about his own alliances, and the answer the sūrah wants is the one the angels gave.

## Verse 18:51

> I never called them to witness the creation of the heavens and the earth or ˹even˺ their own creation, nor would I take the misleaders as helpers

**An Argument That Runs From Creation to Worship**

The verse is an argument, and its shape is compact. The ones being taken as patrons were not present at the creation of the heavens and the earth. What was not a partner in making the world cannot be a partner in receiving its worship.

The inference is one the Qur'an makes repeatedly. Sūrat al-Raʿd puts it as a question: *“Why ˹then˺ have you taken besides Him lords who cannot even benefit or protect themselves?”* (13:16). The gap between creating and being worshipped is treated as unbridgeable.

**"Not Even Their Own Creation"**

That identification removes the last ground of the practice. A being whose own creation it did not witness cannot be the source of anything for anyone else, and the worship offered to it has no object capable of receiving it.

**The Verse That Makes the Previous Question Answer Itself**

Read against the verse before it, this one functions as a proof rather than a separate point. The question was whether anyone would take Iblīs and his descendants as protectors instead of God. The answer is that the proposed protectors were not present at anything that matters.

**The Same Argument in Sūrat al-Raʿd**

The parallel states the reasoning at length: *“Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, ‘Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth?’ Say, ‘Allah!’ Ask ˹them˺, ‘Why ˹then˺ have you taken besides Him lords who cannot even benefit or protect themselves?’”* (13:16).

Sūrat al-Raʿd adds the point that Sūrat al-Kahf leaves implicit — that the partners cannot help themselves, let alone anyone else. Both passages move from creation to worship, and both treat the move as obvious.

## Verse 18:52

> And ˹beware of˺ the Day He will say, “Call upon those you claimed were My associate-gods.” So they will call them, but will receive no response. And We will make them ˹all˺ share in the same doom

**A Command Issued to Fail**

The scene opens with an instruction: call on the ones you claimed were God's partners. The command is given to people who know that calling will achieve nothing, and the speaker knows it too.

That is what makes the verse a rebuke rather than a test. A test is designed to discover something; this command is designed to display something already settled. The hearers are asked to do the thing they spent their lives doing, in front of everyone, at the moment when it can be seen to fail.

The Qur'an stages this scene repeatedly. Sūrat al-Anʿām: *“˹Consider˺ the Day We will gather them all together then ask those who associated others ˹with Allah in worship˺, ‘Where are those gods you used to claim?’”* (6:22). Sūrat al-Naḥl asks where the so-called associate-gods are, for whose sake the believers were opposed (16:27 — *“Then on the Day of Judgment He will humiliate them and say, “Where are My ˹so-called˺ associate-gods for whose sake you used to oppose ˹the believers˺?””*). Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ uses almost the same words as this verse (28:62 — *“˹Watch for˺ the Day He will call to them, “Where are those you claimed were My associate-gods?””*).

The recurrence across four sūrahs is unusual even by Qur'anic standards, and it indicates that the scene was central to the message rather than incidental to it.

**Why the Qur'an Returns to This Scene**

The repetition across several sūrahs is not redundancy. Each occurrence adds something: in al-Anʿām the question is asked, in al-Naḥl the humiliation is named, in al-Qaṣaṣ the partners are called for directly, and here the silence and the shared doom are recorded.

The cumulative effect is that the scene becomes familiar to the hearer long before it happens. The Qur'an rehearses the Day in advance so that the rehearsal can do its work in the present.

That is also why the scene is addressed to the Makkans in particular. They were the people for whom the invocation of partners was a daily practice, and the verse asks them to imagine performing it once, at the wrong time.

**The Partners Disown the Call**

The Qur'an supplies the other side of the exchange elsewhere. The beings invoked do not merely fail to answer; they deny the relationship. *“Those ˹misleaders˺ against whom the decree ˹of torment˺ is justified will cry, ‘Our Lord! These ˹followers˺ are the ones we caused to deviate. We led them into deviance, for we ourselves were deviant. We disassociate ourselves ˹from them˺ before You. It was not us that they used to worship’”* (28:63).

Sūrat al-Naḥl gives the shorter version: the gods answer, *“You are definitely liars”* (16:86).

That detail completes the verse's meaning. The silence in Sūrat al-Kahf is not absence but refusal, and the shared doom at the end of the verse is the outcome of a relationship in which neither party will acknowledge the other.

**The Disavowal in Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ**

The fullest statement of the disavowal appears where the same challenge is issued: *“˹Watch for˺ the Day He will call to them, ‘Where are those you claimed were My associate-gods?’”* (28:62), followed by the reply of those who led others astray (28:63 — *“Those ˹misleaders˺ against whom the decree ˹of torment˺ is justified will cry, “Our Lord! These ˹followers˺ are the ones we caused to deviate. We led them into deviance, for we ourselves were deviant. We disassociate ourselves ˹from them˺ before You. It was not us that they used to worship.””*).

Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ adds what Sūrat al-Kahf leaves in the silence — that the disavowal comes from the misleaders themselves, and that it includes a confession of their own deviation.

Read together, the two passages describe a transaction that ends with both sides denying it ever existed. The worshipper loses everything and the one worshipped loses the claim, and neither can be recovered on that Day.

## Verse 18:53

> The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it

**Sight Before Contact**

The verse orders three moments: they see the Fire, they know they will fall into it, and they find no way out. The seeing comes first, and the interval between seeing and falling is where the verse locates its weight.

**Ẓannū: Certainty or Apprehension**

The verb translated as *realize* is *ẓannū*, which in Arabic can denote firm conviction or mere supposition. The commentators divide accordingly.

The majority reading takes it as certainty: they know it is their destination. Al-Rāzī offers a second reading built on the word's weaker sense — they see the Fire from a distance, but hear its raging so intensely that it seems near, and they fear that they will fall in at that moment. Sūrat al-Furqān supplies the sound: *“Once it sees them from a distance, they will hear it fuming and growling”* (25:12).

Al-Rāzī's version also preserves something the majority reading flattens. The Fire is not yet upon them, and the terror comes from hearing it before seeing it clearly. The interval is filled with sound.

**"No Way to Avoid It"**

Elsewhere the Qur'an explains why. The wicked are not walking toward the Fire of their own motion; they are being driven. *“and drive the wicked to Hell like a thirsty herd”* (19:86), and *“Those who disbelieved will be driven to Hell in ˹successive˺ groups”* (39:71). A herd driven to water has no alternative route, and the comparison is exact.

**Driven in Groups in Sūrat al-Zumar**

The parallel describes the same movement with more detail: *“Those who disbelieved will be driven to Hell in ˹successive˺ groups. When they arrive there, its gates will be opened and its keepers will ask them: ‘Did messengers not come to you from among yourselves, reciting to you the revelations of your Lord and warning you of the coming of this Day of yours?’”* (39:71).

Sūrat al-Zumar adds the exchange at the gate, in which the warning they received is read back to them and acknowledged. Sūrat al-Kahf stops one step earlier, at the moment of seeing.

The two passages together describe the whole approach: driven, arriving, questioned, and answered by their own admission. Sūrat al-Kahf stops at the sight, which is the moment the sūrah's own warning has been pointing toward since its first verse.

## Verse 18:54

> We have surely set forth in this Quran every ˹kind of˺ lesson for people, but humankind is the most argumentative of all beings

**A Claim About What the Qur'an Contains**

The verse makes a statement about its own coverage: every kind of lesson, or parable, has been set out in this Qur'an for people. The claim is about variety rather than quantity — not every lesson, but every kind of lesson.

The Qur'an makes the same claim elsewhere in nearly identical words. *“And We have truly set forth every ˹kind of˺ lesson for humanity in this Quran, yet most people persist in disbelief”* (17:89). Sūrat al-Rūm (30:58 — *“We have certainly set forth every ˹kind of˺ lesson for people in this Quran.”*) and Sūrat al-Zumar (39:27 — *“We have certainly set forth every ˹kind of˺ lesson for people in this Quran, so perhaps they will be mindful”*) repeat it.

The consistency of the wording across four sūrahs indicates a settled claim rather than a rhetorical flourish. It is a statement about the sufficiency of what has been given, made in the context of people asking for more.

The claim is also falsifiable in a specific way. If a category of instruction were missing from the Qur'an, the claim would fail. The verse is not saying that every question is answered but that every kind of lesson has been supplied.

**"The Most Argumentative of Beings"**

The second clause is a superlative, and it is aimed at human beings as a class. Among all created things, this one argues most.

The Qur'an makes the same observation elsewhere with a sharper edge: *“He created humans from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Him˺”* (16:4). The contrast between the origin and the challenge is the point — the creature with the least independent standing is the one most given to dispute.

The contention described is not honest enquiry. It is argument deployed against a conclusion the arguer has already refused, and the verse distinguishes it from the questioning the Qur'an invites elsewhere.

The distinction is visible in the sūrah's own material. The cave youths asked each other how long they had stayed and were told that God knows best. The garden owner's companion asked a question that led somewhere. Neither is the contention described here.

**Two Halves That Belong Together**

The verse's structure is a claim and an obstacle. The message is complete; the hearer is contentious. Nothing is missing on the first side, which means the failure belongs completely to the second.

That pairing answers a recurring demand in the sūrah's context. The Makkans asked for signs — a garden, a house of gold, a ladder into the sky — as though the deficiency were in what had been given. This verse states that the deficiency is not there.

*“And no matter what sign you bring to them ˹O Prophet˺, the disbelievers will definitely say ˹to the believers˺, ‘You are only a people of falsehood’”* (30:58).

**The Same Claim in Sūrat al-Zumar**

The parallel is the briefest of the four: *“We have certainly set forth every ˹kind of˺ lesson for people in this Quran, so perhaps they will be mindful”* (39:27).

Sūrat al-Zumar supplies the purpose that Sūrat al-Kahf leaves to the second clause — mindfulness. The two verses together give both halves of the situation: the instruction is complete, and what it is for is stated.

Sūrat al-Isrāʾ adds the outcome in the same breath as the claim: the lessons are all there, and most people still refuse (17:89 — *“And We have truly set forth every ˹kind of˺ lesson for humanity in this Quran, yet most people persist in disbelief”*). The three verses agree on both halves, which is what makes the diagnosis in the second clause of this one so difficult to answer.

## Verse 18:55

> And nothing prevents people from believing when guidance comes to them and from seeking their Lord’s forgiveness except ˹their demand˺ to meet the same fate of earlier deniers or that the torment would confront them face to face

The verse is built as an exclusion. Nothing prevents belief when guidance arrives, and nothing prevents seeking forgiveness — except one thing, and the thing named is a demand the refusers make themselves.

**Waiting for the Wont of the Ancients**

The first thing they await is described as the *sunnah* of those who came before — the established way of dealing with earlier deniers, meaning the destruction that befell them.

The Qur'an treats the demand as reasonable in its expectation and ruinous in its outcome. God's way of dealing with such people does not change: *“Are they awaiting anything but the fate of those ˹destroyed˺ before? You will find no change in the way of Allah, nor will you find it diverted ˹to someone else˺”* (35:43).

**"Face to Face" and Its Alternative Readings**

The second thing they await is the torment coming *qubulan*. The word is read in three ways, and the commentators record all three.

The plain reading takes it as face to face — the punishment arriving visibly, in a form that can be seen. A second reading gives it the sense of suddenly, which matches the Qur'an's frequent description of divine punishment arriving without warning: *“which will take them by surprise when they least expect ˹it˺”* (26:202). A third reading, with different vowelling, gives the sense of something coming in the future.

The first reading is the strongest, because it pairs exactly with the first demand. They want the fate of the ancients, and they want to see it — the same demand expressed twice, once as history and once as sight.

The second reading is not far from it. Punishment that comes face to face is by definition punishment that comes without preparation, and the Qur'an describes divine punishment arriving in exactly that manner elsewhere (6:44 — *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair”*; 26:202 — *“which will take them by surprise when they least expect ˹it˺”*). A demand for the precedent will be met with the precedent.

Read against the parable of the two gardens, the two verses make the same point from opposite directions: destruction comes to the individual who claims permanence, and to the community that asks to be shown. Neither arrives early, and neither can be avoided once the time comes.

## Verse 18:56
> We do not send the messengers except as deliverers of good news and warners. But the disbelievers argue in falsehood, ˹hoping˺ to discredit the truth with it, and make a mockery of My revelations and warnings

**Two Functions and No Third**

The verse defines the office of the messengers by exclusion. They are sent for two purposes — to give good news and to warn — and the word *except* rules out anything else.

The formulation is standard in the Qur'an. *“Then Allah raised prophets as deliverers of good news and as warners, and revealed to them the Scriptures in truth to judge among people regarding their disputes”* (2:213). Sūrat al-Anʿām uses nearly the same sentence (6:48 — *“We have sent messengers only as deliverers of good news and as warners. Whoever believes and does good, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve”*), and Sūrat al-Nisāʾ states the purpose behind it (4:165 — *“˹All were˺ messengers delivering good news and warnings so humanity should have no excuse before Allah after ˹the coming of˺ the messengers.”*).

The pairing covers the whole of the message. Nothing a messenger brings falls outside good news or warning, and the verse's context is a demand for something beyond both.

The exclusion is stated with the particle *except*, which is the strongest available form. It does not say the messengers are mainly bearers of news and warning; it says they are sent for nothing else.

**What the Pairing Excludes**

They are not sent to compel belief, and Sūrat al-Baqarah makes the limit explicit to the Prophet ﷺ himself: *“We have surely sent you with the truth ˹O Prophet˺ as a deliverer of good news and a warner. And you will not be accountable for the residents of the Hellfire”* (2:119).

**Mockery as the Gravest Form of Denial**

The verse ends with mockery — taking the revelations and the warnings as something to laugh at. The commentators treat this as the worst kind of denial, and al-Rāzī reads it as the sign of a hardened heart.

The assessment is not arbitrary. Denial can coexist with respect; a person may reject a message while taking it seriously. Mockery removes the seriousness, and with it the possibility of reconsideration.

The Qur'an describes the same attitude elsewhere. *“And whenever they learn anything of Our revelations, they make a mockery of it. It is they who will suffer a humiliating punishment”* (45:9). Sūrat al-Māʾidah warns believers against those who have made faith a mockery and amusement (5:57 — *“O believers! Do not seek the guardianship of those given the Scripture before you and the disbelievers who have made your faith a mockery and amusement.”*).

**The Same Description Given of This Prophet**

The two functions are applied to the Prophet ﷺ specifically in several places. Sūrat al-Aḥzāb expands them slightly: *“O Prophet! We have sent you as a witness, and a deliverer of good news, and a warner”* (33:45), adding witnessing to the pair.

Other sūrahs describe the Prophet in the same terms: Sūrat al-Baqarah (2:119 states it — *“We have surely sent you with the truth ˹O Prophet˺ as a deliverer of good news and a warner. And you will not be accountable for the residents of the Hellfire”*), al-Aʿrāf (7:188 states it — *“Say, “I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the Will of Allah. If I had known the unknown, I would have benefited myself enormously, and no harm would have ever touched me. I am only a warner and deliverer of good news for those who believe.””*), Hūd (11:2 states it — *“˹Tell them, O Prophet,˺ “Worship none but Allah. Surely I am a warner and deliverer of good news to you from Him”*), al-Isrāʾ (17:105 states it — *“We have sent down the Quran in truth, and with the truth it has come down. We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a deliverer of good news and a warner”*), al-Furqān (25:56 states it — *“And We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a deliverer of good news and a warner”*) and Sabaʾ (34:28 states it — *“We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a deliverer of good news and a warner to all of humanity, but most people do not know”*).

The consistency shows that the definition in this verse is not a general principle imposed afterwards but the settled description of the office. A messenger who was asked to produce something outside it was being asked to be something else.

**The Purpose Stated in Sūrat al-Nisāʾ**

The reason for the arrangement is given where the same pairing appears: *“˹All were˺ messengers delivering good news and warnings so humanity should have no excuse before Allah after ˹the coming of˺ the messengers. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise”* (4:165).

Sūrat al-Nisāʾ supplies what Sūrat al-Kahf leaves implicit — that the two functions together remove the excuse. Good news without warning would not oblige anyone; warning without good news would leave nothing to accept.

Read beside this verse, the two passages explain both what the messengers do and why mockery of them is treated so severely.

## Verse 18:57

> And who does more wrong than those who, when reminded of their Lord’s revelations, turn away from them and forget what their own hands have done? We have certainly cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend this ˹Quran˺—and deafness in their ears. And if you ˹O Prophet˺ invite them to ˹true˺ guidance, they will never be ˹rightly˺ guided

**The Question the Qur'an Asks Everywhere**

The verse opens with a rhetorical question that appears throughout the Qur'an: who does greater wrong than a person who has been reminded of his Lord's revelations and then turns away from them?

Sūrat al-Sajdah poses it in nearly identical words: *“And who does more wrong than the one who is reminded of Allah’s revelations then turns away from them? We will surely inflict punishment upon the wicked”* (32:22). The same construction appears at 6:21 — *“Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His signs? Indeed, the wrongdoers will never succeed”*, 7:37 — *“Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His revelations?”*, 10:17 — *“Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His revelations? Indeed, the wicked will never succeed”*, 29:68 — *“And who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or reject the truth after it has reached them? Is Hell not a ˹fitting˺ home for the disbelievers”*, and 39:32 — *“Who then does more wrong than those who lie about Allah and reject the truth after it has reached them? Is Hell not a ˹fitting˺ home for the disbelievers”*.

The recurrence gives the question its force. It is not a comparison among sins in the abstract but a settled designation, applied wherever the Qur'an describes the refusal of a reminder already received.

The form is also efficient. A question of this kind does not require an answer, because the answer is contained in it. Nobody is greater in wrong than the person described, and the verse moves straight to the description.

**Veils Over the Hearts and Deafness in the Ears**

The second half of the verse states a condition: coverings placed over their hearts, so that they do not understand the Qur'an, and deafness in their ears.

The statement appears almost verbatim in Sūrat al-Anʿām, describing people who listen to the recitation without comprehending it (6:25 — *“There are some of them who ˹pretend to˺ listen to your recitation ˹of the Quran˺, but We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears.”*), and Sūrat al-Baqarah opens with the same image of sealed hearts and covered sight (2:7 — *“Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment”*).

The commentators have long discussed the relationship between the covering and the turning. The verse itself supplies the order: the turning away is described first, and the covering follows. The condition is not the cause of the refusal but its consequence, and the Qur'an's own arrangement settles the question.

The two images also cover the two channels of reception. The heart is where understanding happens and the ear is where the recitation arrives. Both are described as obstructed, which leaves the person described unable to receive the message by either route.

The final clause uses the particle of permanent negation. Even if the Prophet ﷺ calls them to guidance, they will not be guided — not now, not later, not by any further effort.

Sūrat al-Baqarah states the same conclusion from the other side: *“As for those who persist in disbelief, it is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe”* (2:6). The effort is not what is missing.

The clause is addressed to the one making the effort. Its function is to release the Prophet ﷺ from a responsibility he was carrying, and the sūrah had already told him to stay with those who wanted the guidance rather than looking past them.

**Why the Verse Follows the Description of Contention**

The preceding verse described people who argue with falsehood and mock the revelations. This verse identifies the condition underneath that behaviour.

The progression is diagnostic rather than merely condemnatory. Mockery is the symptom; the covering is the state; the turning away is the act that produced the state. The verse gives all three, in the order in which they occur.

That order is also the verse's warning. Nothing in it suggests that the covering arrives without warning or without a prior refusal.

Sūrat al-Sajdah adds the consequence in the same breath, while Sūrat al-Kahf supplies the mechanism — the covering and the deafness.

## Verse 18:58

> Your Lord is the All-Forgiving, Full of Mercy. If He were to seize them ˹immediately˺ for what they commit, He would have certainly hastened their punishment. But they have an appointed time, from which they will find no refuge

**What Would Happen If He Acted at Once**

The second clause describes an alternative that has not been taken. If God were to seize people for what they have earned, the punishment would already have arrived.

The Qur'an states the same hypothetical with a wider scope elsewhere. *“If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term”* (16:61). Sūrat Fāṭir repeats it almost word for word (35:45 — *“But He delays them for an appointed term.”*).

The scope in those verses is universal, which sharpens this one. The delay being described is not a favour to a particular group but the condition under which everyone is living, including those who have earned punishment.

**Delay as Both Mercy and Test**

The same interval functions as a test. A person who is not punished immediately is given the opportunity to conclude that nothing is wrong, and the Qur'an describes that conclusion repeatedly. Sūrat al-Anʿām names the mechanism: increase followed by a sudden seizure (6:44 — *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise, then they instantly fell into despair”*).

Both functions operate at once, and the verse leaves them in tension deliberately. The delay is genuine mercy and genuine risk, and which it becomes depends on what the person does with it.

**"No Refuge Beyond It"**

The final clause sets the limit. When the appointed time arrives, there is no place of refuge and no means of turning aside.

The wording echoes the description of the Fire earlier in the sūrah, where the wicked find no way of avoiding it. The two verses use the same idea in different registers — one about a people, one about a place — and both state the same finality.

The clause also answers the demand in verse 55. The people described there wanted the punishment to confront them face to face. This verse confirms that it will, and that the confrontation has a fixed date.

**The Same Delay in Sūrat Fāṭir**

Sūrat Fāṭir closes with God's sight rather than with the absence of refuge, which shifts the emphasis from escape to accountability. Both verses agree on the substance: the delay is real, the term is fixed, and the reckoning is certain.

Read with Sūrat al-Naḥl (16:61 — *“If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term. And when their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it”*), the three passages establish the delay as a settled feature of divine dealing rather than a local arrangement.

## Verse 18:59

> Those ˹are the˺ societies We destroyed when they persisted in wrong, and We had set a time for their destruction

**A Reference Back to Societies Already Named**

The verse opens with a demonstrative — *those* — pointing at societies the Qur'an has already described. Nothing new is narrated here; the verse refers back to accounts the audience has heard repeatedly.

The societies in question are the ones whose stories recur throughout the Makkan sūrahs: the people of Nūḥ, ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lūṭ, and the people of Madyan. Their destruction is described at 6:131 — *“This ˹sending of the messengers˺ is because your Lord would never destroy a society for their wrongdoing while its people are unaware ˹of the truth˺”*, 7:96 — *“Had the people of those societies been faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, We would have overwhelmed them with blessings from heaven and earth.”*, 11:100–102 — *“These are accounts, We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺, of the ˹destroyed˺ cities. Some are still standing ˹barren˺, while others have been mowed down”*, 28:59 — *“Nor would We ever destroy a society unless its people persisted in wrongdoing”*, and 46:26–27 — *“Indeed, We had established them in a way We have not established you ˹Meccans˺. And We gave them hearing, sight, and intellect. But neither their hearing, sight, nor intellect were of any benefit to them whatsoever, since they persisted in denying Allah’s signs. And ˹so˺ they were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule We certainly destroyed the societies around you after having varied the signs so perhaps they would return ˹to the Right Path˺”*.

The reference is therefore economical. A single clause summons a body of narrative the hearers already carry, and the verse uses it as evidence rather than recounting it again.

The method works because the stories were public knowledge in that community. The destroyed peoples were named in the Qur'an repeatedly, and their locations were known to traders who passed through them. A reference to them required no elaboration.

The verse's economy therefore carries a specific charge. It assumes the audience already knows the outcome, and asks them to locate themselves in the part of the story that precedes it.

**"When They Persisted in Wrong"**

The condition attached to the destruction is specific. These societies were destroyed for wrong they committed, and the verb means persistence rather than a single act.

That qualification matters, and the Qur'an states the rule explicitly elsewhere. *“Your Lord would never destroy a society until He had sent to its capital a messenger, reciting Our revelations to them. Nor would We ever destroy a society unless its people persisted in wrongdoing”* (28:59).

The two conditions together — a messenger sent and wrongdoing persisted in — define what the verse is describing. The destruction is not arbitrary and not sudden in the sense of unannounced.

**The Appointment Set for the Destruction**

The second clause uses the same word as the previous verse: a *mawʿid*, an appointed time, set for their destruction.

The parallel is deliberate. Verse 58 described an appointed time for the people being addressed; this verse states that the destroyed societies had one too. The arrangement is not a special case but the standing method.

The Qur'an makes the same point directly to the Makkans: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘A Day has ˹already˺ been appointed for you, which you can neither delay nor advance by a ˹single˺ moment’”* (34:30). The fixedness of the term is what the verse insists on.

The word also ties this verse to the garden owner, who denied that any appointment had been set for his return. He was wrong about his own case, and the destroyed societies are the evidence that the appointment is never merely rhetorical.

**The Grip in Sūrat Hūd**

The parallel describes the same event in force: *“Such is the ˹crushing˺ grip of your Lord when He seizes the societies entrenched in wrongdoing. Indeed, His grip is ˹terribly˺ painful and severe”* (11:102).

Sūrat Hūd adds the quality of the seizure that Sūrat al-Kahf leaves unstated, and uses the same condition — societies entrenched in wrongdoing. The two verses describe one action from two angles: the time fixed, and the grip that follows it.

Read together, they complete the warning the verse delivers. The appointment is set, and what arrives at the appointment is described. Sūrat al-Kahf supplies the term; Sūrat Hūd supplies the grip that meets it.

## Verse 18:60

> And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his young assistant, “I will never give up until I reach the junction of the two seas, even if I travel for ages.”

**"My Young Assistant" — Fatā**

The word translated as assistant is *fatā*, which means a young man or a youth as much as a servant. The commentators identify him as Joshua, son of Nun, and the identification is supported by the Prophetic account of the story.

The term's range is worth holding. The Prophet ﷺ instructed people to refer to those in their service as *my young man* or *my young woman* rather than *my slave* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2552). The word carries association rather than ownership, and it is the word used for a companion on a journey.

That fits the relationship described in the rest of the account. The young man travels with Moses, carries their provisions, and speaks to him plainly when he has something to report.

**The Junction of the Two Seas**

The destination is *majmaʿ al-baḥrayn* — the meeting place of the two seas. The Qur'an elsewhere describes two bodies of water, one fresh and palatable and the other salty and bitter, with a barrier between them that neither crosses (25:53 — *“And He is the One Who merges the two bodies of water: one fresh and palatable and the other salty and bitter, placing between them a barrier they cannot cross”*).

That description has led the commentators to identify the two seas with river water and sea water, and the junction with the place where a river meets the ocean. Various locations have been proposed, including the meeting of the Mediterranean with another body of water, and points as far apart as the western edge of North Africa.

What the description does establish is the character of the destination. A place where two kinds of water meet without mixing is a boundary, and the story that begins there is about a boundary of a different kind.

**Why the Location Does Not Matter**

The narrative supplies the reason Moses is travelling. In the Prophetic account of the story, Moses was asked who among the people was most learned and answered that he was. He was admonished for not attributing knowledge to God, and told that a servant of God at the junction of the two seas knew more than he did (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4725; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2380).

The destination is therefore a rendezvous rather than a place of interest. Moses is not travelling to see the junction; he is travelling to meet someone there, and the junction is the marker given to him.

**The Barrier Between the Two Seas**

The description of the two seas appears in full where the Qur'an discusses the signs of God's power: *“And He is the One Who merges the two bodies of water: one fresh and palatable and the other salty and bitter, placing between them a barrier they cannot cross”* (25:53).

Sūrat al-Furqān adds the feature that makes the image useful here — the two waters meet without mixing, held apart by something neither can cross. The junction is therefore a boundary as much as a meeting point.

That is the appropriate setting for a story about two kinds of knowledge that meet without merging, and the sūrah chooses it deliberately.

## Verse 18:61

> But when they ˹finally˺ reached the point where the seas met, they forgot their ˹salted˺ fish, and it made its way into the sea, slipping away ˹wondrously˺

**A Sign Given Before the Journey**

The verse assumes an instruction the Qur'an does not narrate. In the Prophetic account of this story, Moses was told to take a fish in a basket and that where he lost the fish, he would find the man he was travelling to meet (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4725; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2380).

That instruction explains everything in this verse. The fish is not travel food that happens to be lost; it is the marker by which the destination is to be recognised, and its loss is the arrival.

The Qur'an's compression is characteristic. It narrates the event and leaves the arrangement behind it to be recovered, which is why the companions' failure to notice matters so much in the verses that follow.

The arrangement also explains the fish's presence. Two travellers carrying a preserved fish looks like ordinary provision, and that appearance is what allows the sign to operate unnoticed. Nothing about the object announces that it is a coordinate.

The verse's first clause is exact: they reached the point where the seas met. They arrived. Nothing about the arrival is in doubt.

The verse says *they* forgot, in the plural, which puts both travellers at the rock in a state of inattention.

**The Forgetting Is Not an Accident**

The verse says plainly that they forgot their fish. The forgetting is not a mishap in the story but part of its mechanism.

The Qur'an treats forgetting as a serious matter elsewhere. Adam's covenant was broken by forgetting: *“And indeed, We once made a covenant with Adam, but he forgot, and ˹so˺ We did not find determination in him”* (20:115). Forgetting is where intention fails, and the sūrah has already legislated for it — if you forget, remember your Lord (18:24 — *“without adding, “if Allah so wills!” But if you forget, then remember your Lord, and say, “I trust my Lord will guide me to what is more right than this.””*).

Here the forgetting produces delay rather than loss. The destination is not forfeited; it is postponed, and the story spends two verses retrieving it.

The arrangement behind the verse is recorded in both Ṣaḥīḥ collections. Ubayy ibn Kaʿb reported that the Prophet ﷺ described Moses being told: *“At the junction of the two seas there is a slave of Ours who is more learned than you,”* and being given the fish as the means of finding him (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4725).

The report names the young man as Yūshaʿ ibn Nūn and preserves the detail that Moses felt no fatigue until he had passed the place he had been commanded to seek — which is exactly what this verse and the next describe.

Read with the verse, the ḥadīth supplies the instruction; the Qur'an supplies the failure to notice it.

## Verse 18:62

> When they had passed further, he said to his assistant, “Bring us our meal! We have certainly been exhausted by today’s journey.”

**Hunger as the Signal to Return**

Some commentators read the hunger itself as directed. On this reading God induced Moses' appetite to bring the fish to mind, and with it the place where the fish had been lost.

The reading has support in the verse's structure. The request for the meal is what prompts the assistant to remember, and the remembering is what turns them around. Without the hunger there is no question, and without the question there is no return.

Whether or not the hunger is understood as arranged, its function in the story is clear. An ordinary physical need becomes the mechanism by which a missed sign is recovered, and the verse places it immediately after the passing.

The sequencing is tight enough to look deliberate. They pass the place, and the next thing recorded is an appetite. The narrative does not fill the interval with travel; it moves straight from the missed destination to the request that will recover it.

**Weariness Named Only After the Junction**

The second half of the sentence reports exhaustion from the day's journey. The fatigue is stated here and nowhere before.

The Prophetic account makes the timing explicit: Moses felt no weariness until he had passed the place he was commanded to seek (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4725). The fatigue arrives with the passing, not before it.

**A Threshold With No Extension**

The commentators explain the oversight by describing the junction as a *barzakh* — a boundary place between two realms, which has no space of its own.

The sūrah uses the same idea elsewhere. The two seas meet with a barrier between them that neither crosses (25:53 — *“And He is the One Who merges the two bodies of water: one fresh and palatable and the other salty and bitter, placing between them a barrier they cannot cross”*), and a barrier is by definition something one passes through rather than arrives at. The junction is that kind of place.

**Retracing as the Sūrah's Own Remedy**

The sūrah has already legislated for exactly this situation. *“But if you forget, then remember your Lord, and say, ‘I trust my Lord will guide me to what is more right than this’”* (18:24).

The verse was given to the Prophet ﷺ about statements made and then forgotten. Here it describes a journey: a place reached, a sign missed, and a return made once the omission was noticed.

The parallel is not decorative. Both passages treat forgetting as recoverable rather than final, provided the person goes back.

## Verse 18:63

> He replied, “Do you remember when we rested by the rock? ˹That is when˺ I forgot the fish. None made me forget to mention this except Satan. And the fish made its way into the sea miraculously.”

**A Question That Is Not a Question**

The reply opens with *do you remember*, and the commentators note that it is not a genuine enquiry. The young man knows perfectly well that Moses did not see the fish escape.

The technique is economical. Rather than announcing that something remarkable happened, the speaker frames it as a recollection, which is how people actually deliver news they have been sitting on.

**The Report Delivered at Last**

What follows is the account the reader has been waiting for. The fish was forgotten at the rock, and it made its way into the sea.

The timing of the disclosure matters. The assistant had witnessed the escape earlier and had not mentioned it. The request for the meal is what brings it out, which means the story's central piece of information arrives because someone asked for lunch.

**The Rock as the Place of Rest**

The verse supplies a location: the rock where they had taken shelter. The reports identify it as a rock beside a spring, and the Prophetic account describes the two of them laying their heads on it and sleeping (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4725).

That is also why the rock is remembered while the junction was not. A rock is a visible object; a junction of two seas is a condition. People recall places and overlook situations.

**Forgetting and Remembering in the Sūrah's Own Terms**

The sūrah treats this pair as a moral mechanism rather than a mental event. *“But if you forget, then remember your Lord, and say, ‘I trust my Lord will guide me to what is more right than this’”* (18:24).

The instruction given to the Prophet ﷺ is precisely what happens here: the omission is noticed, and the correction follows. The young man remembers and reports; Moses recognises and returns.

The parallel places the episode inside the sūrah's wider teaching. Forgetting is not the end of the matter, provided the remembering arrives in time to change what happens next.

## Verse 18:64

> Moses responded, “That is ˹exactly˺ what we were looking for.” So they returned, retracing their footsteps

**Recognition Without Hesitation**

Moses' reply is immediate and complete. He does not ask where, or when, or how the fish escaped. He says that is what they were looking for.

The speed of the recognition indicates that he knew what the sign was. The Prophetic account confirms it: he had been told that where he lost the fish, he would find the servant he was seeking (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4725). The report from his assistant supplied the missing fact, and the conclusion followed at once.

**Above Every Knower Is One More Knowing**

The Qur'an states the principle this story illustrates. In the account of Joseph: *“We elevate in rank whoever We will. But above those ranking in knowledge is the One All-Knowing”* (12:76).

The commentators draw the connection explicitly. The Makkans had been told that they could test the Prophet's claims by asking about the youths of the cave; this account answers at a different level, by showing that even Moses did not hold all knowledge.

The rebuke is not stated, and it does not need to be. The account has already established the cost Moses was willing to pay, and the cost his audience was not. Sūrat Yūsuf gives the principle; Sūrat al-Kahf gives the case. A man sent with a revelation and a law discovers that another servant has been taught something he has not, and travels until he finds him.

The two passages together answer the question the story raises. Knowledge is distributed, and the distribution is not a measure of rank.

## Verse 18:65

> There they found a servant of Ours, to whom We had granted mercy from Us and enlightened with knowledge of Our Own

**A Servant, Not a Name**

The Qur'an does not name the man Moses travelled to find. He is described as a servant from among God's servants, and that description is all the text gives.

The name comes from the Prophetic account, in which the man is identified as al-Khiḍr (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4725; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2380). The commentators are unanimous in the identification, and it rests on that report rather than on the verse.

It also protects the account from becoming a story about a famous man. The reader is given a servant with two gifts and nothing else, and everything that follows is judged on that basis alone.

**"A Mercy From Us"**

The first thing said about him is that he was granted a mercy from God. The commentators read this as prophethood, or as a spiritual perfection that leaves a person unencumbered by base matter.

The word's breadth is the point. A mercy is not a skill or an achievement; it is something conferred. The verse describes both of the man's distinguishing features as gifts, and neither as acquired, which removes any suggestion that his standing was earned by effort.

The phrasing also echoes the prayer of the cave youths earlier in the sūrah: *“Our Lord! Grant us mercy from Yourself and guide us rightly through our ordeal”* (18:10). The same construction, the same source, and in both cases the mercy is what makes what follows possible.

**"Knowledge of Our Own"**

The second gift is knowledge from God's presence — *ʿilm min ladunnā*. The phrase means knowledge given directly rather than transmitted through a teacher.

The Qur'an sets the limit that makes this remarkable. In the Verse of the Throne: *“no one can grasp any of His knowledge—except what He wills ˹to reveal˺”* (2:255). Knowledge of the unseen belongs to God alone: *“None in the heavens and the earth has knowledge of the unseen except Allah”* (27:65).

What this man holds is therefore a portion granted by will. The Sufi commentators built a vocabulary on the phrase, and al-Ghazzālī and others used *ʿilm ladunī* for knowledge arriving by unveiling rather than by study. Junayd of Baghdad described the same thing as lights unveiled from the recesses of the unseen.

The vocabulary matters because it preserves a distinction the verse makes. Acquired knowledge grows with effort; this kind arrives, and the difference is why Moses had to travel rather than study. The parallel links the two accounts by their vocabulary.

Both describe people given something they could not have produced, and both make the gift the ground of everything that follows.

## Verse 18:66

> Moses said to him, “May I follow you, provided that you teach me some of the right guidance you have been taught?”

**"Right Guidance" — Rushd**

The word Moses uses for what he wants to be taught is *rushd* — sound judgment, right guidance in affairs. It is not a request for information but for the capacity to act correctly.

The same word appears in the prayer of the cave youths at the start of the sūrah, who asked God to grant them mercy from Himself and to guide them rightly through their ordeal (18:10 — *“˹Remember˺ when those youths took refuge in the cave, and said, “Our Lord! Grant us mercy from Yourself and guide us rightly through our ordeal.””*). Both requests are for direction rather than knowledge in the abstract.

The choice of word also fits the account's subject. What Moses will witness are acts whose rightness is not apparent, and what he is asking for is the guidance that would let him see them correctly.

Al-Rāzī connects this verse to the occasion of the sūrah. The Makkans had been advised by the Jews to test the Prophet ﷺ by asking him about the youths of the cave; the account that follows shows a far greater test being passed in the opposite direction.

Moses — a prophet with a revealed law — travels indefinitely and then asks to be taught by a man he has never met. The people being addressed were refusing to learn from the messenger already standing among them.

**The Etiquette of the Sentence**

The request is phrased with a conditional: may I follow you, on the condition that you teach me. The condition is not a demand but a statement of purpose.

That matters for what follows. Khiḍr's reply will set his own condition, and Moses will accept it. The exchange establishes a relationship in which both parties state terms, and the disciple's terms are stated first and most gently.

The Qur'an instructs the Prophet ﷺ in the same direction: *“Exalted is Allah, the True King! Do not rush to recite ˹a revelation of˺ the Quran ˹O Prophet˺ before it is ˹properly˺ conveyed to you, and pray, ‘My Lord! Increase me in knowledge’”* (20:114).

Both passages treat the desire for more knowledge as the correct disposition, and neither treats existing knowledge as a reason to stop asking.

The request therefore carries no claim of its own, and the verses that follow test exactly that absence of claim.

---

## Verse 18:67

> He said, “You certainly cannot be patient ˹enough˺ with me

**The Emphatic Negation**

The particle used is *lan*, which denies the possibility permanently rather than for the present. It is the same particle the garden owner used when he said he would never associate a partner with God, and the same one the poor companion used in his confession.

**A Statement, Not a Refusal**

Khiḍr does not say no. He states a fact about what will happen: you will not be able to bear patiently with me.

The distinction matters. A refusal would end the journey; a prediction leaves it open, and the verses that follow show Moses being accepted as a companion on conditions. The man is describing an outcome rather than declining a request.

The form also respects the person addressed. Moses is not told that he is unworthy or incapable in general. He is told what will happen if he comes, and the information is given before he commits.

The strength of the negation is what makes the verse a foreshadowing. This is not a doubt about Moses' temper but a settled statement that the companionship cannot last, and the story spends eleven verses proving it.

The certainty is also honest. Khiḍr is not managing expectations or testing resolve. He is stating what he knows about the situation the two of them are about to enter.

**A Foreshadowing of the Parting**

The verse predicts the ending. At the close of the account Khiḍr says that this is the parting of their ways and offers the explanation Moses could not bear patiently (18:78 — *“He replied, “This is the parting of our ways. I will explain to you what you could not bear patiently”*).

The certainty is what allows the account to be gentle. Nobody is surprised, nobody is trapped, and the parting at the end is the fulfilment of a statement both men heard before they set out.

The verse is repeated almost verbatim after the first act: *“Did I not say that you cannot have patience with me?”* (18:72), and again after the second with a stronger form (18:75 — *“He answered, “Did I not tell you that you cannot have patience with me?””*).

---

## Verse 18:68

> And how can you be patient with what is beyond your ˹realm of˺ knowledge?”

**The Reason Given for the Prediction**

The previous verse stated that Moses would not be able to bear patiently. This verse explains why, and the explanation is about knowledge rather than temperament.

The connection is causal. Patience is not being asked of Moses in the abstract; it is being asked of him in the face of acts whose meaning he does not have. The verse identifies the absence of knowledge as the thing that makes patience impossible.

**"What You Do Not Encompass"**

The Qur'an uses the same verb to set the limit on all creatures. In the Verse of the Throne: *“no one can grasp any of His knowledge—except what He wills ˹to reveal˺”* (2:255). The encompassing belongs to God, and what creatures hold is what has been released to them.

**The Limit Is Not a Defect**

The Qur'an states the general rule plainly: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘None in the heavens and the earth has knowledge of the unseen except Allah. Nor do they know when they will be resurrected’”* (27:65). Knowledge of hidden consequences is not available to prophets as such.

**The Question's Form**

The exchange is therefore a negotiation between two accurate statements. Khiḍr is right that the patience will fail; Moses is right that he wants to come. The conditions in the following verses are how the two are reconciled.

**The Limit in the Verse of the Throne**

The general statement of the principle appears in the Qur'an's most quoted verse: *“no one can grasp any of His knowledge—except what He wills ˹to reveal˺”* (2:255).

Sūrat al-Baqarah gives the rule; Sūrat al-Kahf gives the case in which a prophet encounters it in person. Both agree that the exception is determined by God's will and not by the seeker's effort.

Read together, they explain why Moses had to travel. The knowledge he wanted could not be reached by study, because it was of the kind that is given.

---

## Verse 18:69

> Moses assured ˹him˺, “You will find me patient, Allah willing, and I will not disobey any of your orders.”

**A Promise With the Correct Qualification**

Moses accepts the warning and answers it with a commitment — and with the phrase the sūrah itself had legislated. *You will find me patient, if God wills.*

Forty-six verses earlier the sūrah instructed the Prophet ﷺ never to say of anything that he would definitely do it tomorrow without adding *if Allah so wills* (18:23–24 — *“And never say of anything, “I will definitely do this tomorrow,””*). Here a prophet makes exactly that qualification, unprompted, in a promise about his own future conduct.

**Two Commitments in One Sentence**

The reply contains two undertakings. He will be patient, and he will not disobey in any matter.

The two cover different failures. Patience addresses the internal reaction — the objection rising before it can be spoken. Obedience addresses the external act — following where the teacher goes and doing what he is told.

Moses undertakes both, and the story will show him keeping the second while failing the first. He never refuses to go anywhere; he speaks when he should not have.

The split is informative. Obedience about actions is easier to hold than patience about perceptions, because an action can be restrained by decision while a reaction arises before decision arrives.

**Why the Qualification Matters Here**

The phrase does more than observe a rule. It acknowledges the exact thing Khiḍr has just predicted.

Khiḍr said Moses would not be able to bear patiently. Moses does not contradict him; he says he will be found patient *if God wills*, which leaves the outcome where the warning put it. The qualification is a concession dressed as a promise.

---

## Verse 18:70

> He responded, “Then if you follow me, do not question me about anything until I ˹myself˺ clarify it for you.”

**Why the Rule Is Necessary**

The phrase about clarifying is matched by the phrase about explaining, and nothing is left outstanding. Every act Moses questioned receives an account, including the one he did not live to question a second time.

Khiḍr accepts the request and sets a single term. The companionship is granted, and the condition attached to it is silence.

The verse makes sense only in light of what is coming. A man is about to hole a boat carrying passengers and kill a boy. Any person of conscience would object, and Moses is a prophet of the Law.

Without the condition the journey would end at the first act. The rule is what makes three acts possible, and three acts are what the explanation at the end requires — a boat, a life, and a wall, covering property, a person, and a legacy.

The condition's second half is fulfilled exactly as stated. *“This is the parting of our ways. I will explain to you what you could not bear patiently”* (18:78), followed by the explanation of all three acts.

---

## Verse 18:71

> So they set out, but after they had boarded a ship, the man made a hole in it. Moses protested, “Have you done this to drown its people? You have certainly done a terrible thing!”

**The Protest Is Correct on Its Terms**

Moses' objection is not a failure of nerve or a lapse in manners. By the law he carries, the act is a grave wrong, and the Qur'an states that law plainly: killing a soul, except for a soul or for corruption in the land, is as though one had killed all people (5:32 — *“That is why We ordained for the Children of Israel that whoever takes a life—unless as a punishment for murder or mischief in the land—it will be as if they killed all of humanity; and whoever saves a life, it will be as if they saved all of humanity.”*).

A man carrying that law cannot watch a ship being holed beneath its passengers and say nothing. The protest is what his prophethood requires, and the story never suggests otherwise.

**"A Terrible Thing"**

The word translated as terrible carries weight — an act of grave seriousness, a monstrous thing. It is the strongest term available, and Moses uses it at the first act.

The escalation across the account is measured in these words. The boat provokes *a terrible thing*; the killing provokes *a horrible thing*; the wall provokes a mild complaint about a fee. The objections get stronger as the acts get harder to justify and then suddenly drop away.

**The Law in Sūrat al-Māʾidah**

The standard Moses is applying is stated in the Qur'an's own terms: *“We prescribed for the Children of Israel that whoever kills a soul—unless for a soul or for spreading corruption in the land—it is as if he had killed all of humanity”* (5:32).

Read against this verse, Moses' protest is not excessive. It is the correct application of a law he was given, to a situation he cannot yet see whole.

That gap is what the whole narrative exists to display, and this verse is where it first becomes painful.

---

## Verse 18:72

> He replied, “Did I not say that you cannot have patience with me?”

The rebuke returns after the second act with an addition: *“Did I not tell you that you cannot have patience with me?”* (18:75).

**The Warning Read Back**

Khiḍr's reply contains no new information. He quotes his own warning from five verses earlier and puts it to Moses as a question.

The form is significant. A question of this shape does not invite an answer; it records that a prediction has been confirmed. The man is not arguing with Moses about the ship. He is noting that the condition has been broken.

The economy of the reply is striking. One sentence, eleven words, and the whole exchange is closed without either party retreating from his position.

The brevity also protects the arrangement. A longer answer would have to justify the hole, and justifying it would break the condition about timing. The rebuke is the only response available to a man who has promised to explain later.

The extra word marks an escalation. The first rebuke refers to something said; the second refers to something told, which is a stronger claim on Moses' memory and a sharper note of the repetition.

---

## Verse 18:73

> Moses pleaded, “Excuse me for forgetting, and do not be hard on me.”

**Forgetting in the Sūrah's Own Vocabulary**

The sūrah has been working with this word since its early verses. The travellers forgot their fish at the rock, and the forgetting delayed the meeting. The assistant attributed his silence to Satan. Here Moses attributes his objection to the same lapse.

**The Same Plea the Believers Make**

The wording recalls the prayer the Qur'an puts in the mouths of believers: our Lord, do not take us to task if we forget or make a mistake (2:286 — *“Do not punish us if we forget or make a mistake.”*).

Classification matters because it determines what happens next. A deliberate breach would end the companionship at once; an acknowledged lapse leaves it open, which is exactly what the following verses show.

The second request is about burden. Moses asks not to be made to carry difficulty on account of what he has done.

The recurrence is not coincidence. The account is about knowledge and its limits, and forgetting is the most common way a person loses hold of what he knows.

The sūrah's remedy is also on record: if you forget, remember your Lord (18:24 — *“without adding, “if Allah so wills!” But if you forget, then remember your Lord, and say, “I trust my Lord will guide me to what is more right than this.””*). Moses does the human equivalent — he remembers the condition and asks to be allowed to continue under it.

**The Same Plea in Sūrat al-Baqarah**

The believers' prayer in Sūrat al-Baqarah asks that forgetting and error not be held against them (2:286 — *“Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford.”*), and the Qur'an records the prayer as answered.

Sūrat al-Kahf shows the same plea in a narrative setting, made by a prophet to a teacher, and granted in silence. The two passages agree that an unintentional lapse is a different category from a deliberate one.

The agreement matters for the account's judgment of Moses. He is not being measured as a man who broke a promise; he is being measured as a man who could not hold it.

The distinction is what allows the account to end with an explanation rather than a dismissal. A teacher does not open the teaching to a student he has condemned.

---

## Verse 18:74

> So they proceeded until they came across a boy, and the man killed him. Moses protested, “Have you killed an innocent soul, who killed no one? You have certainly done a horrible thing.”

**A Protest Stronger Than the First**

Moses' wording escalates along with the act. The ship provoked *a terrible thing*; this provokes *a horrible thing*, and the question is framed with more directness — have you killed a pure soul?

The escalation shows that the protests are not reflexive. Moses assesses each act and grades it, and the grading tracks the gravity of what he sees.

That matters for the account's fairness to him. A man who objected identically to everything would be merely reactive; a man who distinguishes between damaging a boat and killing a child is exercising judgment.

The grading also shows that Moses is not simply repeating himself. Each objection is freshly reasoned, which makes the repeated rebuke harder to dismiss as a teacher losing patience with a tiresome student.

**The Law Moses Carries**

The standard is the one stated in Sūrat al-Māʾidah: a soul may not be killed except for a soul or for corruption in the land, and to kill otherwise is as though one had killed all people (5:32 — *“That is why We ordained for the Children of Israel that whoever takes a life—unless as a punishment for murder or mischief in the land—it will be as if they killed all of humanity; and whoever saves a life, it will be as if they saved all of humanity.”*).

By that standard the act has no defence available on its appearance. There is no prior killing, no corruption, no judgment, no witness. A prophet of the Law watching it has one possible response.

**The Explanation Deferred to Verse Eighty**

The account withholds the reason here and supplies it at the end: the boy's parents were believers, and there was a fear that he would drive them to defiance and disbelief (18:80–81 — *““And as for the boy, his parents were ˹true˺ believers, and we feared that he would pressure them into defiance and disbelief”*).

The verse is therefore complete without its explanation, and incomplete in exactly the way the story intends.

---

The killing of the boy is explained twice later in the sūrah: the servant says that he feared the child would burden his believing parents with rebellion and disbelief, and that God would replace him with one better in purity and mercy (18:80–81 — *““And as for the boy, his parents were ˹true˺ believers, and we feared that he would pressure them into defiance and disbelief”*). The commentators held that the child's death was not a punishment for a crime but a cutting short of harm by the one who gave the life, and the sūrah's whole account is built on the difference between what Moses could see and what the servant knew. The same principle is argued in the sūrah of the Remembrance, where the killing of children for fear of poverty is forbidden (17:31 — *“Do not kill your children for fear of poverty. We provide for them and for you. Surely killing them is a heinous sin”*), so the verse's event is presented as a specific revelation to the servant of the unseen rather than as a general permission.

## Verse 18:75

> He answered, “Did I not tell you that you cannot have patience with me?”

**The Same Rebuke With an Addition**

The reply repeats the earlier rebuke almost word for word, with one change: *did I not tell you* rather than *did I not say*.

The addition is small and does real work. Saying something states it; telling someone something directs it at them. The second rebuke claims not merely that a prediction was made but that it was addressed to Moses personally, which makes the breach harder to treat as an oversight.

The Qur'an's precision here is typical. A single verb carries the escalation, and no further comment is needed.

The change also shifts the ground of the rebuke. The first pointed at a statement Moses had heard; the second points at an instruction given to him personally, which makes the second breach harder to classify as a lapse of memory.

**The Sub-Narrator's Note**

The Prophetic account of this story preserves a comment on the verse. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, transmitting the report, noted that the second blame was stronger than the first (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4725).

The observation confirms that the escalation was noticed by the earliest readers of the account. It is not a modern inference drawn from the wording; it is recorded in the transmission itself.

The note also identifies where the escalation sits — in the blame rather than in the act, which is the same judgment the verse's grammar makes.

The agreement between the transmission and the text is worth holding. The Qur'an's wording and the earliest reading of it point the same way, which removes any suspicion that the escalation is a modern inference.

**The Third Objection in Verse Seventy-Seven**

The pattern completes at the wall. *“They asked them for food, but the people refused to give them hospitality. There they found a wall ready to collapse, so the man set it right. Moses protested, ‘If you wanted, you could have demanded a fee for this’”* (18:77).

---

## Verse 18:76

> Moses replied, “If I ever question you about anything after this, then do not keep me in your company, for by then I would have given you enough of an excuse.”

**A Limit Set by the One Who Broke the Rule**

The verse records Moses imposing a consequence on himself. He does not ask for another chance, offer a further explanation, or appeal to the value of what he is learning. He states the terms under which the companionship should end.

The wording is conditional rather than promissory. He does not say *I will not question you again*; he says that if he does, the arrangement is over. The distinction is honest, and the account will show why it mattered.

**A Commitment Made in the Sūrah's Own Terms**

The sūrah legislated for exactly this kind of statement earlier. *“And never say of anything, ‘I will definitely do this tomorrow,’ without adding, ‘if Allah so wills!’”* (18:23–24).

Moses' formulation here does not carry the qualification, and the verse that follows shows why that matters. He commits to a future conduct in unconditional terms, and the commitment fails within a few verses.

**The Same Instruction Given Earlier in the Sūrah**

The rule about future statements appears with its remedy attached: *“without adding, ‘if Allah so wills!’ But if you forget, then remember your Lord, and say, ‘I trust my Lord will guide me to what is more right than this’”* (18:24).

Sūrat al-Kahf supplies both the legislation and the narrative case, and the two illuminate each other. The qualification exists because people do not know what they will be able to bear, and the account of Moses shows the point without commenting on it.

Read together, the passages explain why the formula was revealed and what happens in its absence.

---

## Verse 18:77

> So they moved on until they came to the people of a town. They asked them for food, but the people refused to give them hospitality. There they found a wall ready to collapse, so the man set it right. Moses protested, “If you wanted, you could have demanded a fee for this.”

**The Act Is Constructive**

The third act reverses the pattern of the first two. A boat was damaged and a boy was killed; here a wall about to fall is set right.

The verse describes the wall's condition precisely — ready to collapse — and the repair as a straightening. Nothing is added or embellished. The act is a repair, done without announcement and without payment.

**An Objection in the Form of a Statement**

Moses does not ask a question. He says: if you wanted, you could have taken a fee for it.

The wording shows how a prohibition shapes speech. A man who cannot ask frames his objection as an observation, and the narrative accepts the framing while counting the substance.

The observation is not a condemnation of Moses. It identifies why the third breach ends the companionship when the first two did not. The earlier protests could be understood as a prophet of the Law discharging his function; this one is closer to a complaint about circumstances.

---

**The Town and the Wall**

The three companions walk into a town and ask its people for food, and the verse reports what happened without softening it: they were refused. Hospitality in that country was not a courtesy but a duty, and the refusal was a sentence on the town's character. It is in that town, after that refusal, that the servant notices a wall about to fall and sets it straight with his own hands. Moses' objection is not about money alone; it is the plain human reaction that a man who has just been told to leave should not be given a job for nothing.
**What the Wall Was Holding**

The reason arrives at the end of the account, and it is the reason the reader is not given yet: the wall belonged to two orphan boys in the town whose father had been a righteous man, and under it lay a treasure they were to inherit; the repair guarded their inheritance until they were grown (18:82 — *““And as for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and under the wall was a treasure that belonged to them, and their father had been a righteous man.”*). The reader who has been walking with Moses is asked to hold two facts that could not both be seen at the time — an inhospitable town, and a wall that belonged to orphans inside it. The lesson the account draws is not that kindness is owed only to the kind. It is that a person sent by God may be doing work whose beneficiaries he will never meet, and whose reasons will not be shown to him until the account is complete.

## Verse 18:78

> He replied, “This is the parting of our ways. I will explain to you what you could not bear patiently

**What the Explanation Presupposes**

Read together, the two passages explain why Moses had to travel and why he had to wait. The meaning he wanted belonged to a category that is given, at a time chosen by the one who gives it.

**Taʾwīl and What the Word Claims**

The word used for the explanation is *taʾwīl* — the interpretation, the inner meaning, the way a thing turns out. In the Qur'an it appears in this sense and in Sūrat Āl ʿImrān, where the *taʾwīl* of certain verses is said to be known only to God (3:7 — *“Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt through their ˹false˺ interpretations—but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah.”*).

The claim embedded in the word is large. If acts have a *taʾwīl*, then events are not exhausted by their appearance, and the appearance is not the standard by which they should finally be judged.

Al-Kāshānī draws a conclusion from the fact that the explanation is given at all. Moses is able to receive the *taʾwīl*, which means he has been prepared for it; the encounter with what was veiled from him is now possible.

The Qur'an uses the word where it distinguishes between clear and ambiguous verses, noting that the *taʾwīl* of the latter is known to none but God (3:7 — *“Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt through their ˹false˺ interpretations—but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah.”*).

Sūrat Āl ʿImrān gives the rule; Sūrat al-Kahf gives a case in which a servant is granted a portion of it. Both agree that this knowledge is not produced by effort and is not available on demand.

---

## Verse 18:79

> “As for the ship, it belonged to some poor people, working at sea. So I intended to damage it, for there was a ˹tyrant˺ king ahead of them who seizes every ˹good˺ ship by force

**The Same Rule in the Sūrah's Own Terms**

The verse states a principle the sūrah has already applied. The garden owner's estate was destroyed because he counted it as permanent; here a boat is damaged so that it will not be taken permanently. Both turns depend on a fact the owner could not see.
The connection is not forced. Sūrat al-Kahf is a sūrah about the difference between what appears and what is, and this verse is its clearest single case — a harm performed to prevent a greater harm, judged by two people with different information.
The verse also answers the objection without retracting it. Moses said the act endangered the people aboard, and on the information he had, it did. The explanation adds a fact rather than correcting a judgment, which is the most the account ever offers.
That is why the parting follows the explanation rather than preceding it. Once the facts are given, there is nothing left to dispute and nothing left to learn in that company, and the account moves at once to the second case.
The explanation therefore answers the objection without retracting the act, and the account holds the two together.

**The First of the Three Explanations**

The passage from 18:79 to 18:82 — *““And as for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and under the wall was a treasure that belonged to them, and their father had been a righteous man. So your Lord willed that these children should come of age and retrieve their treasure, as a mercy from your Lord. I did not do it ˹all˺ on my own. This is the explanation of what you could not bear patiently.””* is the servant's account of what he had done, and it comes after Moses had objected to each act. The first explanation concerns the boat: it belonged to poor men who worked on the sea, and there was a king behind them seizing every vessel by force. The damage was done on purpose to save the boat, and the owner's loss was real. The second and third explanations follow the same pattern: the boy's parents were believers and the child would have led them into rebellion (18:80-81 — *““And as for the boy, his parents were ˹true˺ believers, and we feared that he would pressure them into defiance and disbelief”*), and the wall belonged to two orphan boys in a town whose people had refused them hospitality, with a treasure under it and a righteous father (18:82 — *““And as for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and under the wall was a treasure that belonged to them, and their father had been a righteous man.”*).
**What the Passage Teaches**

The three acts were not random hardship. Each was an arrangement made from knowledge that Moses did not have, and the servant says so in the closing words: this is the interpretation of what you could not bear (18:82 — *““And as for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and under the wall was a treasure that belonged to them, and their father had been a righteous man. So your Lord willed that these children should come of age and retrieve their treasure, as a mercy from your Lord. I did not do it ˹all˺ on my own. This is the explanation of what you could not bear patiently.””*). The scholars drew a rule from the boat's case about harm done to prevent a greater harm, and they drew a larger lesson from the passage as a whole: God's knowledge of what a thing will become is not available to the person living through it. The sūrah places the story after the parable of the two gardens and before the account of Dhū al-Qarnayn, and the three stories make one argument: what a person can see is not the whole of what is happening.

## Verse 18:80

> “And as for the boy, his parents were ˹true˺ believers, and we feared that he would pressure them into defiance and disbelief

**The Explanation the Account Does Not Soften**

The second explanation is the hardest of the three, and the verse makes no try to make it easier. It states the parents' faith, states the fear, and stops.

There is no mitigation, no appeal to a greater good in general terms, and no softening of the fact that a child was killed. The verse gives a reason and leaves the reader holding both the reason and the act.

**What the Verse Claims**

The verse asserts that a child's death prevented two believers from losing their faith. It does not argue for the claim, compare it with alternatives, or acknowledge that the parents might have endured.

The sūrah has already established the value it is measuring against. Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are better in reward and in hope (18:46 — *“Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope”*).

The verse applies that scale to a case where the two terms collide. Children are among the adornments, and faith is among the things that endure; the account describes a situation in which one was taken so that the other would survive.

---

## Verse 18:81

> So we hoped that their Lord would give them another, more virtuous and caring in his place

**The Exchange Stated as the Reason**

The verse completes the explanation of the second act. What was feared is named in the previous verse; what was hoped for is named here, and the two together constitute the whole account of why the boy was killed.

**Two Qualities Answering Two Fears**

The replacement child is described with two terms, and each answers one of the two outcomes feared in the previous verse. *More virtuous* answers defiance; *nearer in mercy* answers disbelief.

The word for mercy is also the word the sūrah has used throughout for what God gives. The child hoped for is described in the same vocabulary as the servant found at the junction and the mercy sought by the youths in the cave.

**What the Parents Could Not Know**

That asymmetry is the hardest feature of the explanation. The good accomplished is real and the knowledge of it is withheld from the people who bore the cost. Ibn Kathīr connects the verses to the Prophetic saying recorded by Ṣuhayb: *“How wonderful is the affair of the believer, for his affairs are all good... If something bad happens to him, he bears it with patience and that is good for him”* (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2999).

**Judging by the Outward**

Al-Rāzī grounds the distinction in a report he attributes to the Prophet ﷺ: we judge by the outward, and God takes charge of the secrets. The authenticated form of the saying is ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb's: *“We only judge now what is manifested outwardly of your deeds. Whoever shows us good, we will trust him and bring him close. It is not for us to judge anything of his inner secrets. Allah will hold him accountable for his inner secrets”* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2641).

The principle is the same in both forms, and it explains the whole exchange. Moses judged by the outward because that is what a judge of the Law has; Khiḍr acted on what the outward did not contain.

Al-Rāzī draws the conclusion that the trajectory of religious knowledge runs from mastery of the Law toward knowledge of the realities of things, and that Moses was sent to Khiḍr after perfecting the first.

---

## Verse 18:82
> “And as for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and under the wall was a treasure that belonged to them, and their father had been a righteous man. So your Lord willed that these children should come of age and retrieve their treasure, as a mercy from your Lord. I did not do it ˹all˺ on my own. This is the explanation of what you could not bear patiently.”

**The Wall and the Treasure Under It**

The third deed is the wall. The servant rebuilt a wall in a town whose people had refused them hospitality, and the reason he gives is that two boys there had a treasure buried beneath it, and that their father had been a righteous man. The verse names the father's quality and nothing else about him. He was not wealthy, not powerful and not from a famous house, and that one quality is the reason two orphans received a repaired wall.

The commentators divide over what the treasure was. Most take it as wealth, buried for the boys to find when they grew up. Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī record the view that it was knowledge, stored for them to reach when they were able to carry it. On either reading the point holds, since the wall kept something safe for children who could not yet protect it.

**A Righteous Parent and Children Under Care**

This verse is the one the teachers of the community quote when they speak about the children of righteous parents being protected. The Book does not say that the boys were righteous. It says their father was, and the safeguard reaches them through him. That is a mercy that extends past one lifetime, and it is the kind of thing the sūrah's account of the unseen has been showing all along: what looks like an accident of timing is an arrangement for people who could not arrange it themselves.

The other two deeds work the same way. The ship belonged to poor men who worked the sea, and it was damaged so that a king seizing every sound vessel would leave it alone (18:79 describes it — *““As for the ship, it belonged to some poor people, working at sea.”*). The boy was taken so that his parents, both believers, would not be burdened by a son who would grow into rebellion, and they were promised a better child in his place (18:80 — *““And as for the boy, his parents were ˹true˺ believers, and we feared that he would pressure them into defiance and disbelief”* and 18:81 — *“So we hoped that their Lord would give them another, more virtuous and caring in his place”* describe it). In each case the outward damage carried an inward rescue.

**How the Story Reached the Community**

The Prophet ﷺ told this account to his people in his own words, and the fullest of the reports is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4727, where Moses is asked who is the most learned of people, answers that it is himself, and is told that there is a servant of God with knowledge he does not have. That report tells the reader why the sūrah places the story here: it is not a tale about a mystery man but a lesson delivered by the Prophet to a community that had just asked him about the people of the cave and the traveller who reached the ends of the earth.

**Judging the Outward and Knowing the Inward**

Al-Rāzī draws a principle from the account. Prophetic judgement works on what people can see, while the reasons behind events lie with God. The authenticated saying of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb states the rule for human judgement in general: we judge only by what appears outwardly, and we are not charged with what is hidden, while God holds a person accountable for his inner secrets (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2641 records it). Al-Rāzī then concludes that religious knowledge moves from mastery of the law toward knowledge of the realities of things, and that Moses was sent to learn the second kind only after he had completed the first.

**The Word Mercy at the Close**

The verse ends by naming what the three deeds were: a mercy from your Lord. The wall was the least dramatic act of the three, and the one whose reason was hardest to see. Its inner sense was that two boys would grow up without losing what their father had left them, and the man who rebuilt it was a servant given knowledge by God rather than a planner working from his own judgement, as the same verse says: and I did not do it on my own command (18:82 states it — *““And as for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and under the wall was a treasure that belonged to them, and their father had been a righteous man.”*).

The three deeds followed one pattern. An outward harm, an inward mercy, and a servant who could see what his companion could not. The servant had asked at the outset whether Moses could bear patiently what he had not yet taken in with knowledge (18:68 asks it — *“And how can you be patient with what is beyond your ˹realm of˺ knowledge?””*), and the three events were the answer to that question. The reading of a life is not available from the outside, and the verse closes not with a general moral but with a mercy, a wall, and the words of the one who had been shown the reason.

---
## Verse 18:83

> They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Ⱬul-Qarnain. Say, “I will relate to you something of his narrative.”

**The Third of the Three Questions**

The sūrah's occasion supplies the frame. The Makkans, advised by Jewish scholars, put three tests to the Prophet ﷺ: the youths of the cave, a traveller who reached the east and the west, and the nature of the spirit. This verse answers the second.

The wording records the question as a live one. *They ask you* is present tense, and the answer is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ in the second person with an instruction to speak. The verse is not a narrative introduction but a response to an actual enquiry.

Some reports hold that people of the Book came to the Prophet ﷺ directly to ask about Dhū al-Qarnayn. The sūrah's opening verses address the same audience, and the placement here completes the set of answers begun with the cave.

**The Name: He of the Two Horns**

The title *Dhū al-Qarnayn* means literally the one of the two horns, and the commentators offer a range of explanations with varying grounds.

Some report that he had what appeared to be two horns on his head, or that the sides of his head were of copper, or that he wore a horned headdress. Others read the two horns as the two extremities of the earth — east and west — which he is said to have circled. A further reading takes the title as marking his rule over both Rūm and Persia, and a last group treats it simply as an epithet for courage.

The geographical reading has the strongest claim from the narrative itself, since the account that follows describes journeys to the setting and the rising of the sun. The physical readings rest on reports without support in the text, and none of them explains why a ruler would be titled after his own anatomy.

**The Same Restraint in the Sūrah's First Answer**

The account of the cave youths was given with the same limitation. The sūrah narrated what it narrated and then stated that only a few know their number, and instructed the Prophet ﷺ not to consult anyone about them (18:22 — *“Only a few people know as well.””*).

Both answers refuse the completeness the questioners were testing for, and both state the refusal openly. The pattern identifies what the sūrah treats as revelation: a given account, bounded, and not expandable by enquiry.

Read together, the two responses answer the same test in the same way, and the third question in the set is answered elsewhere in the Qur'an in the same terms.

---

## Verse 18:84

> Surely We established him in the land, and gave him the means to all things

**Established in the Land**

The verse opens with what God did rather than with what the man did. He was established in the earth — given a footing, made secure in it, able to move through it.

The second gift is expressed with a single word: *sabab* — a means, a way, a cause by which a thing is reached. He was given a *sabab* to everything.

Most take it to include knowledge of things, and al-Rāzī adds the power and the instruments by which what he wanted could be obtained.

**The Same Attribution in the Parable of the Gardens**

The sūrah's earlier parable used identical grammar for a much smaller gift: *“And We gave one of them two gardens of grapevines, surrounding them with palm trees, and placing crops between them”* (18:32).

Read together, they answer the question the seventh verse posed. The adornment is a test, and the two men are its two possible outcomes.

The verse describes two gifts separately. God established him in the land, and God gave him the means to all things. The first concerns position; the second concerns capacity.

---

## Verse 18:85

> So he travelled a course

**The Shortest Verse in the Sūrah**

The verse is three words in English and barely more in Arabic. It records a single movement and stops.

The sūrah uses the same technique elsewhere. Short verses mark transitions and long verses carry content, and the alternation gives the narrative its rhythm. Here the shortest possible statement of travel precedes the most detailed description of a journey's end in the whole account.

The verb is built on the same root as the word used in the previous verse. He was given a *sabab* to all things, and here he follows a *sabab*.

The repetition ties the endowment to its use. The means given are not abstract capacity but an actual route, and the verse describes a man travelling along what he was provided rather than making his own way.

The word's range is worth holding. A *sabab* is a rope, a means, a cause, a way of reaching something. A traveller following a *sabab* is following a line laid down for him, and the narrative will use the same verb twice more.

---

## Verse 18:86

> until he reached the setting ˹point˺ of the sun, which appeared to him to be setting in a spring of murky water, where he found some people. We said, “O Ⱬul-Qarnain! Either punish them or treat them kindly.”

**A Choice Offered From Outside**

The verse's second half is a direct address: O Dhū al-Qarnayn, either punish them or treat them kindly. The options are given to him rather than devised by him.
The delegation is also complete. Nothing in the wording limits the punishment or defines the kindness; both are left to his judgment, which is the largest discretion the narrative grants him anywhere.
Others hold that the instruction came through inspiration rather than revelation, which would leave his status as a righteous ruler rather than a prophet.
**What the Choice Reveals About His Rule**

The two options define the character of the authority he holds. Punishment is available, and so is kindness, and the decision between them is his.
The sūrah has described authority in these terms before. The servant at the junction acted on command and said so explicitly: I did not do it on my own (18:82 — *““And as for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and under the wall was a treasure that belonged to them, and their father had been a righteous man. So your Lord willed that these children should come of age and retrieve their treasure, as a mercy from your Lord. I did not do it ˹all˺ on my own. This is the explanation of what you could not bear patiently.””*).
Dhū al-Qarnayn occupies the same position at a larger scale. He holds the earth and its means, and the first decision the narrative records him making is one whose options were given to him from outside.

**What the Servant of God Saw**

The verse is the first stage of the journey of Dhū al-Qarnayn, and it is told in three stages: the far west, the far east, and the pass between two mountains (18:86-93 — *“until he reached ˹a pass˺ between two mountains. He found in front of them a people who could hardly understand ˹his˺ language”*). At the western end he found the sun setting in a dark muddy spring, as the eye sees it, and he found a people living there. The Book does not describe the shape of the earth or the mechanics of the sunset; it describes what the traveller reached, and the sūrah's earlier verse about the sun running to a place appointed for it (36:38 — *“The sun travels for its fixed term. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing”*) states the same event in the plainest way. The commentators who took the word literally were reading a report of a sight, and those who explained it as the horizon were doing the same. What matters for the passage is that he arrived at the edge of the known world and found people there.
**The Choice He Was Given**

The verse ends with the command: either punish them or treat them kindly. It is the Book's statement about rulers. A man given the power to travel and conquer was not told what verdict to give; he was told that the verdict would be his responsibility. The next two verses record his answer, and the answer is the model the sūrah is after: the wrongdoer punished, and the one who believes and does good told that he will be treated with ease (18:87-88 — *“As for those who believe and do good, they will have the finest reward, and we will assign them easy commands.””*). The sūrah's questions about Dhū al-Qarnayn came from the People of the Book (18:83 — *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Ⱬul-Qarnain. Say, “I will relate to you something of his narrative.””*), and the passage answers them with a ruler whose justice the Book approves.

## Verse 18:87

> He responded, “Whoever does wrong will be punished by us, then will be returned to their Lord, Who will punish them with a horrible torment

**The First Branch of the Answer**

Dhū al-Qarnayn responds to the choice with a statement of policy rather than a decision about a particular group. Whoever does wrong will be punished by us.

The form is general and conditional. No one is named, no sentence is pronounced in advance, and the outcome is made to depend on what the people do after the call reaches them.

**A Ruler Who Does Not Claim the Final Word**

The comparison is implicit and unflattering to the ruler's own power. What he can do is limited and temporary; what follows is neither.

That is the account's portrait of legitimate authority. A man given the means to all things describes his own penalties as the lesser of two, and assigns the greater to someone else.

**The Contrast With the Garden Owner**

The sūrah has already recorded a man describing his own position in the largest available terms: I do not think I see anything better than this, and I do not think the Hour will ever come (18:35–36 — *“nor do I think the Hour will ˹ever˺ come. And if in fact I am returned to my Lord, I will definitely get a far better outcome than ˹all˺ this.””*).

Dhū al-Qarnayn's first speech does the opposite. He asserts a limited authority and affirms the return to God and the punishment that follows it. The two statements are the sūrah's paired measures of what a person says when he holds power.

---

## Verse 18:88

> As for those who believe and do good, they will have the finest reward, and we will assign them easy commands.”

**The Second Branch of the Policy**

The verse completes the answer begun in the previous one. The wrongdoer is punished; the one who believes and does good receives the finest reward and an easy command.

The pairing is the standard Qur'anic formula. Belief and righteous action are joined throughout the Book as the condition of reward, and the verse uses the same construction a ruler would use in stating his policy to a new people.

The form is conditional in both branches. Neither outcome is assigned in advance; both depend on the response the people make to the call.

The construction also puts the two groups on the same footing. Neither is favoured before the invitation reaches them, and the difference between them arises completely afterwards.

The phrase translated as the finest reward is *al-ḥusnā* — the most beautiful, the best. The commentators read it in two ways.

The first takes the beauty as a quality of the reward itself: the reward they receive is the best available. The second reads it as a description of the deed rewarded — the finest deed, which some identify with the testimony of God's oneness and others with believing and doing good together.

**The Same Formula Across the Qur'an**

The pairing of belief and righteous deeds as the condition of reward appears throughout the Book, and the sūrah has already used it. Those who believe and do good will have the Gardens of Paradise as an accommodation is stated later in this same chapter, and the same formula occurs at 2:25 — *“Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow.”*, 3:57 — *“And as for those who believe and do good, they will be rewarded in full. And Allah does not like the wrongdoers.””*, 4:57 — *“As for those who believe and do good, We will admit them into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there for ever and ever.”*, and elsewhere.

**The Reward Described Elsewhere in the Sūrah**

The sūrah has already described what the finest reward consists of: bracelets of gold, green garments of fine silk, and reclining on canopied couches (18:31 — *“There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold, and wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade, reclining there on ˹canopied˺ couches.”*).

That economy is typical of the sūrah. Its terms are established once and then reused, and a reader who has followed the chapter recognises the phrase without further explanation.

The verse describes the reward for faith and good action as the easier path. Not a greater reward, not a higher station — an easier way.

The choice of reward is significant in a chapter about wealth. The garden owner measured his standing by what he had; this verse measures the believer's by what he no longer has to struggle with.

---

## Verse 18:89

> Then he travelled a ˹different˺ course

**A Second of Three Identical Statements**

The verse repeats the formula of verse 85 almost exactly. He followed a means. Three words, no destination, no description of the journey.

The repetition is not careless. The account uses the same sentence to open each of the three journeys, and the sameness is what allows the destinations to stand out.

A reader encountering the phrase for the second time recognises it as a marker. The narrative is signalling a transition rather than narrating travel, and the recognition makes the transition instantaneous.

**The Means Followed Again**

The word is the same one used for what he was given: a *sabab*, a means, a way of reaching something. He was given means to all things, and here he follows a means again.

The repetition ties the endowment to its use across the whole account. Nothing in the three journeys is accomplished by resources outside what was provided, and the narrative keeps saying so in the same word.

The phrase reappears unchanged for the third time, and its stability is what marks the verse as a hinge rather than as another stage of travel.

---

## Verse 18:90

> until he reached the rising ˹point˺ of the sun. He found it rising on a people for whom We had provided no shelter from it

Some commentators hold that Dhū al-Qarnayn was given the same instruction about this people as he received at the western point — to punish or to treat kindly.

**A People Defined by Exposure**

The verse identifies this people by what they lack rather than by what they are. No city, no number, no custom — only the absence of shelter from the sun.

The wording *We had provided no shelter* uses the same construction the Qur'an uses for natural provision generally. Sūrat al-Naḥl describes shade and shelter among the things God made: *“And Allah has made for you shade from what He has created, and He has made for you shelters in the mountains”* (16:81).

The parallel identifies what this people lack as a normal part of provision. Shelter is not an achievement but a gift, and its absence is described as a withholding.

**The Only People Described by What They Lack**

Every other people in this section is identified by a place or an action. This one is identified by an absence: they had nothing to shelter them from the sun.

The same idea appears elsewhere in the Qur'an, where God is described as making shade and shelters in the mountains (16:81 — *“And Allah has provided you shade out of what He created, and has given you shelter in the mountains.”*). The verse here records the opposite condition, and the contrast is what makes the encounter worth telling.

The verse identifies a people by a single deprivation, and the deprivation is shelter from the sun. Not poverty, not weakness, not distance from God — exposure.

---

## Verse 18:91

> So it was. And We truly had full knowledge of him

**The Same Framing in the Sūrah's Opening**

The sūrah began by describing a Book with no crookedness in it, made straight so that it might warn of a severe punishment from God (18:1–2 — *“All praise is for Allah Who has revealed the Book to His servant, allowing no crookedness in it”*). The knowledge claimed there is the same knowledge asserted here.

**A Journey Closed in Four Words**

The verse ends the second journey in the briefest terms available. So it was. Nothing is added about the eastern people, no exchange is recorded, and no decision is reported.

The compression is striking after the detailed account of the western encounter. There, a choice was offered and a policy was stated across three verses; here the arrival is noted and the narrative moves on.

A ruler given the means to all things is encompassed by it, and the account says so in four words. The narrative's largest figure is placed inside the same frame as its smallest.

That is the verse's function. It closes a survey of the earth by restating who knows it.

The economy is not carelessness. The western encounter required a decision and received several verses; the eastern one required nothing and received a clause. The narrative allocates its words according to what each case demands.

---

## Verse 18:92

> Then he travelled a ˹third˺ course

**What the Repetition Signals**

Three identical openings before three different arrivals produce a specific effect: the journeys feel interchangeable and the destinations do not.

That is the distribution the account wants. The travel is routine; what is found is not. By the third repetition the reader has stopped attending to the movement completely, which is exactly the state the narrative needs before introducing the people who will ask for a barrier.

The verse is therefore doing preparation rather than narration. It clears the ground so that the encounter can occupy it.

The signal is therefore structural rather than descriptive. The formula marks the boundary between episodes, and the reader learns to treat it as a divider rather than as information.

The construction appears at verse 85, at verse 89, and here, and each instance is followed by a verse describing what was found.

The verse's brevity is what makes that marking work. Had it been longer, the arrival would not stand out against it.

The account's economy is therefore a rhetorical instrument. Three words of travel, and then a people who can barely speak asking a ruler to build them a barrier — the disproportion is the point.

---

## Verse 18:93

> until he reached ˹a pass˺ between two mountains. He found in front of them a people who could hardly understand ˹his˺ language

**A People Who Could Hardly Understand Speech**

The second clause describes the people by a deficiency: they could barely understand speech. The commentators read it in two ways.

Despite the description, the people speak to Dhū al-Qarnayn in the following verse and he answers them. The commentators offer two explanations: God gave him the ability to understand their language, as He gave Solomon the language of the birds (27:16 — *“And David was succeeded by Solomon, who said, “O people!”*), or an interpreter was present.

The verse does not resolve the question, and the narrative does not dwell on it. The exchange happens and the account moves on.

**The Third Arrival and Its Difference**

The first two journeys ended with observations: a people in the west, a people in the east. This one ends with a people who speak.

The difference is the point. The account has covered the earth and found two nations it merely describes; the third is the one that addresses the traveller, and the narrative expands to accommodate the exchange.

The Qur'an describes speech itself as a gift rather than an attainment. Sūrat al-Raḥmān lists the teaching of speech among the first things given to the human being, and Sūrat al-Naml records Solomon given the language of birds as a specific endowment (27:16 — *“We have been taught the language of birds, and been given everything ˹we need˺.”*).

The parallel identifies comprehension as something conferred. A people who can barely understand speech are not deficient in a moral sense; they are at the edge of a capacity that varies between them.

Read against that, the verse's description is a fact about circumstance, and the account uses it to measure the effort behind the request that follows.

---

## Verse 18:94

> They pleaded, “O Ⱬul-Qarnain! Surely Gog and Magog are spreading corruption throughout the land. Should we pay you tribute, provided that you build a wall between us and them?”

The people begin with what is happening to them: Gog and Magog are spreading corruption in the land. The complaint comes before the request, and the request is framed as a question.

**Yaʾjūj and Maʾjūj: Who They Are**

The names are Yaʾjūj and Maʾjūj, rendered in English as Gog and Magog. The commentators trace them to the line of Japheth, son of Noah, and the same names appear in the Hebrew scriptures and the New Testament as forces of destruction — Gog of the land of Magog in Ezekiel, and the nations gathered at the end in Revelation.

**"Spreading Corruption in the Land"**

The term used is the Qur'an's standard word for corruption on earth, which covers both moral ruin and violent wrongdoing. The commentators explain it concretely: the reports describe Gog and Magog devouring people and consuming the resources of the land, and the people as having suffered slaughter at their hands.

**A Future Unleashing**

The Qur'an mentions Gog and Magog elsewhere in a Last-Day context. Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ describes the barrier holding *“until ˹after˺ Gog and Magog have broken loose ˹from the barrier˺, swarming down from every hill, ushering in the True Promise”* (21:96–97).

---

The wall is built against the two peoples named in the sūrah's own account, and the Book refers to them again at the end of the same sūrah with the promise that they will be let loose on the Day of Judgement and mingled together (18:98–99 — *“On that Day, We will let them surge ˹like waves˺ over one another. Later, the Trumpet will be blown, and We will gather all ˹people˺ together”*), and in the sūrah of the Prophets, where the barrier is described as being breached and the disbelievers pouring out (21:96 — *“until ˹after˺ Gog and Magog have broken loose ˹from the barrier˺, swarming down from every hill”*). The people's offer of tribute is the sūrah's way of showing that the threat was real enough to pay against, and the ruler's refusal of payment — stated in the next verse — is the mark of a different kind of power: the barrier is built as a mercy, not as a contract for revenue.

## Verse 18:95

> He responded, “What my Lord has provided for me is far better. But assist me with resources, and I will build a barrier between you and them

**The Payment Refused**

The reply declines the tribute in its first clause. What my Lord has established me in is better. The ruler does not negotiate the amount or defer the decision; he states that he does not need what they are offering.

Al-Rāzī and Ibn Kathīr both note the parallel with Solomon's answer to the gifts sent by the Queen of Sheba: *“Do you offer me wealth? What Allah has granted me is far greater than what He has granted you. No! It is you who rejoice in ˹receiving˺ gifts”* (27:36).

**The Contrast With the Owner of the Gardens**

The reply ends with a commitment: I will build a barrier between you and them. The undertaking is personal and specific.

The wording is worth holding against the sūrah's own instruction about promising the future. Never say of anything that you will definitely do it tomorrow without adding *if Allah so wills* (18:23–24 — *“And never say of anything, “I will definitely do this tomorrow,””*). The ruler's commitment is stated without the qualification, and the following verses show him carrying it out.

**The Same Answer in Sūrat al-Naml**

Sūrat al-Naml adds the rebuke that Sūrat al-Kahf omits — that it is the givers who rejoice in gifts. The reply here is gentler, declining the payment and asking for labour instead.

The two passages together establish the standard the account applies. Authority established by God is exercised without charge, and the Qur'an records two rulers holding to it.

The verse records the refusal before the work begins. The ruler is offered tribute and declines it, saying what his Lord has established him in is better.

---

## Verse 18:96

> Bring me blocks of iron!” Then, when he had filled up ˹the gap˺ between the two mountains, he ordered, “Blow!” When the iron became red hot, he said, “Bring me molten copper to pour over it.”

**The First Command Names the Material**

The reply to the request is an instruction: bring me blocks of iron. The ruler does not describe a plan or explain a method; he names a material and asks for it.

The command also confirms what the previous verse promised. The people are asked for strength, and the first thing requested from them is material — the iron is theirs to bring, not his to supply.

The verse describes the work in a single clause: he filled the space between the two mountains until they were level. The commentators read this as the gap being packed with iron ingots until the mass reached the height of the surrounding rock.

The method is important. This is not a wall built on the ground but a plug inserted into an opening, with the mountains themselves serving as the two sides of the structure. The geography described two verses earlier is what makes the method possible.

The wording is precise about the extent. The gap is filled to the level of the cliffs, which means there is no low point to climb and no edge to work around.

---

The construction is described command by command — iron, then fire, then molten copper — and the details give the passage the texture of a report rather than a fable: those who worked were the people themselves, their labour was part of the protection, and the material was what the land provided. The Book tells of the same crafts placed in the hands of David and Solomon, where the jinn worked at the temple's structures and a fountain of copper was made to flow (34:12–13 — *“And We caused a stream of molten copper to flow for him, and ˹We subjected˺ some of the jinn to work under him by his Lord’s Will.”*), and where David was taught the making of coats of mail (21:80 — *“We taught him the art of making body armour to protect you in battle. Will you then be grateful”*). The wall's technical description ends with the statement that the people could not scale it and could not breach it (18:97 — *“And so the enemies could neither scale nor tunnel through it”*), which is the point of the whole episode: the protection God grants is beyond the strength of those it keeps out.

## Verse 18:97

> And so the enemies could neither scale nor tunnel through it

**Two Modes of Attack, Both Defeated**

The verse states the result of the construction in the two ways a barrier can be overcome. It could not be scaled from above, and it could not be pierced from below.

The pairing is exhaustive. A wall can be climbed or it can be breached; there is no third approach for an obstacle spanning a mountain gap, and the verse closes both.

The wording is also specific about who failed. The enemies — the same Gog and Magog named in the request — are the ones described, and their inability is stated as a fact about the finished work rather than as a hope about it.

The verse contains no evaluation. It does not say the work was great, or that the builder succeeded, or that the people were saved. It says the barrier could not be overcome.

The restraint is consistent with the account's treatment of the ruler throughout. His endowment was described in the passive, his refusals were stated without commendation, and here the result of his largest work is given in eleven words.

---

## Verse 18:98

> He declared, “This is a mercy from my Lord. But when the promise of my Lord comes to pass, He will level it to the ground. And my Lord’s promise is ever true.”

**"A Mercy From My Lord"**

The ruler's first statement about his completed work attributes it to God. He does not say he built it, that it is his achievement, or that the people are fortunate to have him.

The attribution is the answer to the parable the sūrah told earlier. A man given two gardens said he did not think anything better existed and did not think the Hour would come; a man given the earth says his greatest work is a mercy from his Lord.

The two statements are the chapter's paired verdicts, and neither man was asked for his opinion. Each volunteered it, which is why the account treats both as evidence.

**"My Lord's Promise Is True"**

The final clause is a statement the Qur'an repeats in many places. God's promise is true, and the formula appears in Sūrat al-Nisāʾ, Yūnus, and elsewhere as a fixed affirmation.

That is the chapter's resolution of its central problem. The danger is not that things last but that they are treated as lasting, and the verse removes the danger by stating the date.

**The Contrast With the Claim of Permanence**

The sūrah has already recorded the opposite statement. The garden owner entered his property saying he did not think he saw anything better and did not think it would ever perish (18:35 — *“And he entered his property, while wronging his soul, saying, “I do not think this will ever perish”*).

The two statements are the chapter's paired verdicts on human achievement. One man describes a small estate as permanent; another describes a mountain-spanning barrier as a mercy that will be levelled.

The account therefore judges men by their knowledge of limits rather than by their achievements. The garden owner had less and claimed more; Dhū al-Qarnayn had the earth and claimed nothing that would last.

**The Same Leveling Described Elsewhere**

The Qur'an describes the removal of the earth's most permanent features at the Hour: *“and the mountains will be blown away, becoming ˹like˺ a mirage”* (78:20), and Sūrat Ṭā-Hā records the mountains being wiped out completely (20:105 — *“And ˹if˺ they ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the mountains, ˹then˺ say, “My Lord will wipe them out completely”*).

---

## Verse 18:99

> On that Day, We will let them surge ˹like waves˺ over one another. Later, the Trumpet will be blown, and We will gather all ˹people˺ together

**The Day the Barrier Falls**

The verse opens with the occasion named in the previous one. On that Day — the Day the promise arrives and the barrier is levelled — the narrative moves from the structure to what happens after it.

The commentators place the Day before the Resurrection, and some specify that it comes after the slaying of the Antichrist. The verse itself gives no sequence beyond the two events it names.

The references are consistent across the Book. Sūrat Ṭā-Hā, al-Muʾminūn, al-Naml, Yā-Sīn, al-Zumar, Qāf, al-Ḥāqqah, al-Muddaththir, al-Nabaʾ, and al-Qāriʿah all describe it, and in several places it is the sound that raises the dead.

**The Gathering**

The gathering is the same event the sūrah described earlier, where the mountains are blown away, the earth is laid bare, and none are left behind (18:47 — *“˹Beware of˺ the Day We will blow the mountains away, and you will see the earth laid bare. And We will gather all ˹humankind˺, leaving none behind”*). The verse returns to it from a different direction — not from the removal of the earth's features but from the release of the barrier.

The two descriptions agree on the outcome. Whatever moves against whatever, the Day ends with everyone assembled.

**The Same Gathering Described Earlier in the Sūrah**

The sūrah has already described the assembly in full: *“˹Beware of˺ the Day We will blow the mountains away, and you will see the earth laid bare. And We will gather all ˹humankind˺, leaving none behind”* (18:47).

Read together, they close the sūrah's the teaching about the Last Day. The barrier falls, the surging begins, the Trumpet sounds, and everyone is gathered.

---

## Verse 18:100

> On that Day We will display Hell clearly for the disbelievers

**Hell Presented as an Array**

The verse describes the Fire being displayed — brought out and set in view — for the disbelievers on that Day.

The naming is precise. The verse says the disbelievers, not everyone, which makes the display an act addressed to a specific group rather than a general feature of the Day.

The commentators specify the sequence: the disbelievers see the Fire and hear its raging and groaning before they enter it. Sūrat al-Furqān supplies the sound — once it sees them from a distance, they hear it fuming and growling (25:12 — *“Once it sees them from a distance, they will hear it fuming and growling”*).

The interval is the verse's subject. Nothing happens to these people in this verse except being shown something, and the showing is itself part of what the Day contains.

The sūrah has already described the same state elsewhere: the wicked see the Fire, know they will fall into it, and find no way of avoiding it (18:53 — *“The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it”*). This verse supplies the moment of display that precedes it.

**The Word for Display**

That is consistent with the sūrah's treatment of the Day throughout. The record is laid open, the earth is laid bare, and here the Fire is displayed. Exposure is the Day's characteristic action.

The same verb family describes the earth itself on that Day. Sūrat al-Kahf says the earth will be seen laid bare (18:47 — *“˹Beware of˺ the Day We will blow the mountains away, and you will see the earth laid bare. And We will gather all ˹humankind˺, leaving none behind”*), and Sūrat al-Nāziʿāt describes the land being flattened until no curve or hollow is visible (79:3–4 — *“and those gliding ˹through heavens˺ swiftly and those taking the lead vigorously”*).

The pattern identifies the Day as one in which nothing is sprung on anyone. Everything arrives in the order that makes it fully known, and the display is the first stage of that.

The pattern also connects the Day to the revelation that preceded it. The message was shown before the judgment, and the Fire is shown before the entry. The same method operates across both stages.

**The Same Display in the Sūrah's Earlier Description**

The verse gives the Day's opening action rather than its conclusion. Before any sentence is passed, the Fire is brought into view, and the verse stops there.

The stopping point is the point. Judgment in this chapter is never sudden, and the display establishes that the people concerned see what is coming before anything happens to them.

---

## Verse 18:101

> those who turned a blind eye to My Reminder and could not stand listening ˹to it˺

The verse describes the disbelievers by two failures rather than by any belief they hold. Their eyes were covered, and they were unable to hear.

**The Language of Blindness and Deafness**

The Qur'an uses these two images throughout, and it uses them in a specific sense. Physical sight and hearing are not in question; what is described is a spiritual closure that behaves like a physical one.

Sūrat al-Aʿrāf states the consequence plainly: *“The deeds of those who deny Our signs and the meeting ˹with Allah˺ in the Hereafter will be in vain.”* The same two things — signs and meeting — appear there as they do in the verse that follows here, and the same result follows from them.

The consistency of the pairing across the Book is what gives it force. Blindness and deafness are not decorative metaphors in the Qur'an; they are a technical description of a state, and the state has a named consequence.

The verse here supplies the state; the following verses supply the consequence.

---

## Verse 18:102

> Do the disbelievers think they can ˹simply˺ take My servants as lords instead of Me? We have surely prepared Hell as an accommodation for the disbelievers

**What the Qur'an Says About Such Protectors**

The Book warns repeatedly against seeking protection apart from God, and it identifies the objects people turn to: idols, and Satan and his followers.

The warning is stated in general terms as well. Sūrat al-Shūrā says there is no helper for people apart from God, and the same claim appears in Sūrat al-Raʿd and elsewhere.

The second half of the verse uses a word drawn from hospitality. Hell is described as prepared accommodation — the lodging made ready for a guest.

**The Preparation Already Complete**

That tense is significant in a passage about the Day. Everything else in the section is future — the Fire will be displayed, the deeds will be weighed — and this one clause is stated as accomplished.

The effect is to remove the threat from the category of contingencies. A punishment that will be prepared might not be; a punishment already prepared is a fact awaiting its occasion.

The Qur'an gives this error a simile: *“The parable of those who take protectors other than Allah is that of a spider spinning a shelter. And the flimsiest of all shelters is certainly that of a spider, if only they knew.”* (29:41)

---

## Verse 18:103

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Shall we inform you of who will lose the most deeds

**An Announcement Before the Answer**

The verse is a question that promises information. It does not identify the losers; it offers to tell the hearers who they are, and the following verses supply the answer.

The structure is deliberate. A statement would have been heard and set aside; a question engages the hearer's own judgment before the answer arrives.

The form is also a teaching method used throughout the Qur'an. A question is posed, the hearer supplies an answer from his own assumptions, and the revelation then corrects it. The verse sets that sequence in motion.

The address is direct. The Prophet is told to say it, which means the question is spoken aloud to an audience that is present and that is expected to answer it in his own mind.

The superlative is the verse's central term. Not who will lose, but who will lose the most.

The wording implies a comparison that is already running. There are losers in lesser degrees, and the verse is asking about the extreme case — the person whose loss exceeds everyone else's.

---

## Verse 18:104

> ˹They are˺ those whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life, while they think they are doing good!”

**Effort That Produces Nothing**

The verse identifies the greatest losers by their effort. The word used carries the sense of striving, of movement expended toward an end, and the verse says the striving goes astray.

The description is therefore not of idleness. These are active people, occupied and productive, and their activity is precisely what is lost.

The wording is careful about what fails. The effort goes astray; it is not described as evil or as forbidden. What is wrong is the direction, not the exertion, and the verse locates the loss in the mismatch between the two.

That distinction is the verse's hardest point. A person can work diligently for years and produce nothing, and the loss is total precisely because the work was real.

**Good Seeming Fair to Those Who Do It**

The Qur'an returns to this state repeatedly. The deeds of the wrongdoers are described as made to seem fair to them, and the same language appears in Sūrat al-Anʿām in connection with those who mock the truth.

---

## Verse 18:105

> It is they who reject the signs of their Lord and their meeting with Him, rendering their deeds void, so We will not give their deeds any weight on Judgment Day

**Two Things Rejected**

The verse names the cause of the loss described in the previous verse. These are the people who disbelieve in the signs of their Lord and in the meeting with Him.

The two objects are distinct and they are joined. The signs are what has been given in this life; the meeting is what comes after it. Rejecting the first and rejecting the second are two acts, and the verse treats them as one position.

The pairing is the same one used in Sūrat al-Aʿrāf, where the deeds of those who deny the signs and the meeting of the Hereafter are declared void. The consistency across the Book shows that the two are treated as inseparable.

**The Rejection Is the Cause**

The verse states a causal chain rather than a list. Because they rejected the signs and the meeting, their deeds came to nothing, and because the deeds came to nothing, no weight will be assigned to them.

Sūrat al-Furqān describes the same event in another image: *“Then We will turn to whatever ˹good˺ deeds they did, reducing them to scattered dust.”* (25:23) The deeds are not deleted; they are dispersed.

**The Same Voiding Stated Elsewhere**

Sūrat al-Aʿrāf states the same rule in the same terms: *“The deeds of those who deny Our signs and the meeting ˹with Allah˺ in the Hereafter will be in vain. Will they be rewarded except for what they have done?”* (7:147)

---

## Verse 18:106

> That is their reward: Hell, for their disbelief and mockery of My signs and messengers

**The Recompense Named**

The verse states the result of the description given in the three verses before it. That is their recompense — Hell.

The wording is flat. There is no elaboration on the punishment, no description of its nature, and no try to soften it. The verse identifies the outcome and then explains why it applies.

The verse names two grounds for the recompense: disbelief, and taking the signs and messengers in mockery.

That distinction matters because mockery is a choice about how to treat what has been given. A person may disbelieve privately; the verse describes people who made a public performance of contempt for the signs and for those who brought them.

The two grounds therefore describe two different failures — one of belief and one of conduct — and the verse holds both as reasons.

**Mockery as a Distinct Offence**

The Qur'an treats mockery of the signs as a separate matter from simple rejection, and it names it repeatedly. Sūrat al-Jāthiyah mentions those who took the signs in mockery, and the same charge appears in connection with those who made the revelations a jest.

---

The same end is stated twice in the sūrah's closing movement: their works will be wasted and their deeds void (18:104–105 — *“It is they who reject the signs of their Lord and their meeting with Him, rendering their deeds void, so We will not give their deeds any weight on Judgment Day”*), and here the reason is given — disbelief and mockery of the signs and the messengers. The Book uses the identical pattern in the sūrah of Hūd, where those who prefer the life of this world and its adornment have their works perished in this life and the next (11:15–16 — *“Whoever desires ˹only˺ this worldly life and its luxuries, We will pay them in full for their deeds in this life—nothing will be left out”*), and in the sūrah of the Troops, where the account of what they did is lost (39:65 — *“It has already been revealed to you—and to those ˹prophets˺ before you—that if you associate others ˹with Allah˺, your deeds will certainly be void and you will truly be one of the losers”*). The flatness of the sentence is deliberate: after the long description of the people whose efforts went astray, the reward is stated without ornament, which is the sūrah's way of removing the glamour from what they had chased.

## Verse 18:107

> Indeed, those who believe and do good will have the Gardens of Paradise as an accommodation

**The Other Side of the Same Comparison**

The verse turns from the group described in the previous verses to the group that stands opposite them. Those who believe and do righteous deeds will have the Gardens of Paradise as their accommodation.

The turn is abrupt and unmarked. No transition explains the shift, and the verse simply states the other case in the same terms as the first.

**The Same Accommodation Described in Sūrat al-Sajdah**

Sūrat al-Sajdah uses the identical word for the same reward: *“As for those who believe and do good, they will have the Gardens of ˹Eternal˺ Residence—as an accommodation for what they used to do.”* (32:19)

Read together, the two verses show that the accommodation is not a gift unrelated to the person's life. It corresponds to what he did, which is the same principle the previous verses applied to the weightless deeds.

The wording is declarative. The Gardens will be theirs; nothing is conditional beyond the two conditions already named, and no further qualification is added.

That directness contrasts with the sūrah's earlier descriptions of the reward, which were given as promises inside longer passages. Here the reward is stated as a settled fact in a single clause.

---

## Verse 18:108

> where they will be forever, never desiring anywhere else

**Permanence Stated First**

The verse opens with the duration. They will remain in it forever, and the claim is unqualified.

The statement answers the chapter's central anxiety directly. Every other possession described in the sūrah had an end — the gardens were destroyed, the barrier will be levelled, the years in the cave were counted. This one does not.

The wording is therefore the verse's principal claim. It is not a detail added to a description of the Garden; it is the feature that distinguishes this accommodation from everything else the chapter has described.

The verse completes the comparison the sūrah has been building. The garden owner wanted permanence for a small estate and lost it; the believers are given permanence without asking for it.

The second clause is the verse's most distinctive feature. They will not seek any change from it.

The wording describes an absence of desire rather than the satisfaction of desire. Nothing is said about wanting and receiving; the verse says the wanting does not arise.

---

## Verse 18:109

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If the ocean were ink for ˹writing˺ the Words of my Lord, it would certainly run out before the Words of my Lord were finished, even if We refilled it with its equal.”

**What the Words Are**

The commentators differ over the referent. Some take the Words as God's knowledge and wisdom; others take them as the meanings and benefits contained in revelation; others as God's capacity to create and to command whatever He wills.

The Qur'an describes creation as effected by a Word — when God decrees a thing He says to it "Be", and it is. On that reading the Words are the acts of creation themselves, which are without number.

Others connect the phrase to the passage's context. The preceding verses discuss reward and punishment, and some commentators take the Words here as the promises and threats of the Hereafter, or as what God will say to the people of the Garden.

The verse states the result plainly: the ocean would be exhausted before the Words were finished. The wording puts the two exhaustions in sequence and names which comes first.

**The Same Image in Sūrat Luqmān**

The Qur'an uses the same image with more detail: *“If all the trees on earth were pens and the ocean ˹were ink˺, refilled by seven other oceans, the Words of Allah would not be exhausted. Surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”* (31:27)

**The Sūrah's Two Last Sayings**

The verse is the first half of the sūrah's closing pair, and both halves begin with the command to say. Say: if the sea were ink for the words of my Lord, the sea would be spent before the words were spent. Say: I am only a man like you, and it is revealed to me that your God is one God — so whoever hopes to meet his Lord, let him do good deeds and never join anything in worship with his Lord (18:109-110 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God.”*). The two sentences answer two demands at once: the demand for more revelation, and the demand for something greater than a human messenger. The answer to the first is that the words are not exhausted; the answer to the second is that a man is enough, and that what is asked of the hearer is not a second ocean of ink but a good deed and pure worship. The sūrah began with praise for the One who sent down the Book straight (18:1-2 — *“All praise is for Allah Who has revealed the Book to His servant, allowing no crookedness in it”*), and it ends with the words that cannot be counted and the one condition for meeting Him.

**What Is Being Said About the Words**

The commentators took the words as God's knowledge and His command, and the Book uses the same image with more of the picture in the Sūrah of Luqmān: if all the trees of the earth were pens and the ocean were ink, refilled by seven oceans after it, the words of God would not be finished (31:27 — *“If all the trees on earth were pens and the ocean ˹were ink˺, refilled by seven other oceans, the Words of Allah would not be exhausted.”*). The point in both places is not a puzzle about counting. It is that the One speaking is not limited to what He has already said, and that revelation is a gift drawn from a treasury no creature can measure. The verse closes a sūrah in which the men of the cave, the owner of the two gardens, Moses at the meeting of the two seas and the traveller with the wall each measured their own knowledge against what they had not been told, and each was answered by something they had not counted on.

## Verse 18:110

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God. So whoever hopes for the meeting with their Lord, let them do good deeds and associate none in the worship of their Lord.”

**The Chapter Ends With a Denial of Exception**

The sūrah's last verse opens with the Prophet describing himself as a man like his hearers. After a chapter containing a cave, a journey to the ends of the earth, and a barrier of iron and copper, the closing statement is a claim of ordinariness.

**The Same Claim Made by Other Messengers**

The Qur'an puts the same statement in the mouths of earlier messengers. In Sūrat Ibrāhīm the messengers say to their people: *“We are ˹indeed˺ only humans like you, but Allah favours whoever He chooses of His servants.”* (14:11)

Sūrat al-Isrāʾ records the same reply to a demand for miracles: *“Glory be to my Lord! Am I not only a human messenger?”* (17:93)

The second clause states the content of the revelation in the shortest possible form: your God is one God.

**Hope for the Meeting**

The third clause introduces the condition: whoever hopes for the meeting with his Lord. The verse addresses hope rather than fear, and the object of the hope is the meeting itself.

The choice is significant in a chapter that has described the meeting's consequences in detail. The Fire was displayed, the deeds were weighed, and the two accommodations were named; the closing verse addresses the person who looks forward to it.

The wording also ties back to the verse that described the greatest losers. Those people rejected the meeting; this verse addresses whoever hopes for it, and the two positions are the chapter's final pair.

**What the Verse Says About the Messenger**

The verse is the sūrah's last, and it has the Prophet tell his people that he is a man like them who receives revelation, and then give the two things the community is to hold: that their god is one God, and that whoever hopes to meet his Lord should do good work and associate no one in the worship of his Lord. The Book ends the sūrah with the plainest statement of the messenger's humanity, and the two commands that follow are the whole of the religion in one sentence. The sūrah of the Cave has dealt with knowledge, wealth, power and the end of the world through four stories, and it closes with the servant who receives the revelation rather than with the stories.
**What the Commentators Noted**

The older commentators read the verse as the answer to the men who had asked for a sign or for an angel, and they noted that the sūrah ends with the sentence that would have prevented the mistake of making the messenger something other than a man. The Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān has the Prophet make the same declaration, and the Sūrah of the Heights records his saying that he is a man like his hearers except that he receives revelation. The verse in front of us adds the two duties, which the commentators read as the definition of the hope of meeting God: the right action, and the avoidance of every partner.
**The Sūrah's Close**

The four stories of the sūrah all end with the same lesson, and the last verse states it as an instruction. The Book's arrangement tells the reader that the cave, the garden, the servant and the wall were all about the difference between what a person knows and what God knows, and that the practice of the person who has understood the difference is stated here in one line. The final sentence of the sūrah is therefore a summary of the whole of it.
