# Sūrah an-Nās (Chapter 114) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Lord of Humanity and the Whispering Retreat**

An-Nās, "Mankind," is the one-hundred-and-fourteenth and final chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 6 verses of early Makkan revelation (with some authorities considering it Medinan alongside al-Falaq). As the ultimate concluding chapter of the entire sacred scripture, Sūrah an-Nās brings the grand architectural journey of the Qur'an to a perfect, harmonious cosmic seal. While the first chapter of the Qur'an—Sūrah al-Fātiḥah—opened with humanity calling upon the Lord of the Worlds (*Rabbi al-ʿĀlamīn*) for guidance, Sūrah an-Nās concludes the Book by placing fragile humanity under the sovereign protection of the Lord, King, and God of Humanity (*Rabbi al-Nās, Maliki al-Nās, Ilāhi al-Nās*). While its companion Sūrah al-Falaq sought refuge from *external* physical and occult evils, Sūrah an-Nās seeks refuge from the most dangerous, insidious enemy of all: the *internal* evil of whispered spiritual sabotage (*al-Waswās al-Khannās*), which infiltrates the human chest from both unseen jinn and human conspirators.

**The Structural Architecture and the Three Sovereignties**

The architecture of Sūrah an-Nās is structured with majestic theological symmetry across two movements:
1. The Three Divine Shields of Humanity (1–3): - *“Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of mankind (*Qul aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi al-nās*)!”*—The Cherisher, Sustainer, and Nurturer of human existence. - *“The Sovereign / King of mankind (*Maliki al-nās*)!”*—The supreme Master with absolute political, legislative, and existential jurisdiction over human affairs. - *“The God of mankind (*Ilāhi al-nās*)!”*—The sole, legitimate Object of human love, reverence, and worship.
2. The Threat of the Whispering Retreat (4–6): - *“From the evil of the retreating whisperer (*Min sharri al-waswāsi al-khannās*)!”* - *“Who whispers into the breasts of mankind (*Alladhī yuwaswisu fī ṣudūri al-nās*)!”* - *“From among the jinn and mankind (*Min al-jinnati wa-l-nās*)!”*

**The Grand Seal of the Qur'an: From Fātiḥah to Nās**

The profound philosophical completion of the Qur'an is witnessed in the sublime link between its opening and its close. The Qur'an opens in Sūrah al-Fātiḥah with the human creature praising the Lord of all creation (*Al-Ḥamdu li-Llāhi Rabbi al-ʿĀlamīn*), seeking the Straight Path (*Al-Ṣirāṭ al-Mustaqīm*). Across the subsequent 112 chapters—through laws, covenants, prophetic trials, cosmic oaths, parables, and Last-Day warnings—the Qur'an guides humanity along that path. By repeating the word *Al-Nās* (Humanity) five times in six verses, the Qur'an seals its divine message not with cosmic abstractions, but with deep, compassionate concern for the spiritual safety of the human soul, leaving humanity forever protected in the sanctuary of its Loving Lord.

## Verse 114:1

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I seek refuge in the Lord of humankind

**Rabb Names the One Who Raises a Thing to Its Completion**

*Rabb* is usually rendered "Lord," and the translation loses most of it. In Arabic the word covers ownership, mastery, and — centrally — the sustained bringing-up of something toward its end. A *rabb* provides, arranges, and continues to do so; the word is used of one who nurtures a thing until it is complete.

Applied to God, it names the relation the Qur'an insists on most often: not creation at a moment but provision without interruption. The prophets speak of God in the possessive — *rabbī*, my Lord — and the possessive is where the word does its work. Lordship in this sense is not a rank but a relation, and the relation is personal.

The choice of *rabb* as the first title in a prayer for protection is therefore exact. What the reciter is asking is not for an intervention from a distant sovereign but for the continuing care of the one who has been maintaining him all along. The title also carries an implication about the danger: something is threatening the development of a person, and the one who develops him is being addressed.

**Humankind Rather Than the Worlds**

The Fātiḥah opens the codex with a wider title: *“All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds”* (1:2). This chapter narrows to one of those worlds and repeats it three times. The narrowing is deliberate and it matches the threat.

The danger named in verse four operates inside the human heart. It is not a cosmic catastrophe, not a natural evil, not a political enemy. It is a whisper addressed to a person's own interior, and the titles chosen are titles over exactly that domain: the Lord of the beings who have hearts, the Master of the beings who can be commanded, the God of the beings who worship.

The word *nās* itself is general — people, humankind — with no qualification by tribe, creed, or century. Like *al-nās* in 110:2, it refuses to be narrowed, which allows every reciter to place himself inside it without argument. The prayer is not for a community but for the species, and the person saying it is included by being a member.

**Asking for Refuge Is an Admission That You Are Not Enough**

The act of seeking refuge declares inability. Nobody asks for shelter from what he can handle, and the verse puts that declaration in the mouth of the Prophet ﷺ before it names the danger.

The three titles then supply what the declaration lacks. Al-Rāzī read the chapter as an order to take refuge from the illusion of self-sufficiency, which is the root of sin: human beings claim independence, and the claim is answered by three addresses — my Lord, my King, my God. The Qur'an's picture of the claim is Pharaoh, who said *“I am your lord, the most high!”* (79:24), who declared that he knew of no god for his people but himself (28:38), and who boasted that Egypt and its rivers were his (43:51).

Those three assertions correspond to the three titles in reverse. Pharaoh claimed lordship, sovereignty, and divinity for himself; the chapter assigns all three to God and assigns them over humankind — the same humankind Pharaoh addressed as his people. The prayer is therefore not only a request for safety but a rejection of a political theology, recited by people who live under governments that make the same claim in smaller forms.

**The Book Opens With a Request for Help and Closes With One**

The codex begins with al-Fātiḥa and its central line: *“You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help.”* (1:5). It ends with two chapters whose only act is asking for help, and the last of them addresses God as Lord, Master, and God of humankind — the same three relations the Fātiḥah assumes.

The frame is exact. The opening sūrah states the arrangement in general terms: worship and request, from servants to a Lord. The closing sūrah performs it in a specific case: a human being exposed to a whisper he cannot silence asks the Lord of humankind to be his shelter. Between the two lies the whole Book.

Nothing follows this chapter in the codex. The last word of the Qur'an, on the reading of its final verse, is humankind — the species named three times, protected in the last line, and left with a prayer it is required to repeat.

## Verse 114:2

> the Master of humankind

**Kingship in the Qur'an Is the Language of the Last Day**

The title has a fixed place in the Qur'an's vocabulary, and that place is Last-Day. The Fātiḥah gives God *"Master of the Day of Judgment"* (1:4) — kingship attached to the day when every other authority is suspended. Ṭā Hā states it without qualification: *“Exalted is Allah, the True King!”* (20:114), where the adjective *true* distinguishes this kingship from every borrowed one.

The closing verses of al-Ḥashr list it among the names: *“He is Allah—there is no god except Him: the King, the Most Holy, the All-Perfect, the Source of Serenity, the Watcher ˹of all˺, the Almighty, the Supreme in Might, the Majestic.”* (59:23). And al-Jumuʿa opens with the whole creation under it: *“Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth ˹constantly˺ glorifies Allah—the King, the Most Holy, the Almighty, the All-Wise.”* (62:1).

The pattern in those verses is consistent: kingship is asserted most strongly where the world's own kings are in view. Al-Ḥashr and al-Jumuʿa were revealed in Medina, in a city with rulers, judges, and a Jewish establishment whose authorities were contested. This chapter makes the same assertion about the interior, where the contest is quieter.

**The Counter-Claim This Title Refuses**

The Qur'an's clearest picture of illegitimate kingship is Pharaoh, and it is stated in the first person: *“I am your lord, the most high!”* (79:24). He announced himself as *rabb*, the very word this chapter's first verse assigns to God, and he added the superlative.

The claim had three parts, and the Qur'an records all of them. Lordship: 79:24. Divinity: *“O chiefs! I know of no other god for you but myself.”* (28:38). Sovereignty: *“O my people! Am I not sovereign over Egypt as well as ˹all˺ these streams flowing at my feet?”* (43:51). Three claims, answered here by three titles, in the same order.

The refutation is not an argument against Pharaoh's power, which was real. It is a denial of his title. Power over a territory is not kingship over persons, and kingship over persons is not divinity. The verse that answers this in the Qur'an's own terms is a prayer: *“O Allah! Lord over all authorities! You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from who You please.”* (3:26). Every human king holds his office on loan, and the lender is the Master of humankind.

**A King Whose Court Cannot Be Entered Without Permission**

Kingship carries rules of access, and the Qur'an states them in the Verse of the Throne: *“Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?”* (2:255). The question expects no answer. Nothing approaches this king except as he allows.

That fact governs the prayer in this chapter. The whisperer who works on a human heart has no permission and no standing; he is not an ambassador from a rival power but an intruder. The reciter is therefore not negotiating between two claimants. He is appealing to the one whose jurisdiction is uncontested, against an agent who has none.

The same structure explains why refuge is the correct form of request rather than combat. A subject cannot expel an intruder from a royal house; he reports him to the owner of the house. The chapter's single verb — I seek refuge — is the report, and the three titles establish whose house it is.

The Verse of the Throne adds what makes the appeal effective: *“His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him.”* (2:255). The jurisdiction invoked here is not local, and its exercise does not cost the one who holds it anything.

**The Fātiḥah's Three Titles, Compressed Into Three Verses**

The opening chapter of the codex makes the same three moves in four verses. *“All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds”* (1:2) — lordship. *“You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help.”* (1:5) — divinity, and the request that this chapter's verb repeats.

Read together, the first and last chapters of the Qur'an are the same prayer at two scales. The Fātiḥah states it universally, over all worlds and on the Day; this chapter states it locally, over one species and inside one heart. Both end in a request for help against a road — the Fātiḥah asks for the straight path, this one asks for shelter from the whisperer who works on those walking it.

The correspondence is why the two chapters are recited together in practice, at the beginning of every unit of prayer and at the end of every night's recitation. The Book opens by asking for guidance and closes by asking for protection from what derails it.

## Verse 114:3

> the God of humankind

**Ilāh Names a Function, Not a Kind of Being**

*Ilāh* is ordinarily translated "god," and the translation conceals the word's grammar. An *ilāh* is not a member of a species; it is whatever a person worships. The root carries the sense of turning toward something in devotion, awe, and obedience, and the word denotes the object of that turning.

That is why the Qur'an's central sentence is a negation of the word and not of a being: *lā ilāha illā Allāh* — there is no god except God. The claim is not that other gods fail to exist as objects but that nothing deserves the position. Idols existed in Mecca and were called āliha; the testimony denies them the title, not the wood.

Used here, the word assigns a relation. God is *ilāh al-nās* — the object of humankind's worship — and the assignment is made over the same people named in the two previous verses. The one who provides for them and rules them is also the one they are to worship, and the three statements are one claim stated at increasing depth.

**Why Divinity Is Added to Lordship and Kingship**

A person can accept the first two titles without the third. Provision is observable and rule is enforced; neither requires devotion. The Qur'an's own account of the Meccan audience says exactly this: they acknowledged God as creator and provider while worshipping others alongside Him.

The argument appears as a question whose answer is never disputed: *“Who provides for you from heaven and earth? Who owns ˹your˺ hearing and sight? Who brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living? And who conducts every affair?” They will ˹surely˺ say, “Allah.””* (10:31). Acknowledging lordship was not the point of contention in Mecca; the contention was over who receives worship. That is why the third title comes last and why it is the decisive one.

Adding *ilāh al-nās* to the sequence closes the escape. It says that the one who grows you and rules you is also the one you are made for, and the Qur'an states the purpose in one sentence: *“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me.”* (51:56). Creation and worship are tied together there as they are in this chapter's three verses. The verse was first recited to people whose city contained a shrine with three hundred and sixty idols around it, each called an *ilāh*, each receiving sacrifice, petition, and loyalty.

Not the god of the Arabs, not the god of Muḥammad's tribe, not a god among gods — *ilāh al-nās*, the God of humankind, with the same unqualified noun used in the two verses before it.

**Obedience Is Worship, and That Is What the Whisperer Wants**

The Qur'an calls following Satan worship, and it does so in God's own voice on the Day: *“Did I not command you, O Children of Adam, not to follow Satan, for he is truly your sworn enemy”* (36:60) — addressed to people who never prostrated to him. The charge is following.

That identification explains why this title is placed immediately before the description of the whisperer. The whisperer's method is suggestion, and the aim of suggestion is compliance. A person who acts on a whisper has, in the Qur'an's vocabulary, obeyed it; and obedience given to anything other than God is the substance of worship misdirected.

So the third title is not a doctrinal addition to a prayer for safety. It removes the whisperer's only route. He cannot provide for a person, cannot rule him, and — once *ilāh al-nās* has been said — cannot be obeyed as an object of devotion. What remains is noise in the heart, which is what the following verses describe and dismiss.

## Verse 114:4

> from the evil of the lurking whisperer—

**The Same Word Is Used of the Stars**

The root appears once more in the Qur'an, in an oath at the beginning of al-Takwīr: *“I do swear by the receding stars— which travel and hide.”* (81:15–16). There *al-khunnās* describes bodies that move along a course and withdraw from sight, reappearing on the same course.

The parallel is more than lexical. Both uses describe something that comes and goes on a fixed pattern, something whose presence is periodic rather than constant. The stars withdraw at dawn and return at night; the whisperer withdraws at remembrance and returns at heedlessness.

Arabic oaths of this kind take their force from a visible thing, and the visible thing here is withdrawal. Its first audience could watch the stars retire from the sky every morning. The word that described that retirement was then used for a retreat the eye cannot see, inside a person, at a moment he does not notice.

**Waswasa Is Quiet, and It Repeats**

The verb *waswasa* denotes a sound made low and made again — technically, something said quietly and repeatedly. Neither feature is incidental. A whisper is quiet so that it can be mistaken for the hearer's own thought, and it is repeated so that what was first rejected becomes familiar.

That is the difference between a whisper and an argument. An argument presents itself as coming from outside and invites assessment; a whisper presents itself as arising from within and invites agreement. The person who acts on it usually believes he has thought of it.

Elsewhere the language used for Satan's other operations keeps the same character. He makes evil deeds appear attractive (16:63), he causes forgetting (58:19), he lures with false hopes (47:25), he makes people slip (3:155), he sows discord between people (17:53). Every one of these is a gradual operation on perception rather than a confrontation with the will. Nothing is forced; everything is arranged until the person chooses it himself.

**A Catalogue of Methods, and One Named Enemy**

The Qur'an does not treat the whisperer as an abstraction. He is named, characterized, and assigned a history. He is the Chief Deceiver whom people are warned not to be deceived by (31:33; 35:5); he is a sworn enemy to humankind (2:168; 17:53); he and his soldiers watch people *“from where you cannot see them”* (7:27); he tempted the first two people out of the Garden (7:27).

Each description adds something this chapter's single participle compresses. The invisibility of 7:27 is the withdrawal of the *khannās*; the deception of 31:33 is the quietness of the whisper; the enmity of 2:168 is its persistence. What this verse contributes is the interiority of the operation, which the other descriptions leave to 50:16 and to the verse that follows.

The personal language has a purpose beyond description. A force cannot be resisted by a person; an agent can be refused. Personal language of this kind gives the reciter someone to refuse rather than something to endure, because an enemy who acts on a method can be answered.

**The First Whisper, and the Last Prayer**

The chapter closes the Qur'an, and the danger it names opened human history. *“Then Satan tempted them to expose what was hidden of their nakedness. He said, ‘Your Lord has forbidden this tree to you only to prevent you from becoming angels or immortals.’”* (7:20). The method is identical to the one described here: a quiet suggestion, repeated, framed as information about God's intention.

The Qur'an's final word on the subject is not an analysis of it but a request for shelter from it. Between the first whisper in the Garden and this last prayer stand every case the Book records — Joseph's brothers, the two who conspired at Medina, the man who forgot to mention his lord and was made to stay in prison (12:42). All of them are instances of the same operation.

Ending here is a statement about the human condition rather than a literary device. The last thing the revelation leaves with its readers is the admission that the oldest attack is still running, that it works from inside, and that the answer to it is a sentence anyone can say.

The Qur'an states the same mechanism from the other side: *“Indeed, when Satan whispers to those mindful ˹of Allah˺, they remember ˹their Lord˺ then they start to see ˹things˺ clearly.”* (7:201).

The limits of the whisper are stated explicitly in al-Naḥl: *“He certainly has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord. His authority is only over those who take him as a patron and who—under his influence—associate ˹others˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺.”* (16:99–100).

## Verse 114:5

> who whispers into the hearts of humankind—

**Two Agents of Whispering Appear in the Qur'an**

Qāf attributes the same activity to the soul: *“Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ created humankind and ˹fully˺ know what their souls whisper to them, and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein.”* (50:16). There the whispering is internal, and God's knowledge of it is the point.

This verse attributes it to the *khannās*. The two statements are not in conflict, and the tradition reconciled them by distinguishing levels of the soul. The whispering of 50:16 is not from the soul as such but from the soul in a particular condition — the soul that commands evil, *al-nafs al-ammāra bi-l-sūʾ*, which Joseph's wife's confession names: *“indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil, except those shown mercy by my Lord”* (12:53).

The relation between the two agents is stated in al-Zukhruf: *“And whoever turns a blind eye to the Reminder of the Most Compassionate, We place at the disposal of each a devilish one as their close associate.”* (43:36). The companion is assigned in response to a turning away. What begins as a person's own inclination is then worked on by another, and the two become hard to separate — which is exactly the condition the verse describes.

**The Soul That Commands Evil, and a Report About Blood**

The tradition held both agents together without collapsing them. On one account Satan suggests and the soul inclined to evil follows the suggestion; on the other, the soul has the propensity itself and Satan only finds it. The second view was preferred by those who wanted to keep responsibility entirely inside the person; the first by those who took the Qur'an's descriptions of Satan at face value.

A report in the Ṣaḥīḥ collections gives the relation in an image. The Prophet ﷺ, walking with Ṣafiyyah at night during his seclusion in the mosque, saw an Ansārī man look at them and called him over: "Come here — she is Ṣafiyyah. Satan circulates in the son of Adam as his blood circulates in him." That is Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2039.

The image says what the theology requires. The whisperer is not outside the person's life in the way a stranger is; he moves through it as blood does, reaching every part without being seen in any of them. That is why the verse places the whisper in the chest rather than at the door.

**Humankind Is Named for the Fourth Time**

*Al-nās* appears in verses one, two, three and five. The repetition is the chapter's most obvious feature and its least arbitrary. Each occurrence fixes the same object under a different relation — provided for, ruled, worshipping, attacked — and the fourth fixes it as the target.

The effect on the reciter is inclusion. He cannot hear the phrase "the hearts of humankind" as describing somebody else, because he is a member of the class by birth and there is no way to step out of it. The verse does not say "the hearts of the wicked" or "of the heedless." It says humankind, which includes the prophet who was told to say it.

That generality also settles a question the commentators raised about who is whispered into. The following verse extends the phrase to jinn as well, and the two readings of that extension are discussed there. What this verse establishes is the human case, and it establishes it without exception.

**The Heart Is Where the Three Titles Are Received**

Verses one to three address God as Lord, Master, and God of humankind, and verse five names the place where all three relations are held: the chest. Lordship is received by a person who depends on provision; kingship by a person who obeys; divinity by a person who devotes himself. None of these happens in the hands or the tongue before it happens in the interior.

A person who has already said "my Lord, my King, my God" has established what the whisper is competing with.

The Qur'an describes the hearts of those who believe as finding comfort in the remembrance of Allah, and adds that in that remembrance hearts find comfort (13:28).

## Verse 114:6

> from among jinn and humankind.”

**The Jinn Also Heard This Book**

The other species named in the verse is not a background creature in the Qur'an; it is an audience. Al-Jinn opens with the report: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘It has been revealed to me that a group of jinn listened ˹to the Quran,˺ and said ˹to their fellow jinn˺: ‘Indeed, we have heard a wondrous recitation. It leads to Right Guidance so we believed in it, and we will never associate anyone with our Lord ˹in worship˺.’”* (72:1–2).

Al-Aḥqāf records the same event from the other side: a group of jinn sent to listen to the Qur'an, who told one another to be quiet, and who returned to their people as warners (46:29), urging them to respond to the caller and believe (46:31). And the purpose of both species is stated in one sentence: *“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me.”* (51:56).

That last verse is why the pairing in this chapter is not a curiosity. Jinn and humankind are created for the same thing, addressed by the same revelation, exposed to the same enemy, and given the same refuge. The final verse of the Qur'an names both because both are inside the prayer.

**Seeking Refuge With Jinn Makes the Harm Worse**

Al-Jinn contains the negative counterpart of this chapter. The jinn themselves report: *“And some men used to seek refuge with some jinn—so they increased each other in wickedness.”* (72:6). Men in the pre-Islamic desert, stopping at a place they feared, used to address its spirit for protection; the result was an increase in *rahaq* — arrogance, sin, overweening — for both parties.

The verse explains what the practice did to each side. The man became more afraid and more dependent on a power he had no standing with; the jinn became more contemptuous of him, since he had asked them for what only God can give. A false refuge does not merely fail; it strengthens the thing it was meant to answer.

This chapter is the correction in six verses. The same need — protection from an invisible harm — is met with a different address: not the spirit of the valley but the Lord, Master, and God of humankind. The whole of the two closing chapters can be read as the answer to 72:6, given to people who had been doing the wrong thing for generations.

**Whether "People" Includes Jinn**

A further report bears on the second reading of the phrase. Some exegetes held that *nās* is not restricted to human beings, on the evidence that the Qur'an uses *rijāl* — men, individuals — of jinn in 72:6, where the men who sought refuge and the jinn they sought it from are described in the same sentence. If a word ordinarily applied to people can be applied to jinn, they argued, *nās* may cover both, and the "hearts of humankind" in the previous verse may include the hearts of jinn.

The argument is lexical and it is not decisive. *Rijāl* in 72:6 is used of human men, and the jinn in that verse are the object of the men's appeal rather than the subject of the word. The dominant sense of *nās* throughout the Qur'an is humankind, and the pairing of *jinn* with *nās* in this verse and in 51:56 treats them as two species rather than one class.

The reading was kept because it makes the chapter wider, and because the tradition was reluctant to exclude any creature from a prayer for protection. The narrower reading remains the plain one, and it is enough: the whisperer reaches both species, and the refuge is offered to whoever says it.

**Two Species, One Enemy, One Prayer**

The last verse leaves the Qur'an with a picture of the created world that its readers share with something else. Jinn and humankind are both made for worship, both addressed by revelation, both watched by an enemy they cannot see, and both given the same words of refuge.

The practice attached to those words is continuous. ʿUqba ibn ʿĀmir reported the Messenger of Allah ﷺ saying that verses had been sent down the like of which had never been seen — the two chapters of refuge (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 814a); he reported him using them in wind and darkness on the road between al-Juḥfa and al-Abwāʾ, and heard him lead the congregation with them (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 1463). ʿĀʾisha reported the nightly practice of reciting them and al-Ikhlāṣ into the joined palms, breathing on them, and wiping them over the body three times (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5056).

The Book closes with those words still being said. Its last chapter is not a conclusion but an instruction, and the instruction is being carried out by the people it names every night since it was revealed.

The Qur'an states the existence of human devils explicitly: *“And so We have made for every prophet enemies—devilish humans and jinn—whispering to one another with elegant words of deception.”* (6:112).
