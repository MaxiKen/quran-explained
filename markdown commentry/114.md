# Sūrah an-Nās (Chapter 114) — Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Final Chapter**

An-Nās, "Humankind," is the last chapter of the Qur'an: six verses revealed in Makkah, though some authorities, noting its close pairing with al-Falaq, place it among the Madinan revelations. It stands as the counterpart of Sūrah al-Fātiḥah. The Book opens with a servant praising the Lord of all worlds and asking for guidance (1:2, 1:6); it closes with that same servant asking the Lord, King, and God of humankind for protection. Between the request for guidance and the request for protection lies the whole Qur'an.

The chapter pairs with Sūrah al-Falaq. Al-Falaq seeks refuge from harms that come from outside a person — the night, a spell worked at a distance, an envier's malice. An-Nās seeks refuge from a harm that works from inside: *al-waswās al-khannās*, the whisperer who retreats, who slips suggestions into the human chest. Together the two are known as *al-Muʿawwidhatān*, the two chapters of refuge.

**Three Titles Before One Danger**

Verses 1–3 name God three times over the same people: *Rabbi al-nās*, *Maliki al-nās*, *Ilāhi al-nās* — Lord, King, and God of humankind. Only then, in verse 4, does the danger appear. The proportion is itself an argument: three verses are given to the One being asked, one to the thing feared. The word *al-nās* appears five times in six verses, binding each title and each stage of the attack to the same object — humanity, without qualification of tribe, creed, or century.

**The Enemy Who Runs Away**

*Al-Waswās* is a whisper that is low and repeated, quiet enough to pass for a person's own thought. *Al-Khannās* comes from a root meaning to draw back or slink away, and describes one who keeps retreating. Ibn ʿAbbās explained the pairing: Satan perches on the heart of the son of Adam — when the person forgets God, he whispers; when the person remembers God, he withdraws and slinks away. Evil in this chapter is not an equal power. It is an intruder whose presence depends on heedlessness and ends at remembrance.

## Sūrah an-Nās 114:1

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I seek refuge in the Lord of humankind

**Three Titles Before the Danger Is Named**

The chapter spends its first three verses describing God and only then says what it is asking protection from. Lord, Master, God of humankind — and then the whisperer. Fear naturally narrows attention onto the thing feared; this order reverses the direction. By the time the whisperer is named, the reciter has said the word *humankind* three times and has already placed himself under a governance he did not choose but now acknowledges.

**Rabb: The One Who Brings a Thing to Completion**

*Rabb* is usually translated "Lord," and the translation loses most of it. The word covers ownership, authority, and — centrally — the sustained bringing of something toward its completion. A *rabb* provides, arranges, and keeps arranging; the word is used of one who raises a thing until it is complete. Applied to God it names the relation the Qur'an insists on most often: not creation at a single moment but provision without interruption. The prophets speak of God possessively — *rabbī*, my Lord — and the possessive is where the word does its work. Lordship in this sense is not a rank but a relationship, and it is personal. What the reciter asks for, then, is not an intervention from a distant sovereign but the continuing care of the One who has been sustaining him all along.

**Humankind Rather Than the Worlds**

Al-Fātiḥah opens the Book with a wider title, *"Lord of all worlds"* (1:2). This chapter narrows to one of those worlds and repeats it three times, which suits the threat: the danger described in verse 4 operates inside the human heart, not in the sky or on a battlefield. *Nās* is a general word. Like *al-nās* in 110:2, it refuses to be restricted, so that every reciter places himself inside it without argument. The prayer is not for one community but for the species.

**The Same Command as the Previous Chapter**

Both closing chapters begin *qul aʿūdhu bi-rabb* — say, I seek refuge in the Lord — and for that shared opening they are called *al-Muʿawwidhatān*. Al-Falaq takes refuge with the Lord of the daybreak and lists harms from outside; an-Nās takes refuge with the Lord of people and names a harm from within. Between them the two cover the whole field, which is why the Prophet ﷺ described them as verses the like of which had never been sent down (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 814a), and why the tradition recites them together at night, on journeys, before sleep, and after prayer.

**Seeking Refuge Admits That You Are Not Enough**

Nobody asks for shelter from what he can handle, so the first word of the chapter is a declaration of need. Al-Rāzī read the sūrah as an instruction to take refuge from the illusion of self-sufficiency, which he called the root of sin. The Qur'an's picture of that illusion is Pharaoh, who said, *"I am your lord, the most high!"* (79:24), claimed to know no god for his people but himself (28:38), and boasted that Egypt and its rivers were his (43:51). His three claims — lordship, divinity, sovereignty — are exactly the three titles this chapter assigns to God, over the same kind of people he ruled. The prayer is therefore not only a request for safety but a rejection of every lesser claim to the same three things.

**What the Verse Leaves Unnamed**

Verse 1 has no object of fear in it. The Lord of humankind is invoked, and the sentence stops. The danger arrives three verses later, and even then it is described rather than identified: a whisperer who withdraws, who whispers into hearts, from among jinn and humankind. The withholding has a pastoral effect. A person beginning this prayer spends his first three sentences on lordship, sovereignty, and divinity and only then mentions what frightens him, so that the fear enters a sentence already filled with something else. The Book that opened with a request for help (1:5) closes with two chapters whose only act is asking for it.

## Sūrah an-Nās 114:2

> the Master of humankind

**Malik Adds Authority to Provision**

The first title said Lord; this one says King. *Malik* is the one who commands, judges, disposes, and holds a domain — a different claim from the One who provides and nurtures. A *rabb* maintains; a *malik* rules. The addition matters because the danger in this chapter works on the will, and provision alone does not reach the will. A person can be fed and kept alive and still be commanded by something else; the second title closes that gap by placing him under an authority with the right to be obeyed.

**Three Titles, Three False Refuges Removed**

A reader may take *rabb*, *malik*, and *ilāh* as three words for one thing, since Arabic rhetoric does repeat for emphasis. The commentators did not read it that way, because each title removes a different false refuge. Lordship excludes the belief that a person's condition is nobody's business — that he is unprovided for, or provided for by his own effort alone. Kingship excludes the belief that some other power holds final authority over him, whether a ruler, a custom, or an appetite. Divinity excludes the belief that anything other than God deserves his worship. Each is a live temptation, and a person may hold one while lacking the others; the danger named in this chapter exploits whichever is missing. The order also shows a progression: provision comes first because everyone already accepts it, rule second because it introduces obligation, divinity third because it asks for the heart.

**Kingship in the Qur'an Belongs to the Last Day**

The title has a fixed place in Qur'anic vocabulary, and that place is eschatological. Al-Fātiḥah gives God *"Master of the Day of Judgment"* (1:4), kingship attached to the day when every other authority is suspended. Ṭā Hā states it without qualification: *"Exalted is Allah, the True King!"* (20:114), where *true* separates this kingship from every borrowed one. Al-Ḥashr lists it among the names: *"He is Allah—there is no god except Him: the King, the Most Holy, the All-Perfect..."* (59:23), and al-Jumuʿa opens with creation glorifying *"the King, the Most Holy, the Almighty, the All-Wise"* (62:1). Both of those sūrahs were revealed in Madinah, in a city with rulers, judges, and contested authorities. The pattern is consistent: kingship is asserted most strongly where human kings are in view. This chapter makes the same assertion about the interior, where the contest is quieter. Every human king holds office on loan — *"O Allah! Lord over all authorities! You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from who You please"* (3:26) — and the lender is the Master of humankind.

**A King Whose Court Requires Permission**

Kingship carries rules of access, and the Verse of the Throne states the rule: *"Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?"* (2:255) — a question that expects no answer. That governs the prayer here. The whisperer who works on a human heart has no permission and no standing; he is an intruder, not an ambassador from a rival power. The reciter is not negotiating between two claimants but appealing to the One whose jurisdiction no one contests against an agent who has none. The same verse adds what makes the appeal effective: *"His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him"* (2:255). The jurisdiction invoked is not local, and exercising it costs Him nothing. This is why refuge, not combat, is the correct request: a subject does not expel an intruder from a royal house, he reports him to the owner.

**Master of Persons, Not of Territory**

Every kingship described in Qur'anic narrative is over a land — Egypt under Pharaoh, Sheba under its queen, the kingdoms given to David and Solomon. This verse is different: the object of the kingship is *al-nās*, people. The difference matters because of where the danger sits. A territorial king can guard a border but not a heart. The whisperer attacks from inside a person, and the title raised against him is a title over persons. The specification is the point: the domain in question is the human being, and the sovereignty claimed over him is complete.

**The Fātiḥah's Three Moves, Compressed**

The opening chapter of the Qur'an makes the same three moves in four verses: lordship in *"Lord of all worlds"* (1:2), kingship in *"Master of the Day of Judgment"* (1:4), and divinity in *"You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help"* (1:5). Read together, the first and last chapters of the Book are one prayer at two scales — universal over all worlds and on the Day, then local over one species and inside one heart. The Fātiḥah asks for the straight path; this chapter asks for shelter from what derails those walking it.

## Sūrah an-Nās 114:3

> the God of humankind

**Ilāh Names a Relation, Not a Kind of Being**

*Ilāh* is ordinarily translated "god," and the translation hides the word's grammar. An *ilāh* is not a member of a species; it is whatever a person turns to in devotion, awe, and obedience. That is why the central sentence of Islam negates the word rather than a being: *lā ilāha illā Allāh*, there is no god except God. The claim is not that other gods fail to exist but that nothing else deserves the position. Mecca had idols that were called *āliha*; the testimony denies them the title, not the wood. Used here, the word assigns a relation: God is *ilāh al-nās*, the object of humankind's worship, and the assignment is made over the same people named in the two previous verses.

**Three Relations No One Escapes**

Every human being stands in three relations to power, and the chapter names them in order. He is provided for: something feeds him and keeps him alive. He is governed: something has authority over what he may do. He worships: something receives his ultimate devotion. None of the three is optional, and the only question is their object. A person who denies being provided for still eats; one who denies being ruled still obeys; one who says he worships nothing still devotes himself to something. Al-Rāzī read the sequence as a formula of dependence — my Lord, my King, my God — the three admissions a human being has to make, and making them is itself the refuge.

**Why Divinity Is Added to Lordship and Kingship**

A person can accept the first two titles without the third, since provision is observable and rule is enforced, while neither requires devotion. The Qur'an's account of the Meccan audience says exactly that: they acknowledged God as creator and provider while worshipping others alongside Him. The argument appears as a question whose answer was never disputed: *"Who provides for you from heaven and earth? Who owns ˹your˺ hearing and sight? ... And who conducts every affair?" They will ˹surely˺ say, "Allah"* (10:31). Acknowledging lordship was not the point of contention in Mecca; the contention was over who receives worship. That is why the third title comes last and is the decisive one. *Ilāh al-nās* closes the escape: the One who grows you and rules you is also the One you were made for, as the Qur'an states in a single line, *"I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me"* (51:56).

**The First Audience Already Had Gods**

The verse was first recited to people whose city held a shrine surrounded by three hundred and sixty idols, each called an *ilāh* and each receiving sacrifice, petition, and loyalty. The claim transferred the title, in the hearing of its holders, from the objects in the Kaʿbah to the Lord of humankind. The transfer is total because the phrase is total: not the god of the Arabs, not the god of one tribe, not one god among many, but *ilāh al-nās*, the God of humankind. The Qur'an's case against the idols follows the same line — they cannot create, provide, or hear, and a thing that can do none of these does not answer to the name.

**Obedience Is Worship**

The Qur'an calls following Satan worship, in God's own voice on the Day: *"Did I not command you, O Children of Adam, not to follow Satan, for he is truly your sworn enemy?"* (36:60) — addressed to people who never prostrated to him. The charge is following. That explains why this title sits immediately before the description of the whisperer: his method is suggestion, and the aim of suggestion is compliance. A person who acts on a whisper has, in the Qur'an's vocabulary, obeyed it, and obedience given to anything other than God is worship misdirected. So the third title is not decoration on a prayer for safety; it removes the whisperer's only route. He cannot provide, cannot rule, and cannot be obeyed as a god. What remains is noise in the heart, which the following verses describe and dismiss.

**The Order Is an Argument**

Lord, then King, then God: the sequence moves from what God does for a person, to what God has authority over, to what God deserves. Each step depends on the one before it, and none follows automatically. Later theology organised itself along these same three lines — oneness in lordship, oneness in sovereignty and names, oneness in worship — categories developed centuries afterward that map onto this chapter because the chapter is already built that way. The Meccan failure lay in the third category, and the chapter puts the third last, where the failure was. The order is pastoral as well: a person in fear begins with what he knows, that he is being kept alive, and moves toward what he owes. By the time he reaches the third title, he is not merely protected but oriented.

## Sūrah an-Nās 114:4

> from the evil of the lurking whisperer—

**Khannās Describes a Tactic, Not a Species**

The word is an intensive participle from a root meaning to draw back, to hang behind, to slip out of sight. A *khannās* is not a creature that hides because of what it is; it is one that keeps withdrawing because of what it does, and the form implies repetition — he withdraws, returns, withdraws again. That is a description of behaviour, and it is the only description this verse gives: no shape, no origin, no genealogy. The enemy at the end of the Qur'an is defined by his movement, forward when unnoticed and backward when noticed. An enemy described by his tactics can be countered; an enemy described by his nature could only be fled. What defeats a *khannās* is attention, because his method depends on the moment attention is elsewhere.

**The Same Word Is Used of the Stars**

The root appears once more in the Qur'an, in an oath: *"I do swear by the receding stars—which travel and hide"* (81:15–16). There *al-khunnās* describes bodies that move along a course and withdraw from sight, returning on the same course. The parallel is more than lexical: both describe something that comes and goes on a fixed pattern. The stars retire at dawn and return at night; the whisperer withdraws at remembrance and returns at heedlessness. Arabic oaths of this kind take their force from something visible, and the visible thing here is withdrawal — a phenomenon anyone could watch in the sky every morning. That same word was then given to a retreat the eye cannot see, inside a person, at a moment he does not notice.

**He Withdraws When God Is Remembered**

The exegetical account of the *khannās* is a mechanism: he withdraws from the heart whenever God is remembered, and whispers when the person is heedless. The condition of his presence is the absence of remembrance; the condition of his departure is its return. The Qur'an states the mechanism from the other side: *"Indeed, when Satan whispers to those mindful ˹of Allah˺, they remember ˹their Lord˺ then they start to see ˹things˺ clearly"* (7:201). This is why the chapter is itself an answer to the danger it names. Reciting it is an act of remembrance, and remembrance is what expels the whisperer; the prayer does not merely ask for protection, it performs the condition under which the enemy leaves. That is the mechanism behind the practice of reciting these verses at night, on waking, and after prayer.

**Waswasa Is Quiet, and It Repeats**

The verb *waswasa* means something said quietly and repeatedly, and neither feature is incidental. A whisper is quiet so that it can be mistaken for the hearer's own thought, and repeated so that what was first rejected becomes familiar. That is the difference between a whisper and an argument: an argument presents itself as coming from outside and invites assessment, while a whisper presents itself as arising from within and invites agreement. A person who acts on it usually believes he thought of it. The Qur'an's other descriptions of Satan's work keep the same character — he makes evil deeds appear attractive (16:63), causes people to forget (58:19), lures with false hopes (47:25), makes them slip (3:155), and sows discord between them (17:53). Every one of these is a gradual operation on perception rather than a confrontation with the will. Nothing is forced; everything is arranged until the person chooses it himself.

**He Is Named, Not Only Described**

The Qur'an does not treat the whisperer as an abstraction. He is the chief deceiver people are warned not to be deceived by (31:33; 35:5), a sworn enemy to humankind (2:168; 17:53), one who watches with his soldiers *"from where you cannot see them"* (7:27). The personal language matters: a force can only be endured, but an agent can be refused. The limits of this agent's power are stated plainly: *"He certainly has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord. His authority is only over those who take him as a patron and who—under his influence—associate ˹others˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺"* (16:99–100). The operative word is *sulṭān*, authority. He has access but no jurisdiction; he can reach the heart but cannot rule it, and his authority exists only where it has been granted. That is the moral weight of this chapter: a person who is tempted is not thereby compromised, but a person who complies is. The prayer for refuge is a refusal of patronage, said in advance and repeated daily.

**The First Whisper and the Last Prayer**

The danger this chapter names opened human history. *"Then Satan tempted them in order to expose what was hidden of their nakedness. He said, 'Your Lord has forbidden this tree to you only to prevent you from becoming angels or immortals'"* (7:20). The method is identical: a quiet suggestion, repeated, framed as information about God's intentions. The Qur'an's final word on the subject is not an analysis of the danger but a request for shelter from it, and the cases in between — Joseph's brothers, the prisoner who forgot to mention his companion's case to the king (12:42) — are instances of the same operation. Ending the Book here is a statement about the human condition: the oldest attack is still running, it works from inside, and the answer to it is a sentence anyone can say.

## Sūrah an-Nās 114:5

> who whispers into the hearts of humankind—

**The Location Is the Chest, Not the Ear**

*Ṣudūr*, chests, is the Qur'an's word for a person's interior — where intentions and secrets are kept, including the things a person conceals from himself. Placing the whisper there rather than in the ear is precise. A whisper heard as sound comes from outside and can be identified by its accent, timing, and source. A whisper that arises in the chest has none of those marks; it presents itself as the person's own thought, already formed, already familiar. That is the difficulty the verse names: not that a person is addressed by an enemy, but that he cannot tell the address from himself. Every defence that depends on recognising an external voice fails at that point, which is why the chapter's answer is not vigilance but refuge.

**Two Sources of Whispering in the Qur'an**

Qāf attributes the same activity to the soul itself: *"Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ created humankind and ˹fully˺ know what their souls whisper to them, and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein"* (50:16). This verse attributes the whispering to the *khannās*. The tradition reconciled the two by distinguishing conditions of the soul: the inner whispering of 50:16 comes from the soul that commands evil, *al-nafs al-ammāra bi-l-sūʾ*, which Joseph's confession names — *"indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil, except those shown mercy by my Lord"* (12:53). Al-Zukhruf states how the two agents connect: *"And whoever turns a blind eye to the Reminder of the Most Compassionate, We place at the disposal of each a devilish one as their close associate"* (43:36). The companion is assigned in response to a turning away. What begins as a person's own inclination is then taken up by another, and the two become hard to tell apart — exactly the condition this verse describes.

**Satan Moves Through a Person Like Blood**

A report in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī gives the relation in an image. The Prophet ﷺ, walking at night with Ṣafiyyah during his seclusion in the mosque, saw a man from the Anṣār look at them and called him over: "Come here — she is Ṣafiyyah. Satan circulates in the son of Adam as his blood circulates in him" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2039). The image says what the theology requires: the whisperer is not outside a person's life as a stranger is, but moves through it as blood does, reaching every part without being visible in any of them. That is why this verse places the whisper in the chest rather than at the door.

**No Wall Can Be Built Where the Attack Happens**

External harms have external defences — a house has a door, a road has company, a night has a lamp, and the previous chapter listed them. The harm described here has none of these, because its location is the person himself. A reciter cannot inspect his own interior from outside it. He can notice effects — a sudden resentment, an appetite he did not have an hour ago, a thought about God he would not say aloud — but he cannot verify their origin, and the verification would have to be done by the same faculty under attack. That is the pastoral reason the chapter takes the form it does. It offers no technique for identifying whispers, which would require a standpoint nobody has. It offers an address: three titles of God and one request. A person who cannot diagnose his interior can still place it under a jurisdiction he acknowledges.

**The Fourth Naming of Humankind**

*Al-nās* appears in verses 1, 2, 3, and 5, each time fixing the same object under a different relation — provided for, ruled, worshipping, attacked. The repetition also settles who is whispered to: the verse does not say "the hearts of the wicked" or "of the heedless" but "of humankind," which includes the Prophet who was told to say it. The chest is also where the three titles of verses 1–3 take effect. Lordship is received by a person who depends on provision, kingship by a person who obeys, divinity by a person who devotes himself, and none of these happens in the hands or the tongue before it happens inside. The whisperer attacks the very place where those three relations are held, which is why the prayer states the titles before naming him. Against that, the Qur'an sets the opposite operation on the same ground: *"Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort"* (13:28). Remembrance settles the chest as the whisper unsettles it, and the chapter asks which of the two will hold it.

## Sūrah an-Nās 114:6

> from among jinn and humankind.”

**The Last Word of the Qur'an Is Humankind**

The codex ends on *al-nās*, the word that opened the chapter's three titles. A Book that begins with a request for guidance along a path closes with a request for protection on it, and the final word belongs to the traveller rather than the road. Nothing follows it — no ruling, no narrative, no description of the Day. The last thing the revelation asks of its reader is that he say it: take refuge, in the first person, for himself, without qualification.

**Two Ways to Read the Phrase**

The grammar of *min al-jinnati wa-l-nās* allows two readings, and the commentators recorded both. On the first, the phrase describes the whisperer: whisperers exist among jinn and among people. On the second, it describes those whispered to: the whispering reaches hearts among jinn as well as among people. The first has stronger support, since the Qur'an states the existence of human devils explicitly: *"And so We have made for every prophet enemies—devilish humans and jinn—whispering to one another with elegant words of deception"* (6:112). That verse supplies both the pairing of species and the activity, so the phrase here reads naturally as an extension of it. The second reading is not excluded, and the practical difference is small: on either reading, the one reciting asks protection from a whisperer who may not be a jinn.

**There Are Devils Among Human Beings**

6:112 is the decisive text for the first reading, and its content is not metaphorical. A human whisperer needs no supernatural access; he has a voice, a friendship, a suggestion, and an interest in the outcome. The colleague who flatters a plan, the relative who repeats a grievance, the companion who makes a forbidden thing look like an opportunity — each is doing what the verse describes, and each falls inside the refuge requested here. That extension is what makes the chapter usable in every setting. Without this verse, the danger named in verses 4 and 5 would be limited to a cosmology; with it, the prayer covers a species the reciter meets daily, and covers the case where he cannot tell which he is facing.

**The Jinn Also Heard This Book**

The other species named here is not a background creature in the Qur'an; it is an audience. Al-Jinn opens with the report: *"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'It has been revealed to me that a group of jinn listened ˹to the Quran˺, and said ˹to their fellow jinn˺: "Indeed, we have heard a wondrous recitation. It leads to Right Guidance so we believed in it..."'"* (72:1–2). Al-Aḥqāf records the same event from the other side: a group of jinn sent to listen, told to be quiet, who returned to their people as warners (46:29) urging them to respond and believe (46:31). And the purpose of both species is stated in one sentence: *"I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me"* (51:56). Jinn and humankind are made for the same thing, addressed by the same revelation, exposed to the same enemy, and given the same refuge — which is why the final verse of the Qur'an names both.

**Seeking Refuge With Jinn Only Made the Harm Worse**

Al-Jinn contains the negative counterpart of this chapter, reported by the jinn themselves: *"And some men used to seek refuge with some jinn—so they increased each other in wickedness"* (72:6). Men in the pre-Islamic desert, stopping at a place they feared, would address its spirit for protection, and the result was an increase in arrogance and sin on both sides. The verse explains what the practice did to each party: the man became more afraid and more dependent on a power he had no standing with, and the jinn became more contemptuous of him for asking them for what only God can give. A false refuge does not merely fail; it strengthens the thing it was meant to answer. The two closing chapters of the Qur'an are the correction, given to people who had been doing the wrong thing for generations: the same need for protection from an invisible harm, met with a different address — not the spirit of the valley, but the Lord, King, and God of humankind.

**Whether "People" Includes Jinn**

A further report bears on the second reading. Some commentators held that *nās* is not restricted to human beings, arguing from the Qur'an's use of *rijāl*, "men," in 72:6, where the men who sought refuge and the jinn they sought it from appear in one sentence. If a word ordinarily applied to people can be applied to jinn, they reasoned, *nās* may cover both. The argument is lexical and not decisive: in 72:6 *rijāl* refers to the human men, while the jinn are the object of their appeal rather than the subject of the word. The dominant sense of *nās* throughout the Qur'an is humankind, and the pairing of jinn with *nās* in this verse and in 51:56 treats them as two species rather than one class. The wider reading was kept because it makes the prayer broader; the narrower reading remains the plain one, and it is enough — the whisperer reaches both species, and the refuge is offered to whoever says it.

**Two Species, One Enemy, One Prayer**

The last verse leaves the Qur'an with a picture of a created world that is shared. Jinn and humankind are both made for worship, both addressed by revelation, both watched by an enemy they cannot see, and both given the same words of refuge. The practice attached to those words is continuous. ʿUqba ibn ʿĀmir reported the Messenger of Allah ﷺ saying that verses had been sent down the like of which had never been seen — the two chapters of refuge (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 814a) — and reported him using them in wind and darkness on the road between al-Juḥfa and al-Abwāʾ (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 1463). ʿĀʾisha reported his nightly practice of reciting them and al-Ikhlāṣ into his joined palms, breathing on them, and wiping them over his body three times (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5056). The Book closes with those words still being said: its last chapter is not a conclusion but an instruction, and the instruction has been carried out every night since it was revealed.
