# Sūrah Ṣād (Chapter 38) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Chieftains in the Chamber of Abū Ṭālib**

The historical occasion of Sūrah Ṣād is recorded by Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Tirmidhī about the final illness of the Prophet's protective uncle, Abū Ṭālib. Sensing his imminent demise, twenty-five prominent chieftains of the Quraysh—including Abū Jahl, ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah, and Umayyah ibn Khalaf—came to his sickbed, demanding a negotiated settlement with his nephew: *“Let him leave us to our gods, and we will leave him to his!”* When the Prophet ﷺ arrived, he looked at them and said: *“O my uncle, I ask of them only a single sentence: if they utter it, the whole of Arabia will submit to them, and the non-Arabs will pay them tribute!”* Abū Jahl eagerly replied: *“By your father, we will give you that sentence and ten times more! What is it?”* The Prophet replied: *“Say: There is no deity worthy of worship except Allah, and abandon whatever you worship beside Him.”* The chieftains clapped their hands in outrage, rose, and stormed out, exclaiming: *“Has he made the gods into a single God? Indeed, this is a truly astonishing thing!”* (38:5). Verses 1–8 descended immediately, exposing their refusal not as intellectual doubt, but as wounded tribal pride (*bal al-ladhīna kafarū fī ʿizzatin wa-shiqāq*).

**The Structural Modulation and the Kings Who Repented**

The architecture of Sūrah Ṣād is organized around four distinct movements. The opening movement (1–16) refutes the conspiracy of the Qurayshi elite who marvel that a warner has come from among themselves, reminding them of the ruined ancient coalitions (Noah, ʿĀd, Pharaoh of the Stakes, Thamūd, Lot, and the Forest Dwellers) who were wiped out by a single screaming blast. The second movement (17–40) presents the royal patterns of David and Solomon:

- David (17–26): Endowed with physical strength and continuous penitence (*awwāb*), accompanied by mountains and birds glorifying God at sunrise and evening, his kingdom strengthened with wisdom and decisive speech (*faṣl al-khiṭāb*). It recounts the dramatic parable of the two litigants who scaled the walls of his sanctuary: one possessing ninety-nine ewes demanding the single ewe of his brother. David judges swiftly that the demand is unjust, suddenly perceives that the situation is a divine test of his own heart, and falls down in prostration, turning to God in repentance (*wa-kharra rākiʿan wa-anāb*, 38:24). God forgives him and establishes his royal mandate: *“O David, indeed We have made you a viceregent upon the earth, so judge between the people in truth and do not follow [your own] desire”* (38:26).

- Solomon (30–40): Bestowed upon David as an excellent, penitent servant (*niʿma al-ʿabd, innahū awwāb*). Absorbed one evening in the review of noble, pure-bred war steeds until the sun slipped behind the veil of horizon, Solomon realized they had distracted him from the evening remembrance of his Lord. He ordered them returned, stroked their necks and legs in renunciation, and prayed for an unique sovereign kingdom: *“My Lord, forgive me and grant me a kingdom such as will not belong to anyone after me”* (38:35)—answered by God with the subjugation of the wind blowing gently at his command and the construction abilities of the jinn. The third movement (41–48) portrays the supreme archetype of patience: Job (*Ayyūb*), struck with agonizing physical disease and the loss of family and wealth, crying out: *“Indeed, Satan has touched me with distress and torment!”* Commanded to strike the earth with his foot, a cool spring gushes forth for washing and drinking, restoring his health and doubling his family as a mercy, honored with the divine verdict: *“Indeed, We found him patient, an excellent servant. Indeed, he was one who repeatedly turned [to Us]”* (38:44). The fourth movement (49–88) contrasts the lush gardens of Eden against the scalding fluids of Hell, and concludes with the primordial drama of Adam and Iblīs: God commanding the angels to prostrate to the man fashioned from clay into whom the Divine Spirit was breathed, and Iblīs defiantly refusing out of racial arrogance: *“I am better than him! You created me from fire and created him from clay!”* (38:76). The sūrah closes with the Prophet disclaiming all worldly ambition: *“Say, 'I do not ask you for the Qur'an any payment, and I am not of the pretentious. It is but a reminder to the worlds'”* (38:86–87).

## Verse 38:1

> Ṣãd. By the Quran, full of reminders

**Dhikr Carries Several Meanings and the Verse Uses All of Them**

The word translated *full of reminders* is dhikr, and its range is wider than the English suggests. It can mean honour and renown, the standing a thing has among people; it can mean clarification, the act of making something plain; and it is also used as a title for the revealed Book itself. Each sense has a home in the Qur'an. Of honour: *“Surely this ˹Quran˺ is a glory for you and your people. And you will ˹all˺ be questioned ˹about it˺”* (43:44), and *“and elevated your renown for you?”* (94:4). Of the Book as Reminder: *“We have surely revealed to you a Book, in which there is glory for you. Will you not then understand?”* (21:10).

**Three Ways the Letter Has Been Read**

Exegetes cluster their proposals into three families. The first reads Ṣād as a pointer to names of God that begin with the same letter, such as al-Ṣamad, the One everything needs and who needs nothing, al-Ṣādiq, the Truthful, and al-Ṣāniʿ, the Maker. The second reads it as an abbreviation standing for a whole sentence: ṣadaqa Allāh, God has spoken the truth, or ṣadaqa Muḥammad, Muḥammad has spoken truly. The third takes it simply as a name, either a name of the Qur'an or the name of this chapter.

**An Oath Waiting for Its Answer**

That suppression is a rhetorical gain, not a gap. A stated answer can be argued with; an unfinished oath leaves the hearer completing it privately, and a conclusion a man reaches inside his own head arrives with more weight than one handed to him. Sūrah 50 is built the same way: *“Qãf. By the glorious Quran!”* (50:1), and then the answer is displaced into the reaction of the deniers, *“yet the deniers are astonished that a warner has come to them from among themselves”* (50:2). The pattern is a signature of the Meccan openings. The Book swears by itself, which is unusual — a witness who testifies on his own behalf — and the very strangeness of that act is part of the challenge the chapter is issuing.

**They Asked for a Reminder and Then Received One**

The chapter before this one ends with a wish spoken by the same audience: *“If only we had a Reminder like ˹those of˺ earlier peoples, we would have truly been Allah's devoted servants.”* (37:168–169). Sūrah Ṣād opens by swearing on the thing they said they wanted. The oath is so a direct answer to a request, and the sharpest possible one: the reminder has arrived, in their own language, recited in their own streets, and the opening letter is drawn from the alphabet they use every day.

## Verse 38:2

> ˹This is the truth,˺ yet the disbelievers are ˹entrenched˺ in arrogance and opposition

**ʿIzzah: The Pride That Cannot Accept Direction**

Arrogance renders ʿizzah, a word that in ordinary Arabic means strength, honour and the kind of standing that makes a man hard to move. Used of a human attitude toward truth, it names something narrower and more damaging: the conviction that one is answerable to no one but oneself. The same noun appears in al-Baqarah in a scene that reads like a clinical description of the condition: *“When it is said to them, ‘Fear Allah,’ pride carries them off to sin. Hell will be their proper place. What an evil place to rest!”* (2:206).

Notice what the word does to counsel. A man in that state is not ignorant of the advice he is rejecting; the advice has to be spoken to him first, and then it becomes the occasion of his sin. ʿIzzah is therefore not a lack of information but a refusal of rank.

**Shiqāq: Breaking Off as a Way of Standing**

Opposition translates shiqāq, from a root meaning to split or cleave. The word carries a physical image: two parties on opposite sides of a fissure, each holding its ground. Unlike a mere disagreement, shiqāq describes a position chosen for its own sake, the state of being against, and the commentators gloss it as enmity, defiance and disobedience rolled together. It appears elsewhere in the Qur'an where separation from God's guidance is in view (2:137; 2:176; 22:53; 41:52).

The nuance that makes the word worth its place is this: you cannot be in shiqāq with someone you consider beneath you. Opposition of this kind assumes that the other party has standing, that he must be answered, resisted and outlasted. The Quraysh did not ignore the Prophet; they organised against him, which is itself an admission of weight. Combined with ʿizzah, the pair describes a complete psychology. The first term says *I answer to no one*; the second says *I will spend my strength making sure of it*.

**The Truth Was Never in Question**

By placing *˹This is the truth˺* before the diagnosis, the verse removes the possibility of a fair hearing being denied. The reminder they asked for has come; the Book is what it claims to be; the obstacle is internal. Later the chapter puts the same point as a question: *“Has the Reminder been revealed ˹only˺ to him out of ˹all of˺ us?”* (38:8), and answers it by describing doubt rather than by supplying further proof, because further proof was never what was missing.

**The Same Refusal, Named in al-Baqarah**

Al-Baqarah contains the closest parallel to this diagnosis, and the comparison sharpens both passages. There the trigger is a two-word urging: *“Fear Allah”* (2:206). Here the trigger is an entire Book sworn upon. The mechanism is identical in each case — counsel arrives, pride takes hold, sin follows — and the difference in scale tells the reader that the mechanism is indifferent to how much evidence it is given. A man in ʿizzah can be moved by nothing, and a man outside it can be moved by a sentence.

The Qur'an draws this moral itself when it describes those who see the punishment and then believe, since belief at that point has no effect whatever. It is the posture, not the quantity of proof, that decides.

## Verse 38:3

> ˹Imagine˺ how many peoples We destroyed before them, and they cried out when it was too late to escape

**The Cry Arrives After the Deadline**

The second half of the verse is a single compressed image: they cried out when escape had gone. The timing, not the cry, carries the meaning. A cry is the natural response of a creature in danger, and the verse grants that the cry was real — these people did call, and they did mean it. What it could not do is reverse a decision already executed.

The Qur'an returns to this scene with uncomfortable specificity: *“But as soon as We seize their elite with torment, they start to cry for help. ˹They will be told,˺ ‘Do not cry for help today. Surely you will never be saved from Us’”* (23:64–65). And again: *“When the wrongdoers sensed ˹the arrival of˺ Our torment, they started to run away from their cities. ˹They were told,˺ ‘Do not run away! Return to your luxuries and your homes, so you may be questioned ˹about your fate˺’”* (21:12–13). The reply in both passages is not anger but arithmetic. The window for the cry was the years before it; the cry after the window is simply noise.

**Pharaoh Believed When the Water Closed Over Him**

No case makes the point more sharply than the drowning of Pharaoh. The Qur'an preserves his confession word for word: *“But as Pharaoh was drowning, he cried out, ‘I believe that there is no god except that in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am ˹now˺ one of those who submit.’”* (10:90). The confession is complete, orthodox and sincere in the moment it is uttered. It is also useless, and the next verse says why: *“˹He was told,˺ ‘Now ˹you believe˺? But you always disobeyed and were one of the corruptors’”* (10:91).

The rebuke is not that the faith was false but that it was late, and lateness here is not a matter of minutes. A belief produced by an unavoidable event carries no information about the believer, because it could not have been otherwise. The same principle is stated as a general law elsewhere: *“But their faith was of no benefit to them when they saw Our torment. This has ˹always˺ been Allah's way ˹of dealing˺ with His ˹wicked˺ servants. Then and there the disbelievers were in ˹total˺ loss”* (40:85). Certainty purchased at the price of every alternative has no moral content.

**Warning Is What Makes Seizure Just**

The verse is often read as a threat, and it is one, but its logic is legal before it is fearsome. Destruction is described here as preceded by a term, a respite and a messenger, and it is the combination that makes the outcome an act of justice rather than a stroke of fate. The Qur'an is emphatic that no community is taken unawares: *“We never destroyed a town without it having had warners as a reminder”* (26:208–209), and the phrase *it was too late to escape* only has force because a time existed in which escape was available.

**Running Away Is Not Repentance**

The reply given to the fleeing towns — *“Do not run away! Return to your luxuries and your homes”* (21:13) — is one of the strangest in the Qur'an, and it contains the whole moral of this verse. They are sent back to the very things that absorbed them, and the reason given is so that they may be questioned. Flight from the punishment is not flight from the account. The account requires a person who stayed, who could be asked what he did with his house, his wealth and his years, and who answered.

The verse that lists them later in this same chapter names six communities in two breaths (38:12–13), and that list is offered as a sample rather than a total.

## Verse 38:4

> Now, the pagans are astonished that a warner has come to them from among themselves. And the disbelievers say, “This is a magician, a total liar

**Astonishment Placed in the Wrong Direction**

The verse records a feeling before it records a sentence. Astonishment — ʿajab, the arrest of the mind by something it did not expect — is a legitimate response to a genuine novelty, and a warner is a genuine novelty. What the verse objects to is the direction of the surprise. The astonishing thing, on any honest reckoning, is the message: one God, a resurrection, a book that recites itself and cannot be matched.

Sūrah Qāf opens with the identical structure: *“˹All will be resurrected,˺ yet the deniers are astonished that a warner has come to them from among themselves”* (50:2). The two chapters, revealed close together, share the diagnosis because Makkah kept producing it.

**"From Among Themselves" Was the Real Objection**

Two complaints hide inside that phrase, and the Qur'an answers both elsewhere. The first is class: an orphan of modest means has been chosen over the notables of two cities. The second is species: a human being has been chosen over an angel. On the first, the objection is stated outright in al-Zukhruf: *“And they exclaimed, ‘If only this Quran was revealed to a great man from ˹one of˺ the two cities!’”* (43:31). On the second, the objection is even blunter: *“And nothing has prevented people from believing when guidance comes to them except their protest: ‘Has Allah sent a human as a messenger?’”* (17:94).

The Qur'an's answer to the second is practical rather than defensive. An angel would not have been an usable model. It would have required disguising itself as a man, which would leave the audience exactly as confused as before: *“And if We had sent an angel, We would have certainly made it ˹assume the form of˺ a man—leaving them more confused than they already are”* (6:9). On the first, the answer is a question about who distributes rank at all: *“Is it they who distribute your Lord's mercy?”* (43:32). Both objections assume that revelation is an appointment made by social consensus. Neither explains what a prophet is for.

**Magician and Liar: Two Words That Travel**

The charge is short and it is not original. *Magician* and *liar* are the two labels every rejected messenger receives, and the Qur'an says so in a generalising verse: *“Similarly, no messenger came to those before them without being told: ‘A magician or a madman!’”* (51:52). The pairing is efficient for the accusers, because the two words cover both possibilities at once: either he is producing a deception, or he is producing a delusion, and in either case the content need not be examined.

The charge against Moses is worded almost identically: *“The chiefs of Pharaoh's people said, ‘He is indeed a skilled magician’”* (7:109), and Pharaoh repeats it to his court: *“Pharaoh said to the chiefs around him, ‘He is indeed a skilled magician’”* (26:34). The charge against Jesus is the same in substance: *“How I prevented the Children of Israel from harming you when you came to them with clear proofs and the disbelievers among them said, ‘This is nothing but pure magic.’”* (5:110). A label that has been applied to Moses, Jesus and now Muḥammad is not evidence about any of them; it is evidence about the people who need it.

**The Warner's Credentials, Stated in One Clause**

The same point is made in a rebuke to those who rejected earlier messengers on the identical ground: *“They argued, ‘You are no more than humans like us!’”* (14:10), and *“The people replied, ‘You are only humans like us, and the Most Compassionate has not revealed anything. You are simply lying!’”* (36:15). In each case the humanity of the messenger is offered as a disqualification, and in each case it is the very thing that makes his warning intelligible. A warner from among them is the only kind of warner they could have understood.

## Verse 38:5

> Has he reduced ˹all˺ the gods to One God? Indeed, this is something totally astonishing.”

**The Single Demand They Could Not Concede**

Behind the accusation of magic lies the actual demand, and this verse states it in the accusers' own words. Everything the Prophet asked of Makkah could be negotiated except one thing: the number of gods. Trade, marriage alliances, pilgrimage revenues, the standing of clans — all of it might have survived a reform. The reduction of many deities to one could not, because the number was the load-bearing wall of the city's religious and economic order.

Ibn Kathīr, commenting on this very verse, records from Abū Jaʿfar b. Jarīr al-Ṭabarī, on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās, that when Abū Ṭālib fell ill, men of Quraysh including Abū Jahl came in and complained that the Prophet was insulting their gods. He was sent for, and said: *O uncle, all I want from them is one word which, if they say it, the Arabs will become their followers and the non-Arabs will pay them the jizyah.* They said: *One word? Yes, by your father — and ten words. What is it?* Abū Ṭālib asked what the word was, and the Prophet answered *lā ilāha illa Llāh*.

The chain of the report runs through the interpretive tradition rather than the canonical collections, and its grading is not stated by Ibn Kathīr at this point.

**Why One God Astonished the Chiefs**

The Qur'an marks this pattern as a regularity rather than a scandal: *“Whenever We sent a warner to a society, its elite would say, ‘We truly reject what you have been sent with’”* (34:34). The elite speak first and the multitude echoes. Understanding that order explains why the sūrah spends its next three verses on the chiefs' reasoning rather than on the crowd's confusion. The argument being refuted is the one made in the council, not the one repeated in the street.

Read back through the chapter, the chiefs' astonishment turns out to be honest reporting.

**The Charge of Invention Meets a Standing Challenge**

Once the message is judged novel, the only remaining explanation for the chiefs is that it was made. The accusation of fabrication runs through the Meccan sūrahs like a refrain: *“No, he has fabricated it!”* (21:5). *“This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but a fabrication which he made up with the help of others.”* (25:4); *“Or do they say, ‘He has fabricated it!’?”* (32:3); *“They also say, ‘This ˹Quran˺ is no more than a fabricated lie.’”* (34:43). The charge was brought against Noah in the same terms: *“He is no more than a man who has fabricated a lie about Allah, and we will never believe in him.”* (23:38).

The Qur'an's answer is not a denial but an open invitation, repeated at increasing length as the objection persists. First one chapter: *“Produce one sûrah like it then, and seek help from whoever you can— other than Allah—if what you say is true!”* (10:38). A fabrication can be imitated, and the challenge is addressed to a city of poets who would have relished the chance. Its persistence across the Meccan period is itself an argument; nobody issues a public, testable dare about a forgery he expects to be exposed.

## Verse 38:6

> The chiefs among them went forth saying, “Carry on, and stand firm in devotion to your gods. Certainly this is just a scheme ˹for power˺

**"Stand Firm": Patience Spent in the Wrong Place**

The slogan the chiefs carry is built on ṣabr, the same root the Qur'an uses everywhere for the virtue of the believers — endurance, steadiness, holding your ground when it costs you. The instruction is to be steadfast *in devotion to your gods*, and the irony is complete. The word that elsewhere describes the prophet's patience under insult (38:17) is here deployed as a rallying cry for idolatry.

**"A Thing Desired": The Motive They Invented**

The clause translated *this is just a scheme* rests on an expression meaning, literally, this is a thing wanted — something aimed at, something sought. The accusation is about intention rather than content: the man is after something, and the message is the instrument. What he is after, in the chiefs' reading, is position — the leadership of the Arabs, the authority to speak for the sanctuary, the standing that comes from being obeyed.

This move is worth isolating because it is unfalsifiable in a convenient direction. A man who demands wealth confirms it; a man who refuses wealth has merely hidden his aim better. Nothing he does can count against the charge, and a charge with that property is not a hypothesis but a shield. The Qur'an names the shield elsewhere when it describes people who corrupt and call it reform: *“When they are told, ‘Do not spread corruption in the land,’ they reply, ‘We are only peace-makers!’”* (2:11). Self-description is offered as evidence, and the offer is accepted by nobody who is checking.

**The Ambition Charge Against a Man Who Asked for Nothing**

The specific form of the accusation collapses against a fact the Qur'an states again and again: the Prophet took no payment. He is told to say it in his own words — *“I do not ask you ˹Meccans˺ for a reward for this ˹message˺”* (42:23), *“If I had ever asked you for a reward, you could keep it. My reward is only from Allah. And He is a Witness over all things.”* (34:47) — and earlier prophets said the same thing to their own peoples: *“O my people! I do not ask you for a payment for this ˹message˺. My reward is only from Allah.”* (11:29).

An ambition that takes no money, no office and no immunity is a strange ambition. The chiefs' reading of the motive required them to ignore the visible record of the man's life — his poverty, his losses, the boycott of his clan — in favour of a motive he never displayed. The Qur'an's own summary of the situation is that they were not asking him for a reward, and the message was free: *“even though you are not asking them for a reward for this ˹Quran˺. It is only a reminder to the whole world.”* (12:104).

## Verse 38:7

> We have never heard of this in the previous faith. This is nothing but a fabrication

**An Argument From Silence, Stated Flatly**

Two sentences, and between them lies the whole of the chiefs' case. The first reports an absence: we have not heard this before. The second draws a conclusion from it: therefore it is invented. No premise about God, no objection to monotheism, no examination of the Book — only an appeal to what the speakers' own memory contains.

The inference is invalid in a way worth stating precisely, because the Qur'an depends on the reader seeing it. Absence of prior hearing establishes only that the message is new to the hearer. It says nothing about whether the message is true, since every true thing was once unheard of, and every false custom in history was at some point an innovation too. The move from *we have not heard it* to *it is fabricated* substitutes the limits of an audience for the state of the world. Pharaoh's court made the identical move in identical words: *“We have never heard of this in ˹the history of˺ our forefathers.”* (28:36).

**Novelty Treated as Disproof**

The emotional engine behind the argument is worth naming, because it is not stupid; it is conservative in the ordinary sense. In a society where religion is inherited along with land, water rights and clan standing, novelty is genuinely dangerous.

Its constant claim is continuity: *“In fact, it is a confirmation of what came before, and an explanation of the Scripture. It is, without a doubt, from the Lord of all worlds.”* (10:37). The message is presented as the oldest thing available, the faith of Abraham and Moses, which the chiefs had simply stopped hearing.

**Fabrication Is the Fallback Position**

Once novelty is established as the fact, invention becomes the only available explanation, and the second sentence follows as a matter of course. The charge of fabrication is the standard recourse when a message cannot be answered. The Meccan sūrahs record it in almost every register: *“No, he has fabricated it!”* (21:5). *“This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but a fabrication which he made up with the help of others.”* (25:4); *“This ˹Quran˺ is no more than a fabricated lie.”* (34:43); *“Or do they say, ‘He has fabricated a lie about Allah!’?”* (42:24).

Noah received the same verdict: *“He is no more than a man who has fabricated a lie about Allah, and we will never believe in him.”* (23:38). The repetition across centuries is the Qur'an's point. A charge that recurs unchanged in every age, applied to men who never met and had nothing in common but their message, describes the accusers rather than the accused.

**The Answer Is an Open Challenge, Not a Denial**

The Book's reply to the fabrication charge is one of the most unusual in religious literature: it invites the test. *“Or do they claim, ‘He made it up!’? Tell them ˹O Prophet˺, ‘Produce one sûrah like it then. Seek help from whoever you can— other than Allah—if what you say is true!’”* (10:38), and again at greater length: *“Produce ten fabricated sûrahs like it and seek help from whoever you can —other than Allah—if what you say is true!”* (11:13).

## Verse 38:8

> Has the Reminder been revealed ˹only˺ to him out of ˹all of˺ us?” In fact, they are ˹only˺ in doubt of My ˹revealed˺ Reminder. In fact, ˹they do so because˺ they have not yet tasted My punishment

**Why They Can Afford Their Confidence**

The Qur'an names this mechanism elsewhere with a technical precision. Delay is described as a form of drawing-on, in which prosperity is mistaken for approval and the silence of heaven is read as consent. The same explanation appears in the reply to those who demand haste: *“They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the punishment. Had it not been for a time already set, the punishment would have certainly come to them ˹at once˺. But it will definitely take them by surprise when they least expect it.”* (29:53). Confidence built on the absence of consequence is the most fragile confidence available, and the verse says so by pointing at the very fact that produced it.

**Jealousy Wearing the Clothes of Method**

The substance of the complaint is envy, and the earlier verse in the same speech showed the other half of it — astonishment that a human being should be sent at all (38:4). Here the target is narrower: not the species but the person. Why this man, from among all of us, with his modest means and his lack of standing?

The same objection is voiced explicitly elsewhere: *“And they exclaimed, ‘If only this Quran was revealed to a great man from ˹one of˺ the two cities!’”* (43:31). The Qur'an identifies the feeling underneath with unusual directness: *“Or do they envy the people for Allah's bounties?”* (4:54). Envy of this kind is not a religious position; it is a ranking dispute. The chiefs are not saying the message is false, they are saying the recipient is wrong, and the two claims are often confused because the second is easier to admit in public.

**Doubt About a Choice Answered by Ownership**

What the chiefs' question actually raises is the question of who decides, and the two verses that follow answer it by asking who owns anything. The reply is not that the Prophet was more deserving than the notables, which would concede that deservingness is measurable in Meccan terms. It is that the treasuries of mercy and the kingdom of the heavens are not in their hands to distribute.

This refusal of the premise is what makes the answer final. A ranking dispute can be argued indefinitely, because both sides appeal to the same scale; a dispute about who holds the scale cannot. The Qur'an's answer to *why him* is that the choice is not on the market, and the follow-up question about climbing to heaven (38:10) makes the point physical: if you think you can appoint prophets, go and fetch the appointment yourself.

## Verse 38:9

> Or ˹is it because˺ they possess the treasuries of the mercy of your Lord—the Almighty, the Giver ˹of all bounties˺

**A Question That Answers Itself Before It Ends**

The verse is a rhetorical question in the strictest sense: it asks whether the objectors hold the keys to divine mercy, and the form of the question assumes that nobody could answer yes. Nothing needs to be added after it. The hearer supplies the denial, and supplying it himself makes the point land with a force no assertion could manage.

The construction is the standard Qur'anic method for dealing with a claim it considers absurd. Rather than stating that the claim is false, it exhibits the claim in a form so plainly unanswerable that the claimant is embarrassed by it. The same device governs the verse that follows, which asks whether the objectors own the heavens and the earth (38:10). Both questions are aimed at the same hidden premise in the objection of the previous verse: that the distribution of prophecy is a matter for human judgement.

**Treasuries of Mercy: The Image and Its Reach**

The broader reading, which fits the Qur'an's usage elsewhere, widens the treasury to every mercy God gives, of which prophethood is the highest instance. The same image appears in a rebuke that covers both: *“Is it they who distribute your Lord's mercy? We ˹alone˺ have distributed their ˹very˺ livelihood among them in this worldly life and raised some of them in rank above others so that some may employ others in service. ˹But˺ your Lord's mercy is far better than whatever ˹wealth˺ they amass.”* (43:32). The verse joins the two levels deliberately, moving from livelihood to mercy in a single breath, and it makes the same move here by naming God's giving without restricting it.

**If They Held the Keys, They Would Not Give**

The Qur'an does not stop at the ownership question; it adds a judgement about what these particular men would do with the treasury if they had it. *“Say ˹to them, O Prophet˺, ‘Even if you were to possess the ˹infinite˺ treasuries of my Lord's mercy, then you would certainly withhold ˹them˺, fearing they would run out—for humankind is ever stingy!’”* (17:100). The verse is a psychological observation presented as a certainty, and it completes the argument begun here.

---

## Verse 38:10

> Or ˹is it because˺ the kingdom of the heavens and the earth and everything in between belongs to them? Let them then climb their way ˹to heaven, if their claim is true˺

**An Invitation That Is Actually a Refusal**

*Let them then climb their way* is phrased as a permission and functions as a verdict. Arabic rhetoric frequently uses this imperative form to mean that the thing commanded is impossible, and the effect is stronger than a flat denial: the objector is invited to try what he has implicitly claimed the power to do. If the distribution of revelation belongs to you, go and take your share of it directly.

The command has an unstated premise that gives it its sting. Revelation comes from above; if these men controlled the domain above, ascent would be routine for them. Their inability to perform the act they are invited to perform shows that they never held the office they were disputing. The Qur'an uses this move elsewhere against those who claim to have their own scheme: *“Or have they mastered some ˹evil˺ plan? Then We ˹too˺ are surely planning.”* (43:79). The reply in both cases is to hand the objector the task his claim requires.

**The Rope in Sūrah al-Ḥajj**

The nearest parallel to this invitation is one of the harshest images in the Qur'an, and reading the two together sharpens both. *“Whoever thinks that Allah will not help His Prophet in this world and the Hereafter, let them stretch out a rope to the ceiling and strangle themselves, then let them see if this plan will do away with ˹the cause of˺ their rage.”* (22:15). There the ascent is replaced by a rope and a death, and the point is the same: the objector's remedy lies completely within his own power, and it will not change anything.

**The Real Target Is Envy of Appointment**

What the two verses together are dismantling is a specific human reflex: the belief that a gift has been given to the wrong person, and that the correct recipients are obvious to anyone with judgement. The reflex is common enough that the Qur'an addresses it directly, without reference to prophecy at all: *“Do they have control over shares of the kingdom? If so, they would not have given anyone so much as the speck on a date stone.”* (4:53).

---

## Verse 38:11

> This is just another ˹enemy˺ force bound for defeat out there

**Which Battle the Verse Is Read Against**

Two engagements are proposed as the fulfilment, and the commentators argue for each. The first is Badr, fought in the second year after the Hijrah, where a small Muslim force defeated a much larger Meccan army. Those who prefer it note that Badr was the first decisive defeat and that the Qur'an elsewhere treats it as the turning point: *“Indeed, Allah made you victorious at Badr when you were ˹vastly˺ outnumbered. So be mindful of Allah, perhaps you will be grateful.”* (3:123). The second is the siege of Medina in the fifth year, when the confederated tribes withdrew without a battle; its supporters point to the word *host*, which suits a coalition better than a caravan escort.

**A Defeat Announced Before the Battle Exists**

The verse delivers a verdict in the past-participle on an event that has not happened. The Quraysh are described as a host already routed, and the tense does the work of a proof: what God has decided is spoken of as finished, because nothing in the created order can revise it. The audience hearing this in Makkah had no army to speak of on the Muslim side and every advantage on their own, and the sentence asks them to accept that the outcome is settled anyway.

**Which Battle the Verse Is Read Against**

The Badr reading is the stronger of the two. The verse speaks of a host being *routed*, and a coalition that disperses because of wind and cold is not routed in the sense the word demands; an army broken in the field is. The sūrah of al-Qamar states the same expectation in shorter form — *“˹Soon˺ their united front will be defeated and ˹forced to˺ flee”* (54:45) — and the tradition connects that verse to the day of Badr as well, which suggests the two passages share a single referent.

**Junūd: Hosts, and the Company They Keep**

The word for host, junūd, is the plural of jund, a body of soldiers or a gathered force, and the Qur'an uses it on both sides of its conflicts. God's own forces are junūd: *“and that Our forces will certainly prevail.”* (37:173). So are the armies of unbelievers, and so are the winds and angels sent against them: *“O believers! Remember Allah's favour upon you when ˹enemy˺ forces came to ˹besiege˺ you ˹in Medina˺, so We sent against them a ˹bitter˺ wind and forces you could not see.”* (33:9).

**A Promise Recorded Before It Was Needed**

The verse does not stand alone in the Qur'an's accounting. Sūrah al-Ṣāffāt closes with a declaration that reads like the charter under which this verse operates: *“Our Word has already gone forth to Our servants, the messengers, that they would surely be helped, and that Our forces will certainly prevail.”* (37:171–173). Sūrah Ṣād was revealed after that sūrah, and its eleventh verse is the application of the charter to a specific group of men.

---

## Verse 38:12

> Before them, the people of Noah denied ˹the truth˺, as did ’Âd, Pharaoh of the mighty structures

**"Of the Mighty Structures": One Word, Three Readings**

The title given to Pharaoh rests on awtād, the plural of watad, a peg or stake driven into the ground. The word is used in the same title in sūrah al-Fajr, where Pharaoh is again named *the Pharaoh of mighty structures* (89:10). Commentators offer three explanations. The first takes the pegs as tent-pegs and reads the title as a metonym for a vast army: a camp with many pegs is a camp with many tents, and many tents mean many soldiers. The second takes them as stakes used for execution, recalling the tyrant's practice of pinning victims to the ground. The third takes them as pillars, evoking the Arabic phrase for a man of firm authority — *he has firm pillars*.

The three readings differ in what they make Pharaoh's distinction: military scale, cruelty, or stability of rule. All three are linguistically available, since the same noun carries all three senses in ordinary usage.

**The List as Exhibit, and the Reading the Context Supports**

The surrounding verses decide the question better than the lexicon does. Al-Fajr names Pharaoh immediately after describing oppression and corruption: *“They all transgressed throughout the land, spreading much corruption there. So your Lord unleashed on them a scourge of punishment.”* (89:11–13). A title attached to a catalogue of cruelty is most naturally read as a reference to cruelty. The execution reading therefore has the strongest contextual claim, and it is also the reading that makes the title an indictment rather than a honour.

**ʿĀd: Builders With No Refuge**

ʿĀd is named without elaboration here, and the brevity assumes an audience that knew the name. They were an Arabian tribe, addressed by the prophet Hūd: *“And to the people of ’Âd We sent their brother Hûd. He said, ‘O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. Will you not then fear Him?’”* (7:65). Al-Fajr supplies the detail the Quraysh would have supplied themselves: *“˹the people˺ of Iram—with ˹their˺ great stature, unmatched in any other land”* (89:7–8).

Their relevance to this verse lies in what their strength failed to do. They built on the plains and cut houses into the mountains, and the wind that destroyed them entered through every sealed door. The lesson the Qur'an draws from them is not that building is wrong but that construction is not protection: *“So remember Allah's favours, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land.”* (7:74). Makkah, a trading city whose wealth came from caravans and whose prestige came from a sanctuary, was the natural audience for that lesson.

---

## Verse 38:13

> Thamûd, the people of Lot, and the residents of the Forest. These were ˹all˺ enemy forces

**Three More Names, and the Houses Cut Into Rock**

The list continues without a verb of introduction. Thamūd, the people of Lot, the residents of the Forest: three names appended to the three of the previous verse, and no distinction drawn among them. The absence of any differentiation is itself the message. Six communities, in different centuries, on different ground, with different gods and different economies, are entered in the same register and given the same label.

Thamūd were an Arabian tribe, addressed by their brother Ṣāliḥ: *“O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. A clear proof has come to you from your Lord: this is Allah's she-camel as a sign to you.”* (7:73). They succeeded ʿĀd and inherited their land: *“Remember when He made you successors after ’Âd and established you in the land—˹and˺ you built palaces on its plains and carved homes into mountains.”* (7:74).

**The Residents of the Forest**

The last name is the most obscure, and the obscurity has produced the usual range of identifications. The word translated *the Forest* is al-Aykah, a thicket or dense growth of trees. The residents are most often identified with the people addressed by Shuʿayb, to whom the Qur'an gives a distinct passage: *“The residents of the Forest rejected the messengers when Shu'aib said to them, ‘Will you not fear ˹Allah˺? I am truly a trustworthy messenger to you.’”* (26:176–178).

**"These Were All Enemy Forces"**

The closing phrase returns to the vocabulary of the eleventh verse. Al-aḥzāb, the parties or confederated factions, is the same word used for the coalition that besieged Medina: *“O believers! Remember Allah's favour upon you when ˹enemy˺ forces came to ˹besiege˺ you ˹in Medina˺”* (33:9). The word carries the idea of groups that have banded together against something, and applying it to six ancient communities turns them into a single party spread across time.

The grammatical form allows two readings, and both have been defended. *These are the parties* makes the identification present, placing the Quraysh inside the class. *These were the parties* makes it past, implying that those earlier groups were the real confederations — wealthier, mightier, more many than the Quraysh — and were broken still. The second reading adds a comparison; the first adds an inclusion. The verse is stronger with the first, because inclusion is what the chapter needs.

The Qur'an records their rejection in the plainest terms: *“The people of Lot rejected the messengers when their brother Lot said to them, ‘Will you not fear ˹Allah˺?’”* (26:160–161).

---

## Verse 38:14

> Each rejected their messenger, so My punishment was justified

**"Was Justified": A Word From the Courtroom**

The verb translated *was justified* is ḥaqqa, from the root meaning to be true, to be established, to become due. It is the language of a claim that has been proven and a debt that has matured. The punishment is not described as inflicted, sent or decided; it is described as having become owed, and the owing is the result of the denial rather than of a decree that preceded it.

**One Verb Covers Six Communities**

The verse compresses the histories of the previous two verses into a single sentence, and the compression is the argument. Six peoples, spanning many centuries, differing in wealth, technology, geography and sin, are given one verb between them: they denied. Not *some denied and others compromised*, not *they doubted* or *they delayed* — they denied, and the denial is presented as identical in kind across all of them.

**Denying One Messenger Is Denying the Message**

The phrase *their messenger* is singular for each community, and the Qur'an elsewhere generalises the point: rejecting any one messenger is treated as rejecting the office itself. *“The people of Lot rejected the messengers”* (26:160), says al-Shuʿarāʾ, using the plural for a people who met only one man. The same plural is used for the residents of the Forest: *“The residents of the Forest rejected the messengers”* (26:176).

---

## Verse 38:15

> These ˹pagans˺ are awaiting nothing but a single Blast that cannot be stopped

**One Blast, and Everything Reduced to One Sound**

The verse takes the whole of the preceding history and reduces it to a single acoustic event. The peoples listed before it were destroyed by flood, wind, an overturning of their towns and a blast. This verse says that what remains for the current audience is the last of those means, in its simplest form: one cry, one blast, and nothing before it or after it that could change anything.

That is the verse's quiet rebuke to the confidence of the Quraysh. They measured power in hosts and structures, and the answer given to them is a sound. The smallest thing in the created order — a vibration in the air — is enough to end the largest. Nothing they have is relevant to the transaction, and nothing they lack can prevent it.

**Which Blast Is Meant**

Two readings divide the commentators, and both rest on who *these* refers to. If the pronoun points to the Quraysh alone, the blast is the first sounding of the trumpet, the one that ends the world as it stands. If it points to all the parties named in the previous verses, including the Quraysh, the blast is the second sounding, which raises the dead for the reckoning. The Qur'an describes both in one verse: *“The Trumpet will be blown and all those in the heavens and all those on the earth will fall dead, except those Allah wills ˹to spare˺. Then it will be blown again and they will rise up at once, looking on ˹in anticipation˺.”* (39:68).

The first reading fits the immediate context better. The verses before this one describe the Quraysh as a party awaiting defeat, and the destruction of a community is what the sūrah has been documenting. But the second reading is not excluded, since the two blasts are separated by an interval that no creature experiences as time. What both readings share is the essential claim: a single sound, with nothing in the audience's power to alter it.

**The Blast in the Qur'an's Own Vocabulary**

The Book returns to this image often enough that it becomes a technical term. *“All it took was one ˹mighty˺ blast, and they were extinguished at once.”* (36:29). *“They must be awaiting a single Blast, which will seize them while they are ˹entrenched˺ in ˹worldly˺ disputes.”* (36:49); *“It will only take one Blast, then at once they will see ˹it all˺.”* (37:19); *“But indeed, it will take only one ˹mighty˺ Blast”* (79:13).

---

## Verse 38:16

> They say ˹mockingly˺, “Our Lord! Hasten for us our share ˹of the punishment˺ before the Day of Reckoning.”

**Why They Can Afford to Be Funny**

The confidence behind the joke was diagnosed two verses earlier: *they have not yet tasted My punishment* (38:8). Nothing has happened to them. The warning has been issued for years, the rains have come, the caravans have returned, and the man who delivered it remains without power. Under those conditions a threat begins to look like a rumour, and a rumour can be laughed at.

**A Long Line of Impatient Requests**

This is not an isolated outburst but a habit of speech the Qur'an documents across many sūrahs. *“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment rather than grace, though there have ˹already˺ been ˹many˺ torments before them.”* (13:6); *“They challenge you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment. And Allah will never fail in His promise. But a day with your Lord is indeed like a thousand years by your counting.”* (22:47); *“Do they ˹really˺ seek to hasten Our torment?”* (26:204); *“Do they ˹really˺ wish to hasten Our punishment?”* (37:176).

The recurrence has a purpose beyond recording an insult. It establishes that the demand was made, publicly and repeatedly, and that it was not granted on the schedule the demanders set. The Qur'an's answer to the request for haste is a lesson in scales of time: a day with God is a thousand years by human counting. The men asking for immediate settlement were operating on a calendar that the verse declares irrelevant.

**Delayed Forgiveness, and the Turn to the Portraits**

Al-Raʿd supplies the reply this verse leaves unstated, and the reply belongs beside the mockery: *“Surely your Lord is full of forgiveness for people, despite their wrongdoing, and your Lord is truly severe in punishment.”* (13:6). The two halves belong together. Forgiveness is available while the demand is being made in jest, and severity arrives when the jest has become a settled position.

That is the real answer to the request for haste. The delay they are laughing at is the space in which the offer stands, and the offer is not repeated after the interval closes. The men who asked for their portion early were asking to have the door shut on themselves, and the sūrah declines to oblige them until the term set for them has run.

Positioned where it is, this verse closes the section on the objectors. Their psychology has been described, their slogans quoted, their claim to ownership refuted, their precedent listed, their deadline fixed and their mockery recorded.

The very next verse turns away from them completely, and the turn is the point. A revelation that spent its length arguing with mockers would grant the mockers a standing they do not have. Instead the chapter gives the Prophet an instruction about patience and a name to remember, and the rest of the sūrah is given over to the lives of servants who were tested in earnest.

---

## Verse 38:17

> Be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say. And remember Our servant, David, the man of strength. Indeed, he ˹constantly˺ turned ˹to Allah˺

**The Turn to a Servant's Life, and Why David Comes First**

Three instructions arrive in one verse, and their order is a method. First, an address about endurance: be patient with what they say. Second, a command of recollection: remember Our servant David. Third, a characterisation of the man to be remembered: strong, and constantly turning. Nothing in the verse argues with the mockers of the previous verse; the argument is over and the chapter moves to consolation and instruction.

Of the three servants the chapter will examine, David is placed first, and the placement follows the logic of the argument.

**Patience as an Order, Not a Mood**

The same injunction appears in nearly identical words in three other places: *“So be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say. And glorify the praises of your Lord before sunrise and before sunset”* (50:39); *“So be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say. And glorify the praises of your Lord before sunrise and before sunset, and glorify Him in the hours of the night and at both ends of the day, so that you may be pleased ˹with the reward˺.”* (20:130); *“Be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say, and depart from them courteously.”* (73:10). In each case patience is joined to an act of worship, which tells the reader what kind of endurance is meant.

**Awwāb: The One Who Keeps Coming Back**

The closing phrase describes David as one who turns repeatedly, from awwāb, an intensive form built on the root for returning. The word is not used once of David but three times in this chapter — of him here, of Solomon at verse 30, and of Job at verse 44 — and its repetition across the three portraits is the chapter's central claim about its heroes.

What the intensive form adds is frequency. A man who repents once is a penitent; an awwāb is a man whose return is a habit, whose life contains many turns rather than one. The Qur'an applies the same word to those who guard themselves from sin, which suggests that returning is not a failure of the righteous but their normal operation. The strength of the verse's opening is so qualified by its ending: the strong man is not the one who never slips but the one whose recovery is immediate and repeated.

**The Prayer and the Fast God Loves Best**

The tradition gives concrete form to David's strength, and the report is one of the most specific in the collections. The Prophet ﷺ told ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr that the fasting dearest to God is David's. The prayer dearest to God is David's: he slept half the night, stood in prayer for a third of it, slept a sixth, fasted one day and broke the next (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3420; also recorded at Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1131 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1159).

The arithmetic of the report is what makes it memorable. A night divided into halves, thirds and sixths; a calendar divided into alternating days. Neither schedule is maximal — the man sleeps more than half the night in total, and he fasts only half his days — and the same conversation records the Prophet ﷺ refusing ʿAbdullāh's request for more. The strength praised here is sustainable strength. A devotion that collapses under its own weight is not the model the Qur'an sets before a prophet who is being told to endure years of insult.

---

## Verse 38:18

> We truly subjected the mountains to hymn ˹Our praises˺ along with him in the evening and after sunrise

**Everything Already Praises, and David Is Joined to It**

The praise of creation is not a peculiarity of this verse but a standing doctrine of the Qur'an. Whole sūrahs open with it: everything in the heavens and the earth glorifies God (59:1; 61:1; 62:1; 64:1). The praise is continuous and universal, and it does not depend on anyone hearing it. What this verse adds is not the praise but the coordination: the mountains praise *with him*.

The addition matters theologically. If all things praise at all times, then the mountains praising at David's hours is a special alignment, and alignment of that kind is a sign of office. Two parallel verses make the same arrangement: David is given the mountains and the birds as companions in praise elsewhere in the Book, and in Saba the command is addressed to both together — mountains and birds, returning with him (34:10). The consistent pairing suggests a role rather than an anecdote.

**The Voice of a King and the Answer of a Landscape**

The tradition preserves a detail that makes the scene concrete rather than emblematic. David's voice was proverbial in the Islamic tradition for its beauty. The phrase used to describe it is drawn from music: the Prophet ﷺ told Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī, on hearing his recitation, that he had been given one of the *mizāmīr* — the pipes or flutes — of the family of David (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5048; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 793).

The comparison is informative about what the family of David was known for. A mizmār is an instrument of sustained, carried sound, and to say that a man's voice resembles one is to say that it carries across distance and holds a note. The next verse will describe the birds gathering, and the tradition of David's voice supplies the reason the gathering is plausible within the narrative: a voice that draws a crowd of birds is a voice that would carry into a valley.

**Two Details, and a Vicegerent Whose Prayer the World Joins**

The religious weight of the verse is best read alongside the charge David will be given later in the chapter: he is appointed a representative on earth (38:26). The same idea appears in the general statement about human succession on the earth (6:165). A vicegerent is a stand-in, and a stand-in acts on behalf of the one he represents.

On that reading the mountains' joining is not a miracle granted to a favourite but a consequence of an office. The representative of God on earth prays, and the creation that belongs to God joins its representative's prayer. The arrangement shows what the office is for: not domination of the world but the leading of it in its proper act. David is strong, and the first use of his strength recorded in this chapter is worship, with the landscape as his congregation.

The verse gives only two facts — the subjection of the mountains and the fixed hours — and the two facts are chosen to make a single point. Strength, named in the previous verse, is shown here in operation, and its operation is worship rather than war or building. The chapter will give David a kingdom, a judgement and a fall; its opening description of him is of a man at prayer at dawn, with the hills answering.

## Verse 38:19

> And ˹We subjected˺ the birds, flocking together. All turned to him ˹echoing his hymns˺

**The Gathering, and the Word Shared by Birds and Prophets**

The intensive noun applied to those who turn appears in the previous verse of David, in verse 30 of Solomon and in verse 44 of Job, and here it is extended to the birds. The extension is not a casual reuse. The word describes a repeated turning, an act performed many times and by disposition, and applying it to a flock attributes to the birds a settled pattern rather than a momentary reaction.

**A Voice That Drew a Crowd**

The Islamic tradition consistently explains the gathering by the beauty of David's voice, and the explanation is supported by the phrase used elsewhere in the collections. When the Prophet ﷺ heard Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī recite, he told him he had been given one of the *mizāmīr* of the family of David (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5048; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 793) — a pipe, an instrument whose sound carries and sustains.

**The Gathering, and the Word Shared by Birds and Prophets**

That attribution is what makes the scene a congregation instead of a spectacle. A spectacle is watched; a congregation participates. The chapter's insistence on the word across its three portraits binds the birds into the same category as its prophets, and the binding is a claim about the world: praise is the natural activity of creation, and a human being who performs it well is not alone in performing it.

The men of Makkah measured worth in followers, wealth and rank. The verse answers with a different measure: what gathers around a man when he stands to pray. Nobody can be recruited into that assembly, nobody can pay for a place in it, and it contains creatures that no chief of Quraysh could command. The comparison is quiet, and it is devastating to the values the chapter has been dismantling since its second verse.

---

## Verse 38:20

> We strengthened his kingship, and gave him wisdom and sound judgment

**A Kingdom Strengthened From Outside**

Three gifts are listed, and the grammar of the first is the key to all three. The kingship is *strengthened*, not created or granted outright, and the strengthening is done by God to something David already held. The tradition records that his rule was the most powerful of its time, but the verse's construction attributes the power to an external source: a kingdom is as strong as the aid behind it.

**Three Gifts in Order, and the Qur'an's Own Method**

Faṣl is related to faṣṣala, to set out in detail or to explain, and that verb is used of revelation itself: the Qur'an is described as a Book whose signs have been set out clearly (41:3; 7:52; 11:1). The connection between the two uses is not etymological curiosity. Both describe an act of making distinctions visible — separating truth from falsehood in a case, or separating guidance from error in a text.

On this reading the gift given to David is a share in the activity that defines revelation. God explains; the prophet explains; the judge decides. Each performs a separation that others cannot perform, and each is dependent on the one above. The verse's three gifts therefore form a chain rather than a list: a kingdom held up, an understanding given, and a speech that cuts.

The sequence is not arbitrary. Power comes first, then wisdom, then decisive speech, and the order describes how authority actually operates: a man is given a position, then the capacity to use it, then the instrument through which it is exercised. Each gift is useless without the one before it. Power without understanding is tyranny; understanding without the ability to express a decision is paralysis.

---

## Verse 38:21

> Has the story of the two plaintiffs, who scaled the ˹wall of David’s˺ sanctuary, reached you ˹O Prophet˺

**Two Plaintiffs, and the Address to the Prophet**

The question is directed to the Prophet, and the direction matters for two reasons. The first is the claim it carries: he is being told something he did not witness, about a prophet who lived a thousand years before him, in a society that had no access to the records of that life. The Meccan audience knew this, and the Qur'an elsewhere makes the argument explicit — that news of the unseen is being given to him without his having been present (3:44).

The second reason is pastoral. The sūrah has just told him to be patient and to remember David, and this verse supplies the remembering. The instruction and its content are joined without a transition, which is how the chapter treats every command: the command is followed immediately by the material that makes it bearable.

The Arabic word for the pair, khaṣm, is grammatically singular and is used here with a dual verb, a construction the language permits for nouns that denote a party or a class. Commentators note the usage because it affects how the scene is read: a khaṣm is not simply an opponent but a litigant, a person who has a case and has come to have it heard.

---

## Verse 38:22

> When they came into David’s presence, he was startled by them. They said, “Have no fear. ˹We are merely˺ two in a dispute: one of us has wronged the other. So judge between us with truth—do not go beyond ˹it˺—and guide us to the right way

**The First Words of a Litigant, and Three Requests in One Sentence**

The reassurance comes before the case, which is how a person behaves when he knows his entrance has been wrong. The two men begin by removing the obstacle their own conduct created, and only then state who they are: two parties to a dispute, one of whom has wronged the other.

The litigant asks for three things, and each is distinct. Judge between us with truth. Do not go beyond it. Guide us to the right way. The first is a request for a decision; the second sets a limit on the decision; the third asks for something beyond the decision altogether.

The second request is the most unusual. A plaintiff rarely instructs a judge on the ceiling of his authority, and the request reads less like a courtesy than like a reminder — as though the speaker knows that a judge in this position is capable of exceeding what is due. Commentators connect it to the charge David will be given several verses later, to judge with truth and not follow desire, and the connection suggests that the phrase is placed here as a marker rather than as a pleasantry.

---

The two litigants arrive over the wall of the prayer-room, which is why David is startled, and the three things they ask permission for — do not be afraid, judge between us according to the truth, and do not be unjust — are the etiquette of a hearing stated by the plaintiffs. The sūrah of the Cave uses the same wall-scene convention for the servant's meetings with Moses (18:77 fold), and the Book's setting of the scene at the prayer-room gives the parable its meaning: the case arrives in David's most private place, so the trial is about his office rather than his household. The verse that follows records his decision and the request for forgiveness, which is why the commentators read the episode as a test of judgment rather than a legal record.

## Verse 38:23

> This is my brother. He has ninety-nine sheep while I have ˹only˺ one. ˹Still˺ he asked me to give it up to him, overwhelming me with ˹his˺ argument.”

**The Case Is Not Only About Sheep**

A long tradition reads the ninety-nine ewes as a figure for something else, and the reading goes back to the earliest generations of exegetes. On that account the story corresponds to the account in 2 Samuel of David's desire for the wife of one of his officers, and the ninety-nine to one is taken as the many wives of a king beside the single wife of a soldier. The two visitors are then understood as angels sent with a case that is really about the judge.

The reading has real support and real problems, and both should be stated. Its support is structural: the plaintiff's numbers fit the pattern of a king's household against a commoner's, and David's immediate conviction of the defendant mirrors a self-condemnation. Its problem is that the Qur'an supplies none of the particulars the reading requires — no woman, no husband, no battlefield — and that it must be imported from a text outside the revelation. Ibn Kathīr observed that the connection is not grounded in sound reports, and al-Rāzī argued that even a purified version of the story conflicts with what the Qur'an says of David here and elsewhere. The stronger position is the one that stays inside the text: a case about property and eloquence, in which the test is judicial rather than moral in the narrower sense.

---

## Verse 38:24

> David ˹eventually˺ ruled, “He has definitely wronged you in demanding ˹to add˺ your sheep to his. And certainly many partners wrong each other, except those who believe and do good—but how few are they!” Then David realized that We had tested him so he asked for his Lord’s forgiveness, fell down in prostration, and turned ˹to Him in repentance˺

**A Ruling Issued on One Testimony**

David decides, and the decision is sound on the facts presented. A man with ninety-nine who demands the hundredth from his brother has wronged him, and no defence is likely to alter that. The defect is not in the reasoning but in the procedure: the defendant has not spoken. The verse marks the ruling with a word meaning *eventually* or *he judged*, and the marking has been read as an indication that the judgement came after a process in David's own mind rather than after a hearing.

The absence of the second party is what turns a correct verdict into a failing.

**"But How Few Are They"**

The exception is faith and right action, and the comment attached to it — how few — is one of the Qur'an's recurrent laments. The same observation appears in various forms across the Book: most people do not give thanks (2:243; 40:61), most people do not believe (11:17). The rarity is never offered as a surprise but as a settled finding.

The phrase does two things in this verse. It completes the moral picture by naming what restrains the tendency to exploit a partner, and it lowers the estimate of the human condition in a way that the reader is meant to apply to himself. The partners who do not wrong each other are not the strong or the clever; they are the ones who believe and act well, and there are few of them.

**Four Movements in a Single Recovery**

What follows the realisation is described in four verbs, and their sequence is the chapter's model of repentance. He asked his Lord's forgiveness. He fell down. He prostrated. He turned.

The mechanism is worth naming because the chapter will need it later. Its audience had spent years refusing the message, and the refusal had become habitual. The verse supplies a counter-model: a sequence of four movements that a person can perform at any point, regardless of how far the interval has run.

**The Prostration Is Part of the Verse's Inheritance**

The scene ends with a bodily act that the Islamic tradition has kept alive in its liturgy. This is one of the fifteen places in the Qur'an where a prostration is prescribed on recitation, and the practice is reported from the Prophet ﷺ himself, who prostrated here and distinguished the act from the obligatory prostrations of prayer. The verse therefore carries its own continuation: every reader who recites it repeats, in the body, the movement with which the story closes.

Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said that the prostration of Ṣād is not among the obligatory ones, but that he saw the Prophet ﷺ prostrating when reciting it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1069). The report fixes the act's status: recommended rather than required, and established by practice rather than by command.

The status suits the verse. David's prostration was not an obligation discharged but a return performed, and the liturgical act preserves that character. A reader who prostrates here is not completing a requirement; he is joining a movement the verse describes.

---

## Verse 38:25

> So We forgave that for him. And he will indeed have ˹a status of˺ closeness to Us and an honourable destination

**The Prostration This Verse Carries**

This is one of the places in the Qur'an where a prostration is prescribed upon recitation. Its status has been settled by a report from Ibn ʿAbbās preserved in the most authoritative collection: the prostration of Ṣād is not one of the compulsory prostrations, but he saw the Prophet ﷺ prostrating while reciting it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1069). The act is therefore established by practice and left open by law, and it is classed among the prostrations of gratitude rather than the obligatory ones.

**Zulfā: Nearness as a Rank**

The promise that follows uses a noun meaning nearness — zulfā — and the Qur'an uses the same word elsewhere as a term of rank rather than of feeling. The foremost of the resurrected are those brought near: *“They are the ones nearest ˹to Allah˺”* (56:11); *“So, if the deceased is one of those brought near ˹to Us˺”* (56:88); and the record of the righteous is *“witnessed by those nearest ˹to Allah˺”* (83:21).

The Qur'an is equally clear about how that rank is reached. It is not bought with wealth or family: *“It is not your wealth or children that bring you closer to Us. But those who believe and do good—it is they who will have a multiplied reward for what they did, and they will be secure in ˹elevated˺ mansions.”* (34:37). And it cannot be obtained through intermediaries, which is the specific error the Book condemns: *“As for those who take other lords besides Him, ˹saying,˺ ‘We worship them only so they may bring us closer to Allah,’ surely Allah will judge between all regarding what they differed about.”* (39:3). Nearness is a position granted to obedience, and it is the position David is promised here.

**The Prostration This Verse Carries**

Ibn ʿAbbās is also reported to have explained the difference between the two prostrations in the story: David prostrated in repentance, and those who recite prostrate in thanks, because his repentance was accepted. The distinction is worth keeping. The verse is not a place of mourning in the liturgy; it is a place of thanksgiving for a pardon that was granted, and the bodily act is the reciter's way of joining the scene at its conclusion rather than at its crisis.

**Rank Restored, Not Rank Reduced**

The most important claim in this verse is what it does not say. Nothing in it suggests that David's fall cost him his standing. He is not demoted, not reproved in the text of the promise, not required to make amends to anyone. The two gifts announced here — nearness and a good return — are of the same order as the gifts he held before the trial. The chapter will shortly give him a charge greater than the one he had: to judge among the people with truth as God's authority on earth (38:26).

That sequence answers a question the story might otherwise raise. If a prophet can err in judgement, does his office survive the error? The verse answers that it does, and the answer is not a concession to David's dignity but a statement about the nature of the office. Prophethood is not a reward for infallibility; it is an appointment that includes the possibility of correction, and the correction is part of the appointment.

---

## Verse 38:26

> ˹We instructed him:˺ “O David! We have surely made you an authority in the land, so judge between people with truth. And do not follow ˹your˺ desires or they will lead you astray from Allah’s Way. Surely those who go astray from Allah’s Way will suffer a severe punishment for neglecting the Day of Reckoning.”

**The Charge After the Pardon, and an Appointment That Warns**

The charge is given in four movements, and only the first of them is a favour. David is appointed an authority on earth; he is told to judge with truth; he is told not to follow desire; and he is told what awaits those who stray. Three of the four movements are cautions, and they are addressed to a man who has just been forgiven and promised nearness.

The sequence of the two verses is the most instructive thing about them.

**Khalīfah: What the Word Obliges**

The term translated *authority* is khalīfah, the same word used of the human being placed on earth at the beginning: *“˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, ‘I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.’”* (2:30). The word means a successor or a deputy — someone who acts in the place of another, and whose acts are measured against the standard of the one he represents.

The general form of the appointment is stated elsewhere with its condition attached: *“He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you.”* (6:165). Rank is a test, not a prize. David's kingship is therefore a case of the general rule, and the verse applies the rule to him by name: you have been made an authority, so judge with truth.

**Forgetting the Day of Reckoning Is the Cause**

The final clause gives the reason for the severity of the punishment, and the reason is not the act of injustice but the forgetting that permitted it. Those who stray suffer severely *because* they neglected the Day of Reckoning. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the same forgetting in starker terms: *“And do not be like those who forgot Allah, so He made them forget themselves. It is they who are ˹truly˺ rebellious.”* (59:19).

---

## Verse 38:27

> We have not created the heavens and earth and everything in between without purpose—as the disbelievers think. So woe to the disbelievers because of the Fire

**A Purpose Asserted Against a Conjecture**

The verse makes a claim about the whole of creation and then names the belief it is answering. The heavens, the earth and everything between them were not created in vain — the Arabic term is bāṭil, meaning empty, false, or without consequence. The denial is absolute, and it is directed at a specific alternative view, which the verse identifies as the opinion of those who disbelieve.

The same assertion appears repeatedly in the Qur'an, in almost identical wording: *“We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything in between for sport.”* (21:16; 44:38); *“We have not created the heavens and the earth and everything in between except for a purpose. And the Hour is certain to come, so forgive graciously.”* (15:85); *“We only created the heavens and the earth and everything in between for a purpose and an appointed term.”* (46:3). The repetition across Meccan and Medinan sūrahs shows that this is a foundational claim rather than a passing remark, and the addition of *an appointed term* in one of them identifies what the purpose consists in.

**"That Is the Conjecture of Those Who Disbelieve"**

Al-Rūm and al-Jāthiyah describe the same condition in the same terms. The disbelievers say: *“There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die; others are born. And nothing destroys us but ˹the passage of˺ time.” Yet they have no knowledge ˹in support of this ˹claim˺. They only speculate.* (45:24). The verdict in both verses is identical: the position rests on speculation, and its holders are unaware that they are speculating.

**The Denial in Their Own Words**

The Qur'an quotes the position it is refuting more than once, and the quotations are consistent. *“There is nothing beyond our worldly life. We die, others are born, and none will be resurrected.”* (23:37). The formula has three parts: the world is all there is, life is a succession of generations, and there is no return. Each part follows from the one before it, and together they describe a closed system.

The chapter's answer does not engage the system on its own terms. It states the fact of purpose and identifies the alternative as conjecture, which is a different kind of move: it does not argue within the disbeliever's frame but refuses the frame. The reason is that the frame cannot be entered. A man who holds that nothing is real beyond what he can see cannot be shown a purpose, because purpose is not an object of sight.

**The Verse That Holds the Chapter Together**

There is also a claim about the world in the phrase *everything in between*. The heavens and the earth are the two extremes of the created order, and the space between them contains everything a human being can act upon — the air, the seas, the land, the living things. To say that none of it was made in vain is to say that no part of the human domain is exempt from meaning, including the parts that look accidental.

---

## Verse 38:28

> Or should We treat those who believe and do good like those who make mischief throughout the land? Or should We treat the righteous like the wicked

**Corruption Is Not Simply the Opposite of Righteousness**

The verse pairs belief and good action against the making of mischief, and the pairing is looser than it looks. The ordinary Qur'anic opposite of ifsād, the working of corruption, is iṣlāḥ, the setting of things right, as in the command *“Do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order.”* (7:56). Here the opposition is drawn between the corrupter and the believer who acts well, which widens the contrast from a pair of acts to a pair of lives.

The word for corruption also carries a specific weight in the Qur'an, which treats it as a spreading thing rather than a private fault. Corruption has consequences beyond the corrupter: *“Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people's hands have done, so that Allah may cause them to taste ˹the consequences of˺ some of their deeds and perhaps they might return ˹to the Right Path˺.”* (30:41). A mischief-maker is therefore not merely a sinner but a source, and the verse measures him by what he generated.

**Equality as the Real Injustice**

The verse's force lies in a single idea: to treat unequal things equally is itself an injustice. This is not a claim about reward but about truth. If the corrupter and the righteous receive the same end, then the universe has recorded a falsehood about them, and the falsehood is not repaired by any subsequent event.

The Qur'an states the same point elsewhere without the rhetorical form: *“Is the one who is a believer equal ˹before Allah˺ to the one who is rebellious? They are not equal!”* (32:18), and *“Should We then treat those who have submitted like the wicked?”* (68:35). The flat denial in al-Sajdah — *they are not equal* — is the answer that the questions here require, and the three verses together make clear that the inequality is a fact about reality rather than a decision God might have made otherwise.

**Patience Rests on This Verse**

The instruction given earlier in the chapter — be patient with what they say (38:17) — is made bearable by the claim in this one. Endurance under injustice is possible when the injustice is temporary and the accounting is certain; it is impossible when both are uncertain. The verse supplies the second term.

That is why the passage sits where it does. The histories of the destroyed peoples established that denial has consequences in this world. This verse establishes that the consequences do not end there, and without that assurance the histories would be nothing more than a record of who happened to win.

---

## Verse 38:29

> ˹This is˺ a blessed Book which We have revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ so that they may contemplate its verses, and people of reason may be mindful

**A Book Revealed For a Stated Purpose**

The verse gives the reason for revelation in a purpose clause, and the reason is not obedience in the first instance but contemplation. The Book was sent down *so that they may contemplate its verses*, and a second group is named after it: so that people of reason may take heed. Neither clause mentions belief, law or ritual; the primary function of the revelation is described as an act of thought.

**Contemplation Is Named as the Work**

The verb used for contemplating means a sustained, deliberate looking-at, and the Qur'an elsewhere demands it in the form of a rebuke: *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Or are there locks upon their hearts?”* (47:24). The alternative offered there is not ignorance but obstruction — a heart that has been locked, which is a condition the person has participated in producing.

The same idea is put in the heart elsewhere: *“Surely in this is a reminder for whoever has a ˹mindful˺ heart and lends an attentive ear.”* (50:37). Two conditions are named there, and both are active — a heart that is present and an ear that is listening. Contemplation in the Qur'an is never a passive reception; it is an act performed on the text with attention, and the verse makes that act the purpose of the revelation itself.

---

The purpose clause the verse attaches to the Book is the Book's own standard for reading: the sūrah of the Women asks why they do not contemplate the Qur'an (4:82), and the sūrah of Muhammad commands the same reflection with the warning that hearts can be locked (47:24). The phrase about people of understanding being reminded echoes the sūrah's earlier use of the same words for Solomon (38:29 and 38:44 fold), and the Book's principle is that the Book's guidance is opened by reflection rather than by recitation alone. The verse follows the account of the judgment over David, so the Book is placed in the passage as the standard that corrects men who judge.

## Verse 38:30

> And We blessed David with Solomon—what an excellent servant ˹he was˺! Indeed, he ˹constantly˺ turned ˹to Allah˺

**The Inheritance That Was Not Property**

David had many sons, and the Qur'an singles out the one who succeeded him: *“And David was succeeded by Solomon, who said, ‘O people! We have been taught the language of birds, and been given everything ˹we need˺. This is indeed a great privilege.’”* (27:16). The inheritance is described elsewhere with a word that in ordinary usage means property, and commentators are careful about what it means here: what Solomon inherited was prophethood and sovereignty, not estates.

Both father and son are described in the Qur'an as having been given knowledge and judgement, and their acknowledgement is recorded in the same breath as the gift: *“Indeed, We granted knowledge to David and Solomon. And they said ˹in acknowledgment˺, ‘All praise is for Allah Who has privileged us over many of His faithful servants.’”* (27:15). The verse is careful to place the praise immediately after the privilege, and the placement is a lesson about how a servant speaks about what he has been given.

**"What an Excellent Servant"**

The commendation is therefore not a rating of performance. It identifies a relation: this man was a servant, and being a servant is what makes him excellent. The same exclamation will be used of Job at the end of his account (38:44), which shows that it is not a reward for success — Job's story is one of loss — but a recognition of the same quality in two very different lives.

**Awwāb Again, and Why Solomon Follows David**

The chapter could have taken its prophets in any order, and the order it chooses is instructive. David's account ends with a charge about judging and a warning about desire; Solomon's begins with a horse review, a distraction, and a prayer for a kingdom that cannot be taken from him.

The sequence sets the son against the father deliberately. Both are kings, both are prophets, both are tested, and both return. The differences are equally important: David's trial came through a case brought to him, Solomon's through a possession that held his attention. The chapter is showing that the danger to a servant of God takes more than one form, and that the recovery is the same in each case.

---

## Verse 38:31

> ˹Remember˺ when the well-trained, swift horses were paraded before him in the evening

**Two Adjectives Doing the Work**

The horses are described with two qualities, and each carries meaning. The first means a stance — animals drawn up and standing with the poise of trained stock, the posture that in Arabian usage describes a horse resting on three legs with the toe of the fourth touching the ground. The second means breeding and speed: fine, noble, swift animals.

The pairing tells the reader what kind of property this is. These are not working animals or livestock for trade; they are a king's cavalry, bred, trained and presented. Such animals represent wealth, military capacity and status simultaneously, and in a pastoral society they are the single most visible form of power a ruler can display. The Qur'an elsewhere lists fine horses among the things made attractive to human beings: *“The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people.”* (3:14). The verse places Solomon's horses inside that list without comment.

**Why Horses in Particular**

The choice of subject is not arbitrary. Horses in the Qur'an are associated with war and with beauty at the same time, and the sūrah that opens with them makes the double character explicit: *“By the galloping, panting horses, striking sparks of fire ˹with their hoofs˺”* (100:1–2). They are also created for a purpose that includes display: *“˹He also created˺ horses, mules, and donkeys for your transportation and adornment.”* (16:8).

**The Evening and What It Cost**

The evening also carries a weight of its own in the Qur'an's devotional language. The two ends of the day are the appointed times of remembrance, and David's praise in this same chapter is placed at the evening and after sunrise (38:18). To be absorbed in a display at the evening is therefore to be occupied at precisely the hour the chapter has already identified as belonging to God.

---

## Verse 38:32

> He then proclaimed, “I am truly in love with ˹these˺ fine things out of remembrance for Allah,” until they went out of sight

**"The Love of Good Things"**

The expression translated *these fine things* is a single Arabic word, al-khayr, which means the good or the wealth, and in the usage of the Qur'an and of the Arabs it often means property — horses, cattle, gold. The word appears in the same sense elsewhere: *“and they are truly extreme in their love of ˹worldly˺ gains”* (100:8), where the context is the horses of the sūrah's opening oath.

**Two Prepositions, Two Verdicts**

The clause translated *out of remembrance for Allah* has been read in two directions, and the difference between them is the whole interpretive question of this passage. The standard reading takes the preposition as one of preference: I loved the love of good things *more than* the remembrance of my Lord. On that reading the sentence is an admission of having allowed a good thing to displace a better one.

Al-Rāzī proposed the alternative: I loved the love of good things *on account of* the remembrance of my Lord — that is, his love of anything in this world exists within his devotion and is governed by it. The reading has a motive, which is the defence of prophetic impeccability, and it produces an internally consistent account that requires no unreliable reports. Its weakness is that it makes the following verse inexplicable: a man who has just declared his love properly ordered would have no reason to recall the horses and act upon them. The preference reading remains the stronger, because it is the only one that explains what happens next.

---

## Verse 38:33

> ˹He ordered,˺ “Bring them back to me!” Then he began to rub down their legs and necks

**A Command With Two Possible Objects**

The order is short and its object is a pronoun, which is why the verse has generated so much discussion. The pronoun can refer to the horses, and the command then means *bring the animals back to me*. It can also be read with the sun as its object, since the same pronoun can mean *it*, and the command then means *bring it back to me* — restore the day so that the prayer missed can be prayed in its time.

**Masaḥa: Between Stroking and Striking**

The verb describing what Solomon did to the horses' legs and necks is masaḥa, which means to pass the hand over something, to wipe or to rub. In ordinary usage it is a gesture of tenderness or of grooming. It is also used idiomatically with the sword, in the expression *he stroked them with the sword*, meaning he struck them.

The body parts themselves are a small clue that cuts both ways. Legs and necks are where a groom works and where a sword falls, and the pairing is exactly as ambiguous as the verb. A reader determined to find a slaughter will find it here; a reader determined to find a caress will find that too.

---

## Verse 38:34

> And indeed, We tested Solomon, placing a ˹deformed˺ body on his throne, then he turned ˹to Allah in repentance˺

**"Then He Turned"**

The final clause is the shortest in the verse and the only one the chapter is interested in. The verb is from the same root as the intensive noun used of Solomon in verse 30 — the one who turns repeatedly — and its appearance here confirms what the earlier verse announced. The man described as constantly returning is shown returning.

The sequence is also informative. The turning comes after the trial and not before it. Nothing in the verse suggests that Solomon's turning caused the trial or shortened it; the trial happened, and the response followed. That order protects the account from becoming a formula in which repentance is a technique for escaping difficulty. The turning is a response to being tested, not a means of avoiding the test.

The word also allows a second sense, since the same verb can mean simply to return. Some commentators read it as Solomon's return to his throne or his recovery of health after the trial, and the two senses are not in conflict. A man who turns to God returns to his place, and the chapter is content to let the spiritual and the practical meanings stand together.

---

The trial on the throne is described with the word for the body placed there, and the commentators differed between the readings that identify the figure as a child of Solomon who died, as a jinn who took his form, or as a bodily weakness; what the verse draws out is the turn that follows — he repented. The same root of turning is used of Solomon's own title in the sūrah's earlier verse, where he is called the one who turns to God (38:30), and the Book attaches the pattern to David in the verse before, who asked forgiveness and bowed (38:24). The sūrah's method is to name the office and the fault together, so that the king's standing is measured by the return, not by the absence of error.

## Verse 38:35

> He prayed, “My Lord! Forgive me, and grant me an authority that will never be matched by anyone after me. You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺.”

**"Not Matched by Anyone After Me"**

The second request is for a sovereignty that will not befit anyone after him, and the phrase has been read in two ways. The ordinary reading is temporal: a kingdom such as no later ruler will equal. The alternative takes the preposition as meaning *besides* or *other than*, giving the sense of a kingdom that will not be given to anyone in addition to him.

**The Name That Makes the Request Possible**

The appeal is well formed, because the request is not something that can be earned. No amount of service entitles a man to a kingdom unmatched in history, and Solomon does not claim that it does. He names the attribute under which such a gift could be given and asks under it. The same name appeared earlier in the chapter, attached to the question about the treasuries of mercy (38:9), and the echo is deliberate: the treasury belongs to the Giver, and the Giver gives to whom He will.

---

The request for an unmatched kingdom has been read in two ways, and both are held by the commentators: a kingdom never given to anyone after him, which the tradition supplies with the image of the wind and the jinn in the sūrah of the Prophets (21:81–82), and a dominion whose like no one would claim after him, which many read as a rebuke of the human ambition for power. The prayer begins with the request for forgiveness, and the closing name God is given here, the Giver, appears also among the names at the end of the verse: Solomon asks and God grants, which the sūrah states in the next verse with the gift of the wind. The Book records the outcome of the gift in the sūrah of the Sheba story, where the throne, the birds and the jinn all serve him.

## Verse 38:36

> So We subjected to him the wind, blowing gently at his command to wherever he pleased

**The Answer Arrives as a Gift, Not a Verdict**

The verse begins with the conjunction of consequence, and what follows is the reply to the prayer of the previous verse. There is no preamble, no condition and no comment on the request. Solomon asked for a kingdom unmatched after him, and the first instalment is the wind.

**The Wind Subjected, Not Tamed**

The verb is the same one used of the mountains in David's case (38:18) — a making subservient, a placing under command. The wind is not persuaded, negotiated with or harnessed by technology. It is placed under a man's direction by an act of God, and its obedience is a consequence of that act rather than a property of the wind.

**"Blowing Gently": The Quality of the Gift**

The adjective applied to the wind is one of softness and ease, and it stands in deliberate contrast to the description given elsewhere: *“And to Solomon We subjected the raging winds, blowing by his command to the land We had showered with blessings. It is We Who know everything.”* (21:81). The two verses describe the same gift from two angles — a force that is violent in itself and gentle in its service.

The combination is the point. A power that could destroy is placed under a servant's command and made to carry rather than to break. Saba supplies the measure of the result: *“its morning stride was a month's journey and so was its evening stride”* (34:12). The gentleness is not weakness; it is a force held in reserve, and the holding is what the subjection consists in.

**Command Without Coercion**

The phrase *at his command* describes a relationship that has no human analogue. A king commands soldiers, who can disobey; he commands subjects, who can flee. The wind can do neither, and its obedience is total and immediate.

---

## Verse 38:37

> And ˹We subjected to him˺ every builder and diver of the jinn

**Who the Shayāṭīn Are**

The beings named here are the shayāṭīn, a term the Qur'an uses for the rebellious among the jinn as well as for the rebellious among human beings. The jinn are a created order distinct from humanity: *“As for the jinn, We created them earlier from smokeless fire.”* (15:27). They are addressed by revelation alongside humans, and they are capable of belief and of rebellion.

The word shayṭān describes a character rather than a species, and its application here is informative. The beings subjected to Solomon are not the obedient jinn but the recalcitrant ones, which means the gift is a gift of control over adversaries. That is why the following verse adds a third category — others bound in chains — since some of the same beings required restraint as well as direction.

The same assembly is described in al-Naml as a military body rather than a workforce: jinn, humans and birds rallied and organised (27:17). Solomon's authority over them was therefore not a private arrangement but a public order of rank, which is what makes the gifts that follow intelligible as a kingdom rather than as a set of personal favours.

**Divers in the Sea**

Diving is the second occupation, and al-Anbiyāʾ confirms it with the same economy: *“And ˹We subjected˺ some jinn that dived for him, and performed other duties. It is We Who kept them in check.”* (21:82). The closing clause of that verse identifies the real agent of the subjection, and it is worth carrying across to this one: the divers are held in check by God, not by Solomon.

**The Limits Built Into the Gift**

The subjection is not unconditional, and the Qur'an states the condition plainly: *“And ˹We subjected˺ some of the jinn to work under him by his Lord's Will. And whoever of them deviated from Our command, We made them taste the torment of the blaze.”* (34:12). The jinn work under Solomon, but they answer to God, and deviation is punished by the one who lent the authority.

The arrangement also has a warning attached for the reader. The Qur'an records that human beings sought refuge with jinn and were led further astray by them: *“And some men used to seek refuge with some jinn—so they increased each other in wickedness.”* (72:6). Solomon ruled the jinn and was not harmed by them; ordinary people who traffic with them are harmed. The difference is the authority, and the authority was a gift to one man, not a possibility for all.

---

## Verse 38:38

> and others bound together in chains

**Bound in Chains**

The image is concrete: beings fastened together, restrained by physical bonds. The word used means fetters in which the hands are joined to the neck or to one another, and the plural form indicates that the restraint was applied to a number of them.

**"And Others": A Third Category**

The verse adds a group to the two named before it, and the addition changes the character of the whole account. The builders and the divers were subjected for work; this third group is subjected for restraint. The word *others* is deliberately unspecific, and the absence of a task attached to them is the information the verse is giving.

**Bound in Chains**

The physicality of the image is worth noting, because jinn are elsewhere described as invisible to human beings and as incapable of being held by ordinary means. To describe them as chained is to describe a subjection that operates at a level below the physical — an authority that reaches whatever they are. Al-Anbiyāʾ states the same fact in plainer terms: *“It is We Who kept them in check.”* (21:82). The chains are the visible form of a restraint whose agent is God.

---

## Verse 38:39

> ˹Allah said,˺ “This is Our gift, so give or withhold ˹as you wish˺, never to be called to account.”

**"Give or Withhold"**

The permission that follows is unusually wide. Solomon is told to bestow or to withhold as he sees fit, and no criterion is supplied for the choice. A ruler given that discretion over powers of this magnitude is being trusted in a way that has no parallel elsewhere in the chapter.

**The Gift Answering the Opening Verses**

The verse also answers the objectors of the sūrah's beginning, and the answer is precise. They asked whether they held the treasuries of God's mercy and whether the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belonged to them (38:9–10). Here is a man who was given a treasury to dispose of and a kingdom beyond any other, and the verse says in terms that it is a gift.

**A Sentence That Defines the Chapter's View of Power**

Every portrait in this sūrah is governed by the claim in this verse. David's kingdom was strengthened by God. Solomon's powers were subjected to him. Job's family was returned to him. In each case the possession is described as conferred, and in each case the recipient's response is gratitude or return rather than ownership.

The instruction attached to the building work elsewhere makes the same point from the other side: *“˹We ordered:˺ ‘Work gratefully, O family of David!’ ˹Only˺ a few of My servants are ˹truly˺ grateful.”* (34:13). A gift is met with gratitude, and gratitude is rare. The verse leaves the rarity standing beside the generosity, and the pairing is the chapter's summary of the human condition.

---

## Verse 38:40

> And he will indeed have ˹a status of˺ closeness to Us and an honourable destination

**The Same Promise in the Same Words, and Nearness After Power**

The verse repeats, almost word for word, the promise made to David fifteen verses earlier (38:25). Nearness to God, a honourable destination — the two gifts are given to the son in the same formulation in which they were given to the father, and the repetition is the chapter's clearest statement of what its portraits have in common.

The effect of the repetition is to make the promise a formula rather than a personal reward. A sentence used twice, of two men with very different lives and very different failures, is no longer a comment on either of them. It is a statement about the category they belong to: the servant who returns.

The order of the two gifts is worth attending to. Solomon has just been given the wind, the builders, the divers and the chained, and the verse that follows describes his reward as nearness to God. The greatest accumulation of power in the Book is followed by a promise that contains no power at all.

That contrast is the chapter's judgment on the value of the gifts. Nearness is a relation, and a relation cannot be commanded, spent or lost through mismanagement. The kingdom Solomon asked for was granted in full, and the verse then identifies what he actually has that matters. A reader who remembers the prayer will notice that nearness was not part of it.

**Nearness as the Highest Category the Book Offers**

The word used for closeness here is the same one the Qur'an applies to the highest of the resurrected. Wāqiʿah divides humanity into three groups and names the first of them those brought near: *“They are the ones nearest ˹to Allah˺”* (56:11), and it describes their end: *“So, if the deceased is one of those brought near ˹to Us˺”* (56:88). Al-Muṭaffifīn places the same group at the centre of the highest record: *“witnessed by those nearest ˹to Allah˺”* (83:21).

The Qur'an is equally explicit that this rank is reached by belief and action rather than by wealth or connection: *“It is not your wealth or children that bring you closer to Us. But those who believe and do good—it is they who will have a multiplied reward for what they did, and they will be secure in ˹elevated˺ mansions.”* (34:37). Applied to Solomon, the promise therefore names the highest category available to a human being, and it gives it to a man whose chapter-length record includes a distraction and a theft of his throne. The giving is the argument.

---

## Verse 38:41

> And remember Our servant Job, when he cried out to his Lord, “Satan has afflicted me with distress and suffering.”

**Blame Placed Where It Belongs, and Why Job Comes Third**

The complaint names an agent, and the naming is theologically careful. Job does not say that God has afflicted him, and he does not say that no one has. He attributes the harm to Satan, and the attribution allows the verse to record a man's distress without recording an accusation against God.

The naming also identifies the nature of the test. This is not a random misfortune but an assault, and an assault implies an adversary with an aim. The Qur'an's account of Satan throughout is consistent: he works on human beings through loss, fear and suggestion, and he is permitted to operate within limits set for him. Job's cry places his suffering inside that framework, and the framework is what makes his endurance meaningful rather than merely impressive.

The order of the three servants is not accidental, and the third place is the most instructive. David held a throne and a revelation; Solomon held more than David, including the wind and the jinn. Job held nothing at all. The chapter moves from power to greater power to total loss, and it gives each man the same commendation at the end.

**The Qur'an's Job Is Not a Treatise on Evil**

The Book's longest account of Job is four verses, and the shortest is two. Al-Anbiyāʾ records the same cry in shorter form: *“And ˹remember˺ when Job cried out to his Lord, ‘I have been touched with adversity. You are the Most Merciful of the merciful.’”* (21:83), and the answer follows at once: *“So We answered his prayer and removed his adversity, and gave him back his family, twice as many, as a mercy from Us and a lesson for the ˹devoted˺ worshippers.”* (21:84).

What the Qur'an does not do is turn the story into a philosophical inquiry. There is no council of friends, no debate about why the righteous suffer, no divine speech from the whirlwind. The account is a record of endurance and its outcome, and the omission is a choice about what the story is for. Job is listed among the prophets to whom revelation was given (4:163) and among those God guided (6:84); he is presented as a model, not as a case study.

---

## Verse 38:42

> ˹We responded,˺ “Stomp your foot: ˹now˺ here is a cool ˹and refreshing˺ spring for washing and drinking.”

**Stomp Your Foot: Washing and Drinking, Outside and Inside**

The command is concrete and physical, and its specificity has been read in two ways. The literal reading takes it at face value: the ground was struck, and the spring came out of it. The second treats the striking as the occasion rather than the cause, since a foot cannot open a spring, and reads the command as a way of directing Job's attention to where the water was already about to appear.

Both readings preserve the same point, which is that the water was given in connection with an act of obedience. The Qur'an's other account of Job does not mention the foot at all — it says simply that God answered him and removed his affliction (21:84) — and the addition here is the detail the chapter wants: the servant participates in his own relief.

The two purposes correspond to two kinds of affliction, and the correspondence is the reason the verse names both. Washing addresses what is external — the sores, the skin, the visible marks of the disease. Drinking addresses what is internal — the organs, the blood, the damage no one could see.

**The Rain, the Fields and the Golden Locusts**

The tradition adds two details to the account, and both are recorded in the most authoritative collection. Rain is reported to have fallen on Job's fields until they were full, restoring the property he had lost. And the Prophet ﷺ reported that while Job was bathing, locusts of gold began falling on him, and he started gathering them in his garment. His Lord said to him, *have I not made you rich enough not to need what you see*, and Job answered, *yes, my Lord, but I cannot do without Your blessing* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3391; also 7493).

The second report is the more instructive of the two, because it answers a question nobody asked. A man who has been stripped of everything and is now being given gold does not stop collecting it, and his explanation is not greed but gratitude — he cannot bear to refuse a gift from God. The chapter's portrait of Job ends where the report ends: a man whose relationship to what he has is defined by who gave it.

---

## Verse 38:43

> And We gave him back his family, twice as many, as a mercy from Us and a lesson for people of reason

**A Restoration in Two Parts, a Mercy and a Lesson**

The verse describes a single gift with two components, and the second component is what makes the account distinctive. Job is given back his family — the same family, restored — and then given their like along with them. The doubling is not a replacement of the lost by the new but an addition to the returned.

Commentators differ on the mechanics of the first part. Some hold that the family was brought back to life; others that they were healed of what had afflicted them; others that they had been absent and were returned. The differences matter less than the shared claim, which is that what was lost was not compensated but recovered. The verse is explicit that the same people came back, and the explicitness is what gives the doubling its force.

The two reasons given are a mercy from God and a reminder for people of understanding. The first is directed at Job, the second at everyone who hears the story, and the pairing tells the reader that the account has two audiences.

**"Twice as Many": What the Doubling Signifies**

An increase of the same kind is a different thing from a substitute. A man who loses a child and later has another has not been repaid; a man whose child is returned and who is given more besides has been given something that exceeds the original state. The verse's arithmetic is doing this work, and it is doing it deliberately.

**"People of Reason" Again**

The phrase used here is the same one that appeared in verse 29, where the Book was said to be revealed so that people of reason may be mindful. The word means those who have the kernel of a thing, its inner substance rather than its shell, and its reappearance at the close of Job's account connects the two verses.

The connection is precise. The chapter said that its material exists to be contemplated, and it now names the contemplation it wants: a reader who sees the story of Job and draws the conclusion from it. The people of reason are not those who admire Job's endurance from a distance; they are those who understand what the outcome means for their own afflictions.

The phrase appears repeatedly across the Qur'an in exactly this function, always at the end of a sign or a story, always naming the audience that has understood it (2:179; 3:190; 12:111; 14:52; 39:9). Its use here closes the account of Job the way a full stop closes a sentence, and it marks the reader who has followed as one of them.

---

## Verse 38:44

> ˹And We said to him,˺ “Take in your hand a bundle of grass, and strike ˹your wife˺ with it, and do not break your oath.” We truly found him patient. What an excellent servant ˹he was˺! Indeed, he ˹constantly˺ turned ˹to Allah˺

**The Third Use of the Word, and the Story of the Wife**
The standard explanation is that Job's wife, under pressure from Satan, urged him to say or do something that would bring relief, and that Job, angered by her having listened to the enemy of God, swore to punish her. God then permitted him to strike her with a bundle of grass so that the oath would be fulfilled without injury.
**"We Truly Found Him Patient"**
The commendation uses a verb of finding, and the verb is significant. God did not make Job patient or appoint him patient; He found him so, which means the patience was exercised before it was recognised. The recognition is a verdict on conduct already performed.
The word for patience here is the same root the chapter used in its opening instruction to the Prophet — be patient with what they say (38:17). The connection is deliberate. The man told to endure is shown a servant whose endurance was examined and confirmed, and the confirmation is stated in the divine voice rather than in a narrator's summary. Job's patience is not the reader's impression of him; it is a finding.
Al-Anbiyāʾ makes the same finding in different words, listing Job with Ishmael and Ⱬul-Kifl as men who were steadfast (21:85). The two chapters agree on the verdict and differ only in the company they name, which suggests that the patience is the quality being measured in both places rather than the suffering that occasioned it.

**What the Verse Establishes About Oaths**
The command to take the bundle is the one thing in Job's story the jurists seized on, and the lesson is about fulfilling what a man has sworn rather than about the stroke itself. Job had sworn in his anger, and God provided a way to keep the oath without harming the woman — a light stroke with a bundle of grass, in the traditional explanation — and left him the reputation of a man who kept his word to the letter. The scholars drew two things from it: that an oath made in a moment of anger is still an oath, and that the law's preference is to find a way to fulfil it, if it can be done without injury, rather than to ignore it. The verse does not treat the wife's conduct as the subject; it treats the servant's word as the subject.
**The Commendation and What It Is Attached To**
The sentence that closes the verse — *We found him patient, an excellent servant, always turning back to God* — names two qualities and joins them. The patience is the endurance of the years of illness, and the turning back is what he did with the years: he did not use them to accuse God of anything. The Book rarely puts a commendation this direct, and it is stated in God's own voice, which is the point of the *found*, as the section already notes above. The verse is placed in a run of the sūrah's portraits — David before it, Solomon and Abraham and Isaac and Jacob after (38:45-47) — and the selection is the sūrah's argument: these were men, with households and tempers and oaths, and the Book records them as servants who kept turning back.

## Verse 38:45

> And remember Our servants: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—the men of strength and insight

**"Servants", in the Plural**

The word used is the plural of ʿabd, the same term applied individually to David, Solomon and Job in their own verses. Its plural form here is the first time the chapter speaks of servants as a group, and the change of number is significant.

Servanthood in the Qur'an is not a rank attained by individuals in isolation; it is a condition shared by a line. The three men named are a father, a son and a grandson, and their being described together as servants makes the point that the condition is transmitted and sustained across generations. Al-Anʿām states the same fact in the form of a genealogy: *“And We blessed him with Isaac and Jacob. We guided them all as We earlier guided Noah and those among his descendants: David, Solomon, Job, Joseph, Moses, and Aaron. This is how We reward the good-doers.”* (6:84).

**A Chapter That Has Become a Roster**

From this verse onward the sūrah stops narrating and begins listing. Three names here, three more in verse 48, and then the two destinations. The change of mode is a sign that the argument is complete and the chapter is now assembling its witnesses.

---

The three names are grouped as the men of strength and insight, and the word for insight (*abṣār*) recurs in the sūrah's counsel to the Prophet at the end of the passage: follow the guidance given, and be patient, for God sees what people do (38:45 and the context of the sūrah's close). The Book describes the same quality in Abraham's handling of the star, the moon and the sun in the sūrah of the Cattle, where he reasons his way past each of them (6:75–79), which is the insight the verse names. The plural address to the servants of God gathers the sūrah's five noble figures so that their lives can be read as one pattern of strength and understanding.

## Verse 38:46

> We truly chose them for the honour of proclaiming the Hereafter

**"The Honour of Proclaiming the Hereafter"**

The phrase translated here has been rendered in several ways, and the range of meaning is narrow but real. It can be read as a purification *by means of* something pure — the remembrance of the Abode being the pure thing through which the men were made pure. It can be read as a purifying of them *with* a pure quality. And it can be read, as here, as a honour or distinction conferred, consisting in the constant remembrance of the next world.

**Why the Abode and Not the Garden**

The word used is the Abode — a general term for the dwelling place to come — rather than the Garden or the Fire specifically. The generality is significant. A man who remembers the Garden wants something; a man who remembers the Abode knows that this world is not where he lives, and the knowledge governs everything else.

That is the quality the verse identifies as the distinction of the three men, and it is the quality the chapter has been describing in its portraits from the beginning. David's kingdom, Solomon's wind, Job's restoration — each is presented as a loan, and each recipient is praised for treating it as one.

---

## Verse 38:47

> And in Our sight they are truly among the chosen and the finest

**The Verse That Prepares the Destinations**

Structurally this is the last verse about people before the chapter turns to places. The next verse adds three more names, and then the sūrah describes the honourable destination of the righteous and the evil destination of the transgressors. The verdict here is the bridge between the two halves.

The bridge is a claim about what survives. Wealth, kingdoms, health and family are all described in the preceding verses, and every one of them is temporary. The verdict *in Our sight* is the only thing in the chapter that is not subject to time, and the chapter places it immediately before describing where the two groups end up.

The placement also gives the descriptions that follow their meaning. A garden and a fire are only destinations if something arrives at them, and the verse has just said what arrives: a standing, fixed in a sight that does not change. The reader is told who the travellers are before he is shown the two places they travel to.

---

## Verse 38:48

> Also remember Ishmael, Elisha, and Ⱬul-Kifl. All are among the best

**Ishmael, and Three More Names Under One Description**

The verse adds three prophets to the three named before it, and it describes all three with a single phrase: each is among the best. No individual characterisation is given, no incident is recalled, and no distinction is drawn between them. The verse is a list with a verdict attached.

The first name is the most familiar to the chapter's original audience, and the familiarity is why he is placed first. Ishmael is the ancestor of the Arabs and the son of Abraham, named in the previous verse; his story is told in al-Baqarah, where he and his father raise the foundations of the House (2:125–128), and he appears throughout the Qur'an as a man of his word and a prophet.

**Elisha, Mentioned Twice in the Whole Book**

The second name is al-Yasaʿ, and it occurs only here and in al-Anʿām, where he is listed with Ishmael, Jonah and Lot among those favoured above the worlds: *“˹We also guided˺ Ishmael, Elisha, Jonah, and Lot, favouring each over other people ˹of their time˺.”* (6:86). No narrative is attached to him anywhere in the Qur'an.

**Ⱬul-Kifl: A Title Rather Than a Name**

The third figure is the most obscure in the Qur'an, and the obscurity is built into the name. Ⱬul-Kifl means the possessor of a portion or share, and the word kifl is used elsewhere of a double portion of mercy: *“˹And˺ He will grant you a double share of His mercy”* (57:28). The title has been read as describing a man who bore a double share of prophetic labour and reward.

The same root lies behind the verb meaning to assume responsibility or to pledge, and the commentators developed the reading accordingly: a man who pledged himself to a prophet, or who pledged to fast by day, pray by night and judge without anger. Attempts to identify him with a known prophet — Elijah, Joshua, Zechariah, Ezekiel — rest on these etymologies rather than on evidence, and the majority of commentators hold that he was not a prophet at all but a righteous man. Al-Anbiyāʾ groups him with Ishmael and Enoch as men who were steadfast (21:85), which is the most the texts commit to.

**Why an Obscure Figure Is Included**

The inclusion of Ⱬul-Kifl in a list of prophets is deliberate and instructive. Every other name in the chapter is familiar to the reader; this one is not, and no story is attached to it anywhere in the Book. A man known only by a title and a verdict stands beside Ishmael and Elisha without any explanation.

The notables measured a messenger by his standing in Makkah and found him insufficient (38:4).

---

## Verse 38:49

> This is ˹all˺ a reminder. And the righteous will certainly have an honourable destination

**"This Is a Reminder"**

The verse opens by naming what the chapter has been. Three readings of the demonstrative are available, and each is defensible. It can refer to the Qur'an as a whole: this Book is a reminder. It can refer to the narratives just completed: these stories are a reminder. And it can refer to the sūrah itself, at the point where its second half begins.

The three readings are not rivals, because the Qur'an uses the same word — dhikr — in all three senses, and the opening verse of this chapter swore by the Book as one *full of reminders* (38:1). The verse therefore closes a circle that was opened forty-eight verses earlier, and it closes it with the same noun.

**A Promise Given to a Class, and the Destination Before the Description**

The second half of the verse repeats, almost exactly, the promise made to David in verse 25 and to Solomon in verse 40. An honourable destination — the same two words, the same construction, now applied not to an individual but to the righteous as a group.

---

## Verse 38:50

> the Gardens of Eternity, whose gates will be open for them

**Gardens of Eternity, and Arrival Without an Obstacle**

The destination is named, and the name carries two claims. The first is plurality: gardens, not a garden. The Qur'an speaks of Paradise as containing gardens of different kinds, and the same title for the abode recurs across many sūrahs, from al-Tawbah to Ghāfir. The second claim is permanence: the gardens are gardens of Eden, of abiding, and the word means a settled dwelling rather than a visit.

The combination answers the two objections a reader might raise about any promised reward. Plurality answers the fear of monotony; permanence answers the fear of loss. The chapter has spent its first half describing possessions that could be taken — a throne, a kingdom, a family, a herd of horses — and it now describes a possession defined by the fact that it cannot be.

Raʿd gives the believers' arrival in fuller form, and the fullness makes the point: they enter the Gardens of Eternity together with the righteous of their parents, spouses and descendants, and the angels come to them from every gate with a greeting of peace for their perseverance (13:23–24). Every element of that scene — company, greeting, welcome from every direction — is the opposite of what the chapter will describe in verses 55 to 64.

**The Eight Gates and What They Imply**

The tradition supplies a detail that the verse assumes, and it is recorded in the most authoritative collections. The Prophet ﷺ said that Paradise has eight gates. That each is named for a deed: the one who belonged to the people of prayer is called through the gate of prayer, the faster through the gate of fasting, the giver of charity through the gate of charity. The one who strove through the gate of striving (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1897; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1027). Another report names one of the eight al-Rayyān, and says that none enter by it but those who fasted (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3257).

The reports explain what the plurality of gardens implies: the reward is differentiated by the deed. A person is not admitted through a general entrance but called by name from the door that corresponds to what he actually did. The verse's open gates are therefore not eight identical openings but eight recognitions.

**What the Open Gates Answer**

Nobody stands at these gates deciding who belongs. The gates are open, and the people who arrive are the people who were expected. The chapter's dispute about rank ends in a scene where rank is not mentioned, and the only thing that determines which door a person is called from is what he did.

The same image is turned against the other group later in the Book, and the turning sharpens both. The Fire is described as having seven gates, with a portion assigned to each (15:44), and the difference between assigned portions and open doors is the difference between the two destinations described in this chapter.

---

## Verse 38:51

> There they will recline, calling for abundant fruit and drink

**Reclining: A Posture With a Meaning**

The verse begins with a bodily position, and in the world that produced this text the position carries information. Reclining at a meal was not the ordinary posture of eating; it was the posture of a host at leisure, of a man who has finished his work and is being served. To say that the inhabitants of the Garden recline is to say that nothing is required of them.

The Qur'an uses the same image repeatedly and consistently. The inhabitants are described as reclining on thrones lined up and facing one another (52:20), as facing each other on thrones (37:44), and as resting in shade with their spouses on canopied couches (36:56). The repetition is not decorative. A posture repeated across many sūrahs is a claim about the condition of the people described, and the claim is that the labour of life has ended.

**Calling, Not Fetching**

The second detail is more informative than it looks. The inhabitants call for fruit and drink, and the calling is the whole of their contribution. They do not rise, do not go, do not carry and do not prepare. The desire is expressed and the thing arrives.

The Qur'an states the same arrangement elsewhere with equal plainness: *“And We will ˹continually˺ provide them with whatever fruit or meat they desire.”* (52:22). The provision is continuous and the desire is the only mechanism required. The verse is describing a state in which the gap between wanting and having has closed, and the closing of that gap is what the word *provision* in the next verse will formalise.

The calling is also a social act, and the chapter is aware of it. A person who calls for something is speaking to someone, and the scene therefore contains speech that is not argument, negotiation or defence. The Qur'an notes elsewhere that the drink of the Garden leads to no idle talk and no incitement to sin (52:23), which describes the same conversation from the other side.

**A Scene of Effortlessness, and Drink Without Its Consequences**

The drink is named without qualification here, and the qualification is supplied elsewhere. Al-Ṭūr describes the cup that passes between them as containing no idle talk and no incitement to sin (52:23), which identifies what the drink is by naming what it lacks. The pleasures of the Garden are given without the harms that accompany their counterparts in this world.

The distinction is theologically careful. The Qur'an prohibits wine in this life and promises drink in the next, and the difference is not a contradiction but a description: what is promised is the pleasure without the damage.

Every element of the verse is chosen to remove effort. The inhabitants are already reclining; they call rather than go; the fruit is abundant so no one need economise; the drink arrives without consequence. Nothing in the scene requires exertion, planning or restraint.

That removal is the answer to the chapter's earlier portraits, in which effort was the whole of the matter.

Muḥammad's description of the Garden elsewhere in the Qur'an is equally concrete: rivers of fresh water, of milk that does not change in taste, of wine delicious to drink and of pure honey, with all kinds of fruit and forgiveness from their Lord (47:15).

---

## Verse 38:52

> And with them will be maidens of modest gaze and equal age

**"Of Modest Gaze"**

The same description appears in al-Raḥmān with an addition that sharpens it: *“In both ˹Gardens˺ will be maidens of modest gaze, who no human or jinn has ever touched before.”* (55:56). The addition identifies the restraint as fidelity rather than shyness, and it makes the companionship described here a relation of mutual exclusivity. The verse's four words carry that content, and the Qur'an's other uses of the phrase confirm it.

**Companions, Not Attendants**

The verse adds a third element to the scene, and its grammar places the companions alongside the inhabitants rather than below them. The phrase is *and with them*, which is the language of company rather than of service. Nobody in the scene is described as attending on anybody else, and the absence of service is consistent with the previous verse, in which the fruit and drink arrive without anyone fetching them.

The Qur'an's descriptions of the Garden elsewhere use the same construction. The inhabitants recline with their spouses in shade (36:56), and they are paired with companions of a particular description (52:20; 44:54). The pairing is presented as part of the reward rather than as an addition to it, and the verse here follows that pattern exactly.

**"Of Modest Gaze"**

The first quality named is a restraint of the eyes, and the commentators explain it as looking at no one but one's own companion. The gaze is limited by choice rather than by prohibition, and the limitation is what makes the company exclusive.

The emphasis on the eyes is also an emphasis on attention, which is the subject the chapter has been circling since the horse review. Solomon was undone for a moment by what he looked at; the companions of the Garden are described by what they look at and by the fact that they look nowhere else. The chapter treats the direction of the gaze as a moral fact, and it treats it the same way in both places.

**"Equal Age"**

The second quality is a matching in years, and the word means those of one age or of equal maturity. The detail has a specific function: it removes from the scene any relation of dependency or of unequal standing. Companions of equal age are peers, and the description is a description of peers.

Al-Nabaʾ states the same quality in similar terms, describing companions of equal age (78:33), and al-Wāqiʿah describes them with a different attribute altogether (56:22). The variation across sūrahs suggests that the Qur'an is not building a single fixed portrait but naming qualities that matter: fidelity, compatibility and equality. This verse names two of the three.

The pairing of the two qualities is worth noting as well. Fidelity without equality would describe a servant; equality without fidelity would describe a stranger. The verse gives both, and the combination describes a companion in the full sense — someone who stays and someone who stands beside.

---

## Verse 38:53

> This is what you are promised for the Day of Reckoning

**"For the Day of Reckoning"**

The phrase attaches the promise to an event, and the attachment is the verse's argument. The Garden is not a reward distributed at intervals during life; it is what is promised for the Day of Reckoning, and the Day has been named twice already in this chapter — once in the mockery of the pagans (38:16) and once in the charge to David (38:26).

The three occurrences form a sequence. The pagans asked to have their portion before the Day; David was warned that those who neglect the Day will be punished severely; and here the Day is named as the occasion on which the promise is delivered. The same event is the object of mockery, the ground of warning and the date of fulfilment, and the verse supplies the third of those functions.

**A Promise and Its Date**

Every promise has a date, and the verse supplies the date. The effect is to convert a description into an obligation. What has been described is not a possibility or a hope but a commitment with an appointed occasion attached to it, and the occasion is the one the chapter has already said cannot be postponed.

The pairing of promise and date also answers the mockery of verse 16. The pagans asked to have their portion before the Day of Reckoning, and the verse replies by placing both portions — the Garden and, in the verses that follow, the Fire — on the same date. Neither is available early, and neither can be deferred.

There is a further implication in naming the Day of Reckoning as the occasion. A reckoning is an accounting, and an accounting requires records. The verse therefore ties the promise to the same process by which the transgressors will be judged, which means the two outcomes described in this chapter are produced by one procedure rather than by two separate decisions.

**The Shortest Verse Carrying the Most Weight**

The economy is the chapter's habit at its turning points. The verdict on the three patriarchs was given in a single sentence (38:47), the verdict on the three lesser-known prophets in another (38:48), and the identification of the Garden as a promise in this one. The chapter reserves its elaboration for its arguments and states its conclusions flatly.

---

## Verse 38:54

> This is indeed Our provision that will never end

**Provision, Not Reward**

The verse names what has been described, and the name it chooses is unexpected. Not a reward, not a recompense, not a wage — a provision. The word means what is supplied for the sustenance of a living being, the regular allotment on which a life depends, and its use here places the Garden in the category of food rather than of payment.

**"That Will Never End"**

The claim is stated flatly and without any qualification whatsoever. The provision does not diminish, does not run out and is not withdrawn. Al-Naḥl makes the same contrast with the provision of this world in a single sentence: *“Whatever you have will end, but whatever Allah has is everlasting. And We will certainly reward the steadfast according to the best of their deeds.”* (16:96).

Raʿd makes the same point in the language of the Garden itself: eternal is its fruit as well as its shade, and that is the outcome for the righteous while the outcome for the disbelievers is the Fire (13:35). The eternity is predicated of the fruit and the shade as well as of the place, which is precisely what this verse claims — the supply and not merely the location is without end.

---

## Verse 38:55

> That is that. And the transgressors will certainly have the worst destination

**The Turn to the Other Destination, and Its Closing Formula**

The verse opens with a phrase that closes an account, and the closing is emphatic. What has been described is settled; no further description is needed and no revision is possible. The formula is used in Arabic to end a matter that has been fully stated, and its use here signals that the description of the Garden is complete.

The second half of the verse turns without any connective of contrast. There is no *but* and no *however*; the sentence simply moves from the destination of the righteous to the destination of the transgressors, and the abruptness of the move is the point.

**"The Transgressors"**

The term also connects this verse to the earlier descriptions of the destroyed peoples. The parties of Noah, ʿĀd, Pharaoh, Thamūd, Lot's people and the residents of the Forest were all described with a single verb — they denied (38:14) — and the denial was an exceeding of a limit set by a messenger. The verse is naming the same people under a different heading, and the heading is what determines the destination.

The word also has a specific application to the audience of this sūrah. The men who demanded that their punishment be hastened (38:16) were exceeding a limit in the most literal sense: they were asking for the boundary between warning and seizure to be removed. The verse names the category that such a request belongs to, and it names the destination attached to it.

---

## Verse 38:56

> Hell, where they will burn. What an evil place to rest

The verse names the destination and describes what happens there in a single verb. The inhabitants burn, and the burning is given as a continuous state rather than an event. Nothing is said about how long it lasts, what precedes it or what follows it; the verse gives the place and the condition and stops.

**"What an Evil Place to Rest"**

The word carries an irony that the chapter sets up deliberately. The righteous were described as reclining (38:51), and reclining is the posture of rest; the transgressors are given a bed, and the bed is a place of burning. The two descriptions use the language of repose, and only one of them describes repose. The verse's exclamation is the reader's cue to notice the inversion.

The inversion is also permanent. A bed implies staying, and the chapter has already described the Fire as a place to settle (38:56). What is given to the transgressors is not a punishment with a term but a dwelling with the shape of comfort and the substance of fire.

The Garden received six verses and the Fire receives this one plus the four that follow, but the balance is misleading. The verses describing the Fire are shorter, and the shortest of them is this one. The chapter does not elaborate on the punishment, and the refusal to elaborate is itself a statement.

There is a further reason for the brevity, and it belongs to the chapter's audience. The men of Makkah had asked to have their punishment hastened (38:16), which means they did not believe it existed. A reader in that position is not moved by graphic description; he is moved by certainty. The verse supplies certainty in the shortest form available, and it leaves the particulars to the verses that follow.

---

## Verse 38:57

> Let them then taste this: boiling water and ˹oozing˺ pus

**"Let Them Taste": An Imperative of Retribution**

The verse opens with a command, and the command is addressed to no one in particular. Let them taste is the form Arabic uses for a retributive imperative — an order that expresses the justice of what is happening rather than directing anyone to perform it. The same formula appears in the Qur'an's descriptions of punishment elsewhere, and it always carries the sense of *this is what they earned*.

The verb of tasting is important.

These are the people who asked for their portion to be hastened (38:16), and the verse grants the request in the only terms in which it can now be granted.

**Boiling Water**

The first of the two drinks is described with a word meaning liquid at its maximum heat, and the Qur'an uses it repeatedly for the drink of the Fire (6:70; 37:67; 44:46; 56:42; 56:54; 88:5). Al-Ḥajj describes the application in a way that leaves nothing to the imagination: boiling water poured over the heads of those for whom garments of fire have been cut (22:19).

The choice of water is significant, because water is the universal object of thirst. The punishment is not an exotic substance but the ordinary remedy for thirst taken to the opposite extreme. A person who drinks it does not quench anything, and the drinking is repeated. The verse is describing a punishment shaped like a relief.

The shape is what makes it severe. A punishment that bore no resemblance to a good would be met by resistance; a punishment that arrives in the form of the thing wanted is met by hope, and the hope is disappointed each time. The Qur'an's account of the Fire is full of such deceptions, and this verse supplies the clearest of them.

**Ghassāq: The Cold That Cannot Be Borne**

The second drink is named with a word that occurs only twice in the Qur'an, here and in al-Nabaʾ, where it is paired with the same boiling water: *“There they will not taste any coolness or drink, except boiling water and ˹oozing˺ pus”* (78:24–25). The commentators explain it as a fluid so cold that it cannot be endured, and also as the discharge of the inhabitants of the Fire.

Both explanations are supported by the word's usage, and the pairing in al-Nabaʾ suggests that the cold reading is intended: the verse there says they will taste no coolness, and then names ghassāq as what they will taste instead. On that reading the second drink is the opposite of the first, and the opposition is the point of the pairing.

The rarity of the word is itself informative. A term used twice in the whole Book describes something with no counterpart in ordinary experience, and the verse does not explain it further. The reader is given a name and a temperature and left with both.

---

## Verse 38:58

> and other torments of the same sort

**A List Left Open**

The verse does three words of work and does them deliberately. After naming two specific punishments, the Qur'an adds a category rather than a further item, and the category is open-ended. The reader is told that more exists and is not told what it is.

The method is also honest. A text that invented specific torments would be claiming knowledge it does not have; a text that says *others of the same kind* claims only what has been revealed. The verse keeps the description inside the limits of the revelation, and the limit is what gives the remaining silence its weight.

**Why the Pattern Matters More Than the Items**

The Qur'an makes the same point elsewhere in explicit terms. Al-Nabaʾ excludes relief by name: *“There they will not taste any coolness or drink, except boiling water and ˹oozing˺ pus”* (78:24–25). The exclusion of coolness is the exclusion of the counterpart that would help, and this verse generalises the exclusion: whatever is given, its counterpart is given with it.

The pattern also explains why the punishment is described as a destination rather than as a sentence. A sentence has a term; a condition does not. The chapter named the Fire as a place to settle (38:56), and a settled condition of matched opposites has no internal mechanism for ending.

---

## Verse 38:59

> ˹The misleaders will say to one another,˺ “Here is a crowd ˹of followers˺ being thrown in with us. They are not welcome, ˹for˺ they ˹too˺ will burn in the Fire.”

**A Crowd Rushing In**

The verse shifts from description to dialogue, and the first thing it reports is movement. A crowd is rushing in, and the word used conveys a pressing, hurried entry — the same verb the Qur'an uses elsewhere for a group entering a place in a mass. The arrival is not orderly and it is not chosen.

The contrast with the other destination is exact. The righteous arrive in successive groups at gates already open, and their keepers greet them with peace (39:73). This crowd is thrown in, and the people who are already there are the ones who notice it. The difference between arriving and being thrown is the difference between the two accounts, and the verse states it in a single word.

**The Leaders Speaking First**

The speakers are identified as those who led, and the identification shapes everything that follows. These are the men who set the course for others — the chiefs, the notables, the ones whose opinion the crowd followed in the world. Their first recorded act in the Fire is to complain about the arrival of the people who followed them.

The complaint contains no repentance and no concern. The leaders do not express sorrow at having misled anyone; they express displeasure at the company. Al-Aʿrāf records the same scene with the same ordering: whenever a group enters the Fire it curses the group that came before it (7:38). The verse is describing a habit, not an incident.

The habit connects directly to the opening of the sūrah. The chiefs of Quraysh went out telling people to hold fast to their gods (38:6), and the verse shows the same men standing in the same relation with the same followers, saying something quite different. Leadership survives into the Fire; responsibility for it does not.

**Why the Dialogue Begins Here**

The dialogue is also the chapter's answer to the slogan the chiefs carried through Makkah. *Carry on, and stand firm in devotion to your gods* (38:6) was an appeal to solidarity, and the verse shows what solidarity becomes when the gods have nothing to give. The group is intact, the loyalty is gone, and what remains is a crowd of people refusing each other.

---

## Verse 38:60

> The followers will respond, “No! You are not welcome! You brought this upon us. What an evil place for settlement!”

**An Accusation That Has Already Been Answered**

The Qur'an elsewhere records the same accusation and the answer given to it. Al-Aʿrāf reports the followers saying that it was the leaders who misled them, and asks for a doubled punishment for them; the reply is that the punishment has already been doubled for all of them, though they do not know it (7:38). The doubling applies to both groups, and the reason is that each bears its own burden as well as a share of the other's.

Al-ʿAnkabūt states the principle behind the reply: those who mislead will carry their own burdens and the burdens of others along with them (29:13), and the carrying does not reduce the burden of those who were misled. The verse here records the accusation without recording the answer, and the omission leaves the reader with the futility of it.

**"What an Evil Place for Settlement"**

The closing exclamation repeats the formula used of the place in verse 56, and the repetition is spoken this time by an inhabitant rather than by the text. The judgement the chapter passed on the Fire is confirmed from inside it, by a person who has arrived.

The confirmation is rhetorically effective because of who gives it. A warning issued from outside can be doubted; the same judgement uttered by someone who is inside cannot. The verse places the chapter's verdict in the mouth of a follower in the Fire, and the placement closes the account of the destination.

---

## Verse 38:61

> Adding, “Our Lord! Whoever brought this upon us, double their punishment in the Fire.”

**The Doubling Asked For**

The request is specific: double their punishment. Not punish them, and not punish them as we are punished, but twice as much. The speakers are not asking for justice in any general sense; they are asking for an inequality in their own favour.

The Qur'an records the same request elsewhere with the same wording. Al-Aʿrāf reports the followers saying that their leaders misled them and asking for the torment to be multiplied, and the answer given there is that it has already been multiplied for all of them, though they do not know it (7:38). The answer refuses the inequality while granting the substance, and it does so by telling the petitioners that they do not know what is actually happening.

The doubling the Qur'an describes is a doubling of a different kind. It is not one group receiving twice what another receives; it is each group receiving two punishments, one for its own straying and one for the straying it caused in others. Al-Aḥzāb records the same plea and the same logic (33:67–68). The request for a doubled punishment is granted, and granted to everyone, which is not what the petitioners wanted.

**Burdens Carried and Burdens Shared**

The principle behind the answer is stated plainly in al-ʿAnkabūt: those who sought to mislead others will carry their own burdens, and other burdens along with their own (29:13). The carrying is additive, and the addition does not transfer anything.

That last point is the verse's sharpest implication. A reader might suppose that if the leaders carry extra burdens, the followers carry fewer. The Qur'an says the opposite explicitly, and the commentators note that the punishment of those who were led astray is not lessened by the doubling given to those who led them (7:38–39). The account is not a zero-sum distribution.

**What the Request Reveals**

The petition is a diagnostic. A person who has understood what has happened to him asks something different from this. The speakers have been inside the Fire long enough to know where they are and what it is, and their conclusion is that the problem is located in someone else.

There is also a note of futility in the verse that the chapter leaves unstated. Al-Aʿrāf records the leaders answering their followers with *you were no better than us* (7:39), which is the same refusal to accept a distinction. Both groups want someone else to be worse off, and neither can produce one, and the verse records the request without recording its outcome.

**The Verse in the Chapter's Sequence**

This verse closes the exchange that began with the arrival of the crowd. Three verses of dialogue have been given to the people of the Fire, and they end in a petition that goes nowhere, which is the shape the chapter has given to every argument it has reported from that side.

The placement also answers the mockery of verse 16.

---

## Verse 38:62

> The tyrants will ask ˹one another˺, “But why do we not see those we considered to be lowly

**"Those We Had Counted Among the Evil"**

The description of the missing people is the verse's centre, and it is a description of a judgement rather than of a fact. We had counted them among the evil — we placed them in that category, we assigned them to it, we treated the assignment as settled.

The exclusion was also public. The notables of Quraysh drove the poor believers away from their assemblies and asked whether God had favoured those particular people out of all of them (38:6; cf. 6:53). The verse records the same reasoning from the other end of time, and the reasoning has not changed while its object has disappeared.

**Who the Missing People Are**

The chapter does not name them, and the omission is meaningful. The missing are the poor believers of Makkah — Bilāl, ʿAmmār, Ṣuhayb, Khabbāb and others like them — the people the notables refused to sit with and asked the Prophet to drive away. Their names are absent from this verse as they were absent from the assemblies.

The chapter has already told the reader what happened to these people. They are among the reverent for whom the gates were opened and the provision does not run out (38:49–54), and they are among those the notables called evil. The verse therefore requires no explanation; it requires the reader to remember the description of the Garden.

**The Same Question in Al-Aʿrāf**

The Qur'an records the same astonishment elsewhere, and there it is turned into a question addressed to the astonished. Al-Aʿrāf reports the people of the Fire being asked whether these are the ones they swore God would never show mercy to (7:49), and the asking is itself the reversal, delivered by someone else.

The two treatments differ in a way worth noting. In this verse the question is asked by the people of the Fire and remains unanswered; in al-Aʿrāf it is asked of them and answered by the sight of the believers entering the Garden. The chapter here withholds the answer, and the withholding keeps the speakers inside their own incomprehension.

---

## Verse 38:63

> Were we wrong in mocking them ˹in the world˺? Or do our eyes ˹just˺ fail to see them ˹in the Fire˺?”

**"Did We Take Them in Mockery?"**

The first explanation revisits the behaviour itself. We took them in mockery — we laughed at them, we treated them as objects of derision. The question asks whether that treatment was misplaced.

The chapter has a particular interest in this word. Mockery and derision were the response the notables gave to the message from the beginning, and the Qur'an says elsewhere that those who mocked the believers will be mocked in return (83:29–34). The verse here shows the mockers questioning the practice for the first time, and the questioning arises from an absence rather than from a recognition.

**An Alternative Reading**

The first sentence of the verse can be read differently. Instead of a question — *did we take them in mockery?* — it can be read as a statement: *we took them in mockery*. The commentators record both readings, and the second removes the uncertainty from the first half of the verse.

---

## Verse 38:64

> This dispute between the residents of the Fire will certainly come to pass

**What They Dispute About**

The commentators describe the dispute as being about what led the people of the Fire to their end, and also as a dispute with God. The two objects of the dispute are not separate; the argument about causes and the argument with God are the same argument conducted in two directions.

The chapter has already shown the argument about causes in the exchange between the leaders and the followers.

Its denizens are described as calling out, asking to be returned, and being told that they were reminded and refused (23:99–108).

**Disputing With One Another**

The first object of the dispute is the other inhabitants. The chapter has shown this in detail: the leaders refusing welcome to the followers, the followers accusing the leaders, and both groups asking for someone else to be punished more. The disputing is continuous and productive of nothing.

The Qur'an describes the same condition in other terms elsewhere. Al-Shuʿarāʾ records the inhabitants saying that they were in manifest error when they equated others with the Lord of the worlds, and describes them being thrown into the Fire along with those they followed (26:96–99). The mutual recrimination is a fixed feature of the description.

The feature matters because it inverts what the people of the Fire expected. They spent their lives in a competition for standing, and the verse describes a place where the competition continues with the prizes removed. The dispute is the last form the competition takes, and it is the form in which it can never be won.

**Speaking Across the Barrier**

The commentators note a distinction that the verse's wording preserves. No dispute passes between Hell and the Garden, though speech does. The distinction is between argument and request, and the two are treated differently throughout the Qur'an's accounts.

The speaking is recorded in several places. Its inhabitants call out to the people of the Garden asking for water or for some of what God has provided (7:50), and the hypocrites ask the believers to wait so that they may take some of their light (57:13). Both are requests rather than arguments, and both are refused.

The refusal in al-Aʿrāf is stated as a prohibition: God has forbidden both to the disbelievers (7:50). The blessings of the Garden cannot be transferred, and the reason given is a prohibition rather than a shortage. The chapter has already described the provision of the Garden as inexhaustible (38:54), so the refusal is not about quantity.

**A Truth Stated Twice**

The verse's structure repeats its own assertion. The truth and the confirming particle both do the work of establishing certainty, and the doubling is the same device the chapter used when it called the destination of the transgressors the worst and then exclaimed over it (38:56).

The doubling has a specific function here. The chapter has been reporting speech from the people of the Fire, and reported speech can be taken as rhetorical. The verse removes the possibility by asserting the occurrence twice over, and the assertion closes the entire account of the two destinations that began at verse 49.

---

## Verse 38:65

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I am only a warner. And there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah—the One, the Supreme

**"I Am Only a Warner"**

The first half of the declaration limits the speaker's own role, and the limitation is stated with a restricting particle. Only — nothing more than a warner. The verse is telling the Prophet to say what he is not as well as what he is.

The same formulation appears repeatedly in the Qur'an, and the repetition indicates a persistent objection. The Prophet is described as nothing but a warner in Hūd (11:12), al-Raʿd (13:7), al-Naml (27:92), Sabaʾ (34:46), Fāṭir (35:23) and al-Nāziʿāt (79:45). Each occurrence answers a demand that he produce something other than a warning.

The commentators explain the limitation precisely: his function is to deliver the message, and he does not have knowledge of the unseen except what God gives him. The two halves of the verse are connected by that explanation. The warner does not claim knowledge, and the verses that follow will say so explicitly.

**What a Warner Does and Does Not Do**

A warner delivers information about a danger and leaves the response to the person warned. He does not compel, does not guarantee and does not bear responsibility for the outcome. The verse is defining the role in what it excludes, and the exclusions are the ones the notables of Makkah kept demanding that he violate.

They asked him to produce the punishment he warned about, to bring angels as witnesses, to move mountains and to split the earth (38:16; cf. 17:90–93). Every one of those demands requires the warner to be something other than a warner. The declaration answers all of them at once by refusing the role they were asking for.

The refusal is also a protection. A warner who could compel belief would not be delivering a message but imposing one, and the Qur'an states repeatedly that compulsion is not available to the Messenger (10:99; 88:21–22). The verse's limitation is therefore not a diminishment of the Prophet but a statement of the kind of thing revelation is.

**"There Is No God but God"**

The second half of the declaration is the tawḥīd, and its placement inside the warner's speech is the verse's structural claim. The warning and the declaration of oneness are given as one utterance, and the connection between them is that the warning is about what follows from rejecting the declaration.

A declaration that began with *God is one* would leave the other gods standing alongside; the verse removes them first.

The notables went out saying *carry on, and stand firm in devotion to your gods* (38:6), and their objection was that Muhammad had made the gods into one god.

**"The One"**

The Qur'an pairs this name with the one that follows in five other places, and the pairing is consistent: al-Wāḥid al-Qahhār, the One, the Paramount (12:39; 13:16; 14:48; 39:4; 40:16). In every instance the pairing occurs in a context where a claim to power or lordship is being displaced. Yūsuf uses it to ask whether many lords or the One, the Paramount is better; al-Raʿd uses it to conclude that God is the Creator of all things.

The consistency of the pairing indicates that the two names are not chosen independently. Oneness describes a fact about God; the name that follows describes what that fact means for anyone who contests it. The verse is moving from a statement to a consequence.

---

## Verse 38:66

> ˹He is the˺ Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between—the Almighty, Most Forgiving.”

**"Lord of the Heavens and the Earth"**

The title of lordship is given with its domain specified, and the specification is the largest one available in the Qur'an's vocabulary. Not a tribe, not a city, not a region — the heavens and the earth.

The Qur'an uses this formulation as an argument elsewhere, and the argument has a fixed shape. Al-Raʿd asks who the Lord of the heavens and the earth is, answers *God*, and then asks why lords have been taken besides Him who cannot benefit or protect themselves (13:16). The question is rhetorical because the answer was already accepted by the people being asked.

That is the situation in Makkah as well. The notables did not deny that God created the heavens and the earth; the Qur'an says they would affirm it if asked (29:61; 31:25). Their error was not in the premise but in the inference, and the verse is supplying the inference they refused to draw.

**Whatsoever Is Between Them, and the Name of Forgiving**

The addition of what lies between the two is not padding. It closes a gap that the two terms alone would leave, and the gap is where the idols were thought to operate.

The deities of the Arabian pantheon were not creators. They were intermediaries, and the Qur'an records the claim explicitly: the idolaters said they worshipped them only so that they might bring them nearer to God (39:3). An intermediary requires a domain, and the domain claimed for these beings was the space between heaven and earth.

The commentators note that the combination of the Mighty and the Forgiving refers to God's threat against the disbelievers and His promise to the believers. The two names are not a softening of the first three but a specification of how they apply: might toward those who persist, forgiveness toward those who turn.

The placement of forgiveness last is significant given what the chapter has just described. Sixteen verses have been devoted to the two destinations, and the Fire has been described without softening. The declaration ends on the name of forgiveness, and the ending is the chapter's offer to the men it has been describing.

**Two Names in Combination**

The pairing of the Mighty with the Forgiving occurs in three other places in the Qur'an (39:5; 40:42; 67:2), and in each case the combination carries the same double reference. Al-Zumar states that God created the heavens and the earth with truth and then names Himself the Mighty, the Forgiving — creation and forgiveness in the same breath.

The more frequent pairing in the Qur'an is the Mighty with the Merciful, which occurs dozens of times, most densely in al-Shuʿarāʾ. The choice of the Forgiving here rather than the Merciful is a small variation with a specific effect: forgiveness is directed at an offence already committed, and the chapter has just described people who have committed one.

The four names of the declaration therefore form a sequence rather than a list. One, Paramount, Lord of everything, Mighty, Forgiving. Each term follows from the one before, and the final term is the one addressed to the reader's own situation. The declaration closes with the possibility that the preceding verses have made urgent.

---

## Verse 38:67

> Say, “This ˹Quran˺ is momentous news

**A Great Tiding, and What It Refers To**

The first reading takes the word referentially: this thing you are hearing is a great news. The second takes it prospectively: the news is about the Day, and the Qur'an is the medium by which the news arrives. The third takes it really: what is new and momentous is the declaration of oneness given in the previous two verses.

**A Second Command to Speak**

The chapter gives the Prophet a second declaration to make, and the command is the same word as before. The first declaration concerned who God is; this one concerns what the message is. The two commands are separated by nothing, and the proximity indicates that the second follows from the first.

The imperative form also answers the accusation the notables made. They said the Qur'an was nothing but an invention, or poetry, or magic, or the speech of a man being taught (38:4–7; cf. 16:101; 25:4–5). The verse responds by having the Prophet describe the thing himself, in terms that place it above all of those categories.

**A Great Tiding, and What It Refers To**

The word used is *nabaʾ*, which means news of consequence — information about something that matters, as distinct from ordinary report. The Qur'an distinguishes it from mere talk by using it for events of weight, and the adjective attached here makes the weight explicit.

The commentators give three identifications, and the three are not competing but overlapping. The tiding is the Qur'an itself, or the Day of Resurrection, or the message of God's oneness issuing directly from Him.

All three readings are supported by the immediate context. The chapter has just declared God's oneness, has described the Day of Reckoning and its two outcomes, and is describing the revelation itself. The verse sits at the intersection of the three, and the commentators' plurality indicates that the word is doing all three jobs at once.

**The Same Word in Al-Nabaʾ**

The Qur'an opens an entire sūrah with this word. Al-Nabaʾ begins by asking what they are questioning one another about, and answers: the great tiding, about which they differ (78:1–3). The identification there is the Day of Resurrection, and the difference is stated as the reason for the question.

The chapter here also echoes al-Nabaʾ in another respect. Al-Nabaʾ goes on to describe the Fire and the Garden in detail (78:21–36), and this chapter has just done the same. The sequence of great tiding followed by description of the two destinations is shared by both sūrahs.

---

## Verse 38:68

> from which you ˹pagans˺ are turning away.”

**A Habit Rather Than a Decision, and What Turning Does Not Change**

The grammatical form of the verb indicates a continuing action rather than a single event. You are turning away — the turning is ongoing, repeated, habitual. The verse is not describing a moment of refusal but a settled posture.

The distinction is important to the chapter's account of its audience. The notables did not hear the message once and reject it; they heard it repeatedly and rejected it repeatedly, and they organised their opposition around the repetition. The chapter describes them going out and urging one another to patience in devotion to their gods (38:6), which is an organised response to a continuing message.

The verse's most severe implication is unstated. Turning away from a great news does not reduce the news. The greatness is a property of the thing, not of the response to it, and the verse's construction makes the two independent.

The chapter has already showed this independence in its account of the destroyed peoples. ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot and Pharaoh all turned away, and the turning did not prevent what followed. The roll-call in verses 12–14 is a list of turnings and their outcomes, and the outcomes are unaffected by the turnings.

**Turning in the Chapter's Vocabulary**

The verb also appears in the chapter's account of the prophets. David was told not to follow desire lest it lead him astray from the path of God (38:26), and the warning concerns the same movement — a turning aside from something one knows. The chapter treats turning as the basic act of rejection, and it uses the same word for the individual and the collective.

The Qur'an says elsewhere that no people are destroyed until a messenger is sent to them (17:15), and the chapter has been describing a people who have received the message in detail.

---

## Verse 38:69

> ˹And say,˺ “I had no knowledge of the highest assembly ˹in heaven˺ when they differed ˹concerning Adam˺

**The Highest Assembly**

The phrase means an assembly in the highest region, and the commentators identify it consistently as the assembly of angels. The term is used elsewhere in the Qur'an, and the identification is not disputed in the tradition.

The assembly is described as highest in a way that carries both spatial and rank meaning. It is the gathering of beings who are nearest to God, and the Qur'an describes the angels as ranged in ranks and as those who do not precede Him in speech (21:26–27; 37:165). The assembly is therefore a place of ordered deliberation rather than of debate in the ordinary sense.

**The Dispute They Disputed**

Most commentators identify the dispute as the one about the creation of Adam, and the Qur'an records that discussion in al-Baqarah. The angels ask whether God will place in the earth someone who will spread corruption and shed blood, while they glorify His praise and proclaim His holiness; God answers that He knows what they do not know (2:30).

Verses 71 to 85 will narrate the creation of Adam and Iblīs's refusal, and this verse establishes that the narrator had no independent access to the story.

**The Report About the Expiations**

An alternative identification of the dispute is recorded in a ḥadīth narrated through several chains. The Prophet ﷺ reported that his Lord came to him in the night in the most beautiful form and asked him what the highest assembly was disputing about; he said he did not know, and the question was repeated. Then his Lord placed His palm between his shoulders and he felt its coolness between his breasts, and what is in the heavens and the earth was disclosed to him. When the question was put again he answered: about the expiations. Asked what the expiations are, he said: going on foot to the congregations, sitting in the mosques after the prayers, and performing ablution completely in difficult circumstances. Whoever does that lives well and dies well, and his offences are as on the day his mother bore him (Sunan al-Tirmidhī 3235).

The report is graded ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ by al-Tirmidhī and is transmitted in the Musnad of Aḥmad. Some versions add that the Prophet was then invited to ask for anything, and he asked for the performance of good deeds, the avoidance of bad deeds, love of the poor, forgiveness and mercy, and for the love of God and of those who love Him.

Both identifications can stand together. The dispute about the creation of Adam is the event the verse refers to historically; the report describes a different occasion on which the same phrase was used. The commentators record both, and the verse's wording is general enough to accommodate either.

**The Argument From Ignorance**

The form of the argument is characteristic of the Qur'an's treatment of the prophetic claim. Rather than asserting the source, it establishes the impossibility of the alternative. The Prophet was unlettered, had not travelled to the centres of learning, and had not studied scripture (29:48; 7:157–158). The verse adds a specific instance to that general claim.

The notables had consulted the Jewish scholars of Madīnah for questions to test the Prophet with (cf. 18:9–26), which means they had access to people who had scriptural knowledge.

---

## Verse 38:70

> What is revealed to me is that I am only sent with a clear warning.”

**A Clear Warner, and the Conclusion of the Declaration**

The verse completes the speech the Prophet was commanded to deliver, and the completion answers the confession that preceded it. He did not know the story of the highest assembly; what is revealed to him is that he is a clear warner. The two statements together form the whole of his claim.

The closing quotation mark ends the speech begun at verse 67, and the whole passage — four verses of commanded speech — is the chapter's formal statement of the Prophet's position. Everything after it is narrative.

The adjective attached to the warner is *mubīn*, which means something that makes its meaning plain, that is evident in itself and makes other things evident. The Qur'an uses it of the Book, of the signs, and of the messengers.

Applied to a warner, the adjective has a specific force.

**"Nothing Is Revealed to Me Except"**

The construction is restrictive, and the restriction is the verse's most striking feature. The Prophet is commanded to say that the revelation he receives amounts to this one thing: that he is a warner. Not that he knows the unseen, not that he holds the treasuries, not that he can produce what his opponents demand.

The restriction answers the demands the chapter recorded earlier. The notables asked for the punishment to be hastened, for the heavens to be opened in pieces, for the angels to be brought before them (38:16; cf. 17:90–93). Each demand assumes a revelation containing powers and prerogatives. The verse says the revelation contains a commission.

The same restriction is stated elsewhere in the Qur'an with identical wording, and the repetition indicates that the demand was persistent. The Prophet is told to say that he does not have the treasuries of God, does not know the unseen, and is nothing but a warner (6:50; 46:9; 79:45). The verse here is one instance of a standing answer.

**What Clarity Requires of the Hearer**

A clear warning has a defined effect on the person who receives it. Once the warning has been made plain, the hearer's subsequent state is his own, and the Qur'an states the principle repeatedly: whoever turns away bears the consequence of his turning (cf. 6:104; 10:108; 39:41).

**The Verse as a Summary of the Prophetic Claim**

Taken together with verse 65, this verse gives the chapter's complete account of what the Prophet is. He is a warner and nothing more; he proclaims that there is no god but God, the One, the Paramount; he does not know the hidden matters; he has been sent with a clear warning.

The account is remarkable for what it omits. There is no claim to power, to wealth, to miraculous ability on demand, or to knowledge of the future. The chapter has already noted that the notables found it strange that a man with no standing should bring such a message (38:4–8), and the declaration answers them by refusing every category they were measuring him against.

The refusal is also a test. A claimant who demanded belief on the basis of power would be believed by the powerful and rejected by the weak. A claimant who demands belief on the basis of a clear warning is judged by the clarity alone, and the clarity is available to everyone equally.

---

## Verse 38:71

> ˹Remember, O Prophet˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to create a human being from clay

The Qur'an uses it consistently when the physical constitution of the human being is in view, and its use here prepares the argument Iblīs will make in verse 76.

**The Last Account of the Creation**

The chapter now begins its final narrative, and the narrative is the creation of Adam and the refusal of Iblīs. This is the last such account in the textual order of the Qur'an, and the commentators note that it is most likely the first in the order of revelation. The other accounts appear in al-Baqarah (2:30–39), al-Aʿrāf (7:11–25), al-Isrāʾ (17:61–65) and Ṭā-Hā (20:115–24), with a closely parallel version in al-Ḥijr (15:28–43).

The placement at the end of the sūrah is significant. The chapter has moved from the Qur'an to the pagans' objection, through the stories of David, Solomon, Job and the patriarchs, to the two destinations, and now to the origin of the enmity that the whole sūrah has been describing. The narrative is the root of the account, and the chapter saves it for last.

The timing is also relevant to the argument. A story about the first act of arrogance is placed immediately after an account of people whose arrogance has reached its consequence, and the placement makes the connection without stating it.

**"A Human Being"**

The choice of word matters for what follows. Iblīs's objection will be about material, and the verse has already named the material in the same sentence that names the being. The declaration contains the ground of the objection, and the objection will be shown to rest on something God said openly.

The word also connects this account to the Qur'an's treatment of the messengers. The pagans objected that Muhammad was a man like themselves (38:4; cf. 17:94; 23:33), and the word *bashar* is the one used in those objections. The verse places the objection at the beginning of human history, spoken by a different objector about a different man.

**From Clay, in an Abbreviated Declaration**

The material is named without qualification in this verse, and the other accounts supply the detail. Al-Ḥijr describes it as sounding clay moulded from black mud (15:28), and al-Aʿrāf and al-Isrāʾ record Iblīs repeating the description dismissively (7:12; 17:61).

The naming is not incidental. God states the material Himself, before any objector can raise it, and the stating removes the possibility of concealment being part of the objection. Iblīs will not be exposing a hidden weakness; he will be repeating a fact that was announced.

The material also carries a meaning the commentators draw out. Clay is low, common and formed by another's hand. A being made from it has no claim to independence, and the verse's naming of the material is the first element of the chapter's answer to arrogance.

The commentators note that this verse is an abbreviated version of what God says in al-Ḥijr, where the description of the clay is fuller. The abbreviation is characteristic of the chapter's method: it gives the essential fact and leaves the detail to the parallel accounts.

The same abbreviation applies to the surrounding narrative. The exchange with the angels recorded in al-Baqarah is not given here; the account of Adam and Eve in the Garden, which follows the creation in al-Baqarah and al-Aʿrāf, is omitted completely. The chapter takes only the refusal of Iblīs and its consequences.

---

## Verse 38:72

> So when I have fashioned him and had a spirit of My Own ˹creation˺ breathed into him, fall down in prostration to him.”

**Two Acts of Creation**

The verse describes the making of the human being in two stages, and the two stages are what the rest of the narrative depends on. First the fashioning of the body from the clay named in the previous verse; then the breathing into it of a spirit. The human being is complete only when both have occurred.

The sequence is stated elsewhere in the Qur'an in the third person. Al-Sajdah describes God fashioning the human being and breathing into him of His spirit, and then giving hearing, sight and intellect (32:9). The two accounts agree on the order and on the fact that the fashioning alone does not complete the being.

Al-Rāzī draws the conclusion explicitly: the creation of the human being is completed only through the formation of the body and the breathing of the spirit together, which means human beings have both a body and a soul. The verse is therefore a statement about human constitution as well as a narrative step.

**Rūzbihān al-Baqlī's Reading**

The Sufi commentator Rūzbihān al-Baqlī glosses the passage in a way that brings out its structure. He takes the clay to stand for incapacity and weakness, and the breathing of the spirit to mean being clothed in the lights of divine greatness and magnificence.

His gloss runs through the verse in order. God creates the human being from a material that means incapacity, and then perfects him, distinguishes him with the divine attributes, illuminates him with the light of the divine essence, and gives him life through the life and spirit that proceed from the disclosure of majesty and beauty. Only at that point does the command to prostrate arrive.

The reading is significant because it identifies the two poles the rest of the narrative will turn on. The human being is made of weakness and is honoured by an act of God. Iblīs's error will be to see only the first pole, and to treat what he sees as the whole of the case.

**The Command to Prostrate, and What the Verse Establishes**

The verse ends with a command addressed to the angels, and the command is unusual in its object. They are ordered to prostrate before a created being, and the prostration is directed at Adam rather than at God.

The commentators are careful about what the prostration means. It is an act of honour toward Adam and of obedience toward God, not an act of worship directed at the human being. The command comes from God, which makes the prostration an act of submission to the Commander however it is performed.

The Qur'an repeats the command in several places (2:34; 7:11; 15:29; 17:61; 18:50; 20:116), and the repetition indicates that the command is the pivot of the whole narrative. Everything that follows is a response to it, and the response divides the angels from Iblīs.

The verse establishes three facts that the following verses will use. The human being is made of clay. He is honoured by a spirit attributed to God. He is the object of a divine command to prostrate.

Iblīs will accept the first fact, dispute the second by ignoring it, and refuse the third. The verse has already given the reader everything needed to evaluate the refusal, and it gives it before the refusal occurs.

---

## Verse 38:73

> So the angels prostrated all together—

**An Immediate Obedience, All of Them Together**

The verse records the response to the command in a single clause, and the clause is notable for what it lacks. There is no hesitation, no question, no request for clarification and no delay. The command is given and the prostration follows.

The immediacy is the verse's subject. The angels are not described as considering the command, weighing it against their own standing, or asking why a being of clay should be honoured. They prostrate, and the verse moves on.

The brevity is also a contrast. The account of the angels' obedience takes one verse; the account of Iblīs's refusal and its consequences takes twelve. The disproportion is the chapter's way of saying where the interest lies, and where it does not.

The phrase emphasises unanimity, and the emphasis prepares the exception in the next verse. Every one of them prostrated, and the stating of the unanimity is what makes the single refusal visible.

The Qur'an uses the same emphasis elsewhere. Al-Baqarah says they all prostrated except Iblīs, who refused and acted arrogantly (2:34), and al-Aʿrāf and al-Isrāʾ repeat the formula (7:11; 17:61). The unanimity is stated in every account, and in every account it exists to be broken by one.

The word for together also carries a sense of simultaneity. They did not prostrate in sequence or by rank; they prostrated as one body. The unity of the act is part of what the refusal disrupts.

**The Verse's Function in the Narrative**

The verse exists to establish a baseline. Before the narrative can describe a refusal, it must describe the obedience that the refusal departs from. The unanimity is the norm, and the exception is measured against it.

The chapter uses the same method elsewhere. It describes the patriarchs as men of might and vision before describing anyone's failure (38:45), and it describes the righteous entering the Garden before describing the transgressors entering the Fire (38:49–58). The pattern is to establish what is right before showing what departs from it.

The method has a rhetorical effect. A reader who is shown the norm first evaluates the exception against it rather than on its own terms. Iblīs's refusal will be presented with reasons, and the verse ensures that the reasons are heard after the obedience has been established.

**The Silence of the Angels**

Nothing is recorded as said by the angels in this account. Al-Baqarah records their question about the vicegerent (2:30), and the question is a genuine one; here they are silent, and the silence is obedience.

**A Verse With No Argument in It**

The verse contains no reasoning, no explanation and no justification, and the absence is the point. The angels were given no reason for the command, and they asked for none. The command was enough.

The chapter has been describing an audience that demanded reasons for everything and was satisfied by none. They asked for angels to be sent down, for the Book to be sent in pieces, for the punishment to be brought forward (38:16; cf. 17:90–93). Each demand was a request for a reason, and each request was followed by another.

The angels, who are described elsewhere as not disobeying God in what He commands them and doing as they are commanded (66:6), perform an act of honour toward a being made of clay.

## Verse 38:74

> but not Iblîs, who acted arrogantly, becoming unfaithful

**"He Acted Arrogantly"**

The verb is *istakbara*, from the root of greatness, and it means taking oneself to be great. The form indicates a deliberate act of self-elevation rather than an involuntary feeling. Iblīs did not feel superior; he made himself superior, in his own assessment.

**"And Was Among the Disbelievers"**

The wording is important. Iblīs was not a disbeliever before the command; the refusal made him one. The verse describes disbelief as an outcome of arrogance rather than as an independent state, and the ordering is the chapter's diagnosis of unbelief in general.

The removal matters for the chapter's audience in a practical way. Some of the Quraysh sought refuge with jinn and treated them as powers with independent influence (72:6). The verse demotes the greatest of them to the rank of a disobedient creature, and the demotion removes the ground of that practice.

**What Iblīs Was Before**

The verse does not say what Iblīs was before the refusal, and the omission is informative. The Qur'an indicates elsewhere that he was among the jinn (18:50), and the commentators discuss at length how a jinn came to be in the company of the angels.

The chapter does not narrate the prior standing, and the omission keeps the focus on the act. What Iblīs was is less relevant to the sūrah's purpose than what he did, and the verse gives only what is needed: the refusal, its cause and its consequence.

---

## Verse 38:75

> Allah asked, “O Iblîs! What prevented you from prostrating to what I created with My Own Hands? Did you ˹just˺ become proud? Or have you always been arrogant?”

**A Question Put to the Refuser**

The verse records God addressing Iblīs directly, and the address is a question. The question is not asked for information — nothing about the situation is hidden — and its function is to require an answer. Iblīs will be made to state his reasoning.

The same question appears in al-Aʿrāf in shorter form: what prevented you from prostrating when I commanded you? (7:12). The version here adds the reference to the two hands and the two further questions, and the additions are where the chapter's particular interest lies.

**"What I Created with My Two Hands"**

The phrase is the verse's most discussed element. God describes the creation of the human being as an act performed with His two hands, and the description is unique to this account.

The commentators read it symbolically, and their reasoning is explicit: creation in the Qur'an requires no instrument at all, since God's command when He wills a thing is simply to say *be*, and it is (36:82; cf. 2:117; 16:40). A description of hands must therefore signify something other than a manner of making.

Several explanations are given. Some take the phrase as a mark of the human being's greatness. Others read the two as pointing to paired divine qualities — majesty with beauty, or dominance with gentleness — and understand the human being as the place where such qualities are displayed together. Others again take the pair to cover the whole range of names that fall under dominance and under love.

**Did You Become Proud? The Question as an Offer**

The first of the two questions asks whether the arrogance is new. The verb form indicates a state entered into, and the question asks whether Iblīs has now taken on something he did not have.

The commentators and the narrative structure both indicate that the questions are not only interrogative. A being asked whether he has become proud is being given the opportunity to say so, and the saying would be an admission.

Al-Aʿrāf records what follows such an admission. There God says to Iblīs: it is not for you to be arrogant here, so go out, you are among the humbled (7:13). The expulsion follows the identification of the arrogance, and the identification is what the questions in this verse are seeking.

The chapter's version delays the expulsion by one verse, and the delay allows Iblīs to state his reasoning in full. The reasoning is what the chapter wants on the record, because it is the reasoning its own audience is using.

---

## Verse 38:76

> He replied, “I am better than he is: You created me from fire and him from clay.”

**"I Am Better Than He Is"**

The claim is stated absolutely and without qualification. Not better in some respect, not better for some purpose — better. The superlative is asserted as a fact about the two beings rather than as an opinion about them.

The assertion precedes the evidence, and the order is characteristic of the reasoning the chapter has been examining. A conclusion is stated, and a reason is produced for it. The reason does not generate the conclusion; it is selected to support it.

The commentators note that the same explicit argument appears in al-Aʿrāf, where Iblīs says *I am better than him* in identical terms (7:12). In al-Ḥijr the argument is implicit, expressed through a dismissal of Adam's material (15:33), and in al-Isrāʾ it takes the form of a question: shall I prostrate to one whom You created from clay? (17:61). All four versions contain the same inference.

**And Him from Clay: What the Argument Leaves Out**

The second premise is also true, and God Himself stated it in the previous verse. Iblīs is repeating an announcement rather than disclosing anything, and the repetition is the whole of his contribution.

The notables said of the Prophet that he was a man like themselves, eating food and walking in the markets (25:7; 38:4). Every element of that statement was accurate. The error was not in the description but in what was inferred from it.

The verse therefore isolates the mechanism of the error. True premises, a hidden standard, and a conclusion that does not follow. The chapter will spend its remaining verses showing what follows from the conclusion instead.

The omission is the verse's sharpest feature. Iblīs says nothing about the spirit breathed into Adam, nothing about the command, and nothing about the honour God conferred by making the prostration obligatory.

The previous verse had supplied all three. God announced the material, described the fashioning and the breathing of the spirit, and issued the command to the assembly.

**The Verse and the Chapter's Opponents**

The correspondence between this argument and the argument of the notables is close enough that the chapter does not need to state it. They asked whether God had favoured the poor believers out of all people (38:6; cf. 6:53), which is the same inference from standing to worth. They asked why the revelation did not come to a great man of the two cities (43:31), which is the same inference applied to the choice of a messenger.

What the verse adds is the outcome. The argument is answered in the next verse not by rebuttal but by expulsion, and the expulsion is the chapter's comment on the value of the reasoning.

The comment is severe precisely because it is silent. An argument that is refuted has been taken seriously; an argument that is answered by removal has been judged not to require a reply. The chapter has been treating its opponents' claims the same way from the beginning, answering them with the stories of the prophets rather than with counter-claims.

God did create him from fire, and the Qur'an states it elsewhere: the jinn was created from a smokeless flame (55:15).

---

## Verse 38:77

> Allah commanded, “Then get out of Paradise, for you are truly cursed

**"Get Out of It"**

The command is expulsion from a place, and the place is Paradise. The commentators note that the same command is given in al-Aʿrāf with an additional phrase: get out of it, disgraced and banished (7:18). Al-Ḥijr gives the command in identical words to this verse (15:34).

The expulsion has a specific significance given what Iblīs claimed. He asserted that he was better, and the response removes him from the place where the comparison was being made. The assembly before which he elevated himself is the assembly he is expelled from, and the expulsion is witnessed by the same beings.

The command also reverses the direction of the prostration. Iblīs was ordered to lower himself before Adam; he is now ordered to leave the place where Adam is. The refusal to descend is answered by a removal, and the removal is the same movement without the choice.

**The Verse's Place in the Parallel Accounts**

The commentators note that these verses are identical to 15:34–35, and the identity is exact in both wording and order. The two accounts give the expulsion and the curse in the same terms, which indicates that the chapter is following a fixed formulation.

The parallel accounts differ elsewhere. Al-Aʿrāf adds the phrase about disgrace and banishment (7:18), and al-Isrāʾ records a longer exchange in which Iblīs asks about the one honoured above him (17:62). This chapter's version is the shortest, and the shortness keeps the focus on the expulsion itself.

The consistency across accounts has a function of its own. A story told four times in the same words at its critical point is a story the Qur'an wants fixed in the memory, and the fixing is what allows the chapter to place it at the end without explanation.

**The Refusal to Argue, and What Is Lost and Remains**

The chapter has been making the same point throughout. The notables' claims about rank, wealth and standing were never answered on their own terms. The chapter answered them with the stories of the prophets, with the account of the two destinations, and with the declaration of God's oneness. The claims were bypassed rather than refuted.

The verse takes away a place and confers a curse, and it leaves Iblīs's existence intact. He is not annihilated, and the next verses show him speaking and asking. The loss is of access, not of being.

The distinction is important to the chapter's account of the world its audience inhabits. A being who was destroyed at the moment of refusal would leave no temptation behind him; a being who is expelled and cursed but alive remains an actor, and the Qur'an describes him as an active one throughout.

**The Public Character of the Expulsion**

The Qur'an's accounts elsewhere give the same detail. Al-Aʿrāf adds that he is to go out disgraced and banished (7:18), and the two words describe a condition that is visible to others. The disgrace is not private, and the banishment is not quiet.

---

## Verse 38:78

> And surely upon you is My condemnation until the Day of Judgment.”

**"My Condemation"**

The condemnation is attributed to God in the first person, and the attribution is emphatic. It is not a curse pronounced by the angels, not a natural consequence, not a condition that arose from the act alone. It is God's, and it rests upon him.

The preposition used indicates something lying upon a person, a weight that is carried. The condemnation is described as a burden borne rather than as a penalty applied from outside, and the description matches the Qur'an's treatment of burdens elsewhere (29:13; 35:18).

The first-person attribution also answers Iblīs's claim. He asserted a comparison between two created beings; the verse answers with a statement from the Creator about one of them. The comparison is not disputed — it is replaced by a relation of a completely different kind.

**A Term That Cannot Be Shortened, in the Parallel Accounts**

Al-Ḥijr gives the same statement in identical words (15:35), and the commentators note the identity explicitly. Al-Aʿrāf's version places the condemnation within a longer exchange but retains the same substance (7:18).

The consistency indicates a fixed element of the narrative. Across four accounts the expulsion, the curse and its duration are given in the same terms, and the variation occurs in the surrounding material rather than in the sentence itself.

The verse fixes the term and fixes it in a way that leaves no room for negotiation. The condemnation lasts until the Day of Judgment, and the fixing is what makes the following request necessary.

The necessity is instructive. Iblīs does not ask for the condemnation to be lifted or for its content to be changed. He asks only about time, and the asking shows that he has accepted everything the verse asserted. The concession is complete and is made without being stated.

The chapter makes the same point about its opponents in a different register. They are told repeatedly that the term is set and cannot be advanced or delayed (38:16; cf. 14:44; 16:61). The verse shows the first opponent discovering the same fact.

**What the Condemnation Does Not Remove**

The distinction matters for the chapter's account of the world. The Qur'an describes Iblīs as active among human beings — making things seem fair, whispering, promising falsely (6:43; 16:63; 14:22; 4:120). The verse explains how that activity is possible: the condemnation has a term, and the term has not arrived.

The activity is also limited, and the limit is given in the verses that follow. Iblīs will be granted respite but not authority, and the distinction between the two is the chapter's account of how temptation works.

**The Condemnation and the Chapter's Opponents**

The verse's term connects Iblīs's fate to the fate the chapter has already described. The transgressors were promised the worst destination on the Day of Reckoning (38:53, 55), and the condemnation upon Iblīs runs to the same day.

The convergence is the chapter's way of joining the narrative to the teaching about the Last Day. The story of Adam is not a separate account; it is the origin of the enmity that the two destinations resolve. The verse supplies the link by giving both the same terminus.

---

## Verse 38:79

> Satan appealed, “My Lord! Then delay my end until the Day of their resurrection.”

**Delay My End, and What the Request Sets Up**

The word used means being given respite, a postponement of a term. Iblīs is asking for time, and the time he asks for is not indefinite — it is bounded by the day he has just heard named.

The request concedes the condemnation. A being who disputed the curse would ask for its removal; Iblīs asks only for the interval before it takes full effect. The concession is complete, and it is made in the act of asking.

The pagans of Makkah are inside the period Iblīs asked for, and their opposition to the message is part of what the interval contains.

The Qur'an is careful throughout to state that Iblīs has no authority over God's servants (17:65), and the chapter will make the same point in the verses that follow.

**"Until the Day They Are Resurrected"**

The term requested is the day of universal resurrection, and the commentators connect it to the same event named in al-Aʿrāf (7:14) and al-Ḥijr (15:36–38). The three accounts give the request in nearly identical words, and al-Ḥijr's version of the answer is repeated verbatim from this chapter.

The choice of the resurrection as the terminus is significant. Iblīs does not ask for a fixed number of years or for an indefinite reprieve; he asks for the whole period that ends with the resurrection. The request covers the entire duration of human history.

The coverage is what makes the chapter's account of temptation possible. Human beings live inside the interval Iblīs requested, and the interval is bounded at both ends — by the expulsion at one end and by the resurrection at the other.

**The Verse as a Recognition of God's Power**

The request contains an implicit acknowledgement that is easy to miss. Iblīs asks God to delay his end, which means he accepts that God controls it. The being who claimed to be better than Adam accepts that his own existence is not in his hands.

The contradiction is also visible in the chapter's opponents. The notables claimed independence and standing, and the Qur'an answers by asking who provides for them and who controls life and death (10:31; 29:61–63). The answers they would give contradict the claims they make, and the verse is the archetype of the contradiction.

**The Request and the Requests of the Pagans**

The verse has a close parallel in the behaviour the chapter described at its opening. The pagans asked the Prophet to hasten the punishment and to produce signs on demand (38:16). Iblīs asks God for time. Both are requests made to an authority the requester is defying.

The parallel extends to the form. Each request assumes that the one being defied controls what is being asked for, and each request concedes the control it ignores in the defiance. Iblīs asks for delay from the one whose command he refused; the pagans ask for a sign from the messenger whose message they reject.

## Verse 38:80

> Allah said, “You will be delayed

**A Sentence Left Incomplete**

The verse records the answer to Iblīs's request, and the answer is given in a form that requires the next verse to complete it. You will be delayed — and the sentence stops. The term of the delay is supplied in verse 81, and the two verses form a single statement.

The splitting is not accidental. The commentators note that verses 80 and 81 are repeated verbatim in al-Ḥijr (15:37–38), which means the division belongs to the Qur'an's own arrangement rather than to this chapter's composition. The same break occurs in both accounts.

The first fact of the verse is that the request is answered affirmatively. Iblīs asked for respite and receives it, and the receiving is stated without qualification in this verse.

**Why the Grant Is Given**

The Qur'an does not give a reason for the granting, and the absence of a reason has been read in several ways. The commentators note that from one perspective, while Iblīs misleads people, he is in fact bringing about the end God has decreed for those destined for the Fire.

That reading is supported by the wider account. God says to Iblīs in al-Isrāʾ: incite whoever you can of them with your voice, mobilise against them your cavalry and infantry, manipulate them in their wealth and children, and make them promises (17:64). The commands are permissive in form and describe an activity that serves a purpose Iblīs does not control.

The explanation does not reduce responsibility, and the chapter is careful about this. Iblīs will state in the next verses that he has no power over God's sincere servants, and the Qur'an says elsewhere that he has no authority over the faithful (17:65). The respite creates an opportunity; it does not create a compulsion.

The distinction is the chapter's account of temptation. Something is presented, and a choice is made. The presentation is permitted; the choice belongs to the person. The verse supplies the first half of that account.

The Qur'an's parallel formulation makes the restriction explicit. Al-Ḥijr gives the same two verses in identical words (15:37–38), and the identity indicates that the grant and its limit were transmitted together as one unit. The commentators note the repetition specifically.

## Verse 38:81

> until the appointed Day.”

**A Day Known to God Alone**

The commentators connect the phrase to the description given in the parallel accounts, and they note that the day is known — fixed, recorded and certain — while remaining unknown to the one whose term it is. The two features are held together in a single word.

The combination is what gives the verse its weight. A term that is fixed but undisclosed cannot be planned around. Iblīs knows that his respite ends, and he does not know when the ending arrives relative to any moment he is living through.

The same structure governs the human situation the chapter has been describing. The Qur'an states repeatedly that the Hour is fixed and that its knowledge rests with God alone (7:187; 33:63), and the verse places Iblīs inside that condition rather than outside it.

**The Same Words in Al-Ḥijr**

The commentators note that verses 80 and 81 are repeated word for word in al-Ḥijr (15:37–38). The identity is exact, including the division between the two verses, which means the arrangement belongs to the Qur'an's own transmission rather than to this chapter's composition.

The repetition serves a function. The story of Adam is told in several places with variation in detail, and the variation allows each account to emphasise what its sūrah requires. The elements that do not vary are the ones the Qur'an wants fixed, and the grant with its term is among them.

Al-Aʿrāf gives the same grant in different words and adds the specification that the respite runs to the day of resurrection (7:14–15). The three accounts agree on the substance and differ in formulation, which is the normal pattern of Qur'anic repetition.

The pattern is worth naming because it answers an objection sometimes raised against repetition in the Qur'an. Where the wording is identical, the matter is being fixed; where it varies, the matter is being turned to face a different context. The verse belongs to the first group, and the fixity is why it can be this short.

**An Interval With Two Ends**

The verse supplies the second end of a period whose first end was given in the narrative of the expulsion. Between the command to leave and the appointed day lies the whole of human history, and the verse marks its far boundary.

The Qur'an uses the same structure for the human lifespan. Every soul has a term that cannot be advanced or delayed by a single hour (10:49; 16:61), and the verse applies the structure to the adversary as well. He is not outside the order he is trying to subvert.

The inclusion is the verse's quiet rebuke. Iblīs believed his standing set him apart from the arrangement governing other creatures; the verse places him inside it without comment. A being with a term is a creature, and a creature with a term has no ground on which to claim superiority over another.

## Verse 38:82

> Satan said, “By Your Glory! I will certainly mislead them all

**The Oath and the Method**

The verse's oath is followed in the parallel accounts by a description of the method. Al-Ḥijr has Iblīs saying he will make things seem fair to human beings on earth and mislead them all together (15:39), and the two elements — beautification and misdirection — are the Qur'an's standard account of temptation.

**An Oath Sworn by God's Might**

The verse records Iblīs's response to the grant, and the response begins with an oath. He swears by God's might — the same attribute he was condemned under, and the same one the chapter named in its declaration (38:66).

The Qur'an's parallel account records the oath in slightly different terms. Al-Ḥijr has Iblīs saying *my Lord, for allowing me to stray I will surely tempt them on earth* (15:39), which frames the same intention as a response to the respite. This chapter's version leads with the oath, and the leading emphasises the appeal to divine might.

The emphasis suits this sūrah's argument. Its opponents measured power by wealth and tribal strength, and the chapter answers by locating might in God alone (38:66). The verse has the adversary appeal to that same might, which places him on the same side of the question as the chapter and against his own claim.

**I Will Mislead Them: Why the Intention Is Recorded**

The verb means causing someone to go astray, to lose the way, to err. It is not the word for compelling or forcing, and the distinction is the verse's most important feature.

The distinction matters for the chapter's account of responsibility. A being who compels removes the choice; a being who misleads leaves the choice intact and works on it. The verse describes the second, and the description is what makes the following verses coherent.

The Qur'an states the point explicitly elsewhere. Iblīs will say on the Day of Judgment that he had no authority over people, that he only called them and they responded (14:22). The verse here records the intention in the same terms the later confession will use.

The verse records an intention rather than an act, and the recording has a purpose.

**The Oath as a Strategy**

The verse describes an intention formed in response to a grant, and the response is immediate. Iblīs does not use the respite to reconsider; he uses it to plan, and the plan is stated in the same breath as the receiving of it.

The commentators note that the Qur'an describes this activity in several ways. Iblīs makes things seem fair to human beings (6:43; 16:63; 27:24; 29:38), whispers stealthily (114:4), deludes (31:33; 35:5; 57:14), commands indecency (2:268; 24:21) and makes false promises (4:120; 14:22). The verse is the origin of all those descriptions.

The listing is worth setting beside the oath. The oath is a single sentence; the activity it announces is described in a dozen ways across the Qur'an. The gap between the brevity of the intention and the variety of its execution is the chapter's account of how temptation actually works.

**The Oath and the Method**

The chapter has already shown the technique at work. The notables described devotion to their gods as loyalty to their fathers and their community (38:6–7), which is a fair-seeming presentation of a refusal. The verse names the mechanism that produced it.

## Verse 38:83

> except Your chosen servants among them.”

**The Same Exception in Al-Ḥijr, and What the Verse Offers**

Al-Aʿrāf frames the exception differently, with God stating that Iblīs will have no authority except over those who follow him from among the perverse (7:155 in a related formulation; cf. 15:42). The formulations agree on the group excluded and differ on who states the exclusion.

**An Exception Made by the Adversary**

The verse records Iblīs narrowing his own claim, and the narrowing comes from him rather than from God. He has just sworn to mislead them all, and in the next breath he excludes a group.

The exception is the verse's central fact. A claimant who knows the limits of his power states them, and the stating is a form of knowledge. Iblīs knows that there are people he cannot mislead, and he knows it before he has tried them.

The Qur'an confirms the knowledge elsewhere. God says to Iblīs in al-Isrāʾ: you will truly have no authority over My faithful servants, and enough is your Lord as a guardian (17:65). The verse here has the adversary say what the other account has God say, and the agreement between the two is the point.

**"Your Sincere Servants"**

The phrase uses a form that means people made sincere, or people who are sincere, and the two readings carry different implications. On the first, the sincerity is conferred; on the second, it is achieved. The commentators note both, and the difference concerns where the protection comes from.

The reading that emphasises conferral locates the protection in God's act. The reading that emphasises achievement locates it in the servant's state.

The verse attaches the exception to God — *Your* sincere servants — and the possessive makes the group His rather than theirs.

Its account of David showed a man of the highest standing falling and returning (38:24), which means the relation can be damaged and repaired.

**The Same Exception in Al-Ḥijr, and What the Verse Offers**

The parallel account gives the same exception in the same position, immediately after the oath to mislead (15:40). The wording differs slightly but the substance is identical, and the commentators note the correspondence.

The agreement across accounts has a function. The limit on the adversary is not a local feature of one sūrah but a fixed element of the Qur'an's presentation, and the verse here states it in the form most difficult to dispute — the adversary's own words.

**The Exception Stated by the Wrong Party**

The most striking feature of the verse is who states the limit. God could have imposed the restriction from above, as He does in al-Isrāʾ (17:65); here the adversary states it himself, in the same sentence in which he announces his intention.

The self-statement makes the limit undeniable. A restriction declared by the one who would remove it cannot be treated as an optimistic interpretation offered by the believers. It is the adversary's own assessment of his reach, given before he has begun.

## Verse 38:84

> Allah concluded, “The truth is—and I ˹only˺ say the truth—

**Which Reading Is Stronger**

The forward-looking reading has the better claim, and the reason is structural. The verse is followed by a colon in every reading, and the content that follows is a statement about filling Hell. A preface that guarantees the certainty of what comes next fits that position; a free-standing declaration about God's nature would more naturally stand alone.

The declaration readings are not excluded, and they carry support from the Qur'an's usage elsewhere. God is described as the Truth in al-Ḥajj (22:6) and in al-Muʾminūn (23:114), and the description is established. The verse could be making that claim in compressed form.

The two options are not in real competition. A statement that God is the Truth and that He speaks the truth is also a guarantee of what follows, and the compressed form allows both functions at once. The commentators preserve the plurality because the wording supports it.

**The Ambiguity Retained, and the Verse Before the Threat**

The effect is to slow the reading. A statement whose sense must be worked through cannot be passed over, and the verse is placed immediately before the most severe statement in the narrative. The slowing is deliberate.

The verse's position is what gives it its force.

The two attributes are the ones the chapter named in its declaration (38:66), and the narrative closes on the same pair.

**Truth as the Chapter's Theme**

The word connects this verse to the chapter's earlier statements. The message was described as a great news (38:67), and the disputing of the people of the Fire was confirmed as true (38:64). The verse is the third use of the same idea, and the three uses mark the chapter's structure.

The first use describes the message; the second confirms the account of the Fire; the third guarantees the threat. The progression moves from what is proclaimed to what will happen to what is certain to happen, and each step uses the same word.

The progression answers the chapter's opening objection. The notables called the message an invention and a lie (38:4–5), and the chapter answers at three points by naming truth. The final naming is attached to a threat, and the attachment is the answer's sharpest form.

The threefold naming also mirrors the chapter's threefold structure. Its beginning concerns the Book, its middle the two destinations, and its end the origin of the enmity between them. Each part closes on the word, and the closing ties the parts together without any of them having to summarise the others.

**Why Truth Is Attached to a Threat**

The attachment deserves a moment's attention, because it is unusual. Truth is more often attached in the Qur'an to promises, to guidance and to the description of God Himself. Attaching it to a declaration about Hell reverses the expectation.

The reversal has a purpose. A promise can be dismissed as consolatory; a threat is harder to dismiss, because the person dismissing it has an interest in its falsity. The verse places the guarantee where the interest in denial is strongest, and the placing is deliberate.

The same logic governs the chapter's treatment of the two destinations. The Garden was described with an emphasis particle (38:54) and the Fire with an exclamation (38:56). The verse completes the pattern by attaching truth itself to the second of the two.

## Verse 38:85

> I will surely fill up Hell with you and whoever follows you from among them, all together.”

**The Statement, Addressed to the Adversary Directly**

The verse supplies what the previous verse withheld, and the content is a threat stated in the first person. Hell will be filled, and the filling is described with two groups named: Iblīs himself, and those among human beings who follow him.

The statement is the conclusion of the narrative that began at verse 71. Fifteen verses have covered the creation, the command, the refusal, the expulsion, the curse, the respite and the oath. The narrative ends with a declaration about where all of it leads.

The statement is spoken to Iblīs, and the direct address has consequences. He is told what will happen to him and to those who follow him, and the telling happens before the respite begins.

The telling removes the possibility of ignorance.

**The Same Threat Elsewhere**

The Qur'an makes the same statement in two other places, and the wording is close enough to indicate a fixed formulation. Hūd records that the Word of the Lord will be fulfilled, that Hell will be filled with jinn and humans all together (11:119). Al-Sajdah gives the same sentence as a word that has already gone forth (32:13).

The commentators note the correspondence and add that here and in al-Aʿrāf the promise is made directly to Iblīs rather than stated in the third person (7:18). The direct address changes the character of the statement: it is not a description of a decree but a sentence pronounced on the one hearing it.

The three occurrences together establish the threat as a settled element of the Qur'an's the teaching about the Last Day. It is not a local remark in one sūrah but a repeated declaration, and the repetition indicates that the filling is certain rather than contingent.

The certainty is what distinguishes this threat from the warnings earlier in the chapter. Those were addressed to people who could still avoid them; this one is addressed to a being whose course is already set, and it names an outcome that his activity will not alter. The filling includes him.

**"All Together"**

The phrase closes the statement, and its function is to prevent a reading in which the two groups are treated separately. Iblīs and his followers are filled into the same place at the same time.

The phrase also echoes the account of the angels' prostration, where they prostrated all together (38:73). The narrative began with an unanimous obedience and ends with an unanimous punishment, and the same phrase marks both. The echo is the chapter's way of measuring the distance between the two.

The word has a further implication for the followers. They are not admitted separately or ranked within the Fire; they arrive with the one they followed. The chapter has already described the leaders and followers disputing inside the Fire (38:59–61), and the verse states that the disputing parties share a single destination.

**What the Filling Implies**

The Qur'an connects the filling to a word that has already gone forth (32:13; 11:119), which places it in the category of decreed matters. The chapter has used the same category for the term of the respite — the appointed day — and the two belong together.

## Verse 38:86

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I do not ask you for any reward for this ˹Quran˺, nor do I pretend to be someone I am not

**The Chapter Returns to the Prophet**

The narrative of Adam has closed, and the chapter turns back to the address that occupied verses 65 to 70. The command is the same — *say* — and the content is a statement about the Prophet's own position.

The placement also answers the accusation recorded at the chapter's opening. The notables said the Qur'an was an invention and that the Prophet was seeking standing (38:4–8). The verse has him deny both, in his own voice, at the end of the sūrah.

**"I Ask No Reward of You"**

The denial concerns payment. The Prophet does not ask his audience for anything in return for delivering the message, and the denial removes the most common explanation for a claimant's behaviour.

The Qur'an uses the same formulation for five other prophets, and the repetition is exact. Nūḥ, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lūṭ and Shuʿayb each say *I ask no reward of you* in al-Shuʿarāʾ (26:109, 127, 145, 164, 180). The commentators note the correspondence and understand the phrase as referring to the delivery of the revelation.

Nūḥ states the principle most fully. If you turn away, he says, I have not asked you for any reward; my reward lies only with God, and I am commanded to be among those who submit (10:72). The sentence gives the reason for the denial: the reward comes from elsewhere, so the audience is not being charged.

The placement of the denial in this chapter carries an extra edge. It comes after the story of Solomon, who was given a kingdom no one after him would match (38:35), and after the account of Job, whose losses were returned doubled (38:43). A reader who has just read those accounts knows that God does reward His servants, and the verse distinguishes that reward from payment by an audience.

**Why the Denial Matters, and What the Message Is Not**

The two denials together define the message by exclusion. It is not a transaction, and it is not a performance. What remains is the content, and the content has been given in the verses about God's oneness and the two destinations.

**What the Word Imposes on the Person Using It**

The denial turns on a word worth holding onto, because it describes a cost rather than a lie. A *mutakallif* is not simply someone who is mistaken about himself; he is someone who takes a burden on himself to appear as what he is not. The root carries the sense of load, and the form carries the sense of straining under it.

The distribution is what settles the question. Every feature the word describes is visible in the conduct of the men making the accusation — the contention over rank, the claim to the treasuries of mercy (38:9), the demands that the unseen be produced on schedule. None of it is visible in the conduct of the man receiving the accusation, whose life before the revelation contained no claim of any kind. The verse asks the audience to look at where the strain actually is.

## Verse 38:87

> It is only a reminder to the whole world

**What a Reminder Does and Does Not Do**

The limit is also the audience's exposure. A reminder that is not compelling places the whole weight of the outcome on the person who hears it, and the chapter has been building toward that placement since its second verse.

**"Nothing but a Reminder"**

The verse describes the Qur'an in a single phrase, and the phrase is restrictive. Nothing but — the restriction excludes every other category the audience had proposed. It is not poetry, not magic, not the speech of a man being taught, not an invention.

The restriction also excludes categories the audience had not proposed but might have assumed. The Qur'an is not a history, not a legal code in the first instance, not a book of prophecy about specific events. It is a reminder, and the naming determines how it is to be received.

The Qur'an describes itself this way repeatedly. Al-Anʿām, Yūsuf, al-Qalam and al-Takwīr all use the same formula (6:90; 12:104; 68:52; 81:27), and the consistency indicates that the description is a settled self-characterisation rather than a local remark.

**What a Reminder Presupposes**

The claim is supported by the Qur'an's account of the primordial covenant, in which all human beings acknowledged God's lordship before their birth (7:172). The reminder calls back to that acknowledgement, and the calling is why the message can be recognised rather than merely learned.

**What a Reminder Does and Does Not Do**

A reminder does not compel. It presents, and the presentation leaves the response with the person reminded. The verse's description therefore contains the chapter's account of why the message does not force belief.

The Qur'an states the limit explicitly. The Prophet is told that he is not a controller over people (88:22) and that if God willed, all on earth would believe, so he is not to compel them (10:99). The reminder is the instrument, and the instrument has a defined reach.

The description excludes several things the audience wanted. A reminder is not a sign produced on demand, not a punishment brought forward, not a demonstration of power. Each of those was requested (38:16), and each is outside the category the verse names.

The exclusion is the verse's sharpest edge. The notables had set terms for belief, and the verse states that the message does not meet those terms because it is a different kind of thing. The refusal to meet the terms is not a failure but a definition.

**Reminder Without Coercion**

The description settles a question the chapter's audience kept raising. They asked for signs that would compel belief — angels brought down, the Book sent in pieces, the punishment produced on demand (38:16; cf. 17:90–93). A reminder is not that kind of instrument.

The reclassification is the chapter's final word on its opponents' method. They had been evaluating the revelation against standards drawn from magic, poetry and soothsaying. The verse gives it a category of its own, and the category determines what can be asked of it.

The determination cuts both ways. A reminder cannot compel, and it therefore cannot be blamed for failing to compel; but a reminder also cannot be dismissed, because dismissing it does not change what it recalls. The verse leaves the audience with an instrument that offers them nothing and requires everything.

## Verse 38:88

> And you will certainly know its truth before long.”

**"Before Long"**

The phrase means a time that is near, and the nearness is relative rather than measured. What is long for a person is short in the scale the verse is using, and the commentators differ on which scale is meant.

The phrase has a function beyond its literal sense. It removes the assumption that the interval is open-ended, which is the assumption the chapter has been contesting since the notables asked for the punishment to be hastened (38:16). They believed the delay meant the warning was empty; the verse says the delay is short.

**The Certainty and the Present, and What the Verse Leaves**

The telling is not a threat of punishment but a statement about certainty. A person can be punished without being convinced; the verse promises conviction, and conviction is the more severe of the two because it arrives without the possibility of acting on it.

The chapter has already described that arrival. The people of the Fire are shown asking where the believers are, and continuing to ask without understanding (38:62–63). The verse is describing the same condition from the other side of the interval.

Three things remain when the verse ends. A message that asks nothing of its hearer, a reminder addressed to every world, and a certainty that its truth will be known before long.

**The Knowing and the Two Destinations**

The connection also explains why the chapter gave the description at all. A reader who will come to know the truth does not need to be told in advance; the telling is what makes the difference between knowing in time and knowing too late. The verse names the alternative it is trying to prevent.

The people of the Fire were shown in the same state. They were asking questions whose answers they could no longer act on (38:62–63), and their questions are the form the promised knowledge takes when it arrives after the interval closes.
