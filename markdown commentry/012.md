# Sūrah Yūsuf (Chapter 12) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Year of Sorrow and the Jewish Challenge**

Revealed during the darkest days of the Prophet's Makkan mission—shortly after the catastrophic loss of Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib and the physical brutalization suffered at al-Ṭā'if—Yūsuf descended as a celestial balm upon a grieving Messenger. Historical reports transmitted by al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī indicate that the Jewish scholars of Yathrib (Madinah) prompted the Quraysh to test Muḥammad's prophetic credentials by asking him: *“Why did the family of Jacob migrate from Canaan to the land of Egypt?”* It was an obscure, detailed historical question inaccessible to an unlettered merchant in the pagan desert. Sūrah Yūsuf descended in response, not merely answering the historical inquiry with breathtaking historical precision, but providing an intentional spiritual mirror for the Prophet ﷺ himself: just as Joseph was envied by his brothers, cast into a pit, exiled, and falsely accused, only to eventually forgive them from a throne of supreme power, so too would Muḥammad ﷺ be driven from his home by his Qurayshi brethren, only to grant them sweeping, gracious pardon on the day Makkah was conquered.

**The Shape and Narrative Symmetry**

The architecture of Sūrah Yūsuf is crafted with astonishing dramatic and literary symmetry. The prologue (1–6) opens with Joseph as a young boy recounting to his loving father, Jacob (Yaʿqūb), his luminous dream of eleven stars, the sun, and the moon prostrating before him—a vision Jacob warns him to keep secret from his brothers. The first act (7–20) depicts the toxic jealousy of the brothers, their conspiracy to murder him, his casting into the subterranean well, the false blood stained upon his tunic, and Jacob's immortal response: *“So patience is most fitting (*fa-ṣabrun jamīl*), and Allah is the one sought for help”* (12:18), followed by Joseph's sale into Egyptian bondage for a paltry sum. The second act (21–35) stages the moral furnace of youth: Joseph adopted into the palace of the *ʿAzīz* (the Egyptian minister), his ripening into radiant beauty, the attempted seduction by the minister's wife (Zulaykhā), his desperate flight to the door where his shirt is torn from behind, the testimony of the infant witness, and his heroic choice of incarceration over moral corruption: *“My Lord, prison is more dear to me than that to which they invite me”* (12:33). The third act (36–57) details his prison life: his interpretation of the dreams of the baker and the cupbearer, his unyielding call to monotheism in the dungeon, the forgotten plea, the king's baffling dream of seven fat cows eaten by seven lean ones, Joseph's providential economic planning, his vindication and clearing of all charges, and his elevation to supreme economic treasurer of Egypt. The fourth act (58–93) dramatizes the return of the starving brothers: their failure to recognize Joseph, the testing through Benjamin, the planting of the royal drinking cup, the profound grief of Jacob who weeps until his eyes turn white from sorrow yet never despairs of God's spirit (*rawḥ Allāh*), and the electrifying moment of self-revelation: *“I am Joseph, and this is my brother!”* (12:90). The fifth act (94–101) brings the grand reconciliation: the fragrant shirt cast over Jacob's eyes restoring his sight, the arrival of the parents in Egypt, the raising of the parents onto the throne, and the prostration of the entire family, fulfilling the childhood dream. Joseph's closing prayer (12:101) is one of the most serene supplications in scripture: renouncing worldly majesty to pray: *“Cause me to die a Muslim and join me with the righteous.”* The epilogue (102–111) reflects on the reality of revelation, confirming that prophetic history is not fabricated myth, but an instructional lesson for minds endowed with deep insight.

## Verse 12:1

> Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the clear Book

**A Signature Shared by Six Neighbouring Chapters**

The identical group stands at the head of the chapters immediately around this one. Yūnus, Hūd, Yūsuf, Ibrāhīm and al-Ḥijr each begin *Alif-Lãm-Ra*, and between the third and fourth sits al-Raʿd with the lengthened form *Alif-Lãm-Mĩm-Ra* (10:1; 11:1; 13:1; 14:1; 15:1). Six consecutive chapters, one signature. The clustering is not ornamental. In all but three of the twenty-nine openings the letter-group is followed almost at once by a mention of revelation, and this block is the densest instance of that pattern in the book: letters, then the Book, then what the Book exists to do. Yūsuf contributes a third element its neighbours lack, namely a narrative running nearly a hundred verses without interruption. The signature announces a book; the chapter delivers a story; the third verse resolves the tension by declaring that the story is itself a mode of revelation.

**"Clear" Describes What the Book Does**

Al-kitāb al-mubīn is an active description rather than a passive one. Mubīn names a thing that makes plain, that sets two matters apart until each can be seen for what it is. Applied to scripture it says the Book does not merely contain clarity; it manufactures it — in law, in creed, in the difference between a prophet and an impostor, between a genuine dream and a fever. The same phrase opens two other chapters word for word, *“These are the verses of the clear Book”* (26:2; 28:2), and it is invoked as an oath in a third, *“By the clear Book!”* (44:2). Al-Ḥijr fuses the two descriptions this chapter keeps apart: *“These are the verses of the Book; the clear Quran”* (15:1). The insistence matters because what follows is a story saturated with concealment — a dream withheld, a plot hidden, an identity disguised, a providence operating unseen — and it is introduced by a sentence asserting that the book holding it is the instrument of disclosure. Clarity of this kind is a property of outcomes rather than of surfaces. Nothing in the plot of Joseph is transparent while it is happening; the shirt, the well, the prison and the famine all look like disasters at the time, and each is later shown to have been a step. The adjective promises that this reversal of appearance will be made intelligible, which is a larger claim than promising that the sentences will be easy.

**This Recitation, or the Tablet Behind It**

Two readings of the definite article have competed from the earliest commentaries onward. The first takes *the Book* to be the Qur'an now being recited. The second takes it to be the Preserved Tablet, the register from which revelation descends, elsewhere described as a glorious Qur'an *“˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet”* (85:22). Neither reading is frivolous. The second accounts for the strange double status of a text spoken aloud in a Meccan marketplace and simultaneously stored beyond the world. The first is still the stronger in this place, for the very next verse speaks of sending down and of Arabic, and both are operations performed upon this Qur'an rather than upon the Tablet; the plural *āyāt*, verses, likewise fits a recited text with divisions and pauses. What both readings agree on is the decisive point: the clarity being asserted is not a human accomplishment, and it is not on offer for negotiation. Yūsuf belongs to the Meccan period, and the reports place it toward the end of that period, after the deaths of Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib — the year the biographers call the Year of Sorrow — which falls around the tenth or eleventh year of the prophetic mission, some two or three years before the emigration to Medina.

## Verse 12:2

> Indeed, We have sent it down as an Arabic Quran so that you may understand

**A Formula the Book Uses Again and Again**

This is not an isolated remark. *“And so We have sent it down as an Arabic Quran and varied the warnings in it”* (20:113); *“˹It is˺ a Quran ˹revealed˺ in Arabic without any crookedness”* (39:28); *“˹It is˺ a Book whose verses are perfectly explained—a Quran in Arabic for people who know”* (41:3); *“And so We have revealed to you a Quran in Arabic, so you may warn the Mother of Cities and everyone around it”* (42:7); *“Certainly, We have made it a Quran in Arabic so perhaps you will understand”* (43:3). Five other passages, three of them ending on the same purpose as this one. Al-Shuʿarāʾ adds the channel: the trustworthy spirit brought it down *“into your heart ˹O Prophet˺—so that you may be one of the warners—in a clear Arabic tongue”* (26:193–195). The recurrence shows a settled policy rather than a passing comment, and it shows that the policy is directed at comprehension rather than at ethnic pride.

**The Messenger Speaks the Language of His People**

The general rule sits behind the particular case: *“We have not sent a messenger except in the language of his people to clarify ˹the message˺ for them”* (14:4). A revelation delivered in a foreign tongue to a first audience would have failed its own purpose, since clarification requires shared vocabulary, shared idiom, shared expectations about what a sentence can do. Arabic here is an act of accommodation to hearers, not a claim of superiority for them. The Qur'an itself answers the suspicion that its eloquence was borrowed: *“And We surely know that they say, ‘No one is teaching him except a human.’ But the man they refer to speaks a foreign tongue, whereas this ˹Quran˺ is ˹in˺ eloquent Arabic”* (16:103). The alleged teacher could not have produced it, and the argument turns on the very Arabicness the verse affirms. Language is thus both the gift and the evidence.

**The Objection That Would Have Followed Otherwise**

The Book anticipates the counterfactual and answers it before it is raised. *“Had We revealed it as a non-Arabic Quran, they would have certainly argued, ‘If only its verses were made clear ˹in our language˺. What! A nonArabic revelation for an Arab audience!’”* (41:44). The same verse then supplies the verdict on those who reject it in either form: for the believer it is guidance and healing, while for others it remains sealed. The rhetorical trap is worth seeing plainly. Whatever the language, the objector finds a reason. When the tongue is unfamiliar the complaint is obscurity; when it is the hearer's own, the complaint is that there is nothing extraordinary about it. That is why the purpose clause in this verse locates the outcome in *taʿqilūn* — the work of the mind — rather than in the properties of the words. Clarity is supplied; the binding is left to the one who hears.

**Clarity Claimed Twice in Two Lines**

Verse 1 called the Book clear, and verse 2 explains the medium of that clarity. Placed side by side they make a single argument: the disclosure is real, and it is addressed in the hearer's own idiom. No excuse of unintelligibility survives the pairing. Yet the chapter that follows is full of people who fail to understand what is directly in front of them — brothers who misread a dream, a father whose sons lie to him in his own language, a woman who mistakes refusal for coyness, a prison full of men who cannot interpret their own nights. Clarity in the Book does not guarantee clarity in the reader. The Arabic is straight, as 39:28 insists, without crookedness; the crookedness, when it appears, belongs to the one listening. This is the pivot on which the whole sūrah turns, and it is set here, before a single character enters, so that the story is heard as a test of attention rather than as a chronicle of other people's fortunes. The word *laʿalla*, so that or perhaps, keeps the outcome open.

## Verse 12:3

> We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ the best of stories through Our revelation of this Quran, though before this you were totally unaware ˹of them˺

**A Superlative Attached to One Narrative**

The words are *aḥsan al-qaṣaṣ*: the best of stories, not a good story, not an instructive one. The superlative is explicit and it is attached to a single narrative among all the narratives revelation could have chosen. Elsewhere the same construction is applied to the Book as a whole — *“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ has sent down the best message—a Book of perfect consistency and repeated lessons”* (39:23) — so the Qur'an is comfortable ranking its own discourse. The ranking here is not aesthetic in the modern sense of pleasant. *Ḥusn* in this usage means the story does its work completely: nothing in it is idle, nothing is missing, and every element returns later to complete another. A narrative earns the superlative by the tightness with which its parts interlock, and by the range of human situations it manages to hold inside one arc without breaking.

**The Ignorance Clause Is the Argument**

The second half of the verse does the evidential work. *Though before this you were totally unaware* is not a biographical aside; it is a proof offered to an audience that could check it. The Qur'an uses the move repeatedly and in the same terms. *“This is one of the stories of the unseen, which we reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. Neither you nor your people knew it before this. So be patient! Surely the ultimate outcome belongs ˹only˺ to the righteous”* (11:49). *“This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary’s guardian”* (3:44). *“You were not there ˹O Prophet˺ on the western side of the mountain when We entrusted the Commandments to Moses”* (28:44). Each time the claim is that the teller had no access. A man who never studied scripture, living in a city with no rabbinical academy, recites a Hebrew patriarch's biography with details unknown to his own people. The unawareness is the ground of the miracle, not a footnote to it. Mecca in this period had no synagogue capable of supplying such a narrative and no library at all; the Prophet could not read the scriptures of the People of the Book even had they been present. The verse asks the audience to weigh that fact against the fluency of what they are hearing. It is the same argument the chapter presses again in its last verse, where the message is called a confirmation of earlier revelation and not a fabrication (12:111).

**Consolation Written Into the Form**

The setting supplies the third reason for the superlative. This chapter is placed by the reports in the years after Khadījah and Abū Ṭālib died, when the Prophet stood unprotected in a city that had turned violent toward him, and shortly after the journey to Ṭāʾif ended in stones. Into that moment comes the longest continuous narrative in the book, and its subject is a man betrayed by his own family, sold, slandered, imprisoned on evidence everyone knew was false, and finally raised to authority over the people who destroyed him. The form of consolation is deliberate. Not a command, not a list of promises, but a story — the shape in which a grieving mind can hold hope without having to argue for it. Calling it the best of stories is therefore a claim about function as well as craft: this is the account best fitted to do what the moment required.

## Verse 12:4

> ˹Remember˺ when Joseph said to his father, “O my dear father! Indeed I dreamt of eleven stars, and the sun, and the moon—I saw them prostrating to me!”

**Four Prophets in One Line of Descent**

The boy who speaks is Joseph, and the genealogy standing behind him is one of the most distinguished in the Book. He is the son of Jacob, the son of Isaac, the son of Abraham, and all four are prophets. When the Qur'an lists the line of guided men granted to Abraham, it names Joseph inside it: *“And We blessed him with Isaac and Jacob. We guided them all as We previously guided Noah and those among his descendants: David, Solomon, Job, Joseph, Moses, and Aaron”* (6:84). Ibn ʿUmar reports the Prophet ﷺ saying, “The nobleman, son of the nobleman, son of the nobleman, son of the nobleman — that was Joseph, son of Jacob, son of Isaac, son of Abraham” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3202; the same wording appears in al-Adab al-Mufrad 896). No other figure in the Qur'an is described with four consecutive prophetic generations behind him. That pedigree explains the confidence of the scene: this is not an orphan's fancy but an heir to a house in which dreams have always been a form of knowledge.

**The Address of a Son Who Is Loved**

Joseph says *yā abati*, rendered here *O my dear father*, a vocative form with an extra letter attached that carries affection and nearness. His brothers use a different word for the same man. Their opening appeal is *“O our father!”* (12:11), and their later reports begin *“Our father!”* (12:17). The possessive plural is a claim on shared property; the singular endearment form is a relation. The distinction is small in Arabic and large in practice, and the chapter keeps it consistently. Every sentence Joseph addresses to Jacob in this sūrah uses the intimate form, including the final one spoken from a throne decades later (12:100), while the brothers never once use it. A reader who notices the grammar hears the family's real structure before any of them state it.

**Eleven Stars, a Sun and a Moon**

The count is exact and it is the reason the dream can be checked. Eleven stars correspond to eleven brothers, the sun and the moon to the two parents; some commentators reverse the last pair, assigning the sun to the mother and the moon to the father, on the grounds that the sun's light is borrowed by nothing while the moon's is received, and Jacob's authority in the household was exercised through a gentler presence. When the fulfilment arrives the verse reports it in the same numbers — parents raised to the throne, the family prostrating — and the reader can verify the dream against the event without any explanation being supplied (12:100).

**A Prophet's Dream Is a Channel of Revelation**

Islamic tradition treats the true dream as a genuine mode of knowledge and not as a private curiosity. ʿĀʾishah reports that the first form in which revelation came to the Prophet ﷺ was the true dream, which arrived like the breaking of daylight (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3), and Anas reports him saying that the good dream of a righteous man is one of forty-six parts of prophethood (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6983). The commentators differ about Joseph's standing at this moment. Some hold that the dream itself is already revelation and that he is therefore a prophet from childhood. Others place the beginning of his mission later, at the inspiration given to him in the well (12:15), and read this vision as a sign of a prophetic function not yet conferred — precisely the arrangement found in the life of the Prophet Muhammad, whose true dreams preceded the descent at Ḥirāʾ. The second position is the stronger, since the Qur'an nowhere else grants prophethood to a child and it does mark verse 15 with the explicit language of revelation.

Prostration to another created being belongs to earlier dispensations: the angels were commanded to prostrate before Adam and did so (2:34), and the practice remained lawful in the law of the Children of Israel, which is how the family's bowing in verse 100 can be reported without censure.

## Verse 12:5

> He replied, “O my dear son! Do not relate your vision to your brothers, or they will devise a plot against you. Surely Satan is a sworn enemy to humankind

**Conceal a Blessing From Those Who Will Envy It**

The practical rule Jacob applies became a standing instruction in the tradition. A good vision is not to be broadcast; it is told to those who wish the dreamer well and withheld from those who do not. The distinction underlying that discipline is prophetic: Abū Qatādah reports the Messenger of Allah ﷺ saying that the good vision is from God and the distressing dream from the devil, and that one who sees the second should spit dryly to his left three times and seek refuge from its evil, after which it will not harm him (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2261a). The same collection preserves his instruction that a liked vision be disclosed only to one whom the dreamer loves. [UNVERIFIED: the exact Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim number for the "disclose only to one you love" wording could not be confirmed; the collection and narrator are established, the number is not.] The reason is not superstition but social physics. A blessing announced to a rival becomes a target. The prohibition on envy is itself among the most firmly attested commands in the collections: Anas reports the Prophet ﷺ saying, “Do not hate each other, do not envy each other, do not turn away from each other, but rather be servants of Allah as brothers” (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6065; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2559). Envy is treated there as a rupture of the community's basic bond, not as a private mood. Jacob's counsel is the defensive half of that same teaching.

**The Motive Named, Then Its Author Named**

The structure of the reply is worth isolating. First comes the prohibition: do not relate your vision to your brothers. Then the consequence: they will devise a plot against you. Then the ground of the consequence: Satan is a sworn enemy to humankind. The third clause is not decoration. It relocates the threat from ten jealous young men to an adversary older and more patient than any of them, and it does so without excusing them. The brothers remain responsible; they are simply not the origin. The Qur'an states the same arrangement as a command to the reader: *“Surely Satan is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy. He only invites his followers to become inmates of the Blaze”* (35:6). Take him as an enemy means treat the enmity as declared rather than suspected, which is exactly what Jacob is teaching his son in a single sentence.

**"A Sworn Enemy" Means the Enmity Is Public**

The adjective *mubīn* attached to the enemy means manifest, open, evident — the same word that in verse 1 describes the Book as clear. Applied to Satan it says the hostility has been announced rather than concealed. He declared it before Adam, swore to it, and has operated in the open ever since; what is hidden is his method, not his intention. The Qur'an repeats the phrase across many chapters — among them at 2:168, 2:208, 6:142 and 7:22 — and repeats it most pointedly in an instruction about speech between believers: *“Tell My ˹believing˺ servants to say only what is best. Satan certainly seeks to sow discord among them. Satan is indeed a sworn enemy to humankind”* (17:53). The identical closing formula appears there and here, which links the brothers' resentment to a general mechanism: harsh words between people who love each other are the openings he works in. The repetition is itself the pedagogy. An enemy whose enmity has been declared cannot later claim to have been misunderstood, and that is why the clause sits at the end of a domestic warning. Jacob is telling his son that the resentment brewing among his brothers is not a family quarrel with a family solution, but the local expression of a campaign whose author has stated his objective in advance.

Human plotting appears in the plans of Joseph's brothers, of the women of the city, and of the opponents of every prophet; divine counter-plotting appears where the Qur'an says that they plan and God plans, and that God is the best of planners (8:30).

## Verse 12:6

> And so will your Lord choose you ˹O Joseph˺, and teach you the interpretation of dreams, and perfect His favour upon you and the descendants of Jacob—˹just˺ as He once perfected it upon your forefathers, Abraham and Isaac. Surely your Lord is All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

**Taʾwīl al-Aḥādīth: Reading Events, Not Only Dreams**

The gift named second is *taʾwīl al-aḥādīth*, literally the interpretation of accounts or events, which the translation renders as the interpretation of dreams. The word *aḥādīth* is the plural of *ḥadīth*, a thing spoken of, an occurrence, a report. Three layers of meaning have been drawn from it, and the chapter uses all three. The narrowest is the technical skill of decoding a sleeping vision, which Joseph exercises for two prisoners (12:36–41) and then for a king (12:43–49). The middle layer is the discernment of hidden meanings in scripture, the same faculty praised in *“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ has sent down the best message—a Book of perfect consistency and repeated lessons”* (39:23). The widest is the ability to see what events are doing, which is what Joseph displays when he reads a famine as a plan and a family's arrival as a reckoning. The phrase *taʾwīl* itself means returning a thing to its origin, so the skill is not guessing but tracing an appearance back to the reality that produced it.

**Where Dreams Come From, and Why the Distinction Matters**

The tradition that grew around this gift began by sorting visions into kinds. A true dream is understood to reach the sleeper from an angelic quarter, while a false one arises from the lower self or from a devil's suggestion. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī preserves the threefold division as a saying current among the early Muslims, reported there through Ibn Sīrīn: dreams are the reflection of one's waking thoughts, what Satan suggests to frighten, or glad tidings from God. The prophetic reports draw the same line more sharply — the good vision is from God and the distressing dream from Satan, and the one who sees the second should spit dryly to his left three times, seek refuge and turn over (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2262). Only the first kind carries meaning, which is why interpretation is a discipline rather than a pastime. Joseph's gift presupposes the distinction: to interpret a dream one must first know whether it deserves interpreting at all.

**The Favour Perfected on a Whole House**

Jacob does not stop at his son. The blessing is promised to Joseph *and to the descendants of Jacob*, which extends the gift across a family and a century. Two readings of that extension are traditional. The first takes the blessing to be prophethood, so that the house will produce a line of prophets. The second takes it to include the goods of this world along with the goods of the next, which fits Joseph's case unusually well: he receives revelation and a treasury. Zakariyya's prayer in another chapter asks for an heir who will inherit from him and *“from the family of Jacob”* (19:6), showing that the house remained a byword for a certain kind of blessing long after this promise.

**Two Forefathers and the Two Trials Behind Them**

Abraham and Isaac are named as the precedent, and each name carries a trial that ended in deliverance. Abraham was taken by God as a close friend (4:125) and was rescued from the fire his own people built for him: *“We ordered, ‘O fire! Be cool and safe for Abraham!’”* (21:69; the building of the furnace is reported at 37:97). Isaac's blessing is bound to the altar, where he submitted to his father's commission and was ransomed by a substitute at God's command (37:102–107). The Qur'an does not name the son in that account, and the exegetes differ between Ismāʿīl and Isḥāq; Jacob's phrasing here does not settle the question, since he cites both men as recipients of a completed favour rather than as participants in one event. What the pairing establishes is the pattern the boy should expect: elevation is reached through a trial that looks like destruction, and the favour is called complete only when the trial has been survived.

## Verse 12:7

> Indeed, in the story of Joseph and his brothers there are lessons for all who ask

**For Those Who Ask**

The qualification at the end of the verse restricts the audience. *Li-l-sāʾilīn* — for those who ask, for the inquirer — excludes the indifferent reader and the hostile one in the same breath. A sign is only a sign for someone looking in its direction. The Qur'an makes the same demand elsewhere and in the same idiom, instructing the uncertain to put the question: *“So ask the people of the message if you do not know”* (16:43). Asking is treated as a virtue rather than a deficiency throughout the Book, and here it is the condition of receiving anything from the narrative at all. That condition shapes how the chapter is written. Details are withheld, motives are left implicit, and the plot advances by concealment, so a passive listener loses the thread. The lessons are available, but only to the reader willing to work back through the earlier verses to find them.

**Envy Is the First Lesson and It Fails**

The moral the commentators draw first is the futility of the motive that drives the plot. Envy produced the plan, the plan produced the well, and the well produced Egypt — the exact opposite of what was intended. The Prophet ﷺ warned against the passion in terms preserved in the collections: “Avoid envy, for envy devours good deeds just as fire devours fuel” (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4903). The report is graded weak by al-Albānī, yet its meaning stands on stronger ground, since the prohibition itself is recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim among the commands binding the community together. Al-Rāzī draws the parallel that would have struck the first audience hardest: the same envy that moved Joseph's brothers moved the Prophet's own kin against him, and in both cases the envied party ended up holding authority over the enviers. The lesson is not that envy is ugly. It is that envy is incompetent.

**Patience and Gratitude Are the Two Hinges**

Read as instruction, the story divides cleanly into two postures and the trials that produce them. Joseph in the well, in slavery and in prison is the model of patience; Joseph in the treasury, with his brothers kneeling before him, is the model of gratitude. Neither posture is presented as a temperament. Both are achievements under pressure, and the chapter is careful to show the cost: patience that complains to creatures is not the patience Jacob calls beautiful (12:18), and gratitude that forgets the prison is not the gratitude Joseph offers at the end (12:100). Between the two hinges sits the theme that gives the sūrah its particular consolation — that God's care for His friends runs through their afflictions rather than around them, so the trial is not evidence of abandonment. Love and severity arrive in the same parcel, and the story exists to teach the recipient to read the address.

**A Signpost Placed Before the Story Begins**

Structurally this verse interrupts the narrative at its most exposed point. The dream has been told, the father's interpretation given, and the brothers' resentment is about to speak for the first time in verse 8. Between the promise and the crime the chapter steps out of the story to say what the story is for. The effect on the reader is decisive: from this point the plot is watched rather than simply followed. Every reversal is received as evidence for a claim already stated, and the audience knows in advance that the malice they are about to witness will be defeated by it. A narrative that announces its own meaning before delivering it is unusual, and the announcement converts suspense into recognition. Its final verse generalises the move made here: *“In their stories there is truly a lesson for people of reason. This message cannot be a fabrication, rather ˹it is˺ a confirmation of previous revelation, a detailed explanation of all things, a guide, and a mercy for people of faith”* (12:111).

## Verse 12:8

> ˹Remember˺ when they said ˹to one another˺, “Surely Joseph and his brother ˹Benjamin˺ are more beloved to our father than we, even though we are a group of so many. Indeed, our father is clearly mistaken

**ʿUṣbah: A Word That Measures Strength**

*A group of so many* translates *ʿuṣbah*, a term the lexicographers define as a band of roughly ten to forty people, large enough to act together and to be reckoned with. The brothers choose it deliberately. They are not saying that they are many; they are saying that they constitute a force, the kind of group that defends a household, works its land and carries its name. In a tribal setting that was a real argument. Adult sons were the security of an ageing father, and a clan's standing was measured in grown men rather than in children. The word will return in their oath a few verses later, where the same self-image is used to promise that no wolf could take a boy from among them (12:14). Both uses rest on a single assumption: that strength of this kind entitles its holders to a share of love proportionate to their usefulness.

**"His Brother" Means Benjamin, and the Distinction Is Real**

The brother named only as *his brother* is Benjamin, Joseph's full brother — the one son of Jacob by the same mother. The phrase does careful work. It marks the two youngest as a pair inside a larger family, and it records the fact that provoked the resentment: Jacob kept them nearer to him than the rest, and the commentators report that he would not let Joseph out of his company night or day. Their youth explains the closeness and does not excuse the interpretation the older brothers placed on it. Affection for two children who had lost their mother was read as a verdict on ten men who had not. The same logic will drive the second half of the sūrah, when Benjamin becomes the object of a plot and the brothers discover that they have not learned anything from the first one.

**A Domestic Complaint in the Language of Unbelief**

The final clause is the most alarming in the verse. *Indeed, our father is clearly mistaken* renders *fī ḍalāl mubīn*, in manifest error — the phrase the Qur'an uses elsewhere for real deviation, spoken by disbelievers about their prophets and by prophets about idolatry. Here it is turned on a father by his sons over a matter of household affection. The escalation is diagnostic. When a grievance cannot be resolved by argument, it borrows the vocabulary of the largest available accusation, and the accuser begins to experience a preference as a heresy. Jacob is not only unfair in their account; he has gone astray, which means he cannot be reasoned with and must be worked around. Every plot in this chapter follows from that single substitution of categories.

---

## Verse 12:9

> Kill Joseph or cast him out to some ˹distant˺ land so that our father’s attention will be only ours, then after that you may ˹repent and˺ become righteous people!”

**Two Proposals With a Single Objective**

The verse offers alternatives, and the alternatives reveal what is actually wanted. Death and distant exile are very different acts with very different consequences for the victim, yet they are presented as interchangeable, which means neither is the point. What both achieve is absence. The brothers are not choosing between two harms they have weighed; they are naming two routes to one destination, and the destination is a household in which Joseph no longer appears. The construction *kill him or cast him out* also shows the deliberation at an early, unrestrained stage: the violent option is spoken first and plainly, before the moderate voice of verse 10 arrives to negotiate it downward. The Qur'an preserves that order because it matters morally. A group that has already said the word aloud has crossed a line, and the later compromise does not return them to the far side of it.

The two options also differ in what they leave behind for the father. Death closes the case; exile leaves it open. A boy cast out to some distant land — the land is deliberately unspecified, a place where he cannot be looked for — may live, and a living son may one day be found. The brothers accept that residue of possibility because they judge the distance sufficient, and the text records no debate about it. Their tolerance of uncertainty is what distinguishes this proposal from murder, and it is also what the plot will exploit. Every later development in the sūrah depends on Joseph having survived: the caravan, the Egyptian household, the prison, the granaries, the recognition scene. The option that seemed to leave a loose end was the only one that left a story.

**The Object Is the Father's Face**

The purpose clause names the real target. *So that our father's attention will be only ours* renders a phrase built on the word for face — that his face may be toward you alone. Face here means regard, the direction in which a person's care turns. What the brothers want is not an inheritance, an office or a possession; nothing material is mentioned anywhere in their planning. They want exclusivity of affection, and they want it measured: only ours, with no remainder. That demand explains why the crime had to be total. A rival who is merely reduced still receives some part of the father's regard, and any part is unbearable to someone whose objective is the whole. Envy of this kind cannot be satisfied by improvement in its own position, only by the disappearance of the comparison.

**The Qur'an's Own Verdict on That Arrangement**

Deferred repentance is treated elsewhere in the Book as a category error. *“Allah only accepts the repentance of those who commit evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺ then repent soon after—Allah will pardon them”* (4:17). The condition attached is speed: soon after. The case excluded is named explicitly in the next verse: *“However, repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, ‘Now I repent!’”* (4:18). Between those two positions lies the brothers' proposal, and it is closer to the excluded case than they think. A wrong undertaken with the intention of repenting afterwards is not ignorance; it is calculation, and calculation is precisely what the first verse does not cover. The mercy the chapter eventually shows them is real — Joseph forgives without condition at 12:92 — but it arrives after decades of consequence, not as the cancellation of a pre-arranged instalment.

## Verse 12:10

> One of them said, “Do not kill Joseph. But if you must do something, throw him into the bottom of a well so perhaps he may be picked up by some travellers.”

**The Eldest Brother, and a Name the Qur'an Withholds**

The commentators identify the speaker as the eldest of the ten, though the text gives him no name. Two candidates are offered from the biblical account: Reuben, who in Genesis 37:21–22 tries to rescue Joseph and return him to his father, and Judah, who in Genesis 37:26–27 proposes selling him rather than killing him. The Qur'anic suggestion resembles the second and the intercession described in the transmitted reports resembles the first, which is why the exegetes have not settled the question. Al-Rāzī notes that the action itself — proposing a commercial outcome rather than a violent one — points toward Judah, while others follow the report that the eldest had given his father a pledge. The withholding of the name is consistent with the chapter's treatment of all ten. Their individual identities are irrelevant to the lesson; what matters is that a group produced a moderate voice and still acted.

**The Well as Exile Delegated to Strangers**

The plan has a specific structure worth naming. The brothers do not intend Joseph's death; they intend his removal, and they outsource the outcome. A well on a caravan route is a device for transferring responsibility to travellers who will never be identified. If a passing company lifts him out, he lives somewhere far away and the family is intact in form. If he drowns or dies of exposure, the well did it. Either way no brother holds a knife. Al-Rāzī and others observe that this was precisely the recommendation's appeal: the stain of blood stays off their hands, and the distance they wanted is achieved without the act they feared. The moral cost of that arrangement is not reduced by its ingenuity. A harm arranged so that nobody performs it is still performed, and the sūrah will spend eighty verses collecting the bill.

The vocabulary of the proposal carries the same evasiveness. *Ghayābat al-jubb* — the bottom of the well, more precisely the hidden recess of it — names both a place and a concealment. A *jubb* is an unlined well cut into rock, and its *ghayābah* is the part where light does not reach and where a body cannot be seen from above. The commentators describe the particular well as deep, wide at the bottom and narrow at the mouth. Choosing a location defined by invisibility is a decision about witnesses as much as about geography: nobody will see what happens there, and nobody will be able to look down afterwards and find him. The same noun will do double duty in the story, since concealment is precisely what the plan fails at. He is hidden from his father, and hidden therefore from the brothers' control, and the hiding is what allows him to be found by the wrong people — which turns out to be the right ones.

**God's Plan Runs Through the Instrument of the Crime**

The well is the hinge of the entire narrative, and it was chosen by the plotters. Everything after it depends on the decision made in this verse: the caravan that draws water, the bucket, the market in Egypt, the household of the official, the prison, the interpretation of a king's dream, the granaries, the famine, and finally the reunion. Had the brothers killed him, none of it would exist. The chapter is built so that the instrument of destruction is also the instrument of elevation, and the reader watches the same pit serve two purposes at once. This is the working form of the principle stated elsewhere in the Book — that a person may dislike a thing while God places much good in it (2:216) — displayed rather than asserted. The verse looks like the point at which the plot succeeds. It is the point at which it is commandeered.

## Verse 12:11

> They said, “O our father! Why do you not trust us with Joseph, although we truly wish him well

**"We Truly Wish Him Well" Claims the Virtue of Sincerity**

The word behind *we truly wish him well* is from *naṣīḥah*, sincerity, the quality of wanting good for another without reserve — a term the tradition treats as one of the highest social virtues, the substance of the pledge the Prophet ﷺ took from his Companions to advise every Muslim sincerely. The brothers appropriate it. Their sentence has two emphatic particles wrapped around the claim, which in Arabic produces something close to an oath: we are, without doubt, sincere well-wishers toward him. The irony is exact. Sincerity is being asserted by men who agreed two verses earlier to throw the boy into a well, and the assertion is aimed at the one person in the family with the standing to test it. Al-Rāzī observes that the claim was designed to present love and compassion for Joseph, implying that no harm could be intended. It is the first lie of the chapter spoken directly to the father, and it is a lie about a virtue.

**Credibility Is Built Before the Favour Is Asked**

The division of labour between this verse and the next is a technique. Verse 11 establishes character; verse 12 makes the request. No demand is issued until the demanders have been presented as sincere and the objector as unreasonably suspicious. Modern readers will recognise the sequence from any negotiation in which one side spends its first minutes repairing its reputation rather than stating its case. The Qur'an's decision to reproduce both stages separately, instead of compressing them into one speech, forces the audience to sit through the preparation. The result is that when the request finally comes — send him out with us tomorrow — it arrives already insulated from refusal, and Jacob's hesitation looks like stubbornness rather than insight.

Worth noticing too is what the verse does not contain: an answer. The rhetorical question is left hanging, and the father's reply comes only after the concrete request of verse 12. Jacob does not argue with the accusation. A man asked why he cannot trust his own sons has no reply that does not either insult them or expose a suspicion he cannot substantiate, and the text shows him declining the trap rather than falling into it. He moves the conversation from his character to the boy's safety, which is the only ground on which he can actually stand. That shift costs him the rhetorical exchange and saves him the appearance of accusing ten men of murder to their faces. It also means the brothers' charge goes unanswered on the record, where it will continue to look reasonable to anyone who hears only their side.

**The Father's Suspicion Was Correct, and That Is the Point**

Everything the brothers say here is false, and the verse is constructed so that the reader knows it. The chapter has already shown the private deliberation (12:8), the two proposals for disposal (12:9), and the compromise of the well (12:10). When the polished public version arrives, its falsity is not inferred; it is remembered. That arrangement produces the sūrah's central dramatic effect. Jacob will be deceived, and the audience will not be. His eventual refusal to believe their later story about the wolf will look like paranoia to anyone reading only verse 17, and like accuracy to anyone who has read this one. Distrust is being vindicated in advance, and the vindication depends on the reader holding two versions of the same family in mind at once — the one presented to the father, and the one presented to God.

## Verse 12:12

> Send him out with us tomorrow so that he may enjoy himself and play. And we will really watch over him.”

**A Request That Is Small, Cheerful and Dated**

After the interrogation of the previous verse, the demand arrives disguised as a favour to the boy. Send him out with us *tomorrow* — the adverb does a great deal of work. It forecloses deliberation, since a plan that begins tomorrow cannot be examined at leisure, and it frames any hesitation as obstruction of a child's pleasure. The request is also modest in scale: an outing to the pasture, a place the brothers visit as a matter of routine. Nothing about it looks like the beginning of the worst day of Jacob's life. That ordinariness is the deception's strength. A father can refuse a strange proposal; refusing an everyday one makes him the unreasonable party, which is exactly the position verse 11 was constructed to produce.

**"We Will Really Watch Over Him" Is a Sentence They Will Say Again**

The promise closes with two emphatic particles: we are, indeed, for him, guardians. The word *ḥāfiẓūn* is the same they will use decades later, in the second half of the story, when they ask Jacob to send Benjamin to Egypt: *“So send our brother with us so that we may receive our measure, and we will definitely watch over him”* (12:63). The repetition is not accidental. Jacob's answer the second time quotes their history back at them — *“Should I trust you with him as I once trusted you with his brother ˹Joseph˺?”* (12:64) — and the chapter by this marks the phrase as the family's recurring instrument of deceit. A formula used twice, with the same emphasis and the same result, is a signature. The reader who remembers verse 12 hears verse 63 differently.

**Evil Borrows the Vocabulary of Childhood**

The most unsettling feature of these verses is their tone. The plot to kill or exile a brother has been discussed in verse 9; two verses later the same men are speaking of games and guardianship, and the language of the second conversation is warm. Nothing in the transition registers the shift. This is how the Qur'an portrays hypocrisy at the domestic scale: not as a performance of menace but as a performance of affection, delivered by people who genuinely intend the harm and genuinely enjoy the outing. Al-Qushayrī's remark on their later tears applies here too — that a person who has become thoroughly two-faced gains control of his outward signs, including his eyes. The verse asks the reader to hold both facts at once: the words were kind, and the plan behind them was already fixed.

The grammar of the closing promise deserves a second look. *Wa-innā lahu la-ḥāfiẓūn* stacks two particles of emphasis — the initial *inna* and the *lām* prefixed to the predicate — producing something stronger than a plain statement: we are, indeed, his guardians. Verse 11 used the identical construction for its claim of sincerity.

## Verse 12:13

> He responded, “It would truly sadden me if you took him away with you, and I fear that a wolf may devour him while you are negligent of him.”

**The Grief of Separation Is Named Without Shame**

*It would truly sadden me* is a plain admission that an hour without this son costs him something. The commentators draw the obvious inference: his love was intense enough that brief absence produced real pain, and the reports add that he kept Joseph with him night and day. The spiritual tradition reads that intensity as an image rather than merely a fact. Just as a father could not bear separation from the beloved son, so the believer is instructed not to be forgetful of the Divine Beloved even for a moment, since heedlessness is itself separation and remembrance is togetherness. The comparison is not decorative. The sūrah will show that Jacob's attachment survives every test applied to it — the bloodied shirt, the decades of silence, the second loss — and it will show him still saying that he knows from God what his sons do not know (12:86).

**Naming the Danger Hands Them the Script**

The wolf appears in this verse for the first time, and it appears in the mouth of the man it will supposedly kill. Jacob names a specific hazard of the pasture, and three verses later the brothers return to report exactly that hazard realised: a wolf devoured him (12:17). The irony is structural, not incidental. Their lie requires no invention because their father has already supplied its content; they only have to agree with him. Anyone who has warned a person about a risk and then been told the risk has materialised will recognise the difficulty of proof that follows. The warning becomes the alibi. The Qur'an places the two sentences close enough together that the reader hears the transfer happening, and hears Jacob's own foresight converted into evidence against him.

**A Specific Wolf, or Ten of Them**

Two readings of the definite article have been preserved. The first takes *the wolf* literally and notes that the definite form suggests one particular animal, known to the family, which is why the brothers can report it so confidently and why Jacob can be accused of it later. The second reading rests on a transmitted account of a dream Jacob had: himself on a mountaintop, Joseph in a valley, ten wolves circling the boy, and one wolf withdrawing before the ground opened to hide him for three days. On that account the wolves are the brothers, the one who withdraws is the brother who dissented in verse 10, and the opening in the ground is the well. The report is not established by any chain of the first rank [UNVERIFIED: Jacob's dream of the ten wolves is found in the commentaries without a graded chain], and the simpler reading is the safer: wolves were the standard danger of a Canaanite pasture, and a father would name what he actually feared.

**Foreknowledge Does Not Function as Protection**

The verse raises a question the sūrah answers at length. Jacob sees the danger, names it, argues against it — and loses anyway. Nothing in his perceptiveness prevents the outcome, and the Qur'an's theology of events explains why: *“No calamity befalls ˹anyone˺ except by Allah’s Will. And whoever has faith in Allah, He will ˹rightly˺ guide their hearts ˹through adversity˺”* (64:11). Foresight is a real gift and it is not a shield. What faith supplies is not prevention but orientation inside the event, which is what Jacob displays in verse 18 and again in verse 83. The reader should therefore not measure the father's wisdom by the result. His judgement was correct; the decree ran through it.

The verse also leaves the consent unnarrated. Jacob objects, the brothers answer in verse 14, and the next verse begins *when they took him away* — the permission is inferred from the departure rather than recorded. That omission is deliberate and it protects the father. Nothing in the text shows him convinced by their oath, and verse 18 makes clear he never was. What he granted, on the reading the reports support, was a release under conditions rather than an endorsement of the plan. The elision means the reader must supply the moment of decision, and every supplying of it lands on the same uncomfortable fact: a man who foresaw the danger allowed the outing anyway, because a son cannot be kept from the world forever and because refusing would have confirmed the charge in verse 11.

The brothers answer this objection in the verse that follows.

## Verse 12:14

> They said, “If a wolf were to devour him, despite our strong group, then we would certainly be losers!”

**The Book Will Turn Their Word Against Them**

*Khusr*, loss, is one of the Qur'an's heaviest terms, and it is reserved for a different kind of defeat. The shortest chapter in the Book opens its central claim with it: *“Surely humanity is in ˹grave˺ loss”* (103:2), before listing the four things that rescue a person from it. Elsewhere the word describes the weighing of deeds on the last day: *“But those whose scale is light, they will have doomed themselves, staying in Hell forever”* (23:103). The brothers use it for a social embarrassment that will never occur. The chapter spends the rest of its length showing what actual loss looks like — a brother sold, a father's sight destroyed by grief, decades of guilt, and finally a confession delivered to a ruler who holds their lives in his hands (12:91). Their sentence is true in a sense they did not intend, and the reader is made to wait for it.

**Confidence in Numbers Is Exactly What the Story Dismantles**

Ten men who believe their plurality makes them formidable are the protagonists of a narrative in which plurality achieves nothing. Their plan fails at every stage: the removal does not remove the affection, the lie does not end the suspicion, and the brother they discarded returns holding authority over their food supply in a famine. The chapter's arithmetic runs the other way. One boy in a well, one prisoner in a cell, one interpreter before a king — each alone, each advancing. The Qur'an makes the same point doctrinally elsewhere, that victory does not scale with numbers and that a small company has overcome a large one by God's leave. Within this sūrah the point is made narratively, and verse 14 is where the false premise is stated most plainly so that its collapse can be watched.

**A Promise That Sets Up a Second One**

This assurance is the one Jacob will remember. Decades later, when the brothers ask him to release Benjamin, he refuses to do so on their word alone and demands a solemn oath by God, binding unless they are overwhelmed (12:66). The escalation between the two scenes is exact. In verse 14 they offered a rhetorical flourish and he accepted it; in verse 66 he accepts nothing less than a sworn covenant witnessed by God. The intervening verses explain the change. A man who has once been given an assurance of this kind and found it worthless does not argue about assurances again; he raises the price. The Qur'an does not need to narrate that lesson. It places the two promises far apart in the chapter and lets the distance between them do the teaching.

The later scene supplies the correction to this one. Having been given a rhetorical assurance and lost a son, Jacob no longer accepts assurances of any human kind. He instructs his sons to enter Egypt by separate gates and immediately adds the sentence that undoes the instruction: *“I cannot help you against ˹what is destined by˺ Allah in the least. It is only Allah Who decides. In Him I put my trust”* (12:67). Precaution is taken and then declared useless in the same breath, and the narrator confirms the verdict in the following verse — the plan did not avail them against God's decree at all; it was a desire in Jacob's heart that he satisfied (12:68). Between verse 14 and verse 67 the father has moved from relying on the promises of his sons to relying on nothing but God, and the movement is the real cost of the assurance recorded here.

## Verse 12:15

> And so, when they took him away and decided to throw him into the bottom of the well, We inspired him: “˹One day˺ you will remind them of this deed of theirs while they are unaware ˹of who you are˺.”

**Everything Between the Departure and the Well Is Skipped**

Two clauses stand side by side — they took him away, and they decided to throw him into the well — and between them the narrative places nothing. No journey, no argument, no moment of seizure. The elision is one of the sūrah's most calculated effects. A reader arriving from verse 14 expects the outing, the games, the pasture, and instead receives the pit. The gap is where the transmitted reports insert their material: the brothers carrying the boy in turn while Jacob watched them leave, the throwing down once out of sight, the beating, the elder brother's intervention to honour a promise not to kill him, the rope, the stripping of the shirt, and the hands struck as he clung to the well's edge.

**"We Inspired Him": Revelation Outside Prophethood**

The verb *awḥaynā* describes a communication from God that the Qur'an applies well beyond prophets. It is used of the mother of Moses, instructed to nurse him and then to put him into the river with a promise of return (28:7); of the bees, directed to build in mountains and trees (16:68); and of the earth itself on the last day, which speaks *“having been inspired by your Lord”* (99:5). The term therefore covers a range, from an inward casting of meaning into a heart to a genuine commissioning. The commentators divide accordingly. Some hold that Joseph received this through an angel and that this moment is the beginning of his prophethood, which would place it after the childhood vision of verse 4. Others take it as *ilhām*, inspiration proper — a certainty placed in the heart without an office attached. The first reading has the stronger textual support, since verse 21 later describes God teaching him the interpretation of dreams as part of establishing him in the land, a formation that fits a prophetic career.

**"While They Are Unaware" Has Two Readings**

The final clause is grammatically ambiguous and both readings have been defended. On the first, it belongs to the predicted future: you will tell them of this deed at a time when they do not realise who you are. That is precisely what happens — Joseph recognises them in Egypt and they do not recognise him (12:58) — and al-Bayḍāwī treats the clause as a foreshadowing of that scene. On the second reading the words describe the present: God is revealing to Joseph something of which his brothers, standing above him, are unaware. Al-Rāzī supplies a reason for the concealment on that reading: had they known he was a prophet, their envy would have grown and they would certainly have killed him. The two readings are not rivals in substance. Both assert the same structural fact, that the meaning of this moment is invisible to everyone in it except the One arranging it.

**The Fulfilment Arrives in a Single Question**

The promise is paid out in verse 89, in one sentence and without preamble: *“Do you remember what you did to Joseph and his brother in your ignorance?”* (12:89). Decades have passed; the speakers are ministers of a famine-stricken province addressing supplicants for grain; the addressees have no idea who is speaking. The wording matches this verse almost exactly — this affair of theirs, they unaware — which is how the reader identifies the fulfilment. The verse that follows records the collapse of the concealment: *“Are you really Joseph?”* and the answer, *“I am Joseph, and here is my brother ˹Benjamin˺!”* (12:90). Nothing about the scene is triumphal. The revelation produces confession rather than revenge, and the confession uses the word the brothers had used about themselves in verse 14 in a different sense: we have been sinners (12:91).

## Verse 12:16

> Then they returned to their father in the evening, weeping

**Tears Presented as Proof**

Crying is offered before any claim is made, which makes it function as evidence. Grief displayed is grief believed, and the display removes the burden from the account that will follow. Al-Qushayrī's comment on this scene is the sharpest in the tradition: hypocrisy taken far enough gives a person command of his outward signs, to the point where the eyes can be made to weep at will. The observation is not a cynical generalisation but a diagnosis of what the verse shows. Ten men have just committed a crime together, agreed a story together, and now produce a physiological response together, on cue, at the door of their father's tent. The coordination is the tell, and the reader who has seen verse 15 recognises it immediately. Jacob, who has not seen verse 15, must judge the same performance with no access to its preparation.

**Irony Used as Moral Training**

The gap between what Jacob knows and what the audience knows is the instrument the chapter works with. Everything from this point depends on that gap. When Jacob answers in verse 18 with a flat refusal and an appeal to beautiful patience, a reader without verse 15 would call him unjust — ten distressed sons accused on no evidence. A reader with verse 15 calls him accurate. The chapter thus trains its audience in a specific habit: withhold assent from a well-told account until the arrangement behind it is known. The same habit is required of the reader again and again in what follows, where appearances reverse repeatedly and every surface turns out to have been carrying a second meaning. The shortest verse in the section is doing the longest work.

The technique deserves naming because the chapter depends on it. The Qur'an reports the brothers almost entirely from outside: what they said, what they did, when they came home. Their thoughts are never given. Jacob, by contrast, is rendered from within — his grief, his fear, his suppression of complaint, his knowledge of what they do not know. Interiority is granted to the truthful and withheld from the deceivers, and the withholding is itself a statement. A group that has agreed on a story no longer has ten minds; it has one script, and the text treats it accordingly. The reader is denied the only access that might generate sympathy for them, which is why their eventual confession in verse 91 lands with the force it does. For eighty verses they are a chorus, and only at the end do they become individuals again.

**Two Kinds of Weeping in One Chapter**

Tears appear on both sides of this story and the contrast is exact. The brothers weep in the evening, on schedule, at the door, in a group, over a loss they caused. Their father weeps for years, alone, silently, over a loss he cannot confirm. The later verse describes it without any of the coordination found here: *“He turned away from them, lamenting, ‘Alas, poor Joseph!’ And his eyes turned white out of the grief he suppressed”* (12:84). He turns away from the people present, which is the opposite of performing to an audience, and the grief is described as suppressed rather than displayed. The sūrah sets the two scenes against each other across sixty-eight verses and lets the difference in posture — toward others, away from others — carry the whole moral distinction between them.

## Verse 12:17

> They cried, “Our father! We went racing and left Joseph with our belongings, and a wolf devoured him! But you will not believe us, no matter how truthful we are.”

**"Left Joseph With Our Belongings" Transfers Custody to Objects**

The phrase is exact about a small thing and evasive about a large one. Garments and provisions were indeed left behind; the reports describe the brothers stripping Joseph of his shirt and keeping it for the blood. What the sentence conceals is that the boy was left with no guardian at all. Objects cannot watch a child, and the construction quietly reassigns responsibility from persons to things. This is a familiar move in the grammar of excuse: the passive arrangement replaces the active agent, and what happened happened while everyone was somewhere else. Jacob's later verdict cuts through it in three words — your souls have tempted you (12:18) — restoring the agents their sentence was designed to remove.

**"You Will Not Believe Us" Is the Most Calculated Clause**

Two readings of the final sentence have been preserved, and both are worth holding. The first takes it as an involuntary admission: they know Jacob distrusts them, which is why their earlier approach began by asking what ailed him (12:11). Their awareness of his suspicion is genuine and it testifies against them. The second reads the clause as a deliberate shield — a pre-emptive inoculation that makes disbelief look like prejudice. On this reading the sentence performs a trick: any rejection of the story confirms the prediction that he would reject it, so the rejection cannot be used as evidence that the story is false. The second reading is the more searching, because it identifies a device still in use. A claim that anticipates and disqualifies its own refutation has removed the only check available to the hearer.

**The Verse Reports Speech and Withholds Verdict**

Nothing in this verse says they lied. The narrator records the cry, the three components of the story, and the defensive clause, then stops. The reader has already seen the well, the inspiration and the decision (12:15), so no verdict is needed; the knowledge has been supplied in advance and the lie is recognised rather than proven. That arrangement separates the audience from Jacob permanently. He must judge on evidence; they judge on information. The consequence is a certain humility about his eventual conclusion in verse 18, which turns out to be right and to have been reached without the reader's advantage. The chapter's method throughout is to show rather than to declare, and this verse is the clearest instance: the deception is left standing in its own words, and its falsity is the reader's discovery rather than the narrator's assertion.

The pattern repeats, and the repetition is the point. When the brothers come back from Egypt with Benjamin detained under a charge of theft, they again arrive carrying a coherent account, and again their father will not accept it. His answer is almost word for word the answer he gives here — *“No! Your souls must have tempted you to do something ˹evil˺”* (12:83) — followed again by the vow of beautiful patience. The second time the story they tell is true. Joseph has detained Benjamin deliberately and nobody has stolen anything. Yet the formula Jacob reaches for is the one he learned in this verse, because a man deceived once by his sons keeps no method for separating their truth from their fiction. That is the lasting damage of the lie: it does not merely hide one event, it destroys the family's capacity to believe anything the liars say afterwards.

## Verse 12:18

> And they brought his shirt, stained with false blood. He responded, “No! Your souls must have tempted you to do something ˹evil˺. So ˹I can only endure with˺ beautiful patience! It is Allah’s help that I seek to bear your claims.”

**A Shirt That Appears Three Times**

The garment is the chapter's most carefully managed object. It is brought here stained with a blood that lies; it is requested by Joseph near the end, sent to Egypt's border with instructions to cast it over his father's face (12:93); and it arrives, is thrown, and restores Jacob's sight (12:96). Three appearances, three functions: false evidence, a token of recognition, and a cure. Nothing else in the sūrah is handled with that economy. The first appearance establishes the shirt as the object on which the family's belief will be decided — Jacob is persuaded or not persuaded by what he can see and touch — and the later appearances show the same object doing the opposite work, confirming a truth instead of manufacturing a lie. A reader who forgets verse 18 will not understand why verse 93 is moving.

**"Your Souls Have Tempted You" Is an Accusation Without a Charge**

Jacob's first word is *bal*, no — a rejection of the account, not an alternative account. He then names what he believes has happened: your souls have made the matter appear fair to you. The verb *sawwalat* describes the self dressing up an action until it becomes acceptable to the one performing it. The same words will return in verse 83 after the loss of Benjamin, unchanged, which marks them as his settled diagnosis of his sons rather than a reaction to one event.

**"Beautiful Patience" Names a Specific Kind of Endurance**

*Ṣabr jamīl* is not patience in general but patience qualified. The tradition defines it as endurance without complaint to creatures — the grief is real, the loss is acknowledged, and the appeal is directed to God alone. The phrase appears in the Qur'an as an instruction to the Prophet in the face of denial: *“So endure ˹this denial, O Prophet,˺ with beautiful patience”* (70:5). Jacob uses it at the moment of maximum provocation and again decades later, and in between he will explain what it costs him: *“I complain of my anguish and sorrow only to Allah, and I know from Allah what you do not know”* (12:86). The qualification is the whole content of the term. Patience that rehearses its injury to every available listener is not beautiful; it is negotiation. What Jacob adopts is the refusal to make his sons his audience, which is why his grief later destroys his sight instead of his speech.

**"God's Help Is Sought Against What You Describe"**

The final clause turns away from the sons and toward God, and its object is precise: *ʿalā mā taṣifūn*, against what you describe. Not against you, not against your crime — against your account. What Jacob cannot bear is the narrative, the false words spoken in his tent about his son, and it is help against that which he asks for. An almost identical formula closes a prophet's prayer elsewhere in the Book: *“And We will surely be a sufficient help against what you describe”* (21:112), said to a messenger facing the same problem, the misdescription of the truth by people who benefit from it. The verse by this places a grieving father inside a category the Qur'an reserves for its prophets. He is not only suffering a loss; he is contesting a falsehood with no evidence of his own, and he has identified the only tribunal competent to hear the case.

## Verse 12:19

> And there came some travellers, and they sent their water-boy who let down his bucket into the well. He cried out, “Oh, what a great find! Here is a boy!” And they took him secretly ˹to be sold˺ as merchandise, but Allah is All-Knowing of what they did

**The Scene Changes With No Transition**

One verse ends with a boy at the bottom of a well receiving a promise; the next begins with a caravan arriving. No interval is stated, no journey is described, and no connection is drawn between the two events. The narrative simply cuts. That abruptness is the verse's first argument. What looked like an ending in verse 15 turns out to have been a handover, and the reader is given no time to grieve before the machinery of the next stage is already running. The technique will be used again throughout the sūrah, most sharply at the move from the palace to the prison (12:35). Nothing in this chapter is allowed to conclude; every situation is a corridor into another one, and the corridors are supplied by people who have no idea what they are doing.

**A Caravan, a Water-Boy and a Bucket**

The three details are the ordinary furniture of desert travel and each one matters. A company on the road sends its *wārid* — the one whose job is to go to water — ahead of the camp, and he lets down his bucket, *adlā dalwah*, into whatever well the route provides. Nothing about the scene is remarkable, and that is the design. The reports add that Joseph had been in the well three days and that the company was travelling from Midian toward Egypt, having come on the well by chance after losing its way. [UNVERIFIED: the three days, the Midian origin and the lost-way detail are tafsīr reports without a graded chain; used here as context, not as verified fact.] Whether or not those details are exact, the text is explicit that the arrival was not arranged by anyone in the story. No one sent for the caravan; no one planned the rescue. A lost company, a routine errand and a lowered rope constitute the whole of the deliverance, and the chapter will later insist that the arrangement behind such accidents is divine (12:21).

**"They Hid Him as Merchandise" — Who Are "They"?**

The pronoun is ambiguous and the commentaries divide. On one reading *they* are Joseph's brothers, who concealed the fact that the boy in the well was their brother and allowed him to be taken as goods; on this account the sale in verse 20 is performed by the brothers themselves. On the other reading *they* are the members of the caravan, who concealed the boy from the rest of their own company so that the finders could claim him privately and sell him for their own profit. The transmitted reports support both, and the Qur'an's ambiguity may be intentional — the verse describes a transaction in which every party is hiding something from every other party.

**"Allah Is All-Knowing of What They Did" Is the Hinge**

The closing clause changes the verse from a report into a verdict. Everything in the scene is concealed: the brothers' identity, the caravan's find, the boy's own silence, the water-carrier's shout mistaken for a discovery rather than a theft. The clause asserts that all of it is witnessed. This is the formula the sūrah returns to at every point where human concealment reaches its limit, and it does specific work here, because the transaction is about to move Joseph to a country where no one who knows him will ever see him again. The doctrine attached is the one stated elsewhere in general terms: *“No calamity befalls ˹anyone˺ except by Allah’s Will. And whoever has faith in Allah, He will ˹rightly˺ guide their hearts ˹through adversity˺”* (64:11). The verse is not a comfort offered to the characters. It is information given to the reader, and it is the reason the story can be told as providence rather than as luck.

## Verse 12:20

> They ˹later˺ sold him for a cheap price, just a few silver coins—only wanting to get rid of him

**A Verb That Means Both Buying and Selling**

The word behind *they sold him* is from *sharā*, one of the small number of Arabic verbs that carries an action and its opposite: to sell and to buy, to give something up and to take it on. The Qur'an exploits the double sense repeatedly. The same root describes those who exchange the hereafter for this world, and it describes God purchasing from the believers their lives and wealth in exchange for Paradise (9:111). Here the ambiguity has a practical consequence for interpretation. If the pronoun refers to Joseph's brothers, they sold him; if it refers to the company that found him, they bought him. The transmitted reports divide on the question, with Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid and al-Ḍaḥḥāk reading the brothers and others reading the caravan. Both readings produce the same price and the same indifference, which suggests the ambiguity is not a problem to be solved but a description of a market in which nobody is clearly the responsible party.

**"A Cheap Price" Names a Deficiency, Not a Bargain**

*Bakhs* means deficient, reduced, fallen short of what is due. The same word appears in the speech of the jinn who hear the Qur'an: whoever believes in their Lord fears neither *bakhs* nor *rahaq* — neither a shortfall in reward nor an excess in punishment (72:13). The pairing there is instructive, because it defines *bakhs* as the specific injustice of being given less than what is owed. Some commentators push the point further and hold that any price would have been a *bakhs*, since no sum can be the equivalent of a person, still less of a prophet.

**"Numbered Dirhams" Tells the Reader How Small the Sum Was**

The specification *darāhima maʿdūdah* — dirhams counted out — is a piece of commercial information. Al-Qurṭubī records the practice behind it: Arab traders settled large transactions by weight and small ones by count, so describing a payment as numbered fixes it below the threshold at which weighing became worthwhile, conventionally fewer than forty coins. The detail is therefore not decorative. It tells the hearer that the sum was small enough to be handled one coin at a time, in a transaction too minor to require scales. Ibn Kathīr transmits the amounts reported by the early commentators: twenty dirhams according to Ibn Masʿūd, with Ibn ʿAbbās, Nawf al-Bikālī, al-Suddī, Qatādah and ʿAṭiyyah al-ʿAwfī saying the same, and ʿAṭiyyah adding that the brothers divided it among themselves at two dirhams each. Other reports give twenty-two, and others forty. The biblical account agrees with the lowest figure, recording twenty pieces of silver (Genesis 37:28).

**Twenty Coins for a Prophet, and Egypt Will Pay More**

The valuation is the moral centre of the verse. A boy of the house of Abraham, described in the sūrah as one whom God will choose and teach (12:6), is priced at twenty silver coins and handed over to strangers by people who cannot wait to be rid of him. The chapter then spends the next thirty verses raising that price. The official who buys him instructs his wife to give the boy an honoured place (12:21); the women of the city are undone by his appearance (12:31); a king calls him trusted and esteemed (12:54); and Joseph himself asks to be put in charge of the storehouses of the land (12:55). The movement from twenty dirhams to the treasury of Egypt is not a change of fortune so much as a correction of an error. The verse records the error at its lowest point so that the correction can be measured.

## Verse 12:21

> The man from Egypt who bought him said to his wife, “Take good care of him, perhaps he may be useful to us or we may adopt him as a son.” This is how We established Joseph in the land, so that We might teach him the interpretation of dreams. Allah’s Will always prevails, but most people do not know

**The Buyer Becomes the Patron**

The man who bought Joseph is described only as *the one from Egypt*, and the sūrah never names him. The commentaries supply the name found in Genesis 39:1 — Potiphar, rendered in the Arabic sources as Qiṭfīr or Iṭfīr — and the title by which the chapter will later address him, *al-ʿAzīz*. That title does useful work. *ʿAzīz* carries the senses of grand, powerful, cherished and honoured, and it denotes a high office rather than a crown. The distinction matters because this sūrah also has a king, addressed separately in verses 43, 50, 54 and 76, so the ʿAzīz cannot be the monarch. He is a minister, and some reports identify him as overseer of the treasury or as captain of the guard. The same title will later be applied to Joseph himself (12:78, 12:88), which is the chapter's quiet way of marking the transfer: the boy bought for twenty dirhams ends up holding the office of the man who bought him.

**The Same Sentence Was Spoken Over Moses**

The words are almost identical to those Pharaoh's wife used about a different foundling: *“˹This baby is˺ a source of joy for me and you. Do not kill him. Perhaps he may be useful to us or we may adopt him as a son.”* (28:9). Two powerful households, two boys with no protection, the same two possibilities named in the same order — usefulness, then sonship. That verse closes with a sentence this one only implies: *“They were unaware ˹of what was to come˺”* (28:9). The parallel is deliberate and it is doing theological work. In both cases the household that takes the child in is the household the child will eventually overturn or inherit. Pharaoh's palace raised Moses; the ʿAzīz's house raised Joseph. Neither man could have known it, and the verse records that they did not.

**Adoption in the Sense the Households Meant**

The phrase *we may adopt him as a son* describes a practice the Qur'an later addresses directly. In its Medinan legislation the Book distinguishes between care of a child and the fabrication of lineage: *“Nor does He regard your adopted children as your real children. These are only your baseless assertions”* (33:4), followed by the instruction that adopted children keep their fathers' names, since that is more just in God's sight (33:5). The ruling does not condemn what the ʿAzīz proposed; it regulates it. What the earlier dispensations allowed as full incorporation into a family, this law restricts to guardianship without falsifying descent. Reading verse 21 against 33:4–5 shows the chapter aware of the difference between sheltering a boy and claiming him, and shows Joseph's own history running through the first without ever acquiring the second.

**"Allah's Will Always Prevails" Is the Verse's Conclusion**

The final clause is a maxim, and it is aimed at the plot the reader has just watched. God is *ghālib* over His affair — prevailing, dominant, not thwarted. The brothers willed a well; the caravan willed a profit; the ʿAzīz willed a servant; God willed a prophet in a treasury. The clause *but most people do not know* repeats a formula the sūrah uses twice more (12:40, 12:68) and the Book uses often elsewhere, and it identifies the specific ignorance at issue: not ignorance of facts but ignorance that events are governed.

## Verse 12:22

> And when he reached maturity, We gave him wisdom and knowledge. This is how We reward the good-doers

**"When He Reached Maturity" Uses the Language of Full Strength**

The phrase *balagha ashuddahu* denotes the arrival at full physical and mental strength, the point where a man is complete. The same formula describes Moses in another chapter: *“And when he reached full strength and maturity, We gave him wisdom and knowledge”* (28:14) — a parallel so close that the two verses have long been read together. Elsewhere the Qur'an fixes a different marker for the peak of a life, the age of forty: *“In time, when the child reaches their prime at the age of forty”* (46:15). The chapter does not specify Joseph's age, and the transmitted reports do not settle it either. What the phrase guarantees is that what follows is the act of an adult, not of a boy. The trial in verse 23 is presented as a trial of a formed man with formed desires, and the verse exists to establish that fact before the trial begins.

**The Formula "Thus Do We Reward the Good-Doers" Is a Law**

The closing sentence appears word for word in two other places: at the end of the list of prophets descended from Abraham (6:84) and at the end of the account of Moses reaching his strength (28:14). A phrase repeated verbatim across chapters functions as a stated rule rather than a comment, and the rule is that the gift follows the character. Both recipients in the other two instances are described in the same terms as Joseph is here — men of proven goodness given judgement and knowledge. The sūrah reinforces the identification by applying the adjective *muḥsin*, one who does good, to Joseph four separate times, in the mouths of his fellow prisoners (12:36), in the narrator's summary of his establishment (12:56), in the brothers' report of him (12:78) and in his own final prayer (12:90). The reward named in verse 22 is therefore not a gift to a stranger but a payment on an account the chapter keeps openly.

**Everything Here Is Passive**

The grammar of the verse deserves notice. Joseph does nothing in it. He reaches maturity — a process that happens to him — and then *We gave him* wisdom and knowledge. No striving is mentioned, no study, no ascent. The two things that will define his career are described as received. That is consistent with the whole chapter's theology, in which a well, a market and a prison are all instruments of a plan Joseph did not write. It also protects the later claim he makes in verse 53, that he does not acquit himself because the soul inclines to evil *except those shown mercy by my Lord* (12:53). The man who says that has already been shown, in this verse, where his resources came from. His innocence in the coming scene is not self-sufficiency. It is the use of a gift.

That has a consequence for how the chapter treats the years of slavery. Nothing in verse 22 suggests Joseph earned his standing by diligence in the household, though the reports assume he served well and the ʿAzīz's earlier expectation of usefulness (12:21) implies it. What the verse records is a bestowal that arrives with age and is unrelated to labour. The sūrah by this separates two things a reader is inclined to fuse: the boy's conduct, which was good, and the equipment he received, which was given. Conduct is rewarded, as the closing formula insists; the reward is still a gift. Joseph will spend the rest of the chapter acting on that equipment in circumstances he did not choose, in a prison he did not deserve, and the verse is the reason those actions can be called prophetic rather than merely capable.

## Verse 12:23

> And the lady, in whose house he lived, tried to seduce him. She locked the doors ˹firmly˺ and said, “Come to me!” He replied, “Allah is my refuge! It is ˹not right to betray˺ my master, who has taken good care of me. Indeed, the wrongdoers never succeed.”

**Rāwada Describes Pressure, Not a Single Advance**

The verb *rāwadat-hu* comes from a root meaning to seek something from someone persistently, with repeated urging and manoeuvre. It is not the word for a momentary temptation or a single proposition. The form implies an extended campaign, attempts met by refusals and renewed attempts, which is why the Qur'an uses the same verb again in verse 30 for what the city's women say she has been doing over time, and again in verse 51 when she confesses it herself. Joseph's later prayer speaks of what *they invite him to* in the plural and over a period (12:33), confirming that verse 23 opens a process rather than an incident. The chapter's moral realism depends on that reading: what he resists is not one moment of weakness but a sustained effort to wear him down.

**The Doors Are Locked in the Plural**

*Wa-ghallaqati al-abwāb* — she locked the doors — uses a verb form indicating thoroughness and a plural noun. Not one door but the doors of the rooms between them and the household, fastened properly. The detail eliminates any suggestion of opportunity or accident. This is preparation, carried out before the summons, and the plural tells the reader how much of the house she controlled. It also explains why verse 25 has both of them running toward a door: only one remains, and it leads to the person she least wants to meet. The locked doors serve the plot twice, first as her instrument and then as the reason the encounter ends with her husband standing in the only exit.

**His First Word Is a Refuge, Not a Refusal**

Joseph begins with *maʿādha Llāh* — God be my refuge, I seek shelter in God. The words are not a rejection of her but a relocation of himself. He places the encounter inside a larger frame before answering anything in it, and the frame is God rather than consequence. The same expression returns from his mouth much later, when his brothers ask him to take another man in place of the one accused of theft: *“Allah forbid that we should take other than the one with whom we found our property”* (12:79). Both uses mark the moments where Joseph is asked to do something possible and profitable and answers from a position he cannot be moved out of. The phrase is also the traditional formula of refusal in the Qur'anic idiom, used by the women of the city in verse 51 in a different sense, and its presence here tells the reader that this refusal is not improvised.

**"He Is My Lord" Has Two Referents**

The clause *innahu rabbī* — he is my lord, who has made my lodging good — is the crux of the verse and the exegetes divide on it. On one reading *he* is the ʿAzīz: Potiphar honoured me and I will not betray him. On the other, *he* is God: my true Master is the One who saved me from the well and placed me in this man's favour, and it does not become me to disobey Him. Both readings fit the Arabic and both fit the sentence that follows about the good lodging, which was the ʿAzīz's own instruction in verse 21. The second is the stronger, because Joseph's next clause appeals to God as refuge and because the sūrah elsewhere shows him refusing to rest his case on human favour. The first reading is still preserved in the tradition and adds a real moral layer: loyalty to a benefactor is itself a reason, and the chapter allows both reasons to stand.

## Verse 12:24

> She advanced towards him, and he would have done likewise, had he not seen a sign from his Lord. This is how We kept evil and indecency away from him, for he was truly one of Our chosen servants

**Two Things Turned Away, Named Separately**

*Li-naṣrifa ʿanhu al-sūʾa wa-l-faḥshāʾ* — that We might turn away from him evil and indecency. The two nouns are not a hendiadys. *Sūʾ* is the broad category of what is bad, glossed here as the disloyalty toward his master that the act would have involved; *faḥshāʾ* is the specific term the Qur'an reserves for gross sexual immorality. Naming both covers the whole offence: the betrayal of a household and the transgression of a prohibition. The verb *naṣrifa*, We turn away, is causative and divine — the turning is performed by God, not achieved by Joseph. That is consistent with the previous verse, where his first word was a request for refuge, and with his own later statement that he does not acquit himself, since the soul inclines to evil except where God shows mercy (12:53). The chapter is scrupulous about the distribution of credit.

**"One of Our Chosen Servants" Fixes the Reason**

The final clause gives the ground: *innahu min ʿibādinā al-mukhlaṣīn*. The root *kh-l-ṣ* means to be pure, unmixed, exclusively devoted, and the passive form describes one who has been purified or singled out by God. Some read the participle actively, as one who is sincere in his devotion; the difference is between a status conferred and a quality achieved. The chapter's own evidence favours the conferred sense. Joseph's escape is attributed throughout to divine action — God turned the evil away, God answered his prayer (12:34), God established him in the land (12:21) — and his own summary of the episode is that he was preserved rather than strong. The phrase also places him in a class. He is *one of* God's chosen servants, a member of a group that includes the prophets named elsewhere in the Book, and the classification is what makes the temptation scene a chapter in a career rather than an isolated test.

**The Verse Explains the Accusation That Follows**

Read in sequence, verse 24 disposes of verse 25 in advance. The woman tears the shirt, meets her husband at the door and demands a punishment for the man who wanted to violate her. The reader already has what the household does not: the inclination was resisted, the proof was seen, the evil was turned away by God. Nothing in the coming investigation can therefore surprise the audience, and the trial by torn shirt in verses 26–28 becomes a demonstration for the characters rather than a discovery for the reader. This is the sūrah's method throughout. It gives the audience the inside of events before giving the characters the outside, so that every subsequent claim can be weighed rather than merely followed. The torn shirt, the witness, the household's verdict and the city's gossip all arrive afterwards, and none of them can add anything the reader does not already hold. That arrangement has a moral purpose beyond narrative economy. The chapter is training an audience to distrust a well-presented accusation, having just shown one being constructed by the person with the most to gain from it. The same training was given in the earlier verses, where a bloodied shirt and a set of convincing tears were shown to a father. Two fabricated cases in one sūrah, both involving a garment, both resolved by evidence rather than eloquence.

## Verse 12:25

> They raced for the door and she tore his shirt from the back, only to find her husband at the door. She cried, “What is the penalty for someone who tried to violate your wife, except imprisonment or a painful punishment?”

**Both of Them Running Toward the Same Door**

The verb is *istabaqā*, the two of them competed in speed. He is fleeing; she is pursuing; the same word covers both because both are running. The sūrah has already used this root once, in a very different scene: the brothers told their father *“We went racing and left Joseph with our belongings”* (12:17). The echo is exact and it is not decorative. In the earlier case the racing was an alibi for a crime that had already been committed; here it is the crime being committed, interrupted. Both verses use competitive movement to mark a moment when a household's account of itself is being falsified, and both are followed by a man arriving who has to decide what to believe. The chapter keeps its vocabulary tight enough that a reader can hear the second race as a reprise of the first.

**The Shirt Torn From Behind Is the Verse's Real Content**

Everything else in the scene — the doors, the pursuit, the husband — exists to produce this one physical fact. A tear at the back of a garment identifies the direction of movement and therefore who was in front. The commentators state the inference plainly: a tear at the front would mean she was defending herself against an advancing man, while a tear at the back means she caught hold of a man who was leaving. The verse does not draw the conclusion; it manufactures the evidence and stops. That restraint is characteristic. The sūrah's method is to place objects in front of characters and let the objects decide, which is also how verse 18 worked, when a shirt covered in false blood failed to convince a father because it had no tear in it. Two shirts, two cases, opposite results, and in both the cloth testifies more reliably than the people.

**Two Penalties Offered, and the Lesser One Is Chosen**

She names imprisonment or a painful punishment. Both are severe; the difference between them is presented as a matter for the husband's discretion, which is how a choice is made to look free. The sūrah takes the first option. Verse 33 records Joseph's prayer that prison is dearer to him than what he is being called to, verse 34 records God's answer, and verse 35 records the decision: *“And so it occurred to those in charge, despite seeing all the proofs ˹of his innocence˺, that he should be imprisoned for a while.”* (12:35) The penalty she proposed in anger becomes the sentence pronounced after an investigation that cleared him. That sequence is the chapter's most exact demonstration of how a false accusation works: it does not need to be believed to take effect, only to be available.

**Love That Protects Itself Is Not Yet Love**

The commentators draw a distinction here that the rest of the sūrah develops. Her feeling for Joseph is real, and it is also willing to destroy him. Faced with exposure she does not confess, does not shield him and does not accept blame; she prosecutes. Al-Maybudī's reading is that her love at this stage was incomplete, since a love that saves itself at the beloved's expense has not yet become what the tradition means by love. The chapter will track the change. In verse 32 she admits the attempt in front of the women of the city; in verse 51, years later and with nothing left to gain, she states the whole truth to the king: *“Now the truth has come to light. It was I who tried to seduce him, and he is surely truthful.”* (12:51) The distance between verse 25 and verse 51 is the distance this sūrah measures.

## Verse 12:26

> Joseph responded, “It was she who tried to seduce me.” And a witness from her own family testified: “If his shirt is torn from the front, then she has told the truth and he is a liar

**One Clause of Defence, Using Her Own Verb**

Joseph's entire answer is *hiya rāwadatnī ʿan nafsī* — she sought to seduce me. He does not narrate the locked doors, does not mention the pursuit, does not explain the tear in his garment and does not appeal to his own character. He takes the verb the Qur'an used for her action in verse 23 and gives it back with her as its subject. The economy is deliberate. A slave contradicting the wife of the house has one resource, which is to state the fact in the shortest possible form and let the evidence do the rest. The commentators gloss the sentence as: she wanted indecency with me and I refused and fled. The flight is contained in the clause, because a man who refuses and runs is a man whose garment is torn from behind.

**The Witness Comes From Her Side of the Family**

*A witness from her own people* is the verse's decisive intervention, and its force lies entirely in its origin. A witness from Joseph's side would have been dismissed; a witness from the household staff would have been suspect. Someone belonging to her family has no interest in her disgrace and every interest in protecting it. The transmitted reports identify him in two ways, and both go back to named authorities. ʿAbd al-Razzāq recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās that the witness was a bearded man — an adult male — and Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, al-Ḥasan, Qatādah, al-Suddī and Ibn Isḥāq held the same reading. Al-ʿAwfī reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that he was a babe in the cradle, and a young boy belonging to the ʿAzīz's household is likewise reported from Abū Hurayrah, Hilāl ibn Yasāf, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and al-Ḍaḥḥāk — the reading al-Ṭabarī preferred. What both preserve is the same structural point: the testimony that saves Joseph is supplied by the party least inclined to supply it.

**The Shirt Testifies Twice in This Chapter**

The garment is the chapter's most reliable witness and it appears at both of the story's great deceptions. In verse 18 a shirt smeared with false blood was produced to convince a father that his son had been eaten, and it failed, because a garment that a wolf had supposedly torn was intact. Here a shirt torn in the wrong place destroys a wife's account of what happened in a locked room. The two cases are mirror images: in the first, the absence of damage disproved the story; in the second, the position of the damage disproves it. The sūrah is making an argument about evidence through the repetition of a single object, and it is an argument directed at the reader rather than at the characters. Things leave marks. Marks can be read. A well-constructed lie is still required to account for them.

The two episodes also differ in who was deceived. Jacob, a prophet, examined the shirt and refused the story. The ʿAzīz's household examined the shirt and accepted it. The difference is not intelligence but interest: the father had no motive to disbelieve his sons beyond the evidence, while the husband had every motive to avoid a scandal and still arrived at the correct finding. That is the strength of a physical test. It produces the same answer for a man who wants one result as for a man who wants another, and it does so quickly enough that the parties cannot reorganise their accounts around it. The chapter gives its most decisive verdict to a piece of cloth for exactly that reason.

## Verse 12:27

> But if it is torn from the back, then she has lied and he is truthful.”

**Two Verbs, Two Persons, One Sentence**

The branch is stated with maximum compression: *she has lied, and he is truthful*. The woman is assigned the verb of falsehood; the man is assigned the quality of truth. The asymmetry between the two halves is worth noting. She is described by an act she performed — she lied — while he is described by a standing attribute — he is a truthful person. A lie is an event; truthfulness is a character. The witness therefore does more than settle the case.

**A Test Anyone in the Room Could Have Applied**

No expertise is required to look at a shirt. The witness proposes a procedure that the husband, the servants, the woman herself and Joseph could each perform, and whose result none of them could dispute afterwards. That property is what makes the verse important beyond the scene. An adjudication that depends on a specialist or on an authority can be captured by whoever controls the specialist. An adjudication that depends on an object in plain view cannot. The chapter returns to this idea at its climax, when Joseph refuses release until the king examines the case of the women who cut their hands (12:50) — again insisting on a public inspection of facts rather than a private assertion of innocence. Joseph's method throughout is to move disputes onto ground where evidence, not rank, decides.

**"He Is Truthful" Is a Description the Chapter Will Reuse**

The word applied to Joseph here returns twice, both times in the mouths of people who had no reason to favour him. The cupbearer, sent back to a prison he had left years earlier, addresses him with it: *“Joseph, O man of truth!”* (12:46). The ʿAzīz's wife, confessing to the king with nothing left to protect, closes her statement with it: *“Now the truth has come to light. It was I who tried to seduce him, and he is surely truthful”* (12:51). Three attestations, from a family witness, a fellow prisoner and the woman who accused him, spanning years and separated by every difference of interest. The chapter is assembling a record. By the time Joseph is released, his truthfulness is not his own claim but a fact established by hostile and indifferent witnesses, which is the only form such a claim can usefully take.

**The Accuser Becomes the Accused in Fifteen Words**

Verse 25 gave the woman the initiative: she spoke first, framed the question, proposed the penalties. Verse 27 removes all of it. The branch that applies is the one in which she lied, and the man she prosecuted is described as truthful. No further argument is offered and none is needed, because the next verse reports only that the husband looked at the shirt. The reversal is total and it happens without anyone raising their voice. This is the sūrah's characteristic movement — a position built elaborately over several verses and then dismantled by a single detail — and it is the first time in the Egyptian section that the movement runs in Joseph's favour.

The branch requires very little, and that is the point. The witness does not interrogate the woman, does not ask Joseph to swear, does not call servants or search the house. He proposes one observation and two outcomes, and the case ends when the observation is made. The Qur'an's interest in the episode is therefore not in the drama of the accusation but in the sufficiency of the evidence. A household that had every structural reason to convict an innocent slave is prevented from doing so by a tear in a garment, and the prevention works because the test was proposed before the inspection rather than after it. Procedure, not goodwill, is what saves him.

The husband now states his own conclusion.

## Verse 12:28

> So when her husband saw that Joseph’s shirt was torn from the back, he said ˹to her˺, “This must be ˹an example˺ of the cunning of you ˹women˺! Indeed, your cunning is so shrewd

**He Looks for Himself Before He Speaks**

The verse opens with an act of verification. *Fa-lammā raʾā* — when he saw. The husband does not accept the witness's report at second hand, does not ask a servant to examine the garment and does not defer to anyone's opinion. He inspects the shirt with his own eyes and only then pronounces. That sequence is the reverse of verse 25, where his wife had already delivered a verdict before any inspection took place. The chapter is contrasting two ways of reaching a conclusion in a household dispute: one that begins with a claim and looks for support, and one that begins with an object and ends with a claim.

**Kayd Is the Word This Chapter Thinks With**

The term rendered *cunning* here is *kayd*, and it is one of the sūrah's organising words. It names the brothers' plot against Joseph (12:5), the women's stratagem in this verse, Joseph's prayer that God turn their cunning away (12:33) and God's answer that He did so (12:34), and Joseph's later reference to his Lord's knowledge of their cunning (12:50). The chapter closes with the same vocabulary applied to the brothers' original conspiracy (12:102). Kayd is not a moral evaluation in itself; it is a description of concealed planning, and the sūrah uses it for the malicious and the protective alike. What the word tracks is the fact that every party in this story operates by arrangement rather than by openness, and that the arrangements are visible to God in every case.

**From One Woman to a Class**

The husband's exclamation generalises immediately: this is of the cunning of *you women*, and your cunning is great. A single act by a single person becomes a statement about a category. The move is rhetorically ordinary and analytically empty, and the chapter does not let it stand unexamined. The women of the city who gossip about her in verse 30 are not cunning in this sense; they are merely indiscreet. The women of verse 51 testify against her and in Joseph's favour, saying *“Allah forbid! We know nothing indecent about him.”* (12:51) The class accusation of verse 28 is therefore contradicted by the behaviour of the same class later in the sūrah, and the contradiction is left in the text. What the verse records is how a man in that position speaks, not how things are.

**He Admires the Method Instead of Condemning the Act**

*Inna kaydakunna ʿaẓīm* — your cunning is immense. The adjective is one of scale, not of blame. He does not say the act was vile, does not mention the indecency she attempted, and does not refer to the false accusation she made against a man in his own house. He remarks on the sophistication. That choice of emphasis tells the reader what this household values: competence in manipulation ranks above honesty in conduct. The observation is not a caricature. Verse 29 will show the same priorities in action, when the response to a proven attempt at seduction and a proven false accusation is an instruction to keep quiet and a suggestion that the offender seek forgiveness privately.

**What the Verse Does Not Contain**

No punishment is announced. No vindication of Joseph is offered to anyone outside the room. No inquiry is opened into how the situation arose, why the doors were locked, or why a purchased slave was left alone with the wife of the house. The husband's speech ends with an exclamation and verse 29 begins with damage control. The absence is the verse's most informative feature, because it explains everything that follows: the gossip of verse 30 could not be suppressed by a settlement that never addressed the facts, and the imprisonment of verse 35 could be decided by people who had already seen the proofs and preferred not to act on them. A household that responds to a crime with admiration for its technique will not protect the victim of it.

## Verse 12:29

> O Joseph! Forget about this. And you ˹O wife˺! Seek forgiveness for your sin. It certainly has been your fault.”

**The Sin Is Named**

*Istaghfirī li-dhanbik* — seek forgiveness for your sin. The word *dhanb* is unambiguous, and its use here matters because it is spoken by the husband. The household's own head has classified the act as a sin, in her hearing, in Joseph's. That classification is never withdrawn anywhere in the sūrah, and it is never made public either. The result is a private admission and a public silence, which is the exact structure that will fail in the next verse. It is also why Joseph's imprisonment in verse 35 is so difficult to justify even inside the story: the man who decided it had already used the word *sin* about the accuser and told the accused to say nothing.

**Why the Settlement Collapses Immediately**

Verse 30 opens with the women of the city already talking. The instruction to conceal has failed before the next verse begins, and the sūrah does not explain how. The commentators note that the news spread despite the command, and the reason is not difficult to supply: a banquet with locked doors, a torn garment, a summoned witness and a raised voice in a minister's house are not events that stay inside a house. The collapse of verse 29 is the chapter's demonstration that reputation cannot be managed by instruction. What the husband tried to settle by telling two people what to do is settled instead by what the city decides to say, and the saying will force the woman into a public performance in verse 31 that she cannot afterwards withdraw.

**The Man Addressed First Is the One Who Did Nothing**

The order of the two commands is the verse's final observation. Joseph is spoken to first, and what he is asked for is the harder thing. Keeping silent about an injustice done to you, in the house of the person who did it, with no prospect of redress, is a discipline the Qur'an elsewhere associates with the prophets and calls beautiful patience. The woman is asked only for an inward act she can perform in private. The imbalance is not corrected in this verse; it is corrected much later, when Joseph's silence ends — not with an accusation but with a request that the king examine the case publicly (12:50). He does exactly what he was told not to do, at the moment when doing it serves the truth rather than himself, and the sūrah treats that delay as part of his character rather than a failure of it.

## Verse 12:30

> Some women of the city gossiped, “The Chief Minister’s wife is trying to seduce her slave-boy. Love for him has plagued her heart. Indeed, we see that she is clearly mistaken.”

**The Instruction to Conceal Lasts One Verse**

Verse 29 closed with a settlement: Joseph to be silent, the wife to seek forgiveness, the matter to end. Verse 30 opens with the matter being discussed across a city. Nothing in the text describes the leak. No servant is named, no rumour is traced, and no explanation is offered for how a scene confined to a locked room became public property. The chapter simply cuts from the household to the street, which is how gossip actually behaves — it does not arrive through a narrated channel, it is discovered already in circulation. The contrast of the two verses is the sūrah's comment on the husband's method. Reputation is not a possession that can be secured by instruction, and the man who tried to close the case by telling two people what to say has no control over what a city says.

**"Some Women of the City" Locates the Speakers by Class**

The phrase is *niswatun mina al-madīnah* — women from the city, a partitive construction indicating a group within a larger population rather than the population itself. The commentators identify them as the wives of notables, and the following verses confirm the identification: she invites them to a banquet, they come, and their judgement carries weight enough that she feels compelled to answer it. It is the conversation of the class that sets opinion among the households of the ruling establishment, which is why the woman's response in verse 31 takes the form of a reception rather than a denial.

**What They Say Is Accurate**

The content of the gossip deserves notice because it is true. She has been attempting to seduce her slave-boy, and the verb they use is the same one the narrator used in verse 23 and Joseph used in his defence in verse 26. The city's account matches the evidence exactly. That fact complicates the usual treatment of gossip in moral instruction, and the chapter does not resolve the complication. Speech that is accurate can still be damaging, and it damages here: the woman's response is a public demonstration that will end with a man in prison. The sūrah records the accuracy without endorsing the circulation, and in doing so it distinguishes between the truth of a statement and the rightness of repeating it — a distinction the household in verse 29 tried to make in the opposite direction and failed.

**"Clearly Mistaken" Is the Brothers' Phrase**

The women's verdict is *fī ḍalāl mubīn*, in manifest error — the identical formula the brothers used about their father in verse 8: *“Indeed, our father is clearly mistaken.”* (12:8) The parallel is exact and it is not accidental. In both cases a group observes an intense attachment to Joseph, judges the attachment to be a deviation, and says so in the vocabulary of religion. Jacob was called misguided for loving his son too much; the ʿAzīz's wife is called misguided for desiring a young man too much. The chapter is tracking a pattern: love of Joseph produces an accusation of error from bystanders, and in both cases the bystanders are wrong about the nature of what they are seeing. Jacob's love was prophetic insight. The woman's love will become, in the end, the occasion of a public confession that clears Joseph's name (12:51).

## Verse 12:31

> When she heard about their gossip, she invited them and set a banquet for them. She gave each one a knife, then said ˹to Joseph˺, “Come out before them.” When they saw him, they were so stunned ˹by his beauty˺ that they cut their hands, and exclaimed, “Good God! This cannot be human; this must be a noble angel!”

**The Beauty of Joseph Is a Reported Fact, Not a Described One**

The Qur'an never describes Joseph's appearance. No adjective for his face, no comparison, no measurement. What the text supplies instead is the effect of his appearance on other people, and it does so repeatedly. The tradition adds a statement of its own: the Prophet ﷺ reported meeting Joseph in the third heaven during the Night Journey (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 164a), and the narrations of that journey describe him as having been given half of beauty — understood by the commentators either as half the beauty that exists among people, or as half of a beauty whose remainder belongs to someone the speaker excluded from the comparison out of humility. The chapter's method is more economical than any description: it shows a room of women cutting their own hands and calling him an angel, and lets the reader infer what it declines to state.

**The Scene Is Constructed, and the Construction Is the Point**

What the woman staged is a demonstration of her own excuse, and it works so well that she says so in the next verse. But the demonstration has a second result she did not arrange: it establishes Joseph's innocence before the entire female establishment of the city, in a room she filled and a moment she chose. The chapter allows her plan to succeed at what she intended and to fail at what she could not control. This is the same structure that governs the brothers' plot — a scheme that achieves its immediate object while producing an outcome its authors never contemplated — and it is why the sūrah can describe concealment and manipulation as real forces without ever treating them as decisive. The knives were hers. The hands were theirs. What was proved in the room was not what she had planned to prove.

The wounds are not forgotten afterwards. When Joseph is finally summoned out of the prison, he refuses to leave until the case is examined, and the case he names is this one: *“Go back to your master and ask him about the case of the women who cut their hands. Surely my Lord has ˹full˺ knowledge of their cunning”* (12:50). The evidence he asks the king to investigate is not the torn shirt or the witness but the banquet, because that is where the city's own women testified against themselves with their hands. The reports describe the fruit they were given as citrons, which required a knife and attention; the verse records only the cutting. Either way the scene produces a group of witnesses who cannot afterwards claim that the woman's desire was unfounded or that Joseph was a man capable of the act he was accused of, and it is that group Joseph sends the king to.

## Verse 12:32

> She said, “This is the one for whose love you criticized me! I did try to seduce him but he ˹firmly˺ refused. And if he does not do what I order him to, he will certainly be imprisoned and ˹fully˺ disgraced.”

**"He Refused" Is the Chapter's Verdict, Spoken by the Accuser**

The word for his refusal carries the sense of holding oneself back, of abstention maintained under pressure. Coming from her mouth it is the strongest available testimony, because she is the only person in the room who knows what happened behind the locked doors. The husband's finding in verse 28 rested on a torn shirt. Her statement rests on experience. The chapter thus accumulates its proof of Joseph's innocence from every interested party in turn: the family witness, the master of the house, the women of the city, and finally the woman herself. By the end of verse 32 nobody in Egypt who has spoken about the matter has said anything against him, and the only accusation ever made has been formally withdrawn by its author in public.

**The Threat Names Two Punishments**

*He will certainly be imprisoned and disgraced.* The two are joined, and the second is the more serious in a household of this kind. Imprisonment is a location; disgrace is a status, and for a slave whose standing rests entirely on the confidence of his master, the loss of it is the loss of everything. The threat also reveals what she now wants. Verse 25 sought his punishment as revenge; verse 32 seeks his compliance through the prospect of punishment. The demand is unchanged — do what I order — and the instrument has been switched from prosecution to intimidation. The commentators note that the command refers back to the original summons of verse 23, so the campaign described by the persistent verb in that verse is still running, now with the whole city's female establishment as its audience.

**A Public Setting, A Private Demand**

The scene has moved from a locked room to a banquet hall, and the demand has not changed with it. That is the verse's structural irony. Everything that made the earlier encounter wrongful — seclusion, concealment, the absence of witnesses — has been removed, and what remains is the same instruction, now issued openly and backed by an open threat. The chapter is showing that the wrongness of the proposal was never a matter of setting. Publicity does not legitimise it; consensus does not legitimise it; the fact that everyone in the room agrees does not legitimise it. Joseph will answer accordingly, addressing not the room but God, and the sūrah will treat his refusal as the only sane response available to anyone in that hall.

The second half of her threat deserves attention on its own. *Disgraced* renders a word meaning small, humbled, brought low — the state of a person stripped of standing and made aware of it. The Qur'an reserves the same term for the final condition of the arrogant: *“Surely those who are too proud to worship Me will enter Hell, fully humbled”* (40:60). A woman threatening a slave with humiliation is using the vocabulary the Book applies to the ultimate humiliation of those who refused God. The inversion is complete and the chapter will perform it. The man threatened with disgrace leaves the prison to be told he is esteemed and trusted (12:54), asks for the storehouses of the country (12:55), and ends up holding the position of the man who once owned him (12:78). Nothing she threatened materialises, and everything she commanded is refused.

## Verse 12:33

> Joseph prayed, “My Lord! I would rather be in jail than do what they invite me to. And if You do not turn their cunning away from me, I might yield to them and fall into ignorance.”

**He Answers a Room by Addressing God**

The demand came from a woman, backed by her guests, with a threat attached. Joseph's reply is not to any of them. *Rabbi* — my Lord — opens the sentence and the rest of it is spoken upward. There is no argument with the threat, no negotiation about conditions, no appeal to the guests' judgement, no reference to the finding of his innocence two verses earlier. He bypasses the entire room. The move is not evasion; it is an accurate assessment of the situation. Nothing said in that hall could have changed what was being demanded, and the only party capable of changing it was not present in it. The chapter records the prayer in full, which makes the verse a model of where to direct a request when every human avenue has been closed by people who control them.

**"Fall Into Ignorance" Uses Sin's Own Vocabulary**

The word he chooses for what he fears becoming is *jāhilīn*, the ignorant — the plural of the term the Qur'an uses for those who act foolishly or sinfully. The idiom is established elsewhere in the Book: repentance is promised to those *“who commit evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺ then repent soon after”* (4:17), where the ignorance named is not absence of information but the condition of a person acting against what he knows. Joseph has just been vindicated as a truthful man and has heard the household admit that his accuser sinned. He knows precisely what is being proposed. What he fears is the state in which knowledge stops governing conduct, and he names it accurately. The prayer is therefore not a request for information but for the maintenance of a disposition.

**A Prayer Answered in the Next Verse**

The structure of the exchange is exact. Joseph asks that God turn their cunning away; verse 34 reports that his Lord answered him and turned their cunning away, using the same verb and the same object. The general law behind the exchange is stated elsewhere without ambiguity: *“Your Lord has proclaimed, ‘Call upon Me, I will respond to you’”* (40:60). What is unusual here is the form the response takes. The cunning is not turned away by removing the demand, by softening the woman's heart or by opening a door. It is turned away by making prison acceptable to him, which is a change in the man rather than in his circumstances. The sūrah treats that as a genuine answer, and in doing so it defines divine assistance in a way the reader is unlikely to have expected: help arrived as an increased capacity to accept a worse outcome.

The shape of the prayer is worth comparing with another from the same family of stories. Moses, after the blow that killed a man, says three things in one sentence: *“My Lord! I have definitely wronged my soul, so forgive me.”* (28:16), and the verse reports the forgiveness in the clause that follows. Joseph's prayer is equally compact but has a different object. Moses asks for a past act to be erased; Joseph asks for a future act to be prevented. Neither prayer contains a preamble, an explanation of circumstances or a list of merits, and both are answered within a verse or two. The chapter's model of supplication is therefore minimal in form and specific in content: one named danger, one request for intervention, no elaboration. That economy is what makes the prayer usable. A reader can hold it in mind and repeat it under pressure, which is presumably why the Qur'an reproduces it in full rather than summarising it.

## Verse 12:34

> So his Lord responded to him, turning their cunning away from him. Surely He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing

**The Answer Uses the Words of the Request**

The verse opens with *fa-stajāba lahu rabbuh*, so his Lord responded to him. The verb is the tenth form of the root for answering — a response given to a request, not a general act of favour — and it is followed by the very phrase Joseph used: turning away their cunning from him. The correspondence is deliberate and complete. He asked for one specific thing in one specific form and the narrative reports the granting of that thing in the same terms. Nothing is added, nothing is substituted, and no alternative blessing is offered in place of the one requested. The Qur'an rarely documents a supplication and its answer in adjacent verses with this precision, and when it does the pairing is meant to be read as evidence of the general promise stated at 40:60, that the Lord responds to those who call on Him.

**An Answer That Grants the Worse of Two Options**

Joseph asked that the cunning be turned away, and what he received was prison. The verse does not treat that as a partial answer or a delayed one. It reports the response as complete, and the following verse reports the imprisonment as the outcome. The theology here is precise and uncomfortable: God's answer to a request for protection can consist of placing the petitioner inside the harm he feared, in a condition where the harm no longer does what it was meant to do. The prison does not seduce him, does not disgrace him in his own estimation, and does not separate him from God. It is still a prison, and the chapter will keep him in it for years (12:42). Deliverance in this verse means the plot's failure, not the prisoner's release.

**What the Verse Establishes for the Rest of the Chapter**

Everything after this point depends on the fact recorded here. Joseph enters the prison as a man whose resistance has been divinely secured, which is why the sūrah can put him in a cell for years without worrying about what the cell will do to him. It also fixes the interpretation of the earlier temptation scene: he did not escape it by strength of character, and the text has now said so twice, once in verse 24 and once here. The chapter's portrait of prophetic virtue is therefore not a portrait of capacity. It is a portrait of dependence exercised consistently, at the moment of maximum pressure, in the direction of the only party able to help. That is the lesson the sūrah wants from the Egyptian section, and verse 34 is where it is stated.

The answer's cost is recorded in the next two verses and should not be softened. Joseph goes to prison and stays there. Verse 42 states the length: *so he remained in prison for several years*, the consequence of one man's forgetfulness rather than of any new decision about him. The prayer of verse 33 was granted in full and the grant did not shorten his sentence by a day. Readers expecting divine intervention to look like release will find this verse difficult, and the difficulty is intentional. What was promised was protection from the cunning, and the cunning was defeated; what was not promised was comfort. Joseph's own summary of the whole episode, spoken from a throne at the end of the chapter, mentions the prison as an act of kindness: *“He was truly kind to me when He freed me from prison”* (12:100). The kindness he identifies is the release, not the years.

## Verse 12:35

> And so it occurred to those in charge, despite seeing all the proofs ˹of his innocence˺, that he should be imprisoned for a while

**"For a While" Leaves the Term Undefined**

*Ḥattā ḥīn* — until a time. The reports explain the intention: imprisonment as a temporary measure, long enough for the rumours about the ʿAzīz's wife to die down. The reasoning is intelligible and entirely self-serving. A slave cleared of a charge cannot simply be restored to his position in the household without keeping the story alive, and removing him from view allows the gossip to exhaust itself. The phrase conceals the duration from everyone, including Joseph, and the chapter will not resolve it for years. Verse 42 records the outcome of the indefinite term: the man who should have secured his release forgot, *so he remained in prison for several years*. A measure adopted for a while becomes the longest single period of Joseph's life in the narrative.

**The Prison Is Where the Chapter Turns**

The decision looks like the defeat of the story's hero and functions as its mechanism. Inside the prison Joseph meets two servants of the king, interprets their dreams, and by this establishes the reputation that will reach the palace when the king has a dream of his own (12:36–49). Every subsequent development — the release, the exoneration, the appointment to the storehouses, the arrival of his brothers in a famine — runs through the cell. The household's attempt to remove Joseph from sight places him in the one institution in Egypt connected to the king's own household. The chapter does not remark on this. It reports the imprisonment in a single verse and lets the next verse introduce two young men entering the same door, which is how the sūrah handles providence throughout: the arrangement is visible in the sequence, never in the commentary.

The decision also illustrates a technique that has not changed. A scandal involving a powerful household can be ended in two ways: by punishing the offender or by removing the person who reminds people of the offence. The first requires admitting the offence, which the settlement of verse 29 was designed to avoid. The second requires only that the reminder disappear. Joseph is the reminder — his presence in the house, his cleared name, the torn shirt everyone has heard about — and the household therefore solves the problem it created by disposing of the evidence that it created it. The verse is careful to say that the proofs were seen before the decision was taken, because the technique depends on the decision-maker knowing the truth. Ignorance would make the imprisonment an error. Knowledge makes it a method.

## Verse 12:36

> And two other servants went to jail with Joseph. One of them said, “I dreamt I was pressing wine.” The other said, “I dreamt I was carrying ˹some˺ bread on my head, from which birds were eating.” ˹Then both said,˺ “Tell us their interpretation, for we surely see you as one of the good-doers.”

**Two Servants of the King, Imprisoned for a Different Reason**

The word for the two men is *fatayān*, young men or servants, and the commentaries identify them as members of the king's household — one his cupbearer, the other his baker. The reported occasion is a plot to poison the king: the baker was caught and imprisoned, and the cupbearer was imprisoned because the king suspected him of complicity. The biblical account preserves the same two offices and the same setting, with the pair committed to custody in the place where Joseph was confined (Genesis 40:1–3). The Qur'an gives no reason for their arrest at all, which is characteristic of its handling of everything except Joseph. What matters for the story is not why they are there but that they belong to the royal household, and that fact is the hinge on which the entire second half of the chapter turns.

**The Verse Opens the Second Movement of the Story**

Structurally, verse 36 closes the Egyptian household and opens the prison. The seduction arc has ended with an innocent man in a cell and his accusers unpunished; the elevation arc begins with two royal servants asking him a question. The gift promised by his father in verse 6 and stated as the purpose of his establishment in verse 21 is now about to be exercised for the first time in public. The chapter has been careful to establish that this gift is a divine teaching rather than an acquired technique, and equally careful to establish that Joseph's character survived the test that preceded it. Both qualifications are needed for what comes next, because the interpretation he is about to give will decide two men's fates and, through one of them, the fate of a kingdom.

The Qur'an's account differs from the biblical one in ways that are worth noting. Genesis 40 supplies the king's anger, the custody of the two officials under the captain of the guard, Joseph's own dejection at their appearance, and his complaint that nobody has remembered him. This verse supplies two men, two dreams and one request. The emotional interior of the prisoners is not given, the reason for their arrest is not given, and Joseph's state of mind is not given either. What is given instead is their assessment of him, in the final clause, and that assessment does the work all the omitted detail would have done. The chapter's method of characterisation is consistently external: it tells the reader what a person is by reporting what others say about him after long acquaintance, which is the only evidence that carries weight in a story about false accounts.

## Verse 12:37

> Joseph replied, “I can even tell you what kind of meal you will be served before you receive it. This ˹knowledge˺ is from what my Lord has taught me. I have shunned the faith of a people who disbelieve in Allah and deny the Hereafter

**He Answers a Larger Question Than the One Asked**

Two men have asked for an interpretation of two dreams. Joseph's reply begins with a claim about knowledge in general, and the interpretation itself is not delivered until verse 41, four verses later. The delay is not evasion. He is establishing the standing of the thing he is about to give them, because an interpretation delivered by a slave with no credentials would be received as guesswork, and guesswork about a man's fate is worse than silence. The method is deliberate and it is his method throughout: state the ground of the knowledge, then supply the knowledge. The same order will appear when he addresses the king's dream, where the answer is preceded by a plan for the country rather than followed by one (12:47–49).

**"This Is From What My Lord Has Taught Me"**

The attribution is immediate and unqualified. Nothing in the sentence suggests natural aptitude, long study or inherited technique. The knowledge is *mimmā ʿallamanī rabbī*, from what my Lord taught me — a portion of a teaching whose source is named. That connects this verse to two earlier statements: his father's promise that God would teach him the interpretation of dreams (12:6), and the narrator's declaration that God established him in Egypt precisely so that He might teach him it (12:21). The prison is therefore not an interruption of that education but its setting. Joseph's insistence on the source also does practical work in the room: a slave claiming foreknowledge invites a charge of sorcery, and locating the knowledge in God's teaching is the only defence available to him.

**Two Denials Named Together**

The people whose creed he has left are described by two negatives: they do not believe in God, and they are deniers of the Hereafter. The pairing is the Qur'an's standard account of unbelief, and the order matters. Denial of the last day is presented not as a separate error but as a consequence of the first, since a person who acknowledges a Lord is expected to acknowledge an accounting. The two denials also define the two things Joseph's own conduct has depended on. He refused an offer with immediate rewards and immediate risks because he believed in a God who sees and a judgement that follows (12:23–24). He accepted prison because he preferred a later outcome to a present one (12:33). The creed he renounces is the exact inverse of the creed that carried him through the last thirteen verses, and the verse asks two prisoners to notice the difference.

The distinction Joseph is drawing has a practical edge in an ancient setting. Knowledge of the future was a professional industry in Egypt and elsewhere, and the ordinary suppliers of it were diviners, astrologers and soothsayers whose authority rested on technique and secrecy. A prisoner who announces beforehand what food will arrive is making exactly the kind of claim such people made, and the line between prophecy and divination is not self-evident to an audience. Joseph draws it by naming the teacher. What separates his foreknowledge from theirs is not accuracy but source: the diviner's claim rests on his own art, while his rests on a revelation he received and did not produce. The verse therefore performs two acts at once — it establishes a credential and it refuses a profession, and the refusal is what makes the credential acceptable to a believer.

## Verse 12:38

> I follow the faith of my fathers: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. It is not ˹right˺ for us to associate anything with Allah ˹in worship˺. This is part of Allah’s grace upon us and humanity, but most people are not grateful

**"The Creed of My Fathers" Claims Continuity, Not Innovation**

The word *millah* denotes a religious way held in common across a community and transmitted through it. Joseph does not present his belief as something he arrived at, discovered or was granted uniquely. He presents it as inherited, and he names the chain of inheritance. That has an argumentative function in a prison and a larger one in the sūrah. In a prison it answers the obvious objection to a slave preaching to royal servants: he is not introducing a novelty but reporting a tradition older than the Egyptian state. In the sūrah it links the Egyptian section to the Canaanite one, so that the man who refused the ʿAzīz's wife and the boy who dreamed of eleven stars are shown to be operating from a single continuous conviction. The Qur'an names Joseph among the descendants given to Abraham at 6:84, which is the same lineage stated here from the other direction.

**"But Most People Are Not Grateful" Closes the Sermon**

The refrain is one the Qur'an uses repeatedly and in nearly identical form: most of mankind are not grateful, most of mankind do not know. Within this sūrah the same observation closes verse 21 (*most people do not know*) and will close verse 40 in the same terms. The repetition is not filler. Each instance marks a point where a benefit has been conferred and the conferral has gone unrecognised — Joseph established in Egypt, the creed of the prophets delivered to a prison, the upright religion stated plainly. Gratitude here means recognising a favour as a favour, and its absence is what the chapter diagnoses in every party that mishandles Joseph: the brothers who could not see a father's love as anything but a distribution, and the household that could not see an innocent man as anything but an inconvenience.

**The First Public Exercise of His Office**

Verse 38 is the first time Joseph speaks as a prophet to people outside his family, and the setting is the least advantageous available. He has no pulpit, no congregation, no freedom and no future; his audience consists of two men awaiting execution or dismissal, one of whom will forget him entirely (12:42). Nothing he says in this sermon can improve his position, and the sūrah takes care to establish that. The call to God's oneness is delivered where delivery has no price and no reward, which is the standard the Qur'an applies to prophetic speech throughout: the messenger asks nothing for it. What Joseph gains from these four verses is nothing in Egypt. What the chapter gains is a record that his conviction did not depend on his circumstances.

There is a further resonance for the sūrah's first listeners. The same three names appear in the Qur'an's account of Jacob's deathbed, where his children answer their father's question about what they will worship after him: *“We will ˹continue to˺ worship your God, the God of your forefathers— Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac—the One God. And to Him we ˹all˺ submit”* (2:133). That answer is given by the whole family together, including the sons who threw Joseph into a well. Here the identical genealogy is recited by the son they threw, alone, in a foreign prison, to two strangers. The creed survives the family's failure and travels further than the family did. Joseph states in Egypt what his brothers stated in Canaan, and the statement is unchanged by everything that happened in between.

## Verse 12:39

> O my fellow-prisoners! Which is far better: many different lords or Allah—the One, the Supreme

**Many Lords Against One**

The alternatives are stated with exact symmetry. *Arbābun mutafarriqūn* — lords many, dispersed, at odds with one another; the adjective carries the sense of scattered and divided, not merely many. Against them: *Allāhu al-wāḥid al-qahhār*. The comparison is therefore not between one and many in arithmetic but between coherence and fragmentation. Al-Rāzī reads the argument as the standard Qur'anic one from cosmic order: if there were multiple gods, the order of the world would show the fissures that multiple authorities always produce. The Qur'an states the same reasoning directly: *“Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted”* (21:22). Joseph's question invites the prisoners to apply that reasoning to the pantheon they live inside, where every temple holds a different lord and no two agree.

**The Argument Begins With Lordship, Not Existence**

Joseph does not attempt to persuade his hearers that God exists. They are not atheists; they are servants of a king in a country full of temples, and the question of whether divine reality exists would have been meaningless to them. The dispute is about number, quality and allegiance — how many lords there are and whether they are at odds. Starting there is a lesson in method that the Qur'an follows with its own audience in Mecca, who likewise acknowledged a supreme creator while associating others with Him. The argument that such people require is not a proof of God but a comparison of gods, and the comparison is settled by asking which arrangement is better. Verse 39 is the whole of that argument, in one sentence, addressed to two men who would otherwise have spent the night discussing their sentences.

**What the Question Sets Up in the Next Verse**

The comparative form leaves an opening that verse 40 fills. Once the hearers have agreed — or been unable to deny — that one supreme Lord is better than many dispersed ones, the status of the many has to be accounted for, and the accounting is devastating: they are names. The two verses form a single argument in two movements, the first establishing that unity is superior and the second showing that the alternatives have no existence to compare. That the argument is delivered by a prisoner to two other prisoners, in the middle of a chapter about a family's betrayal, is the sūrah's way of insisting that its subject is not the family. The story carries a creed, and the creed is stated where nobody in it has anything to gain.

The comparative interrogative is also the Qur'an's habitual form for this argument, and the pattern is visible elsewhere. The sūrah of Thunder instructs the Prophet to put a series of the same shape to his audience: *“Say, ‘Can the blind and the sighted be equal? Or can darkness and light be equal?’”* (13:16). The questions are not seeking information; they are forcing a ranking the hearer cannot refuse, and once the ranking is given the conclusion follows without further argument. Joseph's question works identically. Nobody can rank dispersed lords above a single supreme one and mean it, so the question is not really open. Its function is to make the hearers say the conclusion themselves, which produces conviction of a different kind from being told. Two men in a cell are led to pronounce a judgement on the religion of the country that imprisoned them, in their own words, to a fellow prisoner.

## Verse 12:40

> Whatever ˹idols˺ you worship instead of Him are mere names which you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized. It is only Allah Who decides. He has commanded that you worship none but Him. That is the upright faith, but most people do not know

**The Same Formula Appears in Two Other Chapters**

The Qur'an uses this exact construction twice more, and both times in the mouth of a prophet confronting an idolatrous people. Hūd says to his community: *“Do you dispute with me regarding the so-called gods which you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized?”* (7:71). The sūrah of the Star says of the pagan deities: *“These ˹idols˺ are mere names that you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized”* (53:23). The verbal identity across three chapters indicates a settled formula rather than a coincidental phrase, and its recurrence places Joseph's prison sermon inside the continuous argument of the prophets. What he says to two servants in an Egyptian cell is what every messenger in the Book has said to every idolatrous nation, in the same words.

**Judgement Belongs to God Alone**

*In al-ḥukmu illā li-Llāh* — the judgement is God's, and no one else's. The exclusive construction is emphatic: not shared, not delegated, not distributed among lords. The verse then moves from judgement to command, and the command is the same one stated elsewhere as a decree: *“For your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him”* (17:23). The sequence is significant. Authority to judge is located in God, and the first exercise of that authority is the command to worship Him alone, so legislation and worship are presented as a single matter rather than two. The household that imprisoned Joseph exercised judgement without authority and reached a decision against the evidence. The verse answers that scene by naming where judgement actually resides.

**"The Upright Faith, But Most People Do Not Know"**

*Al-dīn al-qayyim* — the religion that is straight, upright, self-sustaining. The same description is used of the natural way God has instilled in people: *“So be steadfast in faith in all uprightness ˹O Prophet˺—the natural Way of Allah which He has instilled in ˹all˺ people... That is the Straight Way, but most people do not know”* (30:30). The closing clause of verse 40 is the sūrah's refrain, appearing here for the second time after verse 21 and echoed later at verse 68. It marks the distance between what has been stated plainly and what has been received. Joseph has delivered the argument in four verses, in a prison, to two men. The argument is complete and correct, and the number of people who know it remains small. The verse ends where the chapter began — with clarity offered and understanding not guaranteed.

The sermon as a whole is worth measuring against its setting. Four verses, spoken by a slave to two prisoners, containing a renunciation of the state religion, a genealogy, an argument from cosmic order, a reduction of the pantheon to names, a claim about the location of judgement and a statement of the upright faith. Nothing in it responds to Joseph's own case. He does not mention the seduction, the shirt, the verdict, the banquet or his sentence, and he asks his hearers for nothing — not sympathy, not a message to the king, not help. The two men came to him for a service and received a creed, and the interpretation they asked for is postponed until verse 41. The ordering tells the reader what Joseph considers urgent. Their dreams will be answered, but the answer is not the important thing in the room, and the sūrah arranges the sequence so that nobody can mistake the priority.

## Verse 12:41

> “O my fellow-prisoners! ˹The first˺ one of you will serve wine to his master, and the other will be crucified and the birds will eat from his head. The matter about which you inquired has been decided.”

**Crucifixion in the Egyptian Setting**

The punishment named is *ṣalb*, exposure of the body after execution, which in the ancient Near East served as a public warning rather than merely as a method of killing. The detail that birds will eat from the head specifies that the body will not be buried and will remain where scavengers can reach it. The biblical account preserves the same expectation, with the baker's dream interpreted as execution and his body hung on a tree (Genesis 40:18–19). The Qur'an supplies no legal discussion of the penalty and no comment on the justice of the sentence; the crime, on the transmitted reports, was an attempt on the king's life. What the verse records is the interpretation, not an evaluation of Egyptian law. Egyptian practice is known to have used review display of an executed criminal as a public deterrent, and the wording here fits that setting without passing judgement on it. What carries weight in the chapter is the symmetry of the two sentences: both are delivered in the same voice, in the same breath, to two men who have just been told opposite fates, and neither is softened.

**Why He Answers After Preaching**

The order of verses 37 to 41 is deliberate. Joseph spends four verses on the oneness of God, on the creed of his fathers, on the comparison between many lords and one, and on the emptiness of names invented by ancestors. Only then does he interpret. Nothing in the sermon was a condition he imposed, and nothing in it was designed to embarrass his hearers into compliance. What the sequence establishes is the standing of the interpreter. A man who has just told two royal servants that their country's religion is a set of invented names is not a man whose prediction they can dismiss as the flattery of a prisoner. The creed gives the interpretation its authority, and the interpretation then confirms the creed by coming true.

**The Fulfilment Is Barely Reported**

The chapter does not dramatise the outcome. Verse 42 identifies one of the two as the man Joseph knew would survive, and verse 45 shows him back at court years later; the other is never mentioned again. No execution scene, no burial, no reaction from the king, no moral drawn. The sūrah's silence is consistent with its method throughout — the Egyptian section reports consequences rather than staging them. What the silence achieves is a certain weight. Two lives were disposed of in a single sentence spoken in a cell, and the narrative moves on, which is how the reader experiences the ordinary operation of decree. The interpretation was accurate, the men knew it was accurate, and only one of them lived to remember it. That asymmetry drives everything after it. A dead man cannot repeat a promise, so the record of Joseph's accuracy passes to the one survivor, and it stays with him, unspoken, through the whole of the years of plenty. The chapter is exact about the price of being right: it produces a witness, and witnesses endure by chance rather than by design.

## Verse 12:42

> Then he said to the one he knew would survive, “Mention me in the presence of your master.” But Satan made him forget to mention Joseph to his master, so he remained in prison for several years

**The Only Thing Joseph Asks for Himself in the Whole Chapter**

Across a hundred verses Joseph makes few requests. He asks God to turn a scheme away from him (12:33), he asks to be put in charge of the storehouses (12:55), and he asks at the end to die as one who submits (12:101). Here he asks a fellow prisoner to say a word on his behalf. The request is small, human and entirely reasonable: a man cleared of every charge, held without a term, appeals to the only person leaving the building who has access to power. The chapter records it without criticism and without endorsement, and it records the consequence in the same breath. That consequence is the reason the verse exists. The sūrah is not interested in the propriety of the request; it is interested in what the request cost.

**Satan Is Named as the Cause**

*Fa-ansāhu al-shayṭānu dhikra rabbih* — Satan made him forget the mention of his lord. The verb is causative: an act of forgetting produced in someone. The Qur'an attributes the failure to Satan rather than to the cupbearer's carelessness, which raises the stakes of the verse considerably. A man who simply forgot is a minor character in a minor lapse; a man made to forget by an adversary is part of a contested operation. The chapter has already established the terms of that contest, in the father's warning that Satan is a sworn enemy to humankind (12:5) and in the sūrah's use of *kayd* for both human and diabolic planning. The forgetfulness is therefore not an accident in the plot. It is the plot's continuation by other means. The brothers used a well and a lie; the women used a locked door and an accusation; here the instrument is memory. Each stage of the opposition adapts to the position Joseph happens to occupy, and none of them needs a villain to appear on the page. That economy is a large part of why the sūrah reads as a single sustained argument rather than as a set of episodes.

**"Several Years" Is Left Unquantified**

The phrase is *bidʿa sinīn*, a number of years — the word *bidʿ* covering the range from three to nine, deliberately imprecise. The commentators supply seven, and verse 45 confirms a long interval by describing the cupbearer's remembering as happening *after a period*. The Qur'an's refusal to give a figure is consistent with the way it handles Joseph's suffering generally: the length is stated as long, not as a number, because the number would invite calculation and the experience does not reduce to one. What the imprecision does accomplish is a change of register. The chapter has been counting things carefully — eleven stars, twenty dirhams, seven cows, seven ears — and at the point where Joseph's own endurance is at issue the counting stops. The silence has a clear effect whatever its intent. Estimates in the exegetical literature run from a few years to seven or to twelve, and the Qur'an commits to none of them. What survives the ambiguity is the shape of the sentence: one request, one lapse, and a long undated interval in which the reader is told of nothing happening at all.

**The Tradition's Comment on the Sentence**

The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have commented on Joseph's conduct at the end of this period, in a narration preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī from Abū Hurayrah: after mentioning Abraham's request for reassurance and Lot's wish for a strong support, he said that if he had remained in prison as long as Joseph did, he would have answered the caller — that is, he would have accepted the offer of release without first insisting that his innocence be publicly established (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3372; the same wording is carried in the tafsīr book at Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4694 and in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The remark is a comparison in Joseph's favour, not a criticism. It identifies the request in verse 42 as the smaller matter and the insistence in verse 50 as the greater one. A separate report circulating in the tafsīr literature — that Joseph would not have stayed in prison so long had he not said what he said — is a different matter: Ibn Kathīr records it and rejects it, stating that every chain by which it has been transmitted is weak. The authenticated wording is the one that praises Joseph's patience rather than the one that faults his words.

## Verse 12:43

> And ˹one day˺ the King said, “I dreamt of seven fat cows eaten up by seven skinny ones; and seven green ears of grain and ˹seven˺ others dry. O chiefs! Tell me the meaning of my dream if you can interpret dreams.”

**A Ruler Called King, Never Pharaoh**

The sūrah names the ruler of Egypt *al-malik*, the king, and does so consistently across verses 43, 50, 54, 72 and 76. It never calls him Pharaoh. The contrast with the Moses narrative is exact: the ruler who confronts Moses is always *Firʿawn*, and the word appears nowhere in this chapter. The observation is old and has been pressed into service by modern scholars who argue that the Joseph story is set before the period of the native dynasties, during the rule of the Hyksos, whose foreign kings did not use the pharaonic title. That historical identification remains a proposal rather than a settled finding. What is not a proposal is the Qur'an's own consistency. Two Egyptian courts are described in the Book and they are given two different vocabularies, a distinction a borrower from a single source would have had no reason to preserve.

**Seven and Seven, Stated Twice**

The dream has two series and they share a number. Seven fat cows devoured by seven lean ones; seven green ears of grain alongside seven dry. The repetition of seven across two different images is what makes the dream interpretable rather than merely strange, and it is what the king reports first and most precisely. The parallelism also suggests that the two series signify the same thing in two registers — livestock and crops, the two bases of an agricultural economy. Joseph's answer in verses 47 to 49 treats them as one calendar rather than two, giving seven years of plenty, seven of scarcity and a year of relief, which is fifteen years in total. The arithmetic is the key the dream hands to its interpreter.

**The Chapter Reproduces the Dream in Full for a Reason**

Every number and every image is given, so that a reader can attempt the interpretation before it is supplied. The elements are few, symmetrical and concrete, and the solution — a cycle of agricultural years with a famine in the middle — is available to anyone who takes the images seriously. The sūrah's decision to state the dream at length rather than summarise it turns the reader into a participant. When Joseph's answer arrives in verse 47, it does not feel like privileged information delivered from nowhere. It feels like the correct reading of a text the audience has already been given, which is exactly how the Qur'an wants its own accounts to be received.

## Verse 12:44

> They replied, “These are confused visions and we do not know the interpretation of such dreams.”

**Classifying the Dream as Meaningless**

The answer is a diagnosis. *Aḍghāthu aḥlām* — jumbled dreams, dreams of confused things. The first noun is a plural whose singular denotes a tangled bundle, a handful of mixed sticks or plants gathered together without order. Applied to a dream it describes imagery with no coherent content, fragments assembled by a sleeping mind rather than signs carrying a message. The classification is technical, not dismissive in tone: the notables are saying that what the king saw belongs to a category which has no interpretation, and therefore no interpreter can be faulted for failing to produce one. The reply is well formed, professionally worded, and wrong.

**An Answer That Is Also Convenient**

A king's dream is a dangerous object. If the notables had offered an interpretation and it failed, the failure would be theirs. If they had offered a grim one and it proved wrong, the offence would be worse. Declaring the dream meaningless costs them nothing, protects them from error, and closes the enquiry in a single sentence. The Qur'an does not accuse them of this calculation, but it does not need to; the structure of the reply is sufficient evidence. This is the same posture the household adopted in verse 35, where people who had seen the proofs chose the option that ended the matter rather than the one that followed it. Twice in the Egyptian section the chapter shows an establishment resolving a problem by declaring that no problem exists.

**Taʾwīl, the Word They Claim Not to Possess**

The term they use for interpretation is *taʾwīl*, from a root meaning to bring a thing back to its origin. In Qur'anic usage it covers the interpretation of dreams, the explanation of events whose meaning is not apparent — as when Moses promises to explain what his companion could not bear patiently (18:87) — and the handling of verses whose sense is not self-evident, which 3:7 assigns to God alone while noting that those with deviant hearts pursue their own. In later Islamic usage the same word came to designate the interior interpretive of a text, set alongside *tafsīr*, its outward exposition, though the distinction was never rigid and al-Ṭabarī titled his commentary with *taʾwīl* rather than *tafsīr*. The notables' sentence therefore denies competence in a discipline with a settled name and a recognised method.

**Experts Failing Is the Point of the Verse**

Structurally the chapter needs this answer. If the court had produced a reading, Joseph would have had no route out of the prison and no route into the palace. The sūrah arranges the failure before arranging the reminder: first the council disqualifies the dream, then the cupbearer remembers the man who could not have been disqualified. The order matters because it establishes that Joseph's interpretation is not one opinion among several but the only one offered anywhere in Egypt. When it arrives it meets no competitor, and its verification over the following fifteen years is therefore uncontested. The verse is the mechanism by which a prisoner becomes indispensable.

The failure also disposes of an objection a later reader might raise. Egypt in this period had a professional class of dream interpreters, and the biblical account shows the king summoning all the magicians and wise men of the country for the same purpose (Genesis 41:8). Their inability is reported in both traditions, and the Qur'an's version is the more pointed because it records their reasoning rather than simply their silence. They did not fail for want of effort; they applied a settled classification and reached a settled conclusion. The chapter by this locates the gap that Joseph fills not in the court's diligence but in the limits of its discipline, which no amount of further effort inside that discipline would have overcome.

## Verse 12:45

> ˹Finally,˺ the surviving ex-prisoner remembered ˹Joseph˺ after a long time and said, “I will tell you its interpretation, so send me forth ˹to Joseph˺.”

**"Send Me Forth" Returns Him to the Prison**

The request is a commission. The freed man asks to be sent back to the place he left, on an errand, at the king's expense and with the king's authority. The movement is the verse's quiet reversal: the prison, which was where Joseph's story ended, becomes the place the state sends to for answers. Nothing in the text describes the journey or the arrival; verse 46 opens inside the cell with the cupbearer already speaking. The elision is typical. The chapter skips what its audience can supply and keeps what it needs — the address, the dream restated, and the answer. The ellipsis has a further effect: it denies Joseph any scene of vindication at the precise moment the reader wants one. He is let out of the narrative only to be put back into a cell, and the journey outward is never shown. The chapter reserves his public clearing for later, when it will come from the women's own mouths and from a king's enquiry rather than from an opened door.

**Why the Story Waits Years for This Moment**

The delay between verse 42 and verse 45 is the longest single gap in the narrative, and it is doing three things at once. It keeps Joseph in prison long enough for the release to be an event rather than a formality. It brings the famine close enough that an interpretation has immediate practical value, which is what converts a prisoner into a minister. And it establishes that Joseph's rise was not arranged by anyone who loved him: his father is a thousand miles away, his brothers believe him dead, the household that cleared him has forgotten him, and the one man who owed him a word forgot it for years. When the elevation arrives in verse 56 it is described as God's act, and the preceding verses have removed every human alternative.

**Providence Working Through Forgetfulness**

The mechanism of this verse is the same one named in verse 42. Satan caused a forgetting; the forgetting produced years in prison; the years placed Joseph in Egypt at the moment the country needed an interpreter; the memory then released him into the office. Every link in that chain depends on the failure of a human memory, and the Qur'an describes the failure without softening it. This is the sūrah's theology in miniature. Human beings are unreliable, the unreliability has costs that fall on the innocent, and the costs are still absorbed into an arrangement that nobody involved designed. The verse asks the reader to hold both halves at once: Joseph lost years to a man's forgetfulness, and Egypt gained its grain supply through the same man's recollection.

There is a further consequence worth stating. Because the release comes through a servant's memory rather than through a petition, Joseph's freedom is owed to nobody in a position of power. No patron claims him, no benefactor expects a return, and no courtier can say he made the man. The only debts outstanding when he walks out are to God, who turned the scheme away from him (12:34), and to a cupbearer who remembered late. That arrangement is what allows the following verses to describe his elevation as an act of divine establishment rather than as a reward for political service, and it is why he can ask for the storehouses in verse 55 without appearing to solicit a favour from anyone.

## Verse 12:46

> ˹He said,˺ “Joseph, O man of truth! Interpret for us ˹the dream of˺ seven fat cows eaten up by seven skinny ones; and seven green ears of grain and ˹seven˺ others dry, so that I may return to the people and let them know.”

**The Title He Is Given in a Prison Cell**

*Yūsufu ayyuhā al-ṣiddīq* — Joseph, O man of truth. The word *ṣiddīq* is an intensive form denoting a person for whom truthfulness is a settled characteristic rather than an occasional act. It is the same quality the family witness attributed to him in verse 27 and the same one the ʿAzīz's wife will confirm before the king in verse 51. Three attestations, separated by years and given by people with every reason to say otherwise: a relative of the accuser, a fellow prisoner who watched him in confinement, and the woman who prosecuted him. The commentators note two grounds for the cupbearer's use of the title here — Joseph interpreted his own dream truthfully, and he never told a lie in all the time they shared the cell. Both grounds are experiential. Nobody in Egypt has testified to Joseph's character on the basis of hearsay.

**"Interpret for Us" Speaks on Behalf of a Government**

The request is plural. The messenger is not asking for himself; he carries the question of a king and a council that has already failed to answer it. That plural changes the character of the exchange. In verses 36 to 41 two prisoners asked a fellow prisoner about their own fates, and the answer concerned two men. Here the state asks, and the answer will concern a country's food supply for fifteen years. The sūrah marks the shift by the register of the speech: no sermon, no theological preamble, no reference to creed. Joseph is addressed as an expert by an official envoy, and he responds as one. The envoy's commission is what lifts the exchange above a personal favour. He has been sent, he is expected to come back with something, and what he brings will be reported to a court. Joseph is therefore speaking into a record, and the chapter places his first official act not in the palace but in the cell, addressed to a messenger who has no idea whom he is addressing.

**Joseph Sees Two Opportunities and Takes Both**

The commentators observe that Joseph recognised in this request both a route out of the prison and a chance to serve a country facing an agricultural catastrophe, and that he treated the two as compatible rather than as competing. Nothing in the text suggests he negotiated his release in exchange for the interpretation; he gives the answer and adds nothing about his own case, and the demand about his case comes later and separately (12:50). That separation is what makes verse 50 significant. He does not bargain with the interpretation. He answers the question as asked, and only afterwards insists that the record about him be corrected before he will walk out of the cell.

## Verse 12:47

> Joseph replied, “You will plant ˹grain˺ for seven consecutive years, leaving in the ear whatever you will harvest, except for the little you will eat

**A Prediction and a Policy in One Sentence**

The verse contains a statement of fact and a statement of obligation, and does not separate them. You will sow for seven years is prediction; leave what you harvest in its ear is instruction. The two are joined because the prediction is only useful as the ground of the instruction. A ruler told that seven good years are coming receives news; a ruler told that seven good years are coming and that the harvest must be retained receives a policy. This is what distinguishes Joseph's answer from the one the court declined to give in verse 44 and from the kind of interpretation a diviner would supply. The knowledge is delivered in the form in which it can be acted on, and the acting is included in the delivery.

**The Plan Requires an Office, and the Chapter Knows It**

Nothing in this verse can be carried out by the person speaking it. A prisoner can describe a storage programme; he cannot enforce one across a country for seven years. The demand implied by the instruction will be met in verse 55, when Joseph asks to be placed over the storehouses of the land on the grounds that he is a reliable and skilled keeper. The sequence is worth noting because it reverses the usual order: the competence is demonstrated first, and the request for authority comes afterwards, grounded in the demonstration. The sūrah is describing a form of political legitimacy in which office follows from a plan that has been stated publicly and can be checked against events.

The plan also has a distributional consequence the verse leaves unstated. Retaining the harvest in the ear for seven years removes grain from ordinary markets, which raises prices in the very years when prices would otherwise be lowest. Egypt's farmers will therefore experience the programme as hardship during plenty and as rescue during scarcity, and only the second half of that experience will be visible to them at the time. Joseph's instruction asks a whole country to accept a cost it cannot yet justify, on the word of a prisoner. That is the political difficulty the following verses resolve, and it explains why the office requested later is not only administrative but coercive.

**No Tax Is Imposed, and the Omission Is Notable**

The biblical account of the same consultation has Joseph propose that the crown take a fifth of the produce during the seven plentiful years and store it under royal authority (Genesis 41:34–36). The Qur'anic version contains no fraction, no levy and no reference to royal appropriation. What it prescribes is retention in the ear and restraint in eating, both of which fall on the producers themselves as much as on the state. Whether the difference reflects a different tradition or a deliberate choice cannot be settled from the text, but the effect is clear: the Qur'an's Joseph gives Egypt a plan for managing abundance rather than a plan for taxing it, and the authority he later asks for is over storehouses rather than over revenue.

---

## Verse 12:48

> Then after that will come seven years of great hardship which will consume whatever you have saved, except the little you will store ˹for seed˺

**"After That" Makes the Sequence Inevitable**

The second phase is not presented as a possibility, a risk or a warning contingent on behaviour. It comes *after that* — the phrase fixes it in the calendar established by the previous verse. Nothing Egypt does in the seven good years will prevent the seven bad ones, and nothing it neglects will cause them. The certainty is what gives the plan its urgency. A hazard that may arrive can be insured against gradually; a hazard that will arrive on a known date requires preparation to begin immediately and to continue for seven years without visible reward. Joseph's answer supplies both the date and the inevitability, and the two together are what make his instruction actionable rather than advisory.

**The Years Themselves Devour**

The verb is the one the dream used. Seven lean cows *ate* seven fat ones; now seven hard years *eat* what has been stored. The agent of the consumption has been transferred from animals to time. That transfer is the whole of the interpretation, and the verse performs it without announcing it. The reader who compares verse 43 with verse 48 can see exactly where the symbol was cashed: the devouring in the dream was not a picture of animals but of years eating surplus. The precision of the correspondence is what makes the interpretation convincing to the king and to the reader, and it is achieved without a single explanatory sentence. The correspondence also settles the objection that Joseph invented the famine to advance himself. The dream belonged to the king and reached the court before any interpretation existed, so the order is fixed by the narrative itself: the vision comes first, the reading follows, and the reading is then tested against years that have not yet arrived and cannot be arranged.

## Verse 12:49

> Then after that will come a year in which people will receive abundant rain and they will press ˹oil and wine˺.”

**A Fifteenth Year That Was Not in the Dream**

The king saw fourteen items: seven fat cows, seven lean, seven green ears, seven dry. Joseph's answer accounts for all of them and then adds one more. Nothing in the imagery of verse 43 corresponds to a year of rain and pressing, and no interpretation of the cows or the ears could have produced it. The additional year is therefore not derived from the data given; it is knowledge supplied from elsewhere. The exegetes have long noted the point, because it establishes something the earlier verses could not: Joseph's information exceeds the dream. A skilled interpreter can decode symbols a dreamer supplies. Only someone receiving information can add a term the dreamer never saw. The addition is also a testable one. Joseph is not extending a symbolic pattern; he is introducing a specific claim about weather for which the dream supplied no basis. If the year of rain does not arrive, the interpretation is falsified along with the entire policy built upon it, and the chapter permits that exposure rather than insulating its prophet from it.

**"They Will Press" Returns the Cupbearer's Verb**

The word for pressing is from the root *ʿ-ṣ-r*, and the chapter has already used it. The first of the two prisoners said: *“I dreamt I was pressing wine”* (12:36). The verb that introduced Joseph's public career in Egypt — the dream whose interpretation restored a servant to his post and eventually carried a message to a king — reappears at the end of his forecast as the sign of national recovery. The echo is exact, and it does structural work. The small private vision and the large public one are joined by the same action, which is how the chapter ties the prison to the palace. A reader who notices the repetition hears the whole Egyptian section as one connected movement rather than two separate episodes.

**The Year of Rain Is Outside the Plan, and the Brothers Are Inside the Famine**

The timing of the narrative depends on this verse. Joseph's brothers arrive in Egypt during the years of scarcity, seeking grain from the stores he controls (12:58). Had the fifteenth year come earlier, they would have had no reason to travel and the recognition scene would never have occurred. The chapter is arranging a calendar. The famine is the mechanism that brings a family from Canaan to an Egyptian granary, and the year of relief is placed after that mechanism has done its work. Nothing in the text says so. The reader assembles it from the sequence, which is the method the sūrah has used from the beginning: give the facts in order and let the arrangement speak. Here that method produces a striking result: the one element of the future Joseph cannot control is the element that closes his forecast, and the sūrah leaves it standing at the end of the verse with nothing built upon it. The plan ends exactly at the limit of what planning can reach, and the reader is shown where that limit falls.

## Verse 12:50

> The King ˹then˺ said, “Bring him to me.” When the messenger came to him, Joseph said, “Go back to your master and ask him about the case of the women who cut their hands. Surely my Lord has ˹full˺ knowledge of their cunning.”

**The Prophet's Own Comment on the Refusal**

The tradition treats this moment as the high point of Joseph's patience. Abū Hurayrah reports the Prophet ﷺ saying, after mentioning Abraham's request for reassurance and Lot's wish for a strong support: if I had remained in prison as long as Joseph did, I would have answered the caller (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3372). The comparison is in Joseph's favour and is explicit. The remark identifies what the refusal cost and what it required: a man who had been held for years, offered release at last, chooses to remain inside until his name has been cleared publicly. The statement is not a criticism of anyone else's weakness; it is a measure of the standard Joseph met, delivered by the one person in the tradition qualified to set it. That is why the comment sits beside the narrative rather than against it. It honours the length of the waiting by refusing to pretend that anyone else would have endured it, and it locates Joseph's distinction not in his purity at the locked door but in his patience across all the years that followed the accusation.

**"Ask Him About the Case of the Women" Names the Evidence**

*Mā bālu* — what is the state of, what happened with — introduces the specific matter to be investigated: the women who cut their hands. The reference is to the banquet of verse 31, where the female establishment of the city wounded themselves on seeing Joseph and declared him an angel rather than a man capable of indecency. Joseph chooses that evidence deliberately. The torn shirt of verse 26 was examined inside a household and never published. The husband's verdict of verse 28 was spoken in a room nobody else entered. The banquet was attended by dozens of women of standing, all of whom saw the same thing and all of whom said the same sentence aloud. He points the king to the only testimony in Egypt that was given in public.

**"My Lord Has Full Knowledge of Their Cunning"**

The word is *kayd*, the chapter's term for concealed planning, used of the brothers in verse 5, of the women in verse 28, of the same women in Joseph's prayer at verse 33, and of God's turning of it away at verse 34. Here it is applied to the whole female establishment collectively. The clause has a double function. It states that nothing about the affair was hidden from God, which is the ground on which Joseph has lived since the well. And it declines to pronounce judgement himself. He does not call the women liars, does not describe what they did, and does not ask for their punishment. He refers their planning to a knower and asks a king to ask a question. Both halves of that sentence are necessary to it. Without the appeal to God's knowledge the demand for enquiry would read as an accusation; without the demand the appeal would be a private consolation. Together they allow Joseph to hold his case open before God while pursuing it, in the same breath, before a human court that can actually do something about it.

**Vindication, Not Revenge**

The distinction governs everything that follows. Joseph's demand produces an enquiry whose outcome is a public statement of his truthfulness (12:51) and a woman's admission of her own guilt. It produces no execution, no confiscation, no dismissal and no humiliation of anyone. When the brothers eventually stand before him, decades later and in a famine, he does not use his power to settle the older account either: *“There is no blame on you today”* (12:92). The pattern is consistent. Joseph seeks correction of the record and nothing beyond it, and the sūrah presents that restraint as strength rather than as weakness — the strength of a man who does not need another person to suffer to be free. It is also a strategic choice. A demand for punishment would have made his release conditional on someone's ruin and given the court every reason to delay; a demand for the truth made it conditional only on an enquiry, which a king can order at no cost to himself. Joseph asks for the cheapest available form of justice, and he receives it.

## Verse 12:51

> The King asked ˹the women˺, “What did you get when you tried to seduce Joseph?” They replied, “Allah forbid! We know nothing indecent about him.” Then the Chief Minister’s wife admitted, “Now the truth has come to light. It was I who tried to seduce him, and he is surely truthful

**The King Asks the Question Joseph Specified**

The enquiry happens exactly as the prisoner requested. The king summons the women and puts the matter to them, using a form of words that asks for an account: what was your affair, what did you arrive at, when you sought to seduce Joseph. The verse does not describe the setting, the number of women present or the procedure, but it records that the state conducted the investigation rather than leaving it to the household. That transfer is the substance of Joseph's demand. The case had been settled inside a private residence by a husband with an interest in silence (12:29). It is now reopened before a ruler with no such interest, and the sūrah treats the change of forum as the change that matters.

**The Women's Answer Clears Him in One Sentence**

*Ḥāsha lillāh, God forbid; we know nothing indecent about him.* The exclamation is the same one they used at the banquet (12:31), and its function is the same: to register that the suggestion is unthinkable. What follows is a statement about knowledge rather than opinion — we know no evil of him — and it is spoken collectively by the women of the city's establishment. Their testimony is not that Joseph was attractive or that they understood the ʿAzīz's wife. It is that they have no information damaging to him. Since the accusation against him originated in this same social circle and circulated among these same households, their joint denial removes its entire social basis. The verse therefore does more than acquit him; it withdraws the report.

**"Now the Truth Has Come to Light"**

The wife of the Chief Minister speaks after the others, and her opening words are an idiom of arrival: *al-āna ḥaṣḥaṣa al-ḥaqq*, now the truth has become fixed, settled, established. The verb carries the sense of something that has come to rest after movement, and its use here marks the end of a long displacement. The truth about this affair had been available since verse 28, when a husband examined a shirt and announced the result to two people in a room. What verse 51 supplies is the same truth in public, before the king, in the mouth of the person who displaced it. Her confession is complete: she names herself as the one who sought the seduction, and she names Joseph as truthful. Nothing is qualified, nothing is shared with him, and no mitigating circumstance is offered.

**A Love That Has Changed Its Nature**

The commentators track a progression through her three appearances in the chapter. In verse 25 she protects herself by prosecuting him, which is desire preferring its own survival. In verse 32 she admits the attempt in front of forty women, which is desire seeking exoneration from scorn. Here she admits it before the king, with no advantage to be gained and every disadvantage to be borne, which the tradition reads as the point where her feeling ceases to be self-interested. Al-Maybudī's account is that her love, having begun as appetite, has by now become a recognition of his inward quality — the truthfulness she names in the same breath. Whether or not that reading is pressed, the structural fact stands: her last recorded statement about Joseph costs her everything and gains her nothing.

## Verse 12:52

> From this, Joseph should know that I did not speak dishonestly about him in his absence, for Allah certainly does not guide the scheming of the dishonest

**The Speaker Is Genuinely Uncertain**

No name and no verb of saying introduce this verse. It follows the wife's confession in verse 51 and precedes a statement the majority assign to Joseph in verse 53, and the commentators divide. One group holds that the speaker is Joseph, continuing the speech he began in verse 50 and explaining why he refused to leave the prison: so that his master would know he had not betrayed him in secret. Another holds that the speaker is still the ʿAzīz's wife, completing her confession by adding that Joseph will now know she has told the truth about him behind his back. Both readings are grammatically sound and both are supported by the sequence. The Qur'an's silence about the speaker is the reason the dispute exists, and the dispute itself is worth preserving, because the two readings produce two different lessons from the same words.

**If Joseph Speaks, the Verse Explains the Refusal in Verse 50**

On the majority reading the sentence answers an objection the reader might have raised. Joseph declined release until an enquiry was held, and the enquiry could look like an act of vengeance against a household that had once sheltered him. His explanation removes that appearance: the demand was made so that the ʿAzīz — the man who gave him honourable lodging in verse 21 — would know that his guest had not betrayed him in secret. The object of the investigation is therefore not the woman's punishment but the husband's information. Nothing in the chapter suggests Joseph sought to damage the household that owned him; what he sought was that the head of it should not go on believing a falsehood about a person he had protected. That reading gives the refusal a moral shape beyond self-defence. Joseph is concerned with a third party's belief, and the concern survives his imprisonment; he wants the man who sheltered him delivered from an error, which is a different object from being acquitted, and a considerably harder one to hold on to from inside a cell.

**"God Does Not Guide the Cunning of the Treacherous"**

The closing maxim is the verse's general contribution and it is stated impersonally, so that it applies regardless of who is speaking. *Inna Llāha lā yahdī kayd al-khāʾinīn* — God does not give success to the stratagem of those who betray trust. The word *khāʾin* denotes a person who violates a confidence placed in him, and the chapter has produced several instances: the brothers, entrusted with a child by their father; a wife, entrusted with a guest by her husband; a household, entrusted with a cleared man and imprisoning him anyway. The maxim does not say their schemes failed at once. It says they do not reach their destination, and the sūrah's plot is the demonstration — every concealed arrangement in it ends somewhere its authors did not intend.

**Kayd Is Judged Here for the Last Time**

The chapter's vocabulary of stratagem reaches its verdict in this verse. *Kayd* named the brothers' plot (12:5), the women's device (12:28), the pressure Joseph asked God to remove (12:33), and the removal granted (12:34). Joseph referred to it once more when demanding the enquiry (12:50). Now the word is attached to treachery and denied success. The sequence is a complete argument: concealed planning is real, it is effective in the short term, it costs innocent people years of their lives, and it does not ultimately arrive at what it aimed for. The verse states the last of those four propositions, and the eighty preceding verses have supplied the evidence for the first three. The verdict is delivered by the person with least to gain from it, which is the chapter's final argument for its own reliability. A defendant asserting his innocence proves very little; the accuser confirming it, before a court, in the absence of the accused, ends the matter. Nothing further is needed and nothing further is said about the charge anywhere in the sūrah.

**The Verse Separates the Record From the Remedy**

Note what is absent. No restitution is proposed for the years in prison, no penalty is named for the false accusation, and no procedure is described for correcting a judicial record. What the verse supplies is knowledge: let him know. The chapter consistently treats the correction of a false account as the primary remedy available to an injured person, and treats punishment as secondary or absent. That priority will hold at the end of the story as well, when Joseph holds the power to destroy the brothers who sold him and instead tells them there is no blame on them today (12:92). The maxim about treachery is the theological ground for both: God does not guide the scheming of the treacherous, so the schemer's defeat does not require the victim's revenge.

## Verse 12:53

> And I do not seek to free myself from blame, for indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil, except those shown mercy by my Lord. Surely my Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

**The Three Levels of the Soul**

Islamic moral psychology built a schema on this verse and two others. The first level is the soul that commands evil, named here. The second is the reproaching soul, which the Qur'an swears by: *“And I do swear by the selfreproaching soul!”* (75:2) — the self that turns on itself, blames its own lapses and struggles to correct them. The third is the soul at peace, addressed at the last day: *“O tranquil soul!”* (89:27), the self that has come to rest in the remembrance of God and returns to Him content and accepted. The three are not three kinds of person but three states a single person moves between, and the movement between them is not achieved by the self operating alone. Verse 53 states the starting condition and immediately names the exception that makes any movement possible.

**"Except Those on Whom My Lord Has Mercy"**

The exception is the verse's hinge and it is grammatically open: *illā mā raḥima rabbī*. Some read it as excepting moments or states rather than persons — the soul commands evil except where my Lord shows mercy. Others read it as excepting those who receive mercy. Either way the claim is identical: the commanding self is not overcome by the self. This is consistent with everything Joseph has said about his own preservation. In verse 24 the narrator states that God turned evil and indecency away from him. In verse 33 Joseph prays that God turn the women's scheme away, adding that without it he would incline toward them. In verse 34 the turning is reported as done. Verse 53 generalises the pattern into a doctrine, and it is spoken by the man who is the pattern's chief instance.

**Why This Verse Follows a Public Vindication**

The placement is the argument. Verse 51 gave Joseph a complete exoneration in the highest forum in Egypt. Verse 52 explained the purpose of the enquiry. Verse 53 refuses to convert either into a claim of personal virtue. The sequence produces a specific moral shape: the truth about a person's conduct can be established without that person becoming a monument. Joseph is cleared, and in the same breath he places himself inside the general condition he has just described. The sūrah by this guards its hero against the use readers most want to make of him — as an example of what a sufficiently disciplined person achieves. What he achieved, on his own account, was not to be left to himself. The sequence reverses the ordinary logic of self-presentation. Acquittal normally prompts a claim of merit; here it prompts the denial of one, and the denial is more specific than the acquittal it responds to. Joseph describes the mechanism that kept him from sin rather than the virtue that achieved it, and he locates that mechanism outside himself.

**The Doctrine Applies to the Whole Story**

Read across the chapter, the verse describes every character in it. The brothers' souls made their plan seem fair to them, which is the exact phrase Jacob used in verse 18 and repeated in verse 83. The woman's soul drove a campaign that ended in a public confession. Joseph's soul inclined toward her in the moment described in verse 24 and was turned away. Nobody in this story is exempt from the proposition, and the exemptions are all mercy. That is why the verse can be spoken by either of two people without the meaning changing: it is not a confession of particular guilt but a description of the human condition, and the condition is shared by the guilty and the innocent alike. That sharing is what makes the chapter's moral economy coherent. The brothers, the women of the city, the cupbearer and Joseph are all described by the same sentence, and what separates them is not the strength of the impulse but what each of them did when mercy intervened. The verse supplies the common ground on which every judgement in the sūrah rests.

## Verse 12:54

> The King said, “Bring him to me. I will employ him exclusively in my service.” And when Joseph spoke to him, the King said, “Today you are highly esteemed and fully trusted by us.”

**The Same Order, Given a Second Time**

Verse 50 records the king's first command — bring him to me — and Joseph's refusal to comply until the enquiry was held. Verse 54 repeats the command in identical words, and this time it is obeyed. The repetition is not careless. It marks the difference between a summons issued on the strength of an interpretation and one issued after a public exoneration. The first brought a useful man out of a prison; the second brings a cleared man into a government. The chapter keeps the two summonses separate so that the reader can see what changed between them, and what changed is not the king's need but Joseph's standing. That distinction is the hinge of the episode. The first summons was met with a refusal and an enquiry; the second follows the enquiry and its result. Egypt's crisis has not altered by a single word and the grain is no nearer the storehouses than it was — the only difference is that the man asked to manage it has now been publicly cleared.

**"I Will Make Him Exclusively My Own"**

The phrase *akhtassuhu li-nafsī* uses a verb of specialisation: to single out, to reserve for oneself, to attach exclusively. The king is not offering a post but a relationship. The commentators gloss it as the intention to keep Joseph's abilities for the crown alone, and the wording supports a stronger sense — the man himself is to belong to the king's own circle rather than to any household or department. The offer reverses the terms on which Joseph entered Egypt. He arrived as property, sold for a handful of counted dirhams to a household that used him and then discarded him (12:19–20, 12:35). He leaves the prison as the personal attaché of the ruler of the country.

**"Esteemed and Trusted" Names Two Different Qualities**

*Innaka al-yawma ladaynā makīnun amīn* — today you have, with us, a place of standing and are trusted. The two adjectives are distinct and both are needed. *Makīn* describes rank, position, a settled place of influence; *amīn* describes reliability, the quality of a person who can be given charge of something. A man may have standing without being trusted with resources, and may be trusted without having the standing to act. The king grants both in one sentence, and the pairing is what makes the request in verse 55 possible: Joseph can ask for the storehouses because he has just been declared both powerful enough to hold them and honest enough to be given them. Neither term alone would justify what follows. Power without trust makes a minister dangerous; trust without power makes him useless in a famine. The pairing is what converts a compliment into a job description, and it explains why the very next verse is not an expression of thanks but a demand for authority over the granaries of the country.

**From Twenty Dirhams to the King's Own Service**

The distance covered between verse 20 and verse 54 is the chapter's central reversal, and the sūrah measures it in the same currency both times. A boy was sold for a few counted coins by people who set no store by him (12:20). A man is now reserved exclusively by the ruler of Egypt, declared esteemed and trusted on the day of their first meeting. No intervening verse explains the change by reference to luck, patronage or political calculation. The mechanisms named are the ones Joseph himself possessed and used: knowledge taught by God (12:21, 12:37), conduct that earned the description of a doer of good (12:22, 12:36, 12:56), and a refusal to leave prison until the record was corrected (12:50). The elevation is presented as the outcome of those three things and nothing else.

**The King's Own Belief Is Not Reported**

The verse records the king's esteem and says nothing about his faith. He honours a prophet, employs him, trusts him, and is never described as believing what Joseph preached to two prisoners in verses 37 to 40. The transmitted reports differ: some hold that the king accepted Joseph's religion, others describe him only as a just ruler who recognised a just man. The Qur'an's silence is the safer ground, and it is consistent with the chapter's treatment of every non-Israelite in the story — the ʿAzīz, his wife, the women of the city, the cupbearer — none of whom is given a stated creed except by their own words. What the text does establish is that an upright ruler can recognise and employ prophetic competence without the narrative needing to settle his ultimate standing.

The word *al-yawm*, today, deserves a final note. The king dates his own judgement. Today you have standing with us, today you are trusted — the decree is given a day, and the day is the same one on which the interview took place.

## Verse 12:55

> Joseph proposed, “Put me in charge of the store-houses of the land, for I am truly reliable and adept.”

**"The Storehouses of the Land" Is the Office the Plan Requires**

The words are *khazāʾin al-arḍ*, the storehouses or treasuries of the land, and the commentators understand them as the granaries and the administration attached to them. The choice is exact. Joseph has already delivered a fifteen-year agricultural programme (12:47–49) that requires three things: the ability to compel retention of the harvest during seven years of plenty, the custody of what is retained, and the authority to release it in measured quantities during seven years of scarcity. No other office in Egypt supplies all three. The request is therefore not a bid for status but the operational requirement of a plan he has already published, and the king's agreement in the following verse is the plan's authorisation. The specification is administrative rather than honorific. Storehouses imply collection, measurement, protection and distribution — four functions sustained across fifteen years in a country whose harvest varies by region. Joseph names the office without which his own published programme cannot operate, and the chapter records the naming as the moment a diagnosis becomes a government.

**"I Am a Reliable Keeper, One Who Knows"**

The grounds Joseph gives for his own appointment are two qualities, and they are the qualities the plan demands. *Ḥafīẓ* — one who guards, keeps, is careful with what is entrusted to him. *ʿAlīm* — one who knows, with the sense of practical mastery. The first answers the risk of a granary administration: the man in charge of a country's entire food reserve must not divert it. The second answers the risk of the schedule: the man must know when to withhold, when to release and how much. The pairing is also a quiet restatement of the gifts given to him in verse 22, where wisdom and knowledge were named together as a divine bestowal, and of the character attested by two prisoners in verse 36. The self-description is also a public claim, made to a court, in the first person, after years of being defined by other people's accounts of him. Joseph takes back the definition of his own competence at the moment it becomes useful, and he does so in the plainest available terms — reliable, and knowledgeable — with no adornment and no formula of modesty.

**Asking for Authority Is Not Worldliness Here**

Nothing in the chapter treats this request as a compromise. The narrator's own verdict comes in the next verse, which describes the outcome as God's establishment of Joseph in the land (12:56) and then adds that the reward of the Hereafter is better still (12:57). The contrast is the sūrah's judgement: the office was genuinely given by God, it was rightly sought, and it is still a worldly good ranked below a heavenly one. A reader looking for a scriptural warrant for competent people seeking positions where their competence is needed will not find a clearer one; a reader looking for a warrant for preferring such positions to the Hereafter will find the correction in the very next verse. That correction is what makes the pairing deliberate. The sūrah grants the request, honours the man who made it, and then immediately ranks the reward of the next life above everything Egypt is able to give. The reader is left holding both propositions at once: seek the office when the office serves a purpose, and do not mistake the office for the prize.

## Verse 12:56

> This is how We established Joseph in the land to settle wherever he pleased. We shower Our mercy on whoever We will, and We never discount the reward of the good-doers

**"Thus" Ties This Verse to Verse 21**

The opening word is *kadhālika* — thus, in this manner — and the chapter has used it before about the same subject. When the boy was bought in Egypt, the narrator said: *“This is how We established Joseph in the land, so that We might teach him the interpretation of dreams”* (12:21). The identical formula now describes the same establishing, thirty-five verses later, with a different purpose attached: so that he might settle wherever he pleased. The first was the mechanism of an education; the second is its outcome. The repetition is not redundancy but a bracket. The chapter opens the Egyptian section with an establishing whose aim is knowledge and closes it with an establishing whose aim is freedom of movement, and the reader is left to connect the two.

**Mercy Is Given, Not Calculated**

The middle clause interrupts the sequence with a general statement: *We send Our mercy upon whom We will.* It sits between the description of Joseph's establishment and the statement that God does not discount the reward of the good-doers, and its position is significant. Nothing in the chapter has presented Joseph's rise as the automatic output of virtue. He was sold for a few counted coins by people who set no store by him (12:20), beaten and imprisoned after being declared innocent inside a household (12:35), and forgotten by the one man who owed him a message (12:42). Each of those events delayed rather than advanced him. The clause names the missing variable: mercy, distributed according to a will that the verse does not explain and does not need to.

**"We Do Not Discount the Reward of the Good-Doers"**

The closing phrase is one the Qur'an returns to. The same assurance appears in a Meccan instruction to the Prophet ﷺ: *“And be patient! Certainly Allah does not discount the reward of the good-doers”* (11:115), and Joseph himself will use it at the end of this chapter, speaking to his brothers after the recognition: *“Surely whoever is mindful ˹of Allah˺ and patient, then certainly Allah never discounts the reward of the good-doers”* (12:90). The verb carries the sense of causing to be lost, letting a reward go to waste. The claim is that nothing good is wasted, including the years that produced no visible result. Joseph's imprisonment is the case in point: it produced nothing for him and everything for Egypt.

**The Tradition About the Chief Minister's Office**

The commentators add a report that when the Chief Minister — the ʿAzīz who had bought Joseph and whose household had imprisoned him — died, the king gave Joseph his former position over the treasury, and with it the title of ʿAzīz. The report is not in the Qur'anic text and cannot be verified from it, but it fits the chapter's method of closing loops. The man who said *“Take good care of him”* in verse 21 is succeeded by the person he was told to care for. The title that once designated Joseph's owner now designates Joseph. Whether or not the report is historical, it expresses what the narrative has already shown: every position held over Joseph in Egypt eventually passes to him, and none of them by his own taking.

## Verse 12:57

> And the reward of the Hereafter is far better for those who are faithful and are mindful ˹of Allah˺

**"Better" Is Comparative, and the Comparison Is Explicit**

The verse does not say the reward of the Hereafter is great, or lasting, or sufficient. It says it is better — *khayr* — which requires a term of comparison, and the preceding verse supplies it. Better than establishment in a land, better than settling wherever one pleases, better than the mercy visibly conferred on a servant in this world. The Qur'an makes the same comparison elsewhere in explicit terms: *“And the next life is certainly far better for you than this one”* (93:4), addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, and *“But the reward of the Hereafter is far better, if only they knew”* (16:41), addressed to those who emigrated after persecution. In each case the worldly good is real and the heavenly one is ranked above it.

**Joseph Is the Verse's Own Instance**

Nothing in the verse names Joseph, and that generality is part of its method. But the chapter has just spent thirty-five verses documenting his faith and his mindfulness, and the reader applies the terms to him without being told. He believed in one God and preached that belief to two condemned men with no prospect of gain (12:37–40). He was mindful in a locked room with a woman who wanted him, and credited the restraint to God rather than to himself (12:24, 12:33, 12:53). He stayed in prison rather than accept a release that would have left a false record standing (12:50). The verse describes the reward of people who did those things, and the person who did them has just been given a country. The chapter lets the coincidence stand unremarked. Joseph's patience through the well, the sale, the household of the ʿAzīz and the prison is the same patience the verse rewards, and his fear of God is the same fear it names. General statement and particular case are presented side by side, and neither is explained by reference to the other.

**The Worldly Reward Is Not Cancelled by the Ranking**

A reader might expect the comparison to depreciate Joseph's office. It does not. Verse 56 has already stated that God does not discount the reward of the good-doers, and verse 57 adds that a further reward is better. The two statements are additive, not corrective. The chapter's view of worldly success is therefore neither contemptuous nor admiring: it is real, it is not lost, and it is not the best thing available. The same ordering governs the rest of the story. Joseph will use his Egyptian authority to feed a famine-struck region and to reunite a family, and will describe the outcome in the next world's terms at the end: God was gracious to us, and He lifted us out of the prison (12:90, 12:100). Both of those later utterances keep the two rewards distinct in the same manner. Joseph thanks God for a reunion and for a position he can see, and he describes the outcome in the language of mercy rather than of achievement, so the ranking established here survives the happy ending instead of being quietly dissolved by it.

**The Verse Sets the Standard for the Coming Test**

What follows is a famine, a series of meetings between Joseph and the men who sold him, a planted cup, a detained brother, and a father's grief. Verse 57 supplies the criterion by which all of it is to be judged, and the criterion is not success or failure in this world. The brothers will be tested on the same two qualities named here — faith, in the sense of keeping an oath taken in God's name (12:66), and mindfulness, in the sense of restraint when an easy advantage presents itself. Joseph has already passed the equivalent test. The verse places the standard in front of the reader before the test begins, so that the events of the next forty verses can be read as an examination against a stated measure rather than as a sequence of family complications.

## Verse 12:58

> And Joseph’s brothers came and entered his presence. He recognized them but they were unaware of who he really was

**Why They Did Not Know Him**

The commentators give three reasons, and all three are available in the text. A long period had passed — the brothers last saw Joseph as a boy, and the well incident is separated from this meeting by the years of slavery, the years in the ʿAzīz's household, the years in prison and now the years of administration. He stood before them in the dress and station of an Egyptian ruler, and awe of that station naturally excluded the thought that the man was a Hebrew brother. Above all, they believed him dead. They had left him in a well and told their father a wolf had eaten him (12:17–18). A person assumed for decades to have perished is not looked for in the face of a foreign minister, and the assumption protects itself.

**Why He Knew Them At Once**

Joseph's recognition is instantaneous and the verse offers no explanation for it. He has been separated from these men since childhood; they are ten in number, travelling as merchants, presenting themselves to a government office in a country where they are foreigners. Nothing about their appearance should identify them to him. The commentators note the obvious fact — that he knew his brothers and they did not know him — without attempting to resolve it, which is the right treatment. The sūrah has already established that Joseph's knowledge regularly exceeds what his situation supplies: he interpreted two dreams from no data at all (12:36–41) and added a fifteenth year to a forecast derived from fourteen items (12:49). Recognising ten brothers is the least remarkable of these.

**Contrast With the Biblical Account**

The same meeting is described in Genesis 42:6–8, where Joseph recognises his brothers, remembers his dreams, and speaks roughly to them, accusing them of having come to see the nakedness of the land. The Qur'anic version contains no accusation of spying and no harshness in this verse. What it has instead is the asymmetry of recognition stated as a bare fact, followed by a demand for a brother and a threat of refusal (12:59–60). The two accounts share the recognition and the demand; they differ in the temper attributed to the recognizer. The Qur'an's restraint is consistent with its treatment of Joseph throughout: a prophet is not shown acting from resentment, and the pressure he applies is explained in the following verses as directed toward a specific end rather than as an expression of feeling.

**The Hinge of the Second Half**

Verse 58 divides the chapter. Everything before it concerns Joseph alone — a dream, a well, a market, a household, a prison, a palace. Everything after it concerns Joseph and his family, and the family's story is the reason the sūrah began with a dream about eleven stars prostrating. The verse is short and it does no interpretive work, but it is the point at which the two halves of the chapter connect.

The verse also introduces the second half's method. From here on, the reader knows more than the characters. Every conversation between Joseph and his brothers will be conducted under that inequality, and the sūrah never lets the reader forget it — verse 69 has Joseph identify himself privately to Benjamin while the other nine stand unaware a few yards away. Dramatic irony of this kind is rare in the Qur'an, and the chapter uses it for a specific purpose: to make the brothers' ignorance visible and therefore their eventual recognition overwhelming. Verse 58 sets the condition that makes all of it possible, in eleven words of narrative report and nothing else.

## Verse 12:59

> When he had provided them with their supplies, he demanded, “Bring me your brother on your father’s side. Do you not see that I give full measure and I am the best of hosts

**"A Brother of Yours From Your Father"**

The phrasing is precise and unusual. Joseph does not say "your youngest brother" or name anyone. He asks for a brother of theirs *from their father* — the Arabic specifies the paternal line, which implies that this brother is not from their mother. The commentators identify him as Benjamin, the full brother of Joseph, and note that the Qur'an never gives his name. The specification does two things at once. It tells the reader that Joseph knows the family's composition in detail, which he should not know from anything the brothers have said in this verse. And it identifies the one brother Joseph has most reason to want to see: the son of the same mother, the child left behind when he was taken.

**Why He Wants the Brother Brought**

The commentators offer a practical reason alongside the personal one. The ten brothers arrived with eleven camels and requested eleven loads — ten for themselves and one for a dependent brother at home (12:65 refers to the additional camel-load). Joseph's demand for the brother's appearance functions as verification: a claim about a dependant justifying an extra measure of food during a famine should be supported by the dependant's presence. On this reading the demand is administrative, and it is the kind of control a granary official exercising rationing would naturally impose. The personal motive runs alongside it without contradicting it, and the chapter does not choose between them. Both are consistent with everything the narrative has shown. The chapter is content to leave the motive unstated at this point, and the withholding is a technique it uses elsewhere. Joseph's plans are announced as actions before their reasons are given, so the reader travels alongside the brothers through a sequence whose purpose becomes clear only in the closing scenes, when the family is finally assembled in Egypt.

**A Demand That Tests the Demanders**

The brothers once took a pledge involving a son of Jacob. They asked their father to entrust Joseph to them, promising they would look after him and that they were his sincere advisers (12:11–12). The request in this verse repeats the shape of that earlier one: a demand that a son be handed over, made to the same father through the same intermediaries, and carrying the same implicit promise of safe conduct. The brothers will hear the echo, and their response in verse 61 — we will seek to win him from his father — uses the language of persuasion, which is what they used the first time. The test is whether they will keep the promise they are about to make.

**What the Verse Does Not Say**

The biblical parallel has Joseph accuse the brothers of being spies who have come to see the weakness of the land (Genesis 42:9), and use that accusation to justify detaining one of them. The Qur'anic account contains no accusation. Joseph does not charge the brothers with anything, does not question their honesty, and does not imply that their story about an eleventh brother is a lie. He asks for the brother and states what will happen if he is not brought (12:60). The difference is consistent with the chapter's treatment of Joseph from the beginning: he applies pressure toward a definite end, and the end is the arrival of a specific person rather than the punishment of the men in front of him.

Two further silences deserve notice. The verse does not record Joseph's identity being concealed by any device — no disguise is mentioned, no interpreter is introduced, no claim is made about why the brothers failed to recognise him beyond what verse 58 already stated. And the verse does not explain the demand to the brothers themselves. They are told to bring a brother and given two reasons for trusting the man asking, neither of which discloses who he is. The chapter keeps Joseph's interior hidden from them exactly as it keeps it hidden from the reader, and the effect is that the demand reads as administrative until verse 69 discloses what it was for.

## Verse 12:60

> But if you do not bring him to me ˹next time˺, I will have no grain for you, nor will you ever come close to me again.”

**They Know They Must Return**

The condition works because the brothers cannot avoid the second journey. The famine lasts seven years (12:48) and ten camel-loads will not sustain eleven households through it. Verse 65 makes the calculation explicit: the food they already have is described as meagre, and the additional camel-load is described as something easily secured. Joseph is not creating an obligation that would otherwise not exist; he is attaching a term to a return visit the brothers are certain to make. That is what makes the demand effective without being cruel. It costs them nothing to accept in principle, since they were coming back anyway, and the only real difficulty is the conversation they must now have with their father. That difficulty is a specific one and the narrative knows it precisely. They have already lost a brother, produced a bloodied shirt as evidence, and been disbelieved in everything except the bare fact of the loss. Asking Jacob to hand over another son on the word of an Egyptian minister is asking him to repeat the act that broke his household.

**The Engine of the Remaining Fifty Verses**

Everything that follows depends on this sentence. Verse 61 records the brothers' promise to persuade their father. Verse 62 has Joseph return their money into their saddlebags to encourage a second journey. Verses 63 to 66 contain the negotiation with Jacob, ending in an oath sworn in God's name. Verse 67 gives Jacob's instruction about entering by separate gates. Verse 69 has Joseph take his brother aside and identify himself. Verse 70 plants the cup. None of these events would occur without a condition attached to the brothers' first visit, and the condition is stated in a single verse. The chapter's second half is generated by the sentence in verse 60, which is why the sūrah gives it its own verse rather than folding it into the demand that precedes it.

**Not Revenge, and Verse 62 Proves It**

A man intending retribution against the brothers who sold him would have begun here, at the first meeting, with them standing helpless in his office during a famine. He does not. He gives them their full measure, and then, after they have left, he orders his servants to put their money back into their bags so that they will find it on the road home and be encouraged to return (12:62). The two acts together — food supplied, price refunded — establish the character of the pressure Joseph applies. It is directed at bringing his brother to Egypt, and it is executed at his own expense. Verse 60 sounds like a threat because a threat is what it is, but the chapter immediately supplies the context that determines its purpose.

**The Verse Also Binds Jacob Into the Plot**

The condition cannot be met by the brothers alone. Their father must release Benjamin, and their father has already lost one son to their custody and has said so in the terms he will use again (12:64). Joseph's demand therefore reaches beyond the ten men standing in front of him and lays an obligation on a household in Canaan that does not know the demander's name. The sūrah is constructing the reunion of verse 100 by means of an administrative condition issued in a granary. Nothing in the verse indicates that Joseph intends this; he wants his brother. The larger design is the chapter's own, and it works through a minister's rationing rule.

That design has a further consequence. Because the demand passes through Jacob, the oath that follows is sworn in God's name and witnessed by God (12:66), and the brothers enter Egypt a second time under a religious obligation rather than a commercial one. When the cup is found in Benjamin's bag the test is no longer about grain. It is about whether ten men will abandon a second son of Jacob in a foreign country, having sworn they would not. Verse 60 is the sentence that makes that test possible, and the chapter spends the next twenty verses administering it.

The subtlety is worth naming because the alternative was available to him and was used in the biblical account, where an accusation of spying precedes the demand and one brother is held in prison (Genesis 42:16–19).

## Verse 12:61

> They promised, “We will try to convince his father to let him come. We will certainly do ˹our best˺.”

**The Verb They Use Is the Chapter's Verb of Persuasion**

The brothers say *sanurāwidu ʿanhu abāhu* — we will seek to win him from his father. The root is *r-w-d*, and the chapter has already used it twice in a very different setting. The ʿAzīz's wife was described with the same verb when the city's women reported her conduct (12:30), and she used it of herself when she admitted the attempt before them (12:32) and again before the king (12:51). In each case the verb denotes a sustained, skilful effort to move another person toward something they are resisting. Applied here to ten sons addressing an unwilling father, it carries a weight the English rendering only partly conveys: this will not be a request but a campaign. A campaign has a shape, and this one does. The brothers come back with provisions to display, with a minister's wording to repeat, with money found in their own bags to explain, and with a pledge to offer. Each element arrives in a separate verse, and the accumulation is what finally moves a man whom nothing else has moved.

**What the Campaign Will Cost**

The brothers know their father's position before they speak. Verse 64 will show that Jacob answers their request with a memory of the last time they asked for a son to be entrusted to them. They are not addressing a man who needs persuading on a small point; they are addressing a father who has already lost one child to their care and who has spent years refusing consolation (12:84). The promise in this verse is therefore a promise to overcome a settled grief, and the brothers make it in the same room as the man who is being asked to supply the food. They commit to the difficult part of the task before they have begun it, in front of an official who knows exactly how difficult it is.

**Two Clauses, Two Different Kinds of Certainty**

The verse ends with an emphatic construction: *wa-innā la-fāʿilūn* — and we will surely do it. The first clause promises an effort, and the second promises an outcome. The gap between them is where the verse's tension lies. Effort is within their control; the outcome depends on a father whose refusal they cannot rule out, and on a set of circumstances — a famine, a rationed granary, a minister's condition — none of which they understand. The double assurance is characteristic of these brothers. Their earlier promise about Joseph was equally emphatic (12:12–14), and the emphatic form did not prevent the outcome it guaranteed from failing.

**The Echo of Their First Promise**

Compare the wording with the promise made decades earlier. To a father reluctant to send Joseph out with them, the brothers said they were his sincere well-wishers, asked that he be sent with them to graze and play, and undertook to guard him (12:11–14). The structure is identical to this verse: a request that a son accompany them, a claim of good intention, an undertaking of protection. Jacob recognised the structure then and recognised it again in verse 64. The chapter sets the two promises side by side without comment, and lets the reader supply the obvious question — whether a pledge that failed once can be relied on when it is repeated in the same form. That silence is among the chapter's most economical devices. It does not need to recall the well, the shirt or the invented wolf; the echo in the wording brings all three forward unbidden. What was once done by ten men to their father is now being promised again by the same ten men, in almost the same words, and the verse says nothing about it.

**The Promise Is Not Yet an Oath**

Note what is absent. The brothers swear nothing here. They state an intention to their employer, and the statement binds them to him but not to God. The oath comes five verses later, extracted by their father and sworn in God's name with God as its witness (12:66). The chapter distinguishes carefully between the two: a promise made in a granary to get food, and a covenant made at home under a father's insistence. Only the second carries religious weight, and only the second will be the measure by which the brothers are judged when Benjamin is detained. Verse 61 is the preliminary commitment; verse 66 is the one that counts. The difference is formal and it matters. A promise offered to persuade is an instrument of the persuasion; an oath extracted by a father who has already lost one son is a covenant, with a named witness and a specified release. The chapter measures the distance between the two by placing five verses of negotiation in between them.

## Verse 12:62

> Joseph ordered his servants to put his brothers’ money back into their saddlebags so that they would find it when they returned to their family and perhaps they would come back

**Into the Bags, Not the Hands**

The placement is concealed. Joseph does not hand anything over, does not announce a refund and does not accept thanks. The money goes into the saddlebags, where it will be found later, by accident, at home. The concealment is what allows the discovery to function as evidence. A gift given openly is a courtesy; a gift found unannounced among one's own possessions after a long journey is a fact about the giver's character, and it arrives without the giver present to be flattered or thanked. The brothers will argue from it in verse 65 as though it were testimony, and the verse's arrangement is what makes that possible. Placement turns a refund into a document. Money handed across a counter is a transaction; money discovered inside a sealed bag on the road home is an event with a date, a place and witnesses. The verse arranges the second, and verse 65 shows the brothers using it exactly as an event rather than as a payment they received.

**"And Perhaps They Will Return"**

The second purpose clause states the objective plainly. Everything Joseph does in these verses is aimed at a second journey, and the second journey is aimed at a brother. The chapter has already explained why a second journey is unavoidable — the famine lasts seven years and ten camel-loads will not see a household through it (12:48, 12:65). The refund still makes the return more likely and more welcome. It converts a journey the brothers must make into one they will want to make, and it does so at Joseph's expense. The verse is a small study in how a person with power uses it: he spends his own goods to produce an outcome he cannot compel. The word perhaps is the centre of that study. Joseph controls the grain, the roads and the price, and he cannot compel ten men to return with a brother their father will not release. So he purchases the possibility instead, spending his own goods to leave open a door that no authority in Egypt is able to force.

**Joseph Pays for His Own Demand**

The cost of this arrangement falls entirely on him. He has imposed a condition (12:60), and he now subsidises compliance with it. The grain was given at full measure, the price was restored, and the servants performed the loading on his instruction. Nothing in the verse suggests reimbursement from the treasury, and the commentators take the act as Joseph's own. That is consistent with the pattern established since verse 58: he recognises his brothers, feeds them, sets a condition, and then quietly removes the cost of meeting it. The pressure he applies in verse 60 is real, and so is the generosity that immediately follows it, and the chapter holds both in adjacent verses without reconciling them. Held together they describe a method rather than a mood. Pressure is applied through the threat of exclusion and inducement through a refund nobody requested; neither instrument is concealed from the reader and neither is explained by him. The chapter sets the two out in sequence and allows their combination to stand as the complete account of his policy.

**The Discovery Becomes an Argument in Canaan**

Verse 65 shows the return of the money doing exactly the work this verse intended. The brothers open their bags at home, find what was put there, and use it to answer their father's reluctance: what more could we want, here is our money returned, we can provide for our family and gain an extra camel-load. Jacob's refusal in verse 64 was based on a memory; the brothers' counter in verse 65 is based on an object. The chapter is contrasting two kinds of evidence — a father's experience of his sons and a stranger's unexpected conduct — and it lets the object prevail, at least for the length of an oath. Joseph's instruction in verse 62 is what supplies the object.

## Verse 12:63

> When Joseph’s brothers returned to their father, they pleaded, “O our father! We have been denied ˹further˺ supplies. So send our brother with us so that we may receive our measure, and we will definitely watch over him.”

**They Report the Condition Before the Money**

The order of the plea is worth examining. The brothers open with the loss — further supplies have been withheld from us — and only in verse 65, after the bags are opened, do they mention what was returned to them. Whether the chapter is reporting events in the order they occurred or arranging the argument for effect, the result is the same: Jacob first hears that his sons have come back short, and only later hears that they have come back with their money intact. The sequence shapes his response. Verse 64's refusal is given before the evidence of generosity is produced, which means Jacob rejects the request on grounds that have nothing to do with the minister's character.

**"The Measure Has Been Withheld From Us"**

The phrase denotes what they were refused rather than what they received. The commentators gloss it as the remainder of the food they had sought — specifically the eleventh camel-load requested for a dependant brother at home (12:59, 12:65). The brothers therefore report accurately: they came home with ten loads and without the eleventh. What they do not say in this verse is why. The condition attached to the refusal is stated only as a request — send our brother with us — and the reason behind it is left implicit. A father hearing this account learns that Egypt will not supply further grain unless a son travels there, and he is not told who imposed the condition or on what grounds.

**"Send Our Brother With Us"**

The request is direct and it names no one. The brother is identified only by relation, which is how the chapter refers to Benjamin throughout: never by name, always as the brother from their father (12:59), his brother (12:64), our brother (12:65), his full brother (12:77). The anonymity is a consistent feature of the Qur'anic account and it has an effect on the reader. A character without a name is defined entirely by his relations, and Benjamin's relation to Joseph is the one the narrative is building toward. The brothers use the same form of reference Joseph used, which means the request as they state it echoes the minister's wording exactly. The borrowing is deliberate and it does real work. By using the minister's own phrase they import his authority into their plea, so Jacob hears the request not as his sons' wish but as a condition imposed by the government that feeds them. What Joseph said to them in Egypt becomes their strongest argument in Canaan.

**What Is Left Out of the Report**

Several things the brothers know are absent from what they tell their father. They do not describe the minister — his age, his bearing, his treatment of them beyond the full measure. They do not mention the demand's peculiar form, a brother from their father, which ought to strike a parent as oddly specific. They do not report the refusal of access threatened in verse 60. And they say nothing about the money, which at this point may not yet have been discovered. The report is accurate and incomplete, and its incompleteness is what produces verse 64: Jacob answers the assurance offered and cannot answer arguments he has not been given.

**The Famine Is the Unstated Premise of the Whole Speech**

Behind the three clauses lies a fact the brothers do not need to state because their father already lives inside it. Canaan is in the same scarcity as Egypt; the journey was necessary and the next one will be too. Verse 63 therefore does not argue that the family needs food. That is assumed. What it argues is that the food cannot be obtained again on the present terms, and that the change of terms requires Benjamin. The economy of the plea depends on the assumption, and the chapter relies on the reader having absorbed verses 47 to 49 well enough to supply it. A father hearing this speech is not weighing whether to feed his household; he is weighing whether feeding it costs him a son.

## Verse 12:64

> He responded, “Should I trust you with him as I once trusted you with his brother ˹Joseph˺? But ˹only˺ Allah is the best Protector, and He is the Most Merciful of the merciful.”

**"His Brother" — Joseph Is Never Named Here**

The reference is relational throughout. Jacob does not say Joseph. He says *his brother*, meaning Benjamin's brother, and the phrasing carries a particular force: the son you once asked me to entrust to you was this boy's brother, and the outcome is known to both of us. The avoidance of the name is consistent with the chapter's practice — Benjamin is never named either — and it has an emotional register the commentators note. Jacob's speech about Joseph elsewhere is equally oblique: he will say *my grief* rather than my son's name in verse 84, and his eyes will go white with sorrow (12:84). A man who has not accepted a death does not use the dead person's name in an argument about a living child.

**"God Is the Best of Keepers"**

The second clause changes direction entirely. *Fa-Llāhu khayrun ḥāfiẓan* — God is the best of those who guard. The same noun was used of Joseph's self-description in verse 55, where he asked to be put over the storehouses as a *ḥafīẓ*, a careful keeper. Here the word is transferred to God, and the superlative form does the work: of all who keep a thing, God keeps it best. The clause is Jacob's way of releasing the matter. He cannot guard Benjamin himself, his sons have proved unreliable guardians, and the only remaining custody is divine. The commentators read this as trust in God combined with resignation of the whole affair to Him, which is what the wording supports. The superlative is doing quiet work.

**"The Most Merciful of the Merciful"**

The verse closes with *arḥam al-rāḥimīn*, a superlative of mercy that the Qur'an places in the mouths of prophets at moments of loss. Moses uses it after the golden calf, asking forgiveness for himself and his brother: *“You are the Most Merciful of the merciful”* (7:151). Joseph will use it at the end of this chapter, forgiving the brothers who sold him: *“May Allah forgive you! He is the Most Merciful of the merciful!”* (12:92). Three speakers, three occasions, one formula, and in each case the formula is uttered by someone who has been wronged or bereaved and has no remedy left. Jacob's use of it here places him inside that pattern before he has agreed to anything. The placement matters more than the assent it precedes. The name is offered not as a reason to let the boy go but as a description of the One he is trusting with the outcome, which allows Jacob to consent without ever claiming the journey is safe. He transfers the risk instead of minimising it, and states the transfer in a single phrase.

**Grief That Has Not Diminished Faith**

Between this verse and verse 84 Jacob will lose a second son, weep until his eyes turn white, and be told by his remaining family that he is going to die of grief. His answer there is the chapter's clearest statement of his position: he complains of his grief and sorrow to God alone and knows from God what they do not know (12:86). Verse 64 anticipates that stance. A man who says God is the best of keepers and the most merciful of the merciful, while refusing to trust his own children with a son, is holding two convictions that most people would find incompatible. The chapter presents the combination as faith rather than as contradiction, and the sequel — Joseph and Benjamin both alive in Egypt — vindicates it.

**The Reply Answers the Assurance, Not the Argument**

Jacob responds to *we will definitely watch over him* and to nothing else in verse 63. He does not address the shortage of grain, the need for the eleventh load, or the famine's duration. The omission is deliberate: the material case is not in dispute, and Jacob is not refusing because Egypt's demand is unreasonable. He is refusing because the guarantee offered is the same guarantee that failed before. When the brothers produce new evidence in verse 65 — the money returned, the family's need, an extra load easily obtained — Jacob still does not withdraw the objection. He converts it into an oath, which is a way of accepting the journey while recording his distrust of the travellers. The asymmetry is deliberate. Jacob has no answer to the economic case his sons have made, because the case is sound: without grain the household starves, and the minister has attached a condition to the grain. What he can answer is their reliability, and he answers it by consenting in the same breath as he records that he does not trust them.

## Verse 12:65

> When they opened their bags, they discovered that their money had been returned to them. They argued, “O our father! What more can we ask for? Here is our money, fully returned to us. Now we can buy more food for our family. We will watch over our brother, and obtain an extra camel-load of grain. That load can be easily secured.”

**The Argument Is Built on an Act Nobody Explained**

The brothers know what they found and not why they found it. Joseph's reason is stated in verse 62, and the reader has it; the brothers do not. They interpret the return of their money as generosity and reliability, and they build their case on that interpretation. The chapter allows the inference because it is sound: a man who restores the price of grain he has already delivered is not likely to harm a guest. But the reader knows something further, which is that the money was placed there to bring them back. The generosity and the design are the same act, and verse 65 shows only the generosity being received. The reader holds both halves at once and the brothers hold one. Every element they cite as proof of goodwill — the measure, the refund, the escort — was arranged by a man who intended to keep their brother in Egypt. The verse lets their sincerity stand unqualified, and that is what makes the scene work: they are honestly persuaded by a design they cannot see.

**The Second Plea Repeats the First and Adds a Fact**

Compare verse 63 with verse 65. Both open with an address to the father. Both state the shortage. Both offer the assurance that they will watch over their brother. What verse 65 adds is the money, and the addition changes the character of the plea from a request into a demonstration. The brothers have not altered their promise; they have acquired support for it. The chapter is describing how persuasion actually works in a family under pressure: the same assurance, offered twice, succeeds the second time not because it is stronger but because something else has arrived to carry it. The added fact is what carries the second attempt. Money in the bags cannot be argued with, discounted, or attributed to the brothers' eloquence; it is simply there, and it converts a promise into a demonstration. The chapter is exact about how persuasion works in this family — not through better wording but through evidence arriving from outside the conversation.

**The Verse Sets Up the Oath and the Gates**

Everything in verses 66 to 70 follows from what happens here. The oath is extracted because the argument succeeded but not completely. The instruction about entering by separate gates follows the oath, and is Jacob's last precaution before his sons leave with Benjamin. The private disclosure in verse 69 and the planted cup in verse 70 then test the assurance that verse 65 has just renewed. The brothers will be given a chance to abandon a second son of Jacob in Egypt, and the whole of the chapter's second half depends on whether they take it. Verse 65 is the moment the second son is committed to their care, and the sūrah records it in the brothers' own words rather than in the narrator's.

**Money as the Chapter's Recurring Measure of Value**

The returned price belongs to a pattern the sūrah established early. Joseph was sold for a few counted dirhams by men who set no store by him (12:20), and the chapter used the smallness of the sum to measure the smallness of their regard. Now the same brothers receive money back from a man who values them enough to give it, and they use it to persuade their father. The two transactions are not connected in the text, and the contrast does not need to be stated. A person worth twenty dirhams to his own family is worth a full refund and a granary's hospitality to his brother. The chapter lets the arithmetic speak for itself across forty-five verses. The pattern runs from twenty dirhams for a boy to a refund that buys his brother's return, and it is never commented upon. Joseph was sold at a price, and he now spends his own goods to bring a brother to him; the chapter records both transactions in the same currency and leaves the comparison to whoever is counting.

## Verse 12:66

> Jacob insisted, “I will not send him with you until you give me a solemn oath by Allah that you will certainly bring him back to me, unless you are totally overpowered.” Then after they had given him their oaths, he concluded, “Allah is a Witness to what we have said.”

**A Refusal Turned Into Terms**

Verse 64 opened with a rhetorical question that functioned as a refusal. Verse 66 opens with *I will not send him with you until*, which is not a refusal but a price. Something has changed between the two verses, and the change is the money found in the bags (12:65). Jacob has not been convinced that his sons are reliable guardians; he has been shown that Egypt's minister is generous, and that alters the calculation enough to make the journey acceptable on conditions. The chapter tracks the movement precisely: distrust of the travellers survives, distrust of the destination lessens, and the result is a covenant rather than a prohibition. That conversion is the verse's achievement. A prohibition would have been negotiated away or simply disobeyed; a covenant creates an obligation the sons have sworn themselves and can be held to. Jacob stops trying to prevent the journey and begins trying to bind the travellers, which is the only form of control left to a man who has decided to let his son go.

**The Form of the Oath**

The word Jacob requires denotes a binding covenant, the term the Qur'an uses for the most serious kind of undertaking between people and between people and God. It is not a promise and not a word of honour. It is sworn *bi-Llāh*, by God, and it is given collectively: *fa-lammā ātawhu mawthiqahum*, when they had given him their covenant. The religious form is the point. Jacob has already stated that God is the best of keepers (12:64); he now makes God a party to the arrangement, so that the obligation runs to God as well as to him. A son sent to Egypt under a family promise can be lost to an accident of memory. A son sent under an oath sworn in God's name is held by something the oath-takers cannot quietly set aside.

**The Exception Clause Is Jacob's Own**

*Unless you are totally overpowered* — the commentators gloss this as a general condition meaning unless you reach a situation in which you cannot uphold the oath. The clause is Jacob's addition, and it does two things. It makes the covenant realistic: ten men cannot guarantee an outcome against overwhelming force, and a father who demanded an unconditional guarantee would be demanding something impossible. The clause also becomes the hinge of verse 80, where the eldest invokes it to explain why he will not go home without his brother. Jacob legislates for the possibility of defeat, and verse 80 shows the legislation used exactly as designed.

**"God Is a Witness to What We Say"**

The formula is one the Qur'an uses when a covenant is concluded between two parties who cannot otherwise enforce it. Moses speaks the same words to his future father-in-law when agreeing the term of service for marriage: *“˹Then˺ it is ˹settled˺ between you and I. Whichever term I fulfill, there will be no ˹further˺ obligation on me. And Allah is a Witness to what we say”* (28:28). In both cases the sentence closes a negotiation in which one party is leaving home and placing himself under another's authority. Jacob's use of it here is the chapter's way of recording that the oath is not a private family arrangement but a transaction with a divine counterparty who will remember it even if the sons do not.

**The Oath Is the Measure Applied in Verse 80**

The covenant sworn here is what the brothers are judged against later. When Benjamin is detained and the eldest refuses to return to Canaan, his reason is stated in the oath: *do you not know that your father took a solemn pledge from you in God's name, and how you failed before regarding Joseph* (12:80). The verse invokes both halves of Jacob's arrangement — the covenant about Benjamin and the earlier failure it was designed to prevent — and treats the oath as binding even when keeping it means staying in Egypt indefinitely. Verse 66 is therefore not a piece of family negotiation; it is the instrument the rest of the chapter uses to measure these men. Everything the brothers do afterwards is measured against it. Their return with provisions, their departure from Egypt without Benjamin, their eldest brother's decision to remain — each is weighed in the following verses against a covenant sworn before God in this one. The chapter supplies the standard first and the conduct second, so the reader can judge without being instructed to.

## Verse 12:67

> He then instructed ˹them˺, “O my sons! Do not enter ˹the city˺ all through one gate, but through separate gates. I cannot help you against ˹what is destined by˺ Allah in the least. It is only Allah Who decides. In Him I put my trust. And in Him let the faithful put their trust.”

**A Second Instruction, Practical in Form**

The oath of verse 66 concerned whether Benjamin would travel. This verse concerns how the party will arrive. Jacob gives a specific operational direction: do not enter by one gate, enter by separate gates. The instruction is concrete and it is about a city's architecture — Egypt's capital had multiple gates, and eleven men arriving together through one of them would be conspicuous. The chapter places the direction immediately after the covenant, which means the reader receives both the religious bond and the practical measure as parts of a single act of fatherly care. The pairing is characteristic of the man. Jacob secures an oath and then gives directions about gates, and the chapter places both in his mouth without distinguishing their weight. Care for his sons' souls and care for their physical safety are treated as one continuous responsibility, which is the same understanding of providence Joseph has been working from in Egypt since verse 47.

**"Judgment Belongs to God Alone"**

*In al-ḥukm illā li-Llāh* — the decision is God's and nobody else's. The same formula appears in Joseph's own preaching, where it grounds the demand for exclusive worship: *“It is only Allah Who decides. He has commanded that you worship none but Him”* (12:40). Father and son use identical words about divine authority in different registers — Joseph in a theological argument with two prisoners, Jacob in a practical instruction to eleven sons. The repetition is one of several that tie the two halves of the chapter together, and it shows that the conviction Jacob teaches is the conviction Joseph preaches. The correspondence is exact in substance. Joseph told two prisoners that judgment belongs to God alone and that He commanded the worship of none other; his father, sending ten sons into a famine, states the same principle as the ground of his own composure. Neither knows the other has said it, and the chapter arranges for the reader to hear both.

**The Verse Is Followed Immediately by Its Own Limitation**

Verse 68 begins by reporting that the brothers entered as their father had commanded and then states that it did them no good whatever against God. The chapter therefore places the instruction and its ineffectiveness in adjacent verses. Nothing in verse 67 is contradicted — Jacob said himself that he could not avail them anything — but the sequel confirms it in the narrator's voice. The precaution was taken, the outcome was unaffected, and the trial that followed came anyway. That arrangement is what makes verse 67 a statement about trust rather than about technique: the means are prescribed, performed and shown to be insufficient, all within two verses. That structure makes the verse a statement about reliance rather than about technique. Entering by separate gates is presented as obedience to a father's instruction, and the verse that follows records that it availed nothing against God's decree. What survives the failure is the intention, and the chapter preserves it by showing the measure taken seriously and then exceeded.

**Eleven Men Dispersed at Their Father's Word**

The practical content of the instruction deserves a moment. Eleven travellers arriving at a city's walls would naturally enter together, since travelling as a group is safer in a famine and the group carries the documents, the money and the cargo. Jacob asks them to separate at precisely the point where separation is least convenient, and the chapter records their compliance without comment in verse 68. That compliance is itself informative: these are men who once disposed of a brother and lied about it, and here they follow an instruction whose purpose their father has explained only partly. The oath of verse 66 and the obedience of verse 68 together describe a family that has been changed by what happened at the well, even before the change is complete.

---

## Verse 12:68

> Then when they entered as their father had instructed them, this did not help them against ˹the Will of˺ Allah whatsoever. It was just a desire in Jacob’s heart which he satisfied. He was truly blessed with ˹great˺ knowledge because of what We had taught him, but most people have no knowledge

**The Precaution Is Performed and Then Evaluated**

The verse reports compliance first: they entered as their father had commanded them. The obedience is complete and unremarked on, which is how the chapter records the brothers' conduct when they are not being tested. Then comes the narrator's evaluation, and it is blunt — it would not have availed them anything against God. The two statements occupy a single verse, so the reader is not allowed to hold the precaution as a success or as a failure. It was performed, and it changed nothing. The evaluation is not a criticism of Jacob; verse 67 had already contained the same judgement in his own mouth. What verse 68 adds is the narrator's confirmation, given before the events that will demonstrate it.

**The Trial Arrives Anyway**

The verse's judgement is confirmed within two verses. The brothers enter Egypt, are received, are fed, and then a cup is placed in a saddlebag and a theft is announced (12:70). Benjamin is detained under a law the brothers themselves supplied (12:75–76), and the eldest refuses to return home (12:80). Nothing about entering by separate gates delayed or softened any of it. The chapter is describing a trial that has been arranged independently of the travellers' arrangements, and it states that fact in advance so that the reader does not look for the failure of the precaution as the cause of what follows. The cause is elsewhere, and verse 76 will name it. The deferral is exact. Nothing here explains why the brothers' arrangement fails, and the explanation is withheld for eight verses, until the moment it can be given in full. The chapter is willing to let the reader sit inside an unexplained reversal because it intends that reversal to be understood as part of an arrangement rather than as an accident.

**"He Was a Possessor of Knowledge Because We Taught Him"**

The clause is a statement about the source of Jacob's understanding. He is described as *dhū ʿilm*, a possessor of knowledge, and the possession is immediately attributed to divine teaching: *li-mā ʿallamnāhu*. The same construction governs Joseph's gifts. God established him in Egypt *so that We might teach him the interpretation of dreams* (12:21), and gave him wisdom and knowledge at maturity (12:22). Father and son are described in the chapter's terms as taught rather than as clever, and the attribution matters for how the reader is to take Jacob's conduct. His trust, his precaution and his limits on both are not the product of experience alone. The attribution rules out the reading that Jacob's care is merely accumulated grief. A man taught by God knows something about decree that experience cannot supply, and the verse uses that knowledge to explain how he can impose a precaution and declare it insufficient in the same breath. Both statements come from the same instruction, and the chapter credits the instruction rather than the years.

**"But Most People Do Not Know"**

The coda is one the sūrah returns to. Joseph used it at the end of his sermon on divine authority: *“That is the upright faith, but most people do not know”* (12:40), and the narrator attached it to the statement that God's will prevails over the affair of Joseph's sale (12:21). Here it qualifies Jacob's knowledge: he possessed knowledge because God taught him, and most people lack it. The repetition ties three moments together — a betrayal, a theological argument and a father's parting instruction — under a single claim about the rarity of understanding. The verse does not say what most people fail to know, and the omission leaves the claim general enough to cover every instance the chapter has produced. The instances the chapter has produced are specific enough to fill the gap. Most people do not know that a father's instruction about separate gates will avail nothing, that a planted cup will yield a lawful detention, or that knowledge can be taught rather than accumulated; each of these has been shown, and none of them was known by the people living through it. The generalisation summarises what the reader has just watched rather than adding a new claim.

**A Verse About a Man Who Is Not Present**

Jacob does not travel to Egypt in this section, and the verse keeps him in Canaan while narrating an event in an Egyptian city. The chapter is unusual in this: it reports the sons' entry, evaluates the father's instruction, and assesses the father's knowledge, all while the father is hundreds of miles away and unaware that his sons have arrived. The effect is to hold both locations in a single frame. Canaan contains a grieving man with real knowledge and no information; Egypt contains a ruler with full information and a plan. Verse 68 belongs to both, and its judgement about the uselessness of the precaution is spoken from the side that has neither the information nor the power to act on it.

## Verse 12:69

> When they entered Joseph’s presence, he called his brother ˹Benjamin˺ aside, and confided ˹to him˺, “I am indeed your brother ˹Joseph˺! So do not feel distressed about what they have been doing.”

**The Second Entry and a Private Moment Inside It**

The brothers have arrived again, this time with Benjamin, and the verse opens with their entry upon Joseph. The public business — provisions, measures, the condition of verse 60 — will occupy verse 70. What this verse records is something that happens inside the arrival and apart from it. Joseph takes his brother aside. The chapter gives no setting for the withdrawal: no room, no pretext, no time of day. The withdrawal is simply reported, and the report is what the whole second half of the story has been moving toward since verse 58, when one side recognised and the other did not. Everything the brothers have travelled for arrives in this scene and none of them knows it. The provisions are loaded, the condition is met, the minister has received the brother he asked for, and the recognition building since verse 58 has finally occurred — on one side only. The chapter places the reader inside the moment and keeps the brothers outside it.

**"Do Not Be Grieved at What They Used to Do"**

The consolation is specific about tense. The verb form denotes repeated or continuing action in the past — what they used to do, what they have been doing. The commentators read the clause as Joseph telling his brother that these events now belong to the past and occurred according to God's will. The formulation does two things. It prevents Benjamin from being distressed by what he has just been told, which is the immediate purpose. And it states Joseph's own settled judgement on his brothers' conduct: the matter is closed, it was in the past, and it happened within a divine arrangement. Verse 92 will say the same thing publicly, in the words *there is no blame on you today*. The private form is more specific than the public one will be. Here Joseph addresses the harm done to him by the men standing outside; in verse 92 he releases them from blame in their own hearing. What is said first to a brother in confidence is said later to the offenders themselves, and the earlier version is what makes the later one credible.

**Two Traditions About the Plan That Follows**

The commentators differ on how much Benjamin knew. One account holds that Joseph and his brother together devised the arrangement by which Benjamin would remain in Egypt — that is, Benjamin was a party to the plan of the cup. Another, from al-Ṭabarī, holds that the plan was not known to Benjamin, who was told only who Joseph was and not what was about to happen. Both accounts are transmitted and the Qur'an does not settle the question. The difference affects how verse 70 is read: as a joint operation, or as a device executed around a brother who has been told the truth but not the next step. The chapter's silence leaves the reader with the disclosure and nothing more.

## Verse 12:70

> When Joseph had provided them with supplies, he slipped the royal cup into his brother’s bag. Then a herald cried, “O people of the caravan! You must be thieves!”

**Provisions First, Again**

The verse repeats the order established in verse 59. Joseph prepares their provisions, loads them, and only then acts. The brothers have received what they travelled for before the cup is placed and before the accusation is made. The repetition is not a narrative habit but a consistent feature of Joseph's conduct in this section: whatever he does to his brothers, he does after supplying them. The pattern matters for how the reader is meant to judge the coming events. A man who starves his guests and then accuses them is acting from malice. A man who feeds them fully and then arranges an accusation is acting toward an end the chapter will disclose two verses later. The repetition of the sequence matters.

**Placed in the Brother's Saddlebag**

The verse specifies the location: his brother's saddlebag, the bag containing Benjamin's own belongings. The commentators note that the bag was filled with Benjamin's things, so the cup is hidden among a traveller's ordinary possessions rather than left in plain sight. The choice of bag is the point of the whole operation. Joseph has ten bags available and one brother he wants to keep, and the cup goes into the one that will produce the result he needs under the law the brothers themselves are about to state (12:75). Nothing in the verse explains the reasoning; the explanation arrives in verse 76, where the narrator says God inspired the plan and that Joseph could not have taken his brother under the king's law.

**The Herald's Public Accusation**

*Yā ayyatuhā al-ʿīr, innakum la-sāriqūn* — O people of the caravan, you are indeed thieves. The cry is addressed to the whole party, not to one man, and it is delivered with emphasis. The commentators identify the speaker as the herald who called out after them, and the charge as collective. That collectivity is what forces the exchange of verses 71 to 75: the brothers must answer for the group, deny the group's guilt, and then state what should happen to the individual if the denial is false. A charge against one man could be answered by producing the innocent man's bag. A charge against a caravan requires a general statement of law, and a general statement is what Joseph needs. The generality is also what makes the reward acceptable. A charge naming a suspect would have invited denial; a charge offering a camel-load to whoever produces the object invites cooperation and places every man in the caravan under the same suspicion at once. Joseph needs the brothers to consent to a search, and consent is what a general announcement produces.

**The Engine of the Next Thirteen Verses**

Everything from verse 71 to verse 82 follows from the cry. The brothers turn back and ask what is missing. The herald names the king's cup and offers a camel-load for its return. The brothers swear they came to do no harm. They are asked what penalty applies if they are lying, and they answer with their own family's law. The bags are searched in order, the cup is produced, and Benjamin is retained. The eldest invokes the oath of verse 66 and refuses to go home. The remaining brothers return to a father who has now lost two sons. The whole sequence is generated by an object placed in a bag, and the chapter gives that placement its own verse.

**What the Accusation Costs**

The cry is not free. Eleven men who entered Egypt under a father's oath, who were fed at full measure, whose money was returned, and who have just received their youngest brother's company are now publicly called thieves in a foreign capital during a famine. The reputational cost is real and the brothers will feel it — verse 73 opens with an oath and verse 77 shows them defending the family's honour against a charge that touches Joseph himself. Joseph has weighed that cost against the object he wants, and the chapter reports the weighing in verse 76 rather than here. Verse 70 gives only the act, which is the sūrah's habit when it describes something that will be justified later rather than at the moment of doing.

---

## Verse 12:71

> They asked, turning back, “What have you lost?”

**Three Levels of Knowledge in One Question**

The verse is the chapter's clearest instance of dramatic irony, and the irony is graded. The reader knows everything: the cup was placed by Joseph, in a specified bag, for a specified purpose (12:70). Benjamin knows the first part and has been told not to disclose it (12:69). Joseph knows all of it and is waiting for a particular answer. The nine brothers know nothing. A single four-word question is therefore spoken by a man with no information to an official with complete information, about an object whose location is known to three people in the scene and to the reader. The chapter constructs this arrangement deliberately and lets the question stand alone in its own verse.

**A Whole Verse for Four Words**

The Qur'an frequently allocates a verse to a single short utterance when the utterance carries the narrative forward. Verse 61 did the same with a nine-word promise; verse 95 will do it with a dismissal of an old man's claim. Here four words of dialogue receive a verse of their own, and the allocation signals that the question is a turning point rather than a detail. Everything from verse 72 to verse 82 is generated by the herald's cry and answered by this question. The chapter's pacing gives the reader a moment to register the brothers' ignorance before the mechanism begins to work. Expanding so short a statement into a verse of its own is a deliberate slowing of the scene. The reader has just watched the cup being placed; the brothers have not, and the extra space allows that gap in knowledge to be felt before the search begins. Everything that follows depends on the reader knowing more than the speakers, and the verse makes certain of it.

**The Chapter's Dialogue Verses**

Verses 71 to 75 form a tight exchange: a question, an answer with a reward, a denial under oath, a question about the penalty, and a statement of the penalty. Five verses of dialogue and almost no narration. The sūrah uses this method at several points — the prison conversation of verses 36 to 41, the negotiation with Jacob in verses 63 to 66 — and in each case the absence of narrative comment forces the reader to judge the speakers from their words alone. Here the effect is sharp. The reader hears nine innocent men deny a theft, and hears them state a penalty that will fall on the one innocent member of their party.

**They Have Just Been Fed and Sent On Their Way**

The incongruity of the accusation is worth stating. Joseph loaded their provisions himself, as he had done on the first journey (12:59), and the verse before this one reports the loading in the same terms. The brothers have been served at full measure by the official now accusing them, in a country whose rationing he controls, and they are being called thieves as they travel home with what he gave them. The charge is not only false; it is false in a way the brothers can point to, and verse 73 shows them doing exactly that — appealing to the officials' own knowledge of their conduct in the land. The appeal is available to them only because of the treatment described here. A caravan that had been cheated, shorted or detained would have had no standing to invoke the officials' own observation; these men can, and they do, precisely because the generosity was real and visible. Joseph's earlier kindness is what supplies his brothers with the confidence to protest.

**The Question Also Protects Them**

By asking what is missing rather than denying a charge they have not yet heard specified, the brothers place themselves in the position of cooperating witnesses rather than suspects. That position will matter in verse 73, when they appeal to what the officials already know about them, and in verse 75, when they are asked to state a penalty. A party that had fled or resisted the herald's cry would have had no standing to do either. Their question, innocent as it is, establishes the footing on which the rest of the exchange proceeds — and on which Benjamin is eventually retained.

Verse 71 is the first step, and it is taken voluntarily.

## Verse 12:72

> The herald ˹along with the guards˺ replied, “We have lost the King’s measuring cup. And whoever brings it will be awarded a camel-load ˹of grain˺. I guarantee it.”

**A Charge Turned Into an Incentive**

The verse performs a rhetorical shift. Verse 70 was an accusation: you are thieves. Verse 72 is an offer: whoever brings the cup will be rewarded. The second formulation does not withdraw the first; it works alongside it. The effect on the accused party is to give them something to do other than protest. A caravan told only that it is composed of thieves can deny and move on. A caravan told that a reward is available for producing the missing object has a reason to submit to a search, and the search is what Joseph requires. The offer is therefore not generosity but procedure, and the following verses show the procedure working. The transformation is complete within the verse. What begins as an accusation of theft ends as a contract, with a specified reward, a named guarantor and a search conducted by consent. Joseph has produced, from a planted cup and a camel-load of grain, the legal instrument that will let him detain his brother before nine witnesses who will testify the process was fair.

**The Brothers Are Included in the Offer**

Nobody in the announcement excludes the accused from the reward. The wording is general — whoever brings it — and the brothers, who have just denied nothing and asked only what is missing, are as eligible as anyone in the caravan. That inclusion is what makes verse 73 possible: they answer with an oath about their own character rather than with a suspicion about someone else. The chapter places them in the position of people who could earn a camel-load by finding a cup they know nothing about, and who respond instead by insisting that they did not come to the land to do harm. Their inclusion is what makes the denial necessary.

**The Verse Sets Up the Penalty Question**

Once the object is named and the search is agreed, the next question is what happens if the object is found. That question is asked in verse 74 and answered in verse 75, and the answer will come from the brothers themselves. Verse 72 does not state a penalty; it states only a reward. The omission is the mechanism. Had the herald announced the consequence of theft along with the reward, the brothers would have had nothing to supply and Joseph would have been bound by Egyptian law — which, as verse 76 explains, would not have allowed him to keep his brother. The reward-only formulation is what leaves the penalty open, and what leaves it open is what makes it the brothers' to state.

---

## Verse 12:73

> Joseph’s brothers replied, “By Allah! You know well that we did not come to cause trouble in the land, nor are we thieves.”

**An Oath Opens the Denial**

The brothers' first word is *tallāh*, by God — an oath formula that the chapter uses at moments of maximum seriousness. Jacob's sons swear by it when reporting their first loss of a brother to their father (12:16), and they will use it again when telling their father that his grief will kill him (12:85). Its use here is not decorative. Eleven men accused of theft by an official of the Egyptian crown have no documentary defence, no witnesses of their own and no standing in the country. What they have is an oath, and the verse shows them reaching for it immediately, before any argument. The sequence is telling. An innocent man confronted with a false charge would normally begin with the facts; these men begin with God. The oath does the work that evidence cannot yet do, because the bags have not been searched and because the one fact that would settle the matter is a fact they do not have.

**Two Denials of Different Kinds**

The verse contains a denial of an act and a denial of a character. We did not come to work corruption in the land concerns purpose and conduct. We are not thieves concerns identity. The second is the more sweeping and the more vulnerable, since a person who is not a thief has never stolen, whereas a person who did not come to cause trouble may simply have kept his intentions to himself. The brothers make both claims together, and the chapter records both without comment. Verse 77 will show how the second claim is received by the one person in the scene qualified to judge it. The first denial concerns a cup and is true; the second concerns a family and is false.

**The Irony the Chapter Does Not Spell Out**

These men stole a brother. They took a child from his father, put him in a well, sold him to a passing caravan for a handful of counted coins, and produced a bloodied shirt to explain his absence (12:16–20). The denial *we are not thieves* is true of the cup and false of the larger matter, and the chapter knows it. Nothing in the verse signals the irony; the reader supplies it from sixty verses of prior narrative. Joseph supplies it aloud in verse 77, in a sentence he keeps to himself, and the sūrah's decision to place the judgement inside his mind rather than in the narrator's voice is what gives the moment its force.

**Benjamin's Silence**

One member of the party knows what is in his bag and says nothing. The verse reports the brothers' answer collectively, and Benjamin is included in the collective. Whether he speaks with them, stays silent, or is answered for by the others, the text does not say. The silence is consistent with the instruction recorded in the tradition that Joseph asked him not to disclose their meeting (12:69). A reader aware of that instruction reads this verse differently from the way the officials in it do: the denial is honest for ten of the eleven men present, and the eleventh is bound by a confidence he cannot break in a public accusation. His position is the hardest in the scene and the chapter does not relieve it. He knows the cup is in his bag, he knows who put it there, and he has been told not to grieve over what his brothers used to do. To speak would betray the one man in Egypt who has protected him; to stay silent is to stand accused before nine brothers who believe him guilty.

**The Denial Requires a Penalty to Be Stated**

An accusation answered with a denial has reached an impasse unless someone proposes a test. The brothers have said they are innocent; the officials have said a royal object is missing; the bags are present and unopened. Verse 74 resolves the impasse by asking what should happen if the denial is false, and the question can only be asked because the denial was so emphatic. A hesitant answer would have justified a search on suspicion. An oath produces a demand for a standard: if you are lying, what should the consequence be? The brothers' confidence in this verse is what obliges them to answer that question in the next two.

## Verse 12:74

> Joseph’s men asked, “What should be the price for theft, if you are lying?”

**A Question About Law, Not About Fact**

The officials do not ask whether the brothers stole the cup. That question has already been answered by the oath in verse 73, and pressing it would produce only a repetition. What they ask is what the penalty should be if the denial is false. The shift from fact to law is the whole of the verse's significance. A search establishes whether an object is present; a rule establishes what happens to the person in whose possession it is found. Joseph's men are constructing the second half before the first has been conducted, which tells the reader that the outcome of the search is not in doubt for the people conducting it.

**Why the Accused Are Asked for the Penalty**

The unusual feature of the verse is who is being consulted. In most jurisdictions the penalty for theft is fixed by law and announced by the authority. Here the authority asks the accused to state it. The commentators explain the reason in verse 76: under the king's law Joseph could not have retained his brother. The Egyptian rule, on one account, required the thief to be beaten and to pay double the value of what was stolen, after which he went free. If that rule had been applied, Benjamin would have returned to Canaan with his brothers. The penalty had to come from somewhere else, and the only available somewhere else was the accused party's own custom. Asking them for it is not a courtesy; it is the only route to a lawful detention, and the question is drafted so that the answer cannot be avoided.

**The Chapter's Interest in Legal Mechanism**

Verses 70 to 76 form a compact study in how a legal outcome can be produced without breaking a law. A cup is placed, an accusation is announced publicly, a reward is offered, a denial is sworn, a penalty is requested from the accused, the penalty is stated in the accused's own terms, and the search is conducted in an order that produces the object last. Every step is procedurally ordinary. The chapter reports the whole sequence and then says in verse 76 that God inspired the plan and that without it the objective could not have been reached under the king's law. The device is presented as revelation working through jurisprudence rather than around it.

**The Reader Knows Where the Question Leads**

By verse 74 the reader has been told three things the brothers have not: that the cup is in Benjamin's bag (12:70), that Joseph is Benjamin's brother (12:69), and that Joseph wants his brother to remain. The question about the penalty therefore arrives with its answer already visible. The tension in the verse is not what will happen but how these particular men will bring it about, and the answer is that they will do it themselves, in their own words, under oath, in a jurisdiction that would otherwise have released the accused. The chapter is showing a plan executed through the free speech of the people it affects. That is the chapter's most striking claim about providence. Nothing here is coerced; the brothers are not threatened into stating the law, not misled about its content, and not prevented from saying anything else. They speak freely, accurately and at length, and what they freely say is the instrument that detains their brother. The plan needs their words rather than their silence.

---

## Verse 12:75

> Joseph’s brothers responded, “The price will be ˹the enslavement of˺ the one in whose bag the cup is found. That is how we punish the wrongdoers.”

**A Family Law, Not an Egyptian One**

The clause *that is how we punish wrongdoers* specifies whose law is being stated. Not the king's, not the land's — ours. The brothers are describing the practice of their own household, and they offer it as the rule to be applied if their denial proves false. The distinction is what makes verse 76 necessary. The narrator explains that under the king's law Joseph could not have taken his brother, and one account of the Egyptian rule describes beating plus double restitution, after which the offender went free. The brothers have therefore supplied, unprompted, the only legal basis available for the outcome Joseph wants.

**They Answer Without Hesitation**

The verse records no deliberation. Ten men, one of whom is about to be searched along with the rest, state a penalty that would put the offender into the custody of the official accusing them, and they state it immediately and confidently. The confidence is explicable: they believe the cup is not in any of their bags. A person certain of his innocence does not calculate the cost of a hypothetical penalty. The chapter is careful here. The brothers are not foolish; they are misinformed, and their misinformation is the result of a disclosure made privately to one of them and withheld from the rest (12:69). Hesitation would have been natural, and its absence is the point. Nine men who believe their brother innocent have no reason to qualify a statement of law, and none of them suspects that the law being recited is about to be applied to one of their own. Their confidence is not stupidity; it is the ordinary confidence of people with no reason to think the question concerns them.

**The Verse Establishes the Ground of Verse 79**

When the brothers plead in verse 78 for one of themselves to be taken in Benjamin's place, Joseph's refusal in verse 79 rests on the rule stated here: God forbid that we take anyone other than the one with whose property we found our goods — to do so would make us wrongdoers. The refusal is not arbitrary. It appeals to the standard the brothers supplied in verse 75, under which the offender and only the offender is handed over. Joseph holds them to their own law at the precise moment when relaxing it would serve them, and the chapter presents that as justice rather than as severity. That ground is supplied entirely by the accused. Joseph never states the penalty himself, never argues for it, and never has to defend it when he applies it; he enforces a rule his prisoners announced and then repeated. The chapter is careful to make the detention lawful in the eyes of everyone present, including the nine men who leave Egypt without their brother.

**A Rule Stated in Confidence Becomes a Rule Applied in Grief**

The distance between verse 75 and verse 78 is the chapter's sharpest irony. The brothers state a penalty they believe cannot reach them, and three verses later they are begging to be substituted under it. What was offered as proof of innocence becomes the instrument that detains their youngest brother and keeps their eldest in Egypt (12:80). The sūrah does not comment on the reversal. It reports the statement, reports the search, reports the discovery and reports the plea, and leaves the reader to observe that the law these men invoked was invoked by them, in their own words, about a brother they had once sold and were now about to lose.

## Verse 12:76

> Joseph began searching their bags before that of his brother ˹Benjamin˺, then brought it out of Benjamin’s bag. This is how We inspired Joseph to plan. He could not have taken his brother under the King’s law, but Allah had so willed. We elevate in rank whoever We will. But above those ranking in knowledge is the One All-Knowing

**What the Order Produces in the Brothers**

Ten bags are opened and found empty. Each one narrows the field, and each narrowing makes the eventual discovery harder to contest. By the time the last bag is reached the brothers have watched the accusation fail nine times over, which is precisely why they are unprepared for the tenth. The chapter is describing how certainty is built in a group of observers: repeated confirmation of a negative result produces confidence that the pattern will hold, and the confidence is what makes the exception devastating. Verse 77 records the brothers' response — an attempt to shift the implication away from themselves — which is the response of people who have just watched their certainty collapse. The collapse is total and immediate.

**"Thus Did We Devise a Plan for Joseph"**

The narrator's comment uses the chapter's central word. *Kidnā li-Yūsufa kaydan* — We devised a plan for Joseph. The noun is *kayd*, the same term the sūrah applied to the brothers' plot against a child (12:5), to the women's pressure on Joseph (12:28, 12:33), and to the treachery that God does not guide (12:52). Here the word is attached to God and directed in Joseph's favour. The chapter has spent fifty verses showing *kayd* as a human instrument used against the innocent; it now uses the same word for a divine arrangement that protects him. Verse 52 said God does not give success to the stratagem of the treacherous. Verse 76 shows whose stratagem succeeds.

**"He Could Not Have Taken His Brother Under the King's Law"**

The constraint is stated explicitly, and the commentators explain it. Egyptian procedure for theft, on one account, required the offender to be beaten and to pay double the value of the stolen item, after which he went free. Applied to Benjamin, that rule would have returned him to Canaan with his brothers. Joseph was the ruler's minister and was bound by the ruler's law; he could not substitute a penalty of his own. What made the outcome possible was that the accused party supplied the applicable rule from their own tradition (12:75), and that the rule they supplied retained the offender in the custody of the person stolen from. The verse records the jurisdictional difficulty and its resolution in a single sentence.

**"Unless God Had So Willed"**

The exception is attached to the constraint. Joseph could not have taken his brother under the king's law — but God willed otherwise, and the willing is what produced the brothers' own statement of the penalty. The chapter attributes the mechanism to divine initiative rather than to Joseph's cleverness, and the attribution is the same one made about his knowledge elsewhere: God taught him the interpretation of events (12:21), gave him wisdom and knowledge at maturity (12:22), and here devises the plan through him. A reader who takes the verse at its word sees a prophet executing a revelation rather than a minister manipulating a legal system. That is the reading the verse demands, and it changes the moral register of the episode. If the plan is Joseph's cunning, the detention of Benjamin is a stratagem; if it is willed by God, the same detention is a stage in a family's restoration that neither brother can yet see. The chapter offers both descriptions and subordinates the first to the second.

**"We Raise in Degrees Whom We Will"**

The statement generalises beyond the scene. The same assurance appears in the account of Abraham's argument with his people: *“We elevate in rank whoever We please”* (6:83), and the Qur'an describes God elsewhere as exalted in rank and Lord of the Throne (40:15). In this chapter the raising of degrees has already been demonstrated — a boy sold for counted coins now controls a country's grain — and the clause attaches the demonstration to a principle. Elevation is not the reward of a merit that compels it; it is given according to a will, and the chapter's own hero is its instance rather than its exception. The generalisation is set at the summit of Joseph's career for a reason. He has been raised from a well to a slave household to a prison to the granaries of Egypt, and no step in that sequence was earned by ordinary reckoning. The verse names the mechanism behind it and refuses to make it a system: elevation is willed, not calculated, and nobody can claim it as an entitlement.

## Verse 12:77

> ˹To distance themselves,˺ Joseph’s brothers argued, “If he has stolen, so did his ˹full˺ brother before.” But Joseph suppressed his outrage—revealing nothing to them—and said ˹to himself˺, “You are in such an evil position, and Allah knows best ˹the truth of˺ what you claim.”

**The Claim Reaches a Dead Brother**

There is a further consequence the brothers cannot see. Joseph is, in their account, long dead — devoured by a wolf, according to the report they gave their father (12:17–18). They are now naming him as a thief in a public setting, in a foreign country, before an official of the crown. The slander falls on a person who is standing in front of them. The chapter has produced ironies before, but this is the sharpest: a false statement about a dead man, made to save a living one, addressed to the dead man himself. The verse does not remark on it. It reports the statement and Joseph's response. The report is flat and the flatness is deliberate. Twenty years of grief, a bloodied shirt, a father whose eyes went white and an invented wolf are compressed into a clause offered as legal precedent, and the verse records the clause without marking what it costs to say. What the brothers have turned into an argument was the worst thing that ever happened to their father.

**"God Knows Best What You Describe"**

The second half of the unspoken sentence refers the matter to divine knowledge. The brothers have described a situation — a family prone to theft, a precedent, a character trait — and the description is false. Joseph does not correct it in front of them. He states that God knows best what they are describing, which is both a rejection of their account and a refusal to argue with it. The formula has appeared earlier in the chapter, in Jacob's mouth, when the brothers brought the bloodied shirt: *God alone must be sought for help against what you describe* (12:18). Father and son use the same recourse about the same men and the same habit of describing falsely.

**Restraint as the Chapter's Portrait of Joseph**

Verse 77 belongs to a consistent pattern. Joseph does not reveal himself in verse 58, does not accuse the brothers in verse 59, does not retaliate in verse 70, does not gloat in verse 76 and does not answer here. The disclosure comes in verse 90, after the brothers have been tested and after they have admitted their fault. The chapter is describing a person who withholds a true statement because saying it at the wrong moment would destroy the thing he is trying to accomplish. What he is accomplishing is not revenge but a reunion, and a reunion requires that the brothers arrive at their own admission rather than be confronted with his. That requirement shapes every scene from here to the end. Joseph could have spoken at any moment and the story would have closed; instead he waits through a search, a detention, a private conference and a second journey, until the men who sold him describe their own crime to his face without knowing it. The reunion he is preparing is an admission before it is an embrace.

**The Last Unknowing Insult**

After this verse the brothers stop defending themselves and start pleading. Verse 78 asks for a substitution; verse 80 records the eldest's refusal to leave Egypt; verse 81 has them return home with a report their father does not believe. The insult in verse 77 is therefore the last thing they say about Joseph while believing him dead, and the chapter places it in the mouth of men who will spend the next twenty verses discovering that everything they said about him was wrong. The verse is also the only place in the sūrah where Joseph's feelings about his brothers are recorded at all, and they are recorded as feelings he did not express.

## Verse 12:78

> They appealed, “O Chief Minister! He has a very old father, so take one of us instead. We surely see you as one of the good-doers.”

**A Title Joseph Once Served Under**

The brothers address him as *ʿAzīz*. The chapter has used the word before, and its earlier appearances are uncomfortable. The city's women used it of Joseph's master when gossiping about his wife: *“The Chief Minister's wife is trying to seduce her slave-boy”* (12:30). It was that man's household Joseph was sold into, that man's wife who accused him, and that man's residence he was imprisoned after leaving. The tradition adds that when the Chief Minister died, the king gave Joseph his position and with it his title. The brothers are therefore addressing, by his official style, the person who once stood in that office as a slave — and they do not know it. The address reverses a relationship the reader has already seen from the inside. These men petition the minister of Egypt in the language of petition, and the minister is the boy their father's household lost and their own hands delivered to a caravan. Every formula of respect they use is accurate, and every one of them is spoken to a brother, which is what gives the scene its weight.

**Ten Men Volunteer to Be Taken**

The offer is the verse's most striking element. Ten men volunteer to be substituted for one, in a jurisdiction where the penalty they themselves stated involves being handed over to the person robbed. The offer is not rhetorical; it is made to an official with the power to accept it, and it is refused in the next verse. Whatever else the brothers are, they are not here attempting to escape at Benjamin's expense — they are proposing to pay the cost themselves. The chapter records the proposal without comment and without accepting it, and the refusal is grounded in law rather than in unwillingness.

Note what the offer concedes. Their defence in verse 77 was about character, not about fact, and here it collapses into a request about who pays.

**"We See You Among the Doers of Good"**

The closing clause praises Joseph with the chapter's own central term. *Muḥsin* — a doer of good — is what the narrator called Joseph when he reached maturity (12:22), what Joseph called himself implicitly when describing his conduct in prison (12:36), and what the reward clause of verse 56 refers to. Joseph will use the same noun in verse 90 when identifying himself to these men. The brothers are therefore describing him accurately in the very sentence in which they misunderstand him completely. The compliment is true, and it is addressed to a man they believe to be an Egyptian official, and its truth is confirmed four verses later by the person receiving it. The confirmation comes four verses later, from the man being addressed, in private, to a brother who cannot repeat it. The compliment is therefore true twice: as the brothers' honest assessment of an official who has dealt with them fairly, and as a statement about a prophet that the sūrah has spent seventy verses establishing. Neither speaker knows that both descriptions apply to one person.

**The Appeal Fails, and the Failure Is the Point**

Verse 79 refuses the substitution on legal grounds. Verse 80 reports the brothers' despair and the eldest's decision to remain in Egypt. Verse 81 has nine of them return to Canaan with a report Jacob will not accept. The plea in verse 78 therefore produces nothing — except that it is recorded. The chapter keeps it because it matters that these men asked to be taken instead of their brother, and because the asking is part of the evidence that the family has changed. Joseph hears it. The refusal that follows is a refusal of the mechanism, not of the intention, and verse 92 will show what Joseph eventually does with both.

## Verse 12:79

> Joseph responded, “Allah forbid that we should take other than the one with whom we found our property. Otherwise, we would surely be unjust.”

**The Same Formula He Used at the Door**

Joseph's first word is *maʿādha Llāh* — God be my refuge, God forbid. The chapter has heard the formula before, in his mouth, in the most tested moment of his life. When the Chief Minister's wife locked the doors and said come to me, he answered: *“Allah is my refuge! It is ˹not right to betray˺ my master, who has taken good care of me”* (12:23). The women of the city used the same exclamation when clearing him before the king (12:51). Here it introduces a refusal of a different kind — not a refusal of temptation but a refusal of an accommodation — and its return ties the two moments together. In both cases the formula marks a line Joseph will not cross, and in both cases the line is drawn by reference to God rather than to preference.

**"Then We Would Be Wrongdoers"**

The word is *ẓālimūn*, and it is the chapter's term for the injury Joseph has spent his life receiving. The wrongdoers never succeed, he said at the door of the ʿAzīz's house (12:23). The brothers described their own conduct to their father in the same vocabulary — *we were wrongdoers* — in verse 64's context and again in verse 91. Now Joseph applies the term prospectively to himself: if he accepted the substitution, he would join the category of people he has been injured by. The refusal is therefore not only legal but self-defining. He will not do the thing that has been done to him, and the verse states the reason in his own word for it.

**Why He Cannot Relax the Rule Even If He Wanted To**

The chapter has already explained the constraint. Joseph could not have taken his brother under the king's law (12:76); what made the detention possible was the penalty the brothers supplied from their own custom. If he now set that penalty aside and took a different man, the whole arrangement would lose its basis. The detention of Benjamin would appear arbitrary — an official seizing a member of a foreign caravan for reasons he would not state — and the brothers would have grounds to contest it. Joseph's insistence on the rule is what preserves the legality of the outcome he wants, and the verse shows him maintaining it against the people whom it disadvantages. Maintaining the rule is also what preserves it.

**The Refusal Is Not Cruelty, and the Chapter Shows Why**

Everything Joseph has done for these men in the preceding verses argues against reading the refusal as severity. He fed them at full measure twice (12:59, 12:70), returned their money into their bags without being asked (12:62), and disclosed himself to the brother they are pleading for (12:69). The verse's refusal concerns one thing only: who is detained. It does not withdraw provision, does not impose a fine, does not punish anyone beyond the retention of a single person, and does not close the possibility of a later resolution. The chapter is careful to keep the refusal narrow, and its narrowness is what allows verse 80 to describe the brothers as despairing of persuading him rather than as fleeing from him.

**The Verse Completes the Legal Mechanism**

Verses 70 to 79 form a closed sequence: an object placed, a loss announced, a reward offered, a denial sworn, a penalty requested from the accused, the penalty stated, a search conducted in a deliberate order, the object produced, a substitution offered and refused. Every step is procedural and every step is recorded. The chapter's interest in the mechanism is not antiquarian; it is what allows verse 76 to say that God devised the plan. A divine arrangement executed through ordinary legal steps is the sūrah's account of how providence works in public life — not by suspending procedure but by arranging the statements people make inside it.

The refusal also preserves the position Joseph will occupy in verse 90. When he finally identifies himself, he does so to men who have watched him apply a rule evenly, refuse an advantage offered to him, feed them twice at full measure and return their money unasked. Nothing in the detention can be read back as an act of personal malice, because verse 79 has already shown him declining the one accommodation that would have served the malice reading. The brothers will admit their fault in verse 91 without ever having been accused by him, and this verse is part of the reason the admission is possible.

## Verse 12:80

> When they lost all hope in him, they spoke privately. The eldest of them said, “Do you not know that your father had taken a solemn oath by Allah from you, nor how you failed him regarding Joseph before? So I am not leaving this land until my father allows me to, or Allah decides for me. For He is the Best of Judges

**"They Conferred Privately"**

The word denotes a private consultation, a closed conversation among a group. The Qur'an uses the same noun of intimate counsel elsewhere and states that no such conversation occurs without God's knowledge: *“If three converse privately, He is their fourth. If five, He is their sixth”* (58:7). The chapter's use of it here marks a boundary — the brothers have moved out of the official's hearing and into a discussion among themselves. It is the first time in the Egyptian section that they deliberate as a group, and the deliberation produces the only morally serious statement any of them has made in front of the reader. The deliberation matters because it is deliberation. Nobody prompts them, no official accuses them and no brother confronts them; alone with the consequence of a journey they undertook freely, they reach a judgement about themselves. The chapter shows conscience working without external pressure, which is the condition that makes the confession in verse 91 possible when it finally arrives.

**He Invokes the Oath and the Earlier Failure Together**

The speech has two references and they are joined deliberately. First, the covenant their father took from them in God's name (12:66). Second, their earlier failure about Joseph — the pledge they gave about a son they did not return, and the report they brought their father instead. The eldest does not mention the oath without the failure or the failure without the oath. He sets them side by side so that his brothers hear the second inside the first: we are now in the position we were in before, and last time we did not keep our word. The chapter's whole account of these men turns on that contrast. The contrast is the whole of the chapter's moral argument about them. Two failures are named in one breath: the loss of Joseph, for which they handed their father a false account, and the loss of Benjamin, for which they have sworn before God. The first cannot be repaired and the second they refuse to compound, and that refusal is where the change in them becomes visible.

**"He Is the Best of Judges"**

The clause closes the vow and transfers the decision. The same words appear in the Qur'an's account of a prophet addressing a divided people: *“then be patient until Allah judges between us. He is the Best of Judges”* (7:87). In both cases the formula is used by a person who cannot resolve a dispute by his own action and does not intend to leave it unresolved. The eldest is not abandoning the matter; he is declining to decide it himself and declining to let his brothers decide it by going home. The chapter places the formula in the mouth of the one brother who has begun to understand what the family owes.

**The Verse Divides the Brothers**

Until this point the ten have acted and spoken as one body. Verse 80 splits them: one stays, and the remainder return to Canaan with a report their father will not believe (12:81–83). The division is the beginning of the chapter's reconciliation, because a group that cannot be held together by a lie eventually has to be held together by something else. The eldest's refusal to travel home is the first act in that direction. He will not carry a second false report to Jacob, and he will not appear before his father without the son his father extracted an oath about. The chapter marks the change without naming it. The division produces a family structure the narrative has not had before. Nine sons arrive in Canaan with grain and a report; a tenth remains in Egypt as surety for the eleventh; and the eldest, who until now has spoken only as part of the group, speaks as an individual carrying a commitment of his own.

**The Speech Is Addressed to Men Who Know It Is True**

Nothing in the eldest's argument would persuade a stranger. He cites an oath, an earlier failure, and a decision he cannot make. His audience is nine brothers who were present at the well, who produced the bloodied shirt, who heard Jacob's refusal in verse 64, and who have just watched a second son detained under a penalty they themselves stated. The reckoning is the real event of verse 80.

## Verse 12:81

> Return to your father and say, ‘O our father! Your son committed theft. We testify only to what we know. We could not guard against the unforeseen

**The Eldest Finishes the Speech He Began**

Verse 80 ended with a vow not to leave Egypt. Verse 81 continues the same speech and turns to the nine who are going home, instructing them what to say. The instruction is quoted at length: an address to Jacob composed by one brother for the others to deliver, beginning with the words they are to use and ending with the limits of what they may claim. The chapter's arrangement gives the eldest two verses of uninterrupted speech, which is more narrative space than any of the brothers receives anywhere else in the sūrah.

**Testimony Limited to What Was Seen**

The two qualifying clauses work together. The first restricts the evidence: we testify only to what we know. The brothers watched the search, saw ten bags opened and empty, and saw the object produced from the eleventh. They did not see it placed, and the chapter has told the reader who placed it (12:70). Their testimony goes to what happened in their presence and no further. The second clause denies a capacity rather than a duty: we were not keepers of the unseen. The commentators gloss this as meaning they could not have foreseen what would happen in Egypt when they made their pledge, and they connect it to the exception Jacob wrote into the oath itself — unless you are totally overpowered (12:66). The plea is placed inside the covenant's own terms rather than outside them.

**An Accurate Report for the Second Time**

The chapter has now shown these men deliver two reports to their father about two lost sons, and the difference in kind is complete. The first was composed before the event, supported by a bloodied shirt, delivered in the evening with weeping, and accompanied by the remark that their father would not believe them however truthful they were (12:16–18). The second is composed after the event, supported by named witnesses, delivered without tears, and confined to what was observed. Both are addressed to the same man, both concern a son he will not see for years, and both receive the same answer (12:18, 12:83). The sūrah does not comment on the difference in truthfulness. It presents the two reports and lets the reader hold them together, which is the method it has used for every moral comparison in the chapter.

**The Brothers Are Not Defending Themselves**

Nothing in the speech argues that they acted well. They do not claim to have protected Benjamin, do not explain why they failed to secure his release, and do not mention the offer of substitution that all ten of them made in verse 78 — an offer that would have counted for something with a father looking for evidence of good faith. The report is a bare statement of fact followed by a denial of foreknowledge, and it stops there. The absence of self-defence is consistent with what the eldest has just said to his brothers in private: that their father took an oath from them in God's name, and that they failed him once before about Joseph (12:80). A man who has made that judgement cannot compose a report that argues against it.

---

## Verse 12:82

> Ask ˹the people of˺ the land where we were and the caravan we travelled with. We are certainly telling the truth.’”

**Witnesses Offered Rather Than Proof Produced**

The speech composed in verse 81 ends with a direction to seek verification. The brothers do not ask Jacob to accept their word, and they do not produce an object to support it. They name two groups he can question. The move is unusual for this family and the contrast with their earlier report is exact. When they came home about Joseph they carried evidence — a shirt stained with false blood — and offered no witnesses whatever (12:18). Here they carry nothing and offer witnesses instead. The chapter's comparison of the two reports is completed by this verse: fabricated proof replaces inquiry in the first case, and available inquiry replaces proof in the second. The difference is not only rhetorical. One report was designed to end questioning, and the other is designed to invite it.

**The Claim Is Truthfulness, Not Innocence**

*We are certainly telling the truth.* The assertion is confined to the accuracy of the report and extends no further. The brothers do not claim that Benjamin is innocent, that the detention is unjust, that the penalty was misapplied, or that their father's grief is misplaced. They do not ask for anything. The narrowness is consistent with the limit on testimony stated in the previous verse: a person who says only what he saw can defend the saying, whereas a person who vouches for someone else's innocence cannot. The chapter shows these men learning to make the smaller claim, and the smaller claim is the one that survives — not Jacob's acceptance in verse 83, which is withheld, but the reader's, who has watched the whole search and knows exactly what the brothers did and did not see.

**The Verse Ends a Speech and Begins a Journey**

The closing quotation marks the end of the eldest's address, and the narrative immediately changes location. Verses 80 to 82 have been a single scene in Egypt, conducted privately among nine brothers and one who has vowed to stay. Verse 83 moves to Canaan and to Jacob's answer. The chapter does not describe the journey home, the arrival, or the moment the report is delivered; it passes from the composition of the message to its reception. That economy is typical of the sūrah's transitions. What matters is the message and the answer, and the weeks of travel between them are omitted because nothing in them affects either. The reader is carried from a granary in a famine to a household in mourning in the space of a single verse break.

**A Family That Now Prefers Verification**

The chapter's interest in evidence runs from beginning to end. A torn shirt established Joseph's innocence inside the ʿAzīz's household (12:26–28). A public enquiry before the king established it again, at Joseph's own insistence and at the cost of further years in prison (12:50–51). An oath with an exception clause governed Benjamin's journey (12:66). A stated penalty governed his detention (12:75). And here, at the moment of a second loss, the brothers offer inquiry instead of an object. The progression is never announced, but it is visible across the verses and it runs in one direction. A family that once lied to its father with a bloodied shirt is now composing a report that invites checking, and the chapter records the change without praising it or pretending that it will be believed.

## Verse 12:83

> He cried, “No! Your souls must have tempted you to do something ˹evil˺. So ˹I am left with nothing but˺ beautiful patience! I trust Allah will return them all to me. Surely He ˹alone˺ is the All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

**The Same Answer Given Twice, Decades Apart**

Jacob's reply repeats the answer he gave when the bloodied shirt was brought to him. On that occasion he said: *“No! Your souls must have tempted you to do something ˹evil˺. So ˹I can only endure with˺ beautiful patience!”* (12:18). The opening word and the first clause are identical here, and the second clause returns in a slightly altered form. The repetition is not a habit of speech but a judgement formed by experience: this is the second time his sons have returned from an errand with a story about a missing brother, and the first story was false. A father who has lived through the first account does not need new arguments to doubt the second, and the chapter supplies him with none. He answers the pattern rather than the report.

**"Beautiful Patience"**

The phrase *ṣabrun jamīl* appears only twice in the Qur'an, and both occurrences are Jacob's, in this chapter, in response to the loss of a son. The commentators understand it as patience without complaint to other people — endurance that does not seek relief by protesting to creatures. The adjective does not describe the patience as pleasant or easy. It describes it as unmarred, free of the element that would spoil it. The verse's second use of the term shows the same discipline being applied to a second loss, decades later, by a man whose eyes have already failed from weeping (12:84). The chapter presents that application as prophetic conduct rather than as stoicism, and verse 86 will define the practice by stating where the complaint is actually directed.

**"Perhaps God Will Return Them All to Me"**

The hope is plural and specific. The commentators identify the three whom Jacob expects back: Joseph, Benjamin, and the eldest son who has vowed to remain in Egypt (12:80). Jacob has been told nothing about Joseph except that a wolf devoured him, and nothing about the eldest except that he will not come home until permitted. His expectation that all three will return is therefore not a conclusion drawn from information he has. The commentators describe it as a kind of prophecy, since Joseph was not known to be alive, and they connect it to the claim he makes in verse 86 — that he knows from God what his sons do not know. The verse places in a grieving father's mouth an expectation that the remaining twenty-eight verses fulfil exactly and completely.

**The Two Names Answer the Two Difficulties**

*Innahu huwa al-ʿalīm al-ḥakīm* — He is the All-Knowing, the All-Wise. The pairing is placed at the end of a verse that has raised two problems, and it answers each. Jacob does not know what happened in Egypt and cannot rely on the accounts of the people who were there; the first name states that someone does know. The detention of a second son, after the loss of a first, looks like meaningless repetition; the second name states that events are ordered toward an end. The claim is not that things will turn out well but that they are known and arranged, which is harder to hold and is what distinguishes this verse from ordinary consolation. Verse 100 will report the ordering in Joseph's own words: my Lord is subtle in what He wills.

---

## Verse 12:84

> He turned away from them, lamenting, “Alas, poor Joseph!” And his eyes turned white out of the grief he suppressed

**He Turns Away Before He Speaks**

The verse begins with a gesture and places it first. Jacob turns away from his sons — *tawallā ʿanhum* — and only then speaks. The order separates the two acts that occupy verses 83 and 84. The first was addressed to the brothers and contained a judgement about their souls, a declaration of patience and a hope. The second is addressed to nobody. What Jacob says after turning away is not part of the conversation and is not intended to persuade, explain or answer anyone. The chapter's arrangement makes the lament private without making it secret: the sons hear it, report it and rebuke him for it in the next verse, but the words were not spoken to them.

**The Name He Has Not Used**

*Yā asafā ʿalā Yūsuf* — alas, my grief for Joseph. This is the first occasion in the chapter on which Jacob names his lost son in the presence of other people. In verse 64 he referred to him only as *his brother*, inside an argument about a different child. In verse 83 he spoke of *them all* being returned without specifying who. Here the name arrives, in an exclamation made after he has turned away from the people whose report provoked it. The placement is exact. Jacob can say the name only when he is no longer answering his sons, which suggests that the name belongs to a grief he has been carrying separately from every conversation the chapter has recorded, including the conversations about the son who was lost.

**"He Was Choked With Grief"**

The closing word describes a person who holds sorrow in. *Kaẓīm* denotes one filled with grief who does not let it out, who restrains the expression of what he feels. The term qualifies everything else in the verse. Jacob's lament is a single exclamation; his weeping is prolonged but private; his complaint goes to God alone (12:86). The chapter's portrait of this man's sorrow is a portrait of sorrow contained, and the containment is what damages his sight. He does not rail, does not curse his sons, does not withdraw from worship and does not accuse God of injustice. What he does is turn away, say one name, and weep until he cannot see — and the verse reports all three acts in a single sentence without comment.

**Restraint as the Habit of This Family**

Verse 77 showed Joseph concealing a response inside himself and refusing to disclose it to the men insulting him. Verse 84 shows his father doing the same with grief. Verse 86 will have Jacob state the practice explicitly: I complain of my anguish and sorrow only to Allah. The chapter is describing a shared family discipline, and it presents the discipline in both of its prophets without praising or criticising it. What it does show is the cost. Joseph's suppression costs him years in a prison he could have left; Jacob's costs him his sight. Neither man is depicted as wrong to have endured silently, and neither is depicted as unharmed by the endurance. The sūrah's interest is in the price of restraint rather than in its virtue, and it records the price in the body rather than in argument.

## Verse 12:85

> They said, “By Allah! You will not cease to remember Joseph until you lose your health or ˹even˺ your life.”

**An Oath About Their Father's Grief**

The sons begin with *tallāh*, by God — the same formula they used when reporting the loss of Joseph decades earlier (12:16) and when denying the theft in Egypt (12:73). Here it introduces not a claim about facts but a prediction about their father: you will not stop remembering Joseph until you are wasted or dead. The oath form gives the rebuke a weight it would not otherwise carry. They are not speculating or expressing worry; they are asserting, in God's name, that his grief will continue to a fixed endpoint. The chapter's use of an identical formula for a false report, an honest denial and a rebuke of a grieving father is not explained, and the reader is left to notice that the same words serve all three purposes in this family.

**Two Endpoints, Both Physical**

The verse offers an alternative: until you become *ḥaraḍ* — wasted, enfeebled, ill to the point of collapse — or until you are among the perished. The commentators take the first as a state of exhaustion short of death and the second as death itself. The formulation is a prognosis in the shape of a warning, and it is precise about the mechanism. No illness is named, no enemy is expected, no accident is feared. The grief itself is identified as what will destroy him. Verse 84 has already supplied the evidence for the prediction, so the sons are not threatening their father with anything; they are telling him what they believe is happening to him and what they believe it will end in.

**The Rebuke Comes From the Cause of the Grief**

Nothing in the verse acknowledges this, and the chapter does not acknowledge it either. The men speaking are the ones who proposed disposing of Joseph, agreed on a well, sold him to a passing caravan for counted coins and brought their father a bloodied shirt with a story about a wolf (12:15–18). They are also, on the report they have just delivered, the ones whose journey produced the detention of the son whose loss reopened the wound. Jacob's grief for Joseph has a single identifiable cause, and the cause is standing in front of him asking him to stop. The sūrah reports the rebuke in the sons' own words, without narrative comment, and leaves the reader to supply the fact that makes those words unbearable to overhear.

**Joseph Survives Only in His Father's Memory**

For the whole of the Egyptian section the brothers have believed Joseph dead. The only person in Canaan who still speaks his name is the man mourning him, and he speaks it once, after turning away (12:84). The verse therefore identifies the actual function of the grief inside this household: it is the only place Joseph still exists. To ask Jacob to stop remembering is, from within the family's assumptions, to ask that the last trace of a dead son be removed along with the suffering it causes. The chapter does not put that argument in Jacob's mouth and he never makes it, which is consistent with his restraint throughout. The point is visible in the structure of the exchange rather than stated in it, and it is what makes the rebuke more damaging than the sons appear to understand.

## Verse 12:86

> He replied, “I complain of my anguish and sorrow only to Allah, and I know from Allah what you do not know

**Two Words for a Single Condition**

The verse names two things: *bathth* and *ḥuzn*. The first denotes the innermost distress that a person cannot keep inside and pours out — the grief that escapes, the burden too heavy to carry silently. The second is grief in its ordinary sense, sorrow as a settled state. The pairing is precise and it describes the situation exactly. Jacob has been described in verse 84 as *kaẓīm*, one who holds sorrow in; here he names what is held and what escapes. A person whose eyes have turned white from weeping has both. The chapter gives him two nouns rather than one because one would not cover the case, and because the complaint he makes to God includes the part he has not been able to contain.

**Beautiful Patience, Now Defined**

The commentators connect this verse directly to the phrase Jacob used twice before. Beautiful patience (12:18, 12:83) is understood as patience in which a person complains only to God about his suffering and not to other human beings. Verse 86 is therefore the definition of the term rather than a separate statement. The three verses form a single account of a practice: the phrase is declared at the first loss, declared again at the second, and its content is stated when the practice is challenged. The chapter does not present the practice as an absence of feeling. It presents it as a direction — sorrow of a particular intensity, addressed to a particular recipient. That definition rescues patience from stoicism. A sorrow that is intense, acknowledged and addressed is not a weaker form of endurance than a sorrow that is hidden; on this account it is the harder one, because it requires the grieving person to keep speaking to God while receiving nothing in reply. Jacob has been doing exactly that for twenty years.

**"I Know From God What You Do Not Know"**

The second clause is a claim to knowledge, and it is made to the people who have just predicted his death. The commentators read it as Jacob's intuitive knowledge that Joseph was still alive — a conviction held without evidence and against a report he had accepted for decades. The claim is unusual in form. Verse 83's hope — that God will return them all — is restated here as knowledge, and the restatement is what separates Jacob from his sons in the chapter's economy of understanding. The chapter does not adjudicate between the two positions here, and verse 94 settles the matter without argument.

**The Grounds of the Claim Are Reported but Not Stated**

The commentators offer two explanations for the conviction. One holds that Jacob saw in a dream that Joseph was alive. The other reports that he was informed by the Angel of Death that his soul had not been taken. Neither account is in the Qur'an and neither can be verified from it; the text states the knowledge without stating its source, which is consistent with the way the chapter handles prophetic knowledge elsewhere. Joseph's interpretations are described as taught by God (12:21) without the teaching being narrated. Jacob's conviction is described as knowledge from God without its transmission being narrated. The sūrah's method is to report the fact of the knowledge and leave its mechanism aside.

**Verse 96 Confirms the Claim in Jacob's Own Words**

When the shirt is cast over his face and his sight returns, Jacob says to his children: *“Did I not tell you that I truly know from Allah what you do not know?”* (12:96). The repetition is the chapter's verification. The claim made in verse 86 — against a rebuke, during a famine, with two sons missing and a third stranded — is restated after the first of the three has been restored. Nothing in verse 86 argued for the claim or offered evidence for it. Its vindication arrives ten verses later in the form of a recovered son and a father's reminder that he had said so. The chapter's structure is the argument, and it is the only argument the verse offers.

## Verse 12:87

> O my sons! Go and search ˹diligently˺ for Joseph and his brother. And do not lose hope in the mercy of Allah, for no one loses hope in Allah’s mercy except those with no faith.”

**An Order to Search, Not to Endure**

Verse 86 ended with a claim to knowledge. Verse 87 turns that knowledge into a commission. The verb Jacob uses denotes diligent inquiry — searching with effort, tracking something down, not waiting for news to arrive. The instruction is active and it is addressed to the same sons who have just returned from Egypt and who have just rebuked their father for his grief. He does not ask them to grieve less. He sends them back out. The chapter's sequencing is deliberate: complaint directed to God, knowledge claimed from God, and then a task assigned to human beings. Nothing in the verse suggests that reliance replaces effort, and the effort required is a second journey into a famine.

**Joseph Is Named Alongside Benjamin**

The commission covers two people, and the inclusion of the first is the verse's central fact. Benjamin is in custody, which is why the brothers were sent to inquire about him; but Jacob adds Joseph, a son he has been told for decades was devoured by a wolf and whose name he has spoken once in the entire chapter, after turning away (12:84). The conjunction is not casual. To send men to search for a living detainee and a dead child in the same breath is to state a belief, and verse 86 has already stated it. The sūrah places the belief in an instruction rather than in a declaration, which makes it actionable and testable. Naming both sons also refuses to rank the losses. Joseph has been gone twenty years and Benjamin a matter of weeks; the search is commissioned for both in a single sentence, which treats the older loss as no less recoverable than the newer. That equality is the substance of Jacob's hope and it is carried by the structure of the instruction rather than by any claim about it.

**Why Jacob Believes Joseph Is Alive**

The commentators offer grounds. One account holds that he saw it in a dream. Another reports that he was told by the Angel of Death that Joseph's soul had not been taken. Others connect the conviction to the hope expressed in verse 83 and the knowledge claimed in verse 86, without specifying a source. The Qur'an itself gives no ground at all: it reports the instruction and the prohibition against despair, and leaves the conviction standing on the claim that its author knows from God what his sons do not. The chapter's method throughout is to report prophetic knowledge as a fact and withhold its mechanism, and this verse follows the method exactly. The chapter tests that acceptance over eight verses and vindicates it in verse 96.

**"Do Not Lose Hope in God's Mercy"**

The noun behind the translation denotes relief, ease, the breathing-space that comes after constriction. Jacob is not telling his sons to expect a specific outcome. He is telling them not to abandon the expectation that relief is possible, and he attaches the instruction to a journey whose likely outcome — a second refusal in Egypt, a detained brother, an unrecovered son — argues against it. The commentators read the phrase as God's mercy, and connect it to the forgiveness the brothers will eventually receive (12:92, 12:97). The mercy in view is therefore not only the return of two sons but the restoration of a family that has committed a crime against one of its members and never confessed it.

**"No One Despairs of God's Mercy Except the Disbelievers"**

The prohibition is given a theological ground, and the ground is severe. Despair of divine mercy is placed in the category of unbelief rather than of weakness. The Qur'an makes the same connection elsewhere in explicit terms. A prophet tells his people: *“O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins”* (39:53). Abraham's own household asks: *“Who would despair of the mercy of their Lord except the misguided?”* (15:56). Verse 87 stands inside that tradition, and it is spoken by a father to sons who have every worldly reason to despair and no revealed reason to. The audience makes the sentence an instruction rather than a comfort. These are men who have lost a brother, a surety and now their father's sight, and who are being sent back to the country where all three losses happened. Jacob tells them that despair would be a statement about God, and that the evidence does not entitle them to make it.

## Verse 12:88

> When they entered Joseph’s presence, they pleaded, “O Chief Minister! We and our family have been touched with hardship, and we have brought only a few worthless coins, but ˹please˺ give us our supplies in full and be charitable to us. Indeed, Allah rewards the charitable.”

**The Third Journey Has a Different Purpose**

The commentators note that the brothers travelled to Egypt this time to inquire about Joseph and Benjamin, in accordance with their father's instruction (12:87), rather than only to buy grain. The verse does not report that purpose in their speech. What they say to the official concerns hardship, inadequate goods and a request for full measure. The search commissioned by Jacob is therefore conducted silently, in the same audience as the commercial plea, and the chapter keeps it out of the dialogue. The reader knows both purposes are in the room. The man being addressed knows one of them, since he arranged the other.

That silence has a consequence for the scene. Because the brothers do not mention Joseph, nothing in their speech prompts the question they receive in verse 89. They ask about grain and charity; they are answered about a brother they believe to be dead. The chapter thus makes the disclosure wholly unforced by the visitors. It arises from the person addressed rather than from anything said to him, which is what allows verse 89 to land as a revelation instead of as a reply.

**"Hardship Has Touched Us and Our Family"**

The word denotes affliction that has made contact — hardship that has touched them, not merely threatened them. The commentators gloss it as intense hunger, and the verse extends it beyond the travellers to their household. This is the third or fourth year of a seven-year famine (12:48), the stores carried home on earlier journeys have been consumed, and the family in Canaan is described in the same terms as the family in Egypt. The plea is therefore not a bargaining position. It is a statement of condition, and the condition is what makes the request in the second half of the verse both necessary and difficult to make.

Verse 89 will show what the official actually does with a plea of this kind: he ignores its content entirely and asks about a well.

**"A Few Worthless Coins"**

The brothers describe what they have brought: meagre merchandise, goods of little value. The commentators understand this as the small amount of trade goods or money they possessed to exchange for food. The admission is remarkable in a negotiation. A buyer normally conceals the weakness of his position; these men state it in their opening address, before making their request. The verse is describing people who have nothing left to bargain with and who know it. What they offer instead is a description of their need and an appeal to the official's character, which is the only currency remaining to them in a famine.

The admission also has a history in this chapter. On the first journey they arrived with eleven camels and requested eleven loads (12:59). On the second they had their money returned to them unasked and argued from it at home (12:62, 12:65). Now they bring little and say so. The three journeys trace the exhaustion of a household, and the verse records the endpoint in the brothers' own words rather than in the narrator's. Nothing about their appearance, their numbers or their camels is described; what the reader is given is their own assessment of what they are worth as customers.

**"Give Us Our Supplies in Full"**

The request uses the same standard Joseph set for himself in verse 59, when he asked the brothers whether they could not see that he gave full measure. They have watched him meet that standard twice, and they now ask for it a third time while admitting they cannot pay for it. The request is therefore grounded in his own published conduct rather than in their entitlement. The chapter is showing the brothers arguing from what they have observed — the same method they used in Egypt when denying the theft (12:73) — and the observation happens to be accurate. The man they are addressing is the one who established the standard.

**"Allah Rewards the Charitable"**

The closing clause invokes a religious maxim to an Egyptian official. The brothers have used the same method before, appealing to what their interlocutors already know (12:73), and here they appeal to a principle of recompense. The irony is complete and the chapter does not mark it. They are asking for charity from the most charitable person in the country — a man who fed them at full measure twice, returned their money into their bags unasked (12:62), and has just disclosed himself to the brother they are pleading for (12:69). The maxim they quote is true, and its fulfilment is standing in front of them, and none of them knows it.

## Verse 12:89

> He asked, “Do you remember what you did to Joseph and his brother in your ignorance?”

**A Question Instead of an Answer**

The brothers have asked for grain and for charity. Joseph answers neither. The verse records a question about an event thirty or forty years old, in Canaan, involving a boy and a family that no Egyptian official has any reason to know about. The non-answer is the disclosure. Nothing else in the chapter could have made these men understand who was speaking to them, and the chapter chooses the least expected route: not a name, not a sign, not a removal of the barrier between them, but a sentence that only one person in the world could have constructed. The plea of verse 88 is left hanging, and the reader is given the reason in the following verse.

**"What You Did to Joseph and His Brother"**

The commentators specify the content of the question. It concerns the separation of Joseph from Benjamin when they were young, and the harm done to Joseph together with the injustices done to Benjamin — debasing him and reviling him after his brother was taken. The chapter's own narrative supports the first half: the brothers planned the disposal of one son (12:8–10), executed it (12:15), and reported it with a bloodied shirt (12:16–18). The treatment of the remaining child is not narrated anywhere in the sūrah, and the question supplies it. Joseph and Benjamin are named together as joint objects of the same conduct, which is a claim the brothers have never had to answer before. The pairing also revises the family's account of itself. For twenty years the brothers have lived with one crime and one victim; this sentence places a second victim beside the first and describes what followed as continuous rather than concluded. Benjamin's separation, their father's blindness and their own guilt are gathered into a single act with a single set of authors.

**The Question Names What an Egyptian Could Not Know**

Ten brothers stand before the minister of Egypt's granaries. They have told him about a father, a brother and a household in Canaan. They have not told him about a well, a caravan, a sale or a shirt. The question refers to all of it, and refers to it as something the hearers did. The disclosure is therefore epistemic rather than physical: no curtain is drawn back, no identity is announced, and the recognition has to be produced by the content of a sentence. The chapter has prepared for this moment since verse 58, where one side knew and the other did not, and it resolves the asymmetry with words rather than with a reveal. The resolution makes the scene a recognition rather than a reveal.

**The Injury Is Named Before the Confession**

The order matters for everything that follows. Joseph states what was done before the brothers admit anything. Verse 91 has them acknowledge preference and sin; verse 97 has them ask their father to pray for their forgiveness. Neither admission comes first. The chapter is describing a reconciliation initiated entirely by the injured party: he names the injury, he supplies the mitigation, he discloses himself, and he forgives (12:92), all before any confession is offered. The structure excludes the possibility that the forgiveness is a response to repentance. It is before it, and it is what makes the repentance possible. The order makes the confession specific rather than general. A man who says we were wrong has said almost nothing; a man who has just heard the injury enumerated — you did this, in ignorance, to these two — can only respond by accepting the enumeration. Joseph's description is the form his brothers' admission will take, and the chapter supplies the form first.

## Verse 12:90

> They replied ˹in shock˺, “Are you really Joseph?” He said, “I am Joseph, and here is my brother ˹Benjamin˺! Allah has truly been gracious to us. Surely whoever is mindful ˹of Allah˺ and patient, then certainly Allah never discounts the reward of the good-doers.”

**"I Am Joseph, and Here Is My Brother"**

The answer has two halves and the second is as important as the first. Joseph identifies himself and immediately presents Benjamin, who is standing beside him — the brother whose detention produced this journey, whose bag carried the cup, and who has known the truth since verse 69. The disclosure therefore resolves two losses at once, and it does so in a single sentence. A father in Canaan believes both sons are gone; the verse puts both of them in the same room, named and present. The chapter has withheld this moment for thirty-two verses and delivers it in nine words, which is characteristic of how the sūrah handles its climaxes.

**Two Conditions Named Together**

Mindfulness — *taqwā*, the guarded consciousness that produces restraint — and patience. The chapter has documented both in Joseph's case and their absence in his brothers'. Restraint kept him from the door of the ʿAzīz's house (12:24) and from answering the insult in verse 77. Patience held him through years in a prison he could have left (12:50). The brothers' conduct is the inverse: a plot that seemed fair to them (12:18), a report composed in advance (12:16–18), a penalty proposed for a brother (12:75), a slander about a dead one (12:77). The maxim names the two qualities whose absence produced the injury Joseph has just described. The absence is what eighty-nine verses have been documenting. Fear of God would have stopped the well; patience would have carried the twenty years that followed. Joseph offers the maxim neither as rebuke nor as boast, and he does not need to: the two qualities he names are the two his brothers lacked, and they are hearing them described by the man who supplied both.

**"God Does Not Discount the Reward of the Good-Doers"**

The closing phrase returns the chapter to its own earlier statement. Verse 56 attached it to Joseph's establishment in Egypt: *“We never discount the reward of the good-doers”* (12:56). The same assurance appears as an instruction to the Prophet ﷺ in a Meccan sūrah: *“And be patient! Certainly Allah does not discount the reward of the good-doers”* (11:115), and in the context of hardship borne in a righteous cause (9:120). Joseph's use of it here is the chapter's own verdict on his story, spoken by him rather than by the narrator. The verb means to cause to be lost, and the claim is that nothing is lost — including the years that produced no visible result at the time.

**The Chapter's Theology in Joseph's Mouth**

Verse 90 is the point at which the sūrah's argument and its narrative coincide. Everything the chapter has said about knowledge taught by God (12:21), mercy given according to a will (12:56), the soul that commands evil (12:53), and stratagem that does not reach its destination (12:52) is now summarised by the person those statements were about. He does not explain himself, describe his suffering, or ask for an account of it. He states a rule and a fact: God has been gracious to us, and the reward of the mindful and patient is not discounted. Verse 100 will apply the rule to the whole story in his father's presence; verse 92 will apply it to the men standing in the room.

**Thirty Years Answered in Two Sentences**

The disclosure resolves more than an identity. It answers the report about the wolf (12:17), the bloodied shirt (12:18), the slander in verse 77, the grief that cost Jacob his sight (12:84), the rebuke in verse 85 and the commission in verse 87. Every one of those events was caused by a false belief about a single fact, and verse 90 states the fact. The chapter's economy is striking: thirty or forty years of consequence are undone in a sentence of nine words, and the sentence is followed not by an accounting but by a maxim about patience. Joseph does not collect what is owed to him. He states the rule that explains how he survived, and he states it to the men whose conduct made the survival necessary, which is the closest thing in the sūrah to an explanation of the last three decades.

## Verse 12:91

> They admitted, “By Allah! Allah has truly preferred you over us, and we have surely been sinful.”

**An Oath Introduces an Admission**

The brothers begin with *tallāh*, by God — the formula they have used throughout this chapter. They swore by it when reporting the loss of Joseph with a bloodied shirt in hand (12:16), when denying the theft in Egypt (12:73), and when predicting that their father's grief would kill him (12:85). Here the same formula introduces the first true statement about themselves that the chapter records. The repetition is not accidental. A family that has sworn to a lie, sworn to a denial and sworn to a prediction now swears to a confession, and the chapter uses the identical opening word for all four so that the difference is located entirely in the content. The uniformity makes the four statements comparable, and the comparison is unflattering. Each of Joseph's disclosures has been sworn and each of the brothers' responses has been sworn too, while the content of the two sets could hardly be further apart. The chapter lets the formal parallel run so that the moral distance between the speakers is measured by the reader without narration.

**"God Has Preferred You Over Us"**

The verb denotes preference, choosing one thing over another. The brothers are not only congratulating Joseph on his station; they are attributing the difference between them to a divine act. The commentators do not restrict the preference to any single respect, and the chapter supports several at once: Joseph received knowledge and wisdom at maturity (12:22), the interpretation of events (12:21), an office over the country's stores (12:55), and an establishment in the land (12:56). The brothers name the preference in the general form because that is the form in which they have just experienced it — a man they sold is feeding them, forgiving them and instructing them. The preference is stated as something observed rather than as doctrine accepted. They have watched Joseph govern a famine, spare a brother, refuse revenge and express a maxim about patience, and their comparison between him and themselves is drawn from that record. The confession is not a theological claim but a summary of the last thirteen verses, offered by the people who lived them.

**The Dream of Verse 4 Answered by the Dreamer's Brothers**

Joseph's first vision showed eleven stars, a sun and a moon prostrating to him (12:4), and his father warned him not to relate it to his brothers lest they plot against him (12:5). The plot happened anyway. Verse 91 is the beginning of the fulfilment: ten of the eleven stars are standing in front of Joseph, addressing him as the one God has preferred over them, and admitting a fault. The prostration itself is reserved for verse 100, when the parents arrive. The chapter divides the fulfilment across two scenes and places the brothers' submission in words here and in gesture later, which is why the recognition scene occupies ten verses rather than one. The length measures what the fulfilment costs. One verse could have reported the brothers bowing; ten are needed to carry a search, a detention, a private conference, a journey home, a father's blindness, a second journey, a disclosure, an admission and a forgiveness. The dream is fulfilled at the end of a moral process rather than at the end of a plot.

**The Admission Follows the Maxim, Not the Disclosure**

Order matters here. Joseph identified himself and stated a rule — whoever is mindful and patient does not have his reward discounted (12:90). The brothers' confession comes after the rule and before the forgiveness. Nothing in the chapter suggests that the confession caused the forgiveness; verse 92 will grant it unconditionally, and Joseph has already named the injury and supplied the excuse in verse 89. The sequence therefore describes a confession that is not a plea. The brothers are not bargaining. They have been told who is speaking to them and what he thinks about patience, and they respond by agreeing with the second half of that assessment rather than by asking for anything. That ordering tells us what the chapter considers decisive.

**What the Brothers Still Do Not Know**

The confession is comprehensive in tone but limited in knowledge. The brothers do not yet know that Joseph placed the cup in Benjamin's bag (12:70), that he searched their bags in a calculated order (12:76), that he disclosed himself privately to Benjamin before any of this (12:69), or that their detention of a second brother was arranged rather than accidental. Verse 100 will supply part of the explanation in Joseph's own terms, attributing the separation to Satan's stirring of rivalry between him and his brothers. Until then the brothers are confessing a crime they remember while standing inside a sequence they do not understand, and the chapter leaves them in that position for nine more verses.

## Verse 12:92

> Joseph said, “There is no blame on you today. May Allah forgive you! He is the Most Merciful of the merciful

**He Does Not Forgive in His Own Name**

The second clause is a prayer: may God forgive you. Joseph has removed the reproach that belonged to him, and the sin that does not belong to him he refers to God. The distinction is exact and it matters. A person can waive an injury done to himself; he cannot absolve an offence against God. The brothers' crime involved both — a brother betrayed and a father deceived, a trust broken before God and an oath sworn in His name (12:66). Joseph's sentence handles the first by renouncing it and the second by interceding for it, and the chapter will show the intercession being taken up by the father in verses 97 and 98. The referral is both humble and practical. Forgiveness given in God's name cannot be measured against the forgiver's feelings, which is what makes it reliable; it also opens a route for the brothers' remaining obligation, since an offence against God requires an approach to God. Joseph supplies the first half here and the chapter supplies the second in verses 97 and 98.

**"The Most Merciful of the Merciful"**

The superlative closes the verse and it belongs to a family of usage the chapter has already established. Jacob used the same formula when refusing to trust his sons with Benjamin, pairing it with the statement that God is the best of keepers (12:64). Moses used it after the golden calf, asking forgiveness for himself and his brother (7:151). In each instance the formula is spoken by someone who has been wronged or bereaved and has no remedy left. Joseph's use of it here places him inside that pattern and answers his father's earlier words from the same vocabulary — the son forgives in the terms the father trusted. The shared vocabulary binds the two halves of the family. Jacob used the same superlative when he consented to let Benjamin go, transferring the risk to God rather than minimising it; Joseph uses it when he releases his brothers from blame, transferring the debt rather than collecting it. Father and son are described in identical terms at the two decisive moments of the sūrah.

**The Qur'an's General Ground for This Act**

Nothing in the verse argues for forgiveness, but the Qur'an supplies the argument elsewhere in explicit terms. Believers are told: *“Let them pardon and forgive. Do you not love to be forgiven by Allah?”* (24:22). The reward for pardoning is stated as exceeding the equivalence that justice would require: *“whoever pardons and seeks reconciliation, then their reward is with Allah”* (42:40), and *“whoever endures patiently and forgives — surely this is a resolve to aspire to”* (42:43). Verse 92 is the narrative instance of those rulings, and verse 90 has already supplied the other half of the pairing — patience — in Joseph's own mouth moments before. The pairing is completed across two verses by the same speaker. Joseph describes himself as one who fears God and is patient, and then forgives in the name of the Most Merciful; the qualities he claims and the quality he invokes belong to one account of why the amnesty is possible. The chapter refuses to separate the ethics from the theology behind them.

**The Forgiveness Precedes the Full Account**

Verse 100 will contain Joseph's own reading of the last thirty years: God was kind to him when He freed him from prison and brought his family from the desert after Satan had stirred up rivalry between him and his brothers. The amnesty in verse 92 is given before that explanation, and before the brothers have any idea of the arrangement they were inside. They are forgiven without being shown the mechanism, and the mechanism is disclosed to a family already reconciled rather than as a condition of reconciliation. The chapter's order is deliberate: forgiveness first, understanding afterwards, and the understanding attributed to a third party rather than to the offenders. Both choices protect the offenders. Forgiveness offered before the account means the brothers are not required to justify themselves to be released; understanding attributed to God means their eventual comprehension is treated as a gift rather than as an achievement. Joseph removes from the transaction every element that would have let him hold something over the men standing in front of him.

## Verse 12:93

> Go with this shirt of mine and cast it over my father’s face, and he will regain his sight. Then come back to me with your whole family.”

**The Third Shirt in the Chapter**

Shirts have carried the chapter's evidence from beginning to end. The first was stained with false blood and produced to a father as proof that a wolf had devoured his son (12:18). The second was torn from behind and examined by a household witness, establishing that Joseph was fleeing rather than attacking (12:26–28). The third is this one, and its function reverses the first: a garment belonging to Joseph is carried to Jacob and restores what a garment falsely attributed to him destroyed. The chapter never comments on the pattern. It simply uses the same object three times, for a lie, for a truth and for a healing, and leaves the reader to notice the sequence. The sequence is the chapter's most sustained symbolism and it is never explained. A garment dipped in false blood establishes a death, a garment torn from behind establishes an innocence, and a garment carried to Canaan establishes a healing. Each is offered as evidence, each is trusted by whoever receives it, and the third undoes what the first asserted twenty years earlier.

**A Prediction Stated as Certain**

*He will regain his sight.* Joseph does not say perhaps, or if God wills, or that he hopes. The prediction is unconditional, and the commentators explain it as based on divine inspiration rather than on medical judgement. Nothing in Joseph's knowledge of his father would support the claim: he has not seen Jacob in decades, does not know that his eyes have failed, and has received no report of the injury. Verse 84 describes the whiteness of the eyes in Canaan, and verse 93 predicts its reversal in Egypt. The chapter therefore attributes the prediction to the same source as Joseph's other knowledge, which verse 21 described as taught by God.

**The Tradition About the Shirt's Origin**

One account records that this shirt was made of the silk of Paradise and was brought to Joseph by Gabriel while he was in the well (12:15). The report is not in the Qur'an and cannot be verified from it, but it explains why the exegetes looked for something unusual about the garment. A shirt that heals blindness by being placed on a face invites an account of its nature, and the tradition supplies one. The Qur'an's own text is content to describe the shirt as Joseph's — *my shirt* — and to attach the healing to a command rather than to a property of the cloth. Attaching the healing to the command keeps the theology clean. A cloth with intrinsic power would become an object of veneration; a cloth that heals because a prophet said to cast it is an instrument, and the power stays where the chapter puts it. The wording is careful to make the shirt a means rather than a cause, which is why it is called his shirt.

**Verse 96 Reports the Fulfilment in the Same Terms**

The bearer of good news arrives, casts the shirt over Jacob's face, and he regains his sight (12:96). The chapter reports the healing in a clause as short as the prediction, and Jacob's response is to remind his children that he had told them he knew from God what they did not know. The verification is therefore complete and public: a man blind from weeping receives his sight by means of a garment sent from Egypt by a son he was told had been eaten by a wolf. Verse 93 is the instruction, verse 94 is the anticipation of its effect, and verse 96 is the report. The chapter gives the healing three verses and no explanation.

**The Shirt Travels the Route the Bloodied Shirt Travelled**

The object's journey reverses an earlier one. Decades before, a garment belonging to Joseph was carried from a well in the wilderness to Jacob in Canaan, stained with blood that was not his, and produced as evidence of his death (12:18). Now a garment belonging to Joseph is carried from Egypt to Jacob in Canaan and produced as the means of his recovery. The same route, the same object type, the same recipient, and the same sender in both cases — the brothers, who carried the first lie and now carry the second truth. The chapter does not draw the comparison. It arranges the two journeys so that the reader draws it, which is the method it has used for every structural echo in the sūrah.

## Verse 12:94

> When the caravan departed ˹from Egypt˺, their father said ˹to those around him˺, “You may think I am senile, but I certainly sense the smell of Joseph.”

**He Is Speaking to People Around Him**

The verse specifies an audience. The commentators identify those present as members of his own tribe or household who were with him at the time, rather than the returning sons, who are still on the road. The specification resolves an apparent problem in the sequence: the brothers now know Joseph is alive, having met him in verse 90, so a remark dismissing their father's perception of Joseph cannot come from them while they are travelling. The people answering in verse 95 are therefore understood by some as those in Canaan. Other commentators attribute the reply to the sons, and the difference affects how verse 95 is read without changing what verse 94 says. The distinction changes the character of the doubt that follows. If the reply comes from his sons it is the scepticism of men who have spent twenty years disbelieving their father; if it comes from bystanders it is the ordinary reaction of a household to an old man's claim. Both readings make verse 95 a rebuke, and the chapter does not choose between them.

**The Verse Fulfils the Claim of Verse 86**

Jacob told his sons that he complained of his sorrow only to God and that he knew from God what they did not know (12:86). Verse 87 sent them to search for Joseph and his brother on the strength of that knowledge. Verse 94 is the knowledge itself, arriving as a scent before the search party returns. The chapter does not connect the verses explicitly, but the sequence is unambiguous: a claim to know, a commission to search, and then the arrival of the thing searched for ahead of those who were sent to find it. Jacob is shown to have been right about the fact and wrong about nothing else, and the sons who doubted him are still on the road.

**A Prophet Disbelieved by His Own Household**

The verse sets up a pattern the chapter will complete in verse 95 and reverse in verse 96. A man perceives something true, anticipates that he will not be believed, and is not believed. The dismissal comes from the people closest to him, who have watched his grief for decades and have concluded that it has damaged his judgement. The Qur'an's account of prophecy repeatedly involves this structure — a messenger whose household or town treats his knowledge as madness or delusion. Here the structure is domestic and the accusation is gentle: not heresy but senility. Verse 96 will report the arrival of the bearer of good news and the restoration of Jacob's sight, and the household's assessment will be withdrawn in his own words.

**Contrast With the Biblical Account of the Same News**

The parallel passage describes a different reaction. When Jacob's sons tell him that Joseph is alive and ruler over Egypt, the biblical account reports that his heart grew faint because he did not believe them, and that his spirit revived only when he saw the wagons Joseph had sent to carry him (Genesis 45:26–28). Disbelief precedes the evidence and is overcome by it. The Qur'an reverses the order entirely: Jacob perceives Joseph's approach before any messenger arrives, predicts the dismissal he will receive, receives it, and is then vindicated by the arrival of the thing he already sensed. The two accounts share the wagons and the revival; they differ about whether the father needed convincing. The difference concerns the father's epistemic position. In one account Jacob's spirit revives when he sees the wagons; in this one he is already convinced before the wagons are visible, and their arrival answers a household's doubt rather than his own. The Qur'an gives him knowledge that the evidence comes to ratify, and it makes that knowledge the subject of two verses.

## Verse 12:95

> They replied, “By Allah! You are definitely still in your old delusion.”

**The Shortest Verse in the Chapter's Final Movement**

Four words of dialogue occupy a verse of their own, and the allocation is a marker of weight. The chapter has used the same technique throughout its closing section: a promise given its own verse (12:61), a question given its own verse (12:71), a report given its own verse (12:81). Here a dismissal receives the same treatment. The brevity is not a lack of content but a concentration of it. Everything the family has believed about Joseph for decades, everything they have said to Jacob about his grief, and everything they are about to have corrected is contained in a single sentence, and the chapter lets the sentence stand alone. Its brevity gives the correction weight. A longer verse would have reasoned with the household, explained the scent or appealed to Jacob's record; this one asserts that they will find him neither senile nor deluded, and leaves the assertion to be tested by what arrives. The chapter is confident enough in the coming event to introduce it in a single unadorned sentence.

**Who Is Speaking, and When**

The identity of the speakers is disputed, and the dispute matters. Some commentators attribute the reply to those of Jacob's tribe who were with him in Canaan, which fits the timing: the caravan has only just departed Egypt (12:94) and the sons cannot yet be present. Others attribute it to the brothers, reading the exchange as part of a conversation that occurs on their return. The chapter does not settle the question, and both readings leave the sense intact: the people closest to Jacob dismiss his perception of Joseph. On the first reading the dismissal comes from a household that has never had cause to reconsider; on the second it comes from men who have just met the person being discussed.

**Two Charges in Two Verses**

Verse 85 accused Jacob of a grief that would kill him. Verse 95 accuses him of a delusion that has lasted decades. The two charges are different in kind: the first concerns the effect of his sorrow on his body, the second concerns the truth of his belief. Together they describe a family that has concluded both that their father is being destroyed by his mourning and that the object of the mourning is not real. The chapter does not have anyone answer these charges in argument. Verse 96 answers them with an event — a bearer of good news arrives, a shirt is cast over a face, and sight returns.

**Verse 96 Answers in Jacob's Own Words**

The reply is not an argument against the charge of delusion. It is a reminder that the charge was made against a claim already stated in verse 86 and now verified. The chapter's method is to answer a dismissal with an event and then let the dismissed person note that he had said so. Nothing in verse 95 is refuted directly; it is simply overtaken, in the space of one verse, by the arrival of what it denied.

**The Delusion Was the Only Accurate Belief in the Room**

The verse's irony is exact and the chapter states none of it. The people dismissing Jacob as deluded are either his household, who have believed for decades that a wolf ate his son, or his sons, who have just met that son in an Egyptian granary and watched him forgive them. On either reading the accusation is false in a way the accusers either cannot know or have just been shown. What the dismissed man believed — that Joseph was alive and that God would return all three sons to him (12:83) — is the only belief in the exchange that the sequel confirms. The chapter therefore closes the family's argument not by persuading anyone but by arriving at the father's position from a direction nobody in Canaan expected.

## Verse 12:96

> But when the bearer of the good news arrived, he cast the shirt over Jacob’s face, so he regained his sight. Jacob then said ˹to his children˺, “Did I not tell you that I truly know from Allah what you do not know?”

**A Bearer of Good News From Among the Brothers**

The verse names its messenger by function rather than by name: the bearer of good news. The commentators identify him as the same brother who interceded when the others plotted to kill Joseph and proposed the well instead (12:10). The identification is not in the Qur'anic text, but it fits the chapter's method of assigning pivotal acts to particular individuals, and it gives the scene a shape worth noting. The man who once persuaded his brothers to spare a child now carries the news that the child is alive, and he carries it in the garment the grown man has sent. The chapter does not comment on the arrangement and does not name him.

**Healing Reported Without a Mechanism**

The verse does not say how the shirt restored the sight. It does not describe a property of the garment, a fragrance, a medical effect or a stated miracle. The commentators supply explanations — a heavenly scent belonging to the shirt, a miracle granted because both father and son were prophets — and the Qur'an supplies none. The text states a command, a performance and a result. That refusal to explain is consistent with the chapter's treatment of every extraordinary element in it: Joseph's interpretations are attributed to divine teaching (12:21) without the teaching being narrated, and Jacob's knowledge is attributed to God (12:86) without its transmission being described. The consistency gives the episode its character. Sight is restored and no account is offered of how; a scent is perceived across a distance and no account is offered of that either. The chapter reports both events in the same register as it reports a famine forecast, treating them as statements about God's action rather than as phenomena requiring an explanation.

**Biblical Parallel Reverses the Order of Belief**

The corresponding passage describes a father who does not believe. When Jacob's sons tell him Joseph is alive and ruler over all Egypt, the biblical account reports that his heart grew faint, for he did not believe them; his spirit revived only when he saw the wagons Joseph had sent to carry him (Genesis 45:26–28). The Qur'anic sequence runs the other way. Jacob perceives his son's approach before any messenger arrives (12:94), anticipates the dismissal (12:94), receives it (12:95), and is then confirmed by the arrival of the shirt. Both accounts contain a father restored and a household convinced. They differ about whether the father ever needed convincing. The reversal changes who the verse is about. If belief arrives with the wagons the scene belongs to Jacob's relief; if belief precedes them the scene belongs to the household's error, and Jacob has been right throughout. The Qur'an chooses the second arrangement, and in doing so makes the father's knowledge the subject of three consecutive verses instead of an outcome reported in passing.

**One Son Returned, Two Still to Come**

The verse reports the healing and the reminder, and stops. Joseph is alive and has sent a garment; Benjamin's status is not mentioned here, and the eldest brother's vow (12:80) is not yet resolved. Verse 93's second command — bring me your family, all together — is still outstanding, and verses 99 and 100 will report its fulfilment. The chapter therefore places the restoration of Jacob's sight before the reunion of the family, so that the father sees his sons arrive rather than being told about them. Verse 83's hope concerned all three; verse 96 confirms one of them by an object, and leaves the rest to the journey that follows. The partial confirmation keeps the sequence moving.

**The Verse Completes the Chapter's Account of Knowledge**

Three people in this story have known more than those around them, and the chapter tracks each. Joseph knew his brothers from the moment they entered (12:58) and kept the knowledge for thirty-two verses. Benjamin was told privately and kept it for eleven (12:69). Jacob claimed knowledge from God while blind, grieving and dismissed (12:86), and verse 96 is his confirmation. The three cases differ in kind: Joseph's knowledge came from sight, Benjamin's from a disclosure, Jacob's from a source the text never names. What they share is that each was held against the judgement of the people nearby, and each was verified by an event rather than by an argument. Verse 96 is the last of the three verifications and the only one that restores something physical.

## Verse 12:97

> They begged, “O our father! Pray for the forgiveness of our sins. We have certainly been sinful.”

**A Second Confession, Addressed to the Other Victim**

The brothers have already admitted their fault. In Egypt, immediately after the disclosure, they told Joseph that God had preferred him over them and that they had certainly been sinful (12:91), and they were forgiven in the next verse (12:92). Verse 97 repeats the admission to a different person, in almost the same words, and asks for something different: not forgiveness from the injured party but intercession with God. The chapter requires two confessions because the crime had two victims. What was done to Joseph was done to a brother; what was done to Jacob was done to a father who was deceived for decades and whose sight failed from the grief. The second confession completes the moral accounting. An offence against Joseph has already been named, admitted and forgiven by Joseph himself; the offence against the father has not, and it is the offence that produced the blindness healed in the previous verse. The brothers address the injury whose consequences they can still see, in the person whose eyes have just been restored.

**They Ask Their Father to Pray**

The request is specific in form. *Istaghfir lanā* — seek forgiveness for us. They do not ask Jacob to forgive them, although the deception was directed at him. They ask him to pray, which transfers the matter to God and places their father in the position of intercessor rather than judge. The distinction is precise and it reflects the chapter's understanding of offences. An injury between people can be waived by the person injured, and Joseph waived his (12:92). A sin against God cannot be waived by anyone; it can only be asked about, and the asking is properly done through someone whose standing with God is acknowledged. The brothers acknowledge theirs in the form of the request. The intercessory form identifies where they believe the remaining problem lies.

**The Conversation Happens After the Return Home**

The commentators record that this exchange took place after the brothers came back from Egypt, which means the request is made in Canaan and not in the granary. The timing matters for the scene. Jacob has recovered his sight (12:96), has reminded his children of the knowledge he claimed (12:86), and is now facing the sons who deceived him decades earlier about the son whose shirt has just healed him. The confession arrives in that room. The chapter gives it a verse of its own and gives the answer another, which is the allocation it reserves for statements of weight. The allocation separates the request from its occasion. The brothers do not ask for intercession at the moment of their confession in Egypt, in front of Joseph, while the shame is fresh; they ask later, in Canaan, after everything has been reported to their father. The chapter gives the request its own space so that it reads as a settled intention rather than as a reaction.

**No Explanation, No Mitigation, No Excuse**

The verse is short and it contains nothing but the request and the admission. The brothers do not explain why they acted, do not describe their youth or their jealousy, do not claim that their father's preference for Joseph provoked them (12:8), and do not repeat the mitigation Joseph himself supplied in verse 89 — that they acted in ignorance. They state the fact and ask for prayer. The chapter's economy here is a moral judgement as much as a narrative one. A confession that explains itself is partly a defence; a confession that does not is what verse 97 contains, and verse 98 shows it accepted. That distinction is what verse 98 answers. A request for intercession supported by circumstances would invite an assessment of the circumstances; a request supported only by an admission invites mercy, and mercy is what Jacob promises. The chapter builds the exchange out of two halves — a confession containing nothing but itself, and a reply addressed to the One who forgives.

**Jacob's Answer Comes in the Following Verse**

The chapter separates the request from the response, giving each its own verse. Nothing in verse 97 predicts whether the prayer will be granted, promised or refused. Jacob has reason to refuse: he rejected their first report outright (12:18), doubted their second (12:83), was rebuked by them for his grief (12:85) and called deluded for his hope (12:95). The reader who has followed the chapter knows what verse 98 will say, and knows it because Jacob has already been described in verse 64 as a man who places his sons in God's keeping rather than holding them to account. The verse ends on the request, and the pause before the answer is the chapter's. The pause is the only hesitation the chapter allows him. Jacob has been described as patient, as knowing and as refusing to despair, and here he is asked by his sons to intercede for them; the request is recorded and nothing else is, with the answer arriving in full in the verse that follows. The reader is shown a father taking the request seriously enough to defer it.

## Verse 12:98

> He said, “I will pray to my Lord for your forgiveness. He ˹alone˺ is indeed the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

**"My Lord"**

The phrase is Jacob's habitual address and it appears at the moments of his greatest strain. He complained of his anguish and sorrow only to God (12:86); he described God as the best of keepers when refusing to trust his sons with Benjamin (12:64); he declared beautiful patience twice after losing a child (12:18, 12:83). The intercession promised here is made to the same Lord, in the same terms, by the same man. The chapter's portrait of Jacob is built from these short addresses, and the consistency is the point: the person who complained only to God is the person who prays for the people who caused the complaint. That continuity is the verse's quiet argument. The same address appears where Jacob redirects his grief and here, where he undertakes to intercede for the people who caused it. The chapter places both uses of the phrase in his mouth and lets the reader see that a complaint carried to God and a prayer offered for an offender belong to a single practice rather than to two temperaments.

**"The All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful"**

The two names close the verse, and their pairing is one the chapter has already used. Joseph ended his statement about the soul that commands evil with the same two names: *Surely my Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful* (12:53). Father and son both conclude a difficult speech with an assertion about God's capacity to forgive, and in both cases the assertion concerns offences they did not commit. The names are not a formula appended to a promise. They are the ground of the promise: a father can undertake to pray for the forgiveness of a deception because the One being prayed to is defined by forgiving. The two names are chosen for the situation rather than for their splendour. A deception sustained across decades requires a forgiveness that covers a long offence, and a household whose father was blinded by grief requires a mercy reaching the consequences as well as the act. Jacob names precisely the attributes the request depends on, and names them as the ground of his promise rather than as a hope.

**Both Injured Parties Have Now Prayed**

Joseph said: may God forgive you, and He is the Most Merciful of the merciful (12:92). Jacob says: I will pray to my Lord for your forgiveness, and He is the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. The two prayers cover the two halves of the offence and are recorded in adjacent scenes. The chapter's account of reconciliation is therefore complete before the family is physically reunited. Verse 99 reports the arrival in Egypt and verse 100 the prostration, but by the end of verse 98 nothing remains outstanding between the brothers and the people they wronged. The reunion that follows is the confirmation of a settlement already reached, not its occasion. The settlement is therefore complete before the family meets. Joseph has forgiven, the brothers have confessed twice, and Jacob has undertaken to ask God's forgiveness for them; nothing is outstanding when the household arrives in Egypt. The reunion that follows is not the moment the family is reconciled but the moment a reconciliation already achieved becomes visible to everyone in it.

**The Verse Ends the Family's Moral Arc**

From verse 8 to verse 98 the chapter has traced a single movement: envy, plot, disposal, false report, decades of grief, a second loss, a search, a disclosure, an admission, a forgiveness, a request and a promise of intercession. Verse 98 is the last of these, and after it the narrative has no further moral business to conduct. What remains is reunion, recognition of a dream, a migration and a prayer. The placement means that when the brothers appear in verse 100 they appear as forgiven men rather than as petitioners.

**Intercession Is Asked of a Father, Not of a King**

The brothers could have asked Joseph to pray for them. He had already done so in verse 92, and his standing with God is described throughout the chapter in knowledge taught, mercy conferred and reward preserved. They ask their father instead, and the chapter records the choice without comment. What it reflects is the shape of the offence: the deception was directed at Jacob, the grief was his, and the intercession properly belongs to him. Joseph's role in the reconciliation was to waive his own injury and to explain the larger arrangement (12:100). Jacob's role is to pray. The two roles are not interchangeable, and the chapter assigns each to the person it belongs to.

## Verse 12:99

> When they entered Joseph’s presence, he received his parents ˹graciously˺ and said, “Enter Egypt, Allah willing, in security.”

**He Received His Parents**

The verb denotes taking someone in, giving them a place, drawing them close to oneself. It is the same verb the chapter used when Joseph drew Benjamin aside to disclose himself (12:69), and its use here for the parents marks the reunion as an act of shelter rather than of ceremony. The verse specifies the parents and does not mention the brothers, who have already been received, forgiven and provided for. The order is one of honour: Joseph's first act on the family's arrival is directed at his father and mother, and the greeting given to them precedes anything said to the party as a whole. Verse 100 will continue the sequence by raising them to the throne.

**"Enter Egypt in Security"**

The word denotes safety, freedom from fear — the condition the chapter has been describing the absence of since verse 8. Joseph's own entry into Egypt was as cargo, hidden in a well and sold for counted coins (12:19–20). His brothers' entries were as anxious purchasers during a famine, once accused of theft publicly and once threatened with exclusion (12:60, 12:70). Jacob's household has lived for decades under a false report and a famine. The invitation names the opposite of all of it in a single word, and it is addressed to the whole family at the moment of arrival rather than after a period of adjustment. The timing makes it a welcome rather than a reassurance. Security announced after a period of adjustment would be a report; announced at the gate it is a gift conferred on arrival, and it answers the particular insecurity this family has lived with — a son sold into this country, a son detained in it, and a household that twice travelled to it afraid of starving.

**The Qur'an Uses the Same Formula Elsewhere**

The combination of entry and security appears in two other contexts worth noting. The angels' greeting to the inhabitants of Paradise is an invitation to enter in peace and security (15:46). And the Qur'an records the fulfilment of a vision given to the Prophet ﷺ in the same terms: *Allah willing, you will surely enter the Sacred Mosque, in security* (48:27). Verse 99 stands inside that pattern — an entry promised, an entry granted, and the granting marked by the removal of fear. The parallel with 48:27 is particularly close, since both concern a vision disclosed in advance and fulfilled at a stated destination, and both attach the divine exception to the promise. The shared formula also imports a shared condition. Wherever security is promised in these terms it is promised by God and held subject to His will, so it cannot be treated as a possession. The family entering Egypt receives something belonging to the category of divine grant rather than political protection, which is how the chapter has treated every other good thing in the story.

**"If God Wills" Attaches to a Promise of Safety**

The exception is not a hedge about whether the family will enter Egypt; they are standing at the entrance as the words are spoken. It attaches to the security. Joseph is a ruler with authority over the country's stores and a personal position with the king (12:54), and he still qualifies the promise of safety with a reference to divine will. The chapter has shown him doing the same at every point where his own power is greatest: he declines to absolve himself from the condition of the soul (12:53), attributes his preservation to God's turning of a scheme away (12:34), and ascribes his establishment to mercy given according to a will (12:56). The exception here belongs to that pattern rather than to uncertainty.

**Security Is the Chapter's Final Gift**

The sūrah's movement can be described as a passage from fear to security. A boy is afraid of a dream his father warns him not to relate (12:5). He is thrown into a well by people who intend his death (12:9–10). He is sold, imprisoned on a false charge, and forgotten by the one man who owed him a message (12:42). His father fears for a second son, weeps until his eyes fail, and is called deluded for hoping (12:84–95). Verse 99 names the end of that sequence in a single word, and it names it as something given rather than achieved. Nobody in the family secured themselves; they were fed, forgiven, healed and received. The passive runs through the entire sūrah. Joseph was saved from the well, taught, released and elevated; Jacob was given patience, knowledge of his son and the return of his sight; the brothers were fed, spared and forgiven. What arrives at the gate of Egypt is the last item in a long list of things done for this family rather than by it.

## Verse 12:100

> Then he raised his parents to the throne, and they all fell down in prostration to Joseph, who then said, “O my dear father! This is the interpretation of my old dream. My Lord has made it come true. He was truly kind to me when He freed me from prison, and brought you all from the desert after Satan had ignited rivalry between me and my siblings. Indeed my Lord is subtle in fulfilling what He wills. Surely He ˹alone˺ is the All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

**Raised to the Throne**

Joseph seats his parents on the throne. The commentators gloss the action as elevating them to a place of honour — the royal seat itself, according to one account — and the gesture is the chapter's clearest statement of what the elevation of verse 56 amounts to in practice. A man who has settled wherever he pleased in Egypt gives the highest seat in the room to his father and mother. The verse does not describe the throne's material or the setting, and it does not record any objection from the Egyptian court. What it records is an ordering of honour, performed in public, in which the ruler of the country's stores places two arrivals from the desert above himself. The publicity is the point.

**"This Is the Interpretation of My Old Dream"**

Joseph supplies the interpretation himself, and the word he uses is *taʾwīl* — the same term his father used when warning him not to relate the vision (12:6), the term the narrator used for what God would teach him (12:21), the term both prisoners asked for (12:36), and the term the court declared itself unable to supply (12:44). The chapter's central technical word is used throughout for the meaning of a dream as an event that will occur. Here it reaches its final instance: the interpretation of verse 4 is not a statement but a scene, in which eleven brothers and two parents bow to the boy who dreamt. Joseph names the connection himself, which is the only time in the sūrah that an interpretation is identified by the person who received it.

**"He Was Kind to Me When He Brought Me Out of Prison"**

The summary of thirty years mentions the prison and omits the well. The commentators explain the omission: Joseph had already told his brothers that there was no blame on them that day (12:92), and to name the well now would be to reopen a matter he had closed. The choice of the prison as the point of deliverance is also accurate in another sense. It was the prison that produced the two dreams, the cupbearer's memory, the king's summons and the office. What God brought him out of, in the sequence that led to this throne, was the cell — and the verse attributes the kindness to that event rather than to the injury that preceded it. The choice of reference is striking and not accidental. Joseph's summary of God's kindness passes over the well, the sale and the accusation, and even the elevation to office, to settle on the moment of release. What he remembers is being taken out of a place where he had been forgotten, and the verse lets that memory stand as the representative instance of favour in his life.

**"After Satan Had Ignited Rivalry Between Me and My Siblings"**

The explanation of the brothers' conduct is given to a third party. Joseph does not say they plotted, envied, sold him or lied. He says Satan stirred up hostility between him and his brothers, and the phrasing includes himself in the relation — *between me and my brothers* — rather than placing the fault entirely on one side. The commentators describe the move as tantamount to making an excuse for them, consistent with the mitigation he offered in verse 89 when he said they acted in ignorance. The chapter's account of the crime is thus stated three times in three registers: as an act of the brothers' souls made to seem fair to them (12:18), as an act done in ignorance (12:89), and as an enmity stirred up by Satan (12:100).

**"The All-Knowing, the All-Wise"**

The verse closes with the two names, and they close the story in the same terms in which Jacob held it during its worst point. Verse 83 ended with the same pairing, spoken by a blind father who had just rejected his sons' report and hoped for the return of three children. Verse 100 ends with the pairing spoken by a son seated in front of that father, with the family assembled and the dream fulfilled. The chapter's last theological statement is therefore identical to its darkest one, and the identity is the argument: the names that made sense as hope in Canaan make sense as fact in Egypt, and nothing about them has changed except what the speaker can now see. That is the verse's final claim about the story. Every element of it — the dream, the well, the shirt, the prison, the famine, the cup — was already arranged when it happened, and the names invoked here describe the arrangement rather than a response to it. Joseph's recognition adds nothing to the events; it changes his description of them.

## Verse 12:101

> “My Lord! You have surely granted me authority and taught me the interpretation of dreams. ˹O˺ Originator of the heavens and the earth! You are my Guardian in this world and the Hereafter. Allow me to die as one who submits and join me with the righteous.”

**The Chapter's Only Extended Prayer From Joseph**

Joseph prays several times in this sūrah, and the chapter quotes him briefly each time: a plea for the women's scheme to be turned away (12:33), a preference for prison over what he is being called to (12:33), a statement about the soul that commands evil (12:53). Verse 101 is different in kind. It is a sustained address with a structure — gifts acknowledged, God named, a request for the manner of his death, a request for his company afterwards. The chapter places it at the end of the narrative, after the prostration, after the interpretation of the dream, after the explanation of the brothers' conduct. Everything Joseph has been given is now in his hands, and the first thing he does with it is pray.

**"You Have Granted Me Authority"**

The word denotes sovereignty, dominion, rule — and the prayer qualifies it. Joseph has been given something of it, not all of it. The commentators explain the qualification by reference to his actual position: the highest standing in Egypt next to the king (12:54, 12:56). He controls the granaries, settles wherever he pleases, and holds a personal attachment to the ruler, and he describes all of it as a portion. The verse is the chapter's own assessment of worldly power, spoken by the person holding most of it. Nothing in the prayer claims the authority as earned, deserved or permanent; it is stated as a gift received, in the same clause as the second gift.

**"And Taught Me the Interpretation of Dreams"**

The second gift is named with the same verb the chapter has used from the beginning. Joseph's father told him that God would teach him the interpretation of dreams (12:6); the narrator reported that God established him in Egypt to teach him that interpretation (12:21); two prisoners asked him for it (12:36); a court declared itself unable to supply it (12:44). Verse 101 is the only place where Joseph himself states that he was taught. The prayer therefore confirms the chapter's central claim about his knowledge from inside his own mouth, at the end of the story, after every prediction he made has been verified by events. The verification is what makes the claim modest rather than proud. Every interpretation Joseph offered has been fulfilled: two prison dreams, a king's vision of fifteen years, a blind father's sight, and a reunion he predicted to his brother in confidence. He names the skill at the end of the record, after the reader has watched it work, and attributes it to the One who taught it.

**"My Guardian in This World and the Hereafter"**

The word denotes a protector, an ally, one who is close to a person and responsible for him. The Qur'an states the general form of the claim elsewhere: *Allah is the Guardian of the believers—He brings them out of darkness and into light* (2:257), and it describes those under that protection as people for whom there will be no fear and no grief (10:62). Joseph applies the term to himself across both abodes, which is a larger claim than protection in this world alone. He has been guarded in a well, in a market, in a household, in a prison and in a palace, and the prayer states that the same guardianship extends past death. The extension makes the prayer more than thanksgiving. Joseph has been guarded through a well, a slave market, a locked room and a prison, and the verse asks that the same relationship continue where no event can confirm it. He describes a guardianship tested across thirty years and requests it beyond the period in which it is capable of being tested.

**"Allow Me to Die as One Who Submits"**

The request concerns the manner of his death rather than its timing. The Qur'an makes the same request a general instruction to believers: *“Be mindful of Allah in the way He deserves, and do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission ˹to Him˺”* (3:102). Joseph's formulation is personal and it is placed at the moment of his greatest success, which gives it a particular force. A man who has just been bowed to by his family, who holds authority over a country and who has seen a childhood vision fulfilled asks not for more of any of it but that he should die in a state of submission. The chapter's ranking of the two worlds, stated in verse 57, is here enacted.

The Qur'an opens another sūrah with the same title: *“All praise is for Allah, the Originator of the heavens and the earth”* (35:1).

## Verse 12:102

> That is from the stories of the unseen which We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. You were not present when they ˹all˺ made up their minds, and when they plotted ˹against Joseph˺

**Story Ends, Address Begins**

Verse 101 closed with a prayer spoken by Joseph. Verse 102 turns away from the narrative entirely and addresses the person recounting it. The shift is abrupt and complete: no transition, no summary, no closing formula for the story. The chapter has told a continuous account for a hundred verses and now steps outside it to say where the account came from. The sūrah's structure is therefore framed — it opens with a statement about revelation (12:1–3) and closes its narrative with a statement about revelation — and the framing is what tells the reader that the story was never the chapter's only subject. The shift is abrupt and unsignposted. A hundred verses of continuous narrative close with a prayer, and the next verse turns to a reader who has been invisible until now. The chapter's structure claims that the narrative existed for the sake of the address, and the absence of any transition is what makes the claim without having to argue for it.

**A Council That Was Private**

The moment specified is not a public event. The brothers' deliberation is described in verses 8 to 10 as a conversation among them — a proposal to kill Joseph or cast him into a land where he would be forgotten, a counter-proposal to throw him into a well, an agreement. Nobody outside the group was present, and the chapter has already noted that the plan was concealed from the father, executed with a false report and supported by a bloodied shirt (12:16–18). Verse 102 selects that scene as the test case. A private conspiracy, hidden from the only adult in the family and never disclosed until decades later, is the kind of thing whose detailed narration requires a source other than observation.

**The Unlettered Prophet**

The Qur'an describes its recipient in terms that bear directly on this verse: the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, whose description people find in the Torah and the Gospel (7:157). The description is relevant to the argument in verse 102 because it removes the ordinary explanations for detailed knowledge of the past. A person who does not read cannot have acquired the account from scripture; a person who was not present cannot have acquired it from the participants; a person who lived centuries later cannot have acquired it from witnesses. The verse states the absence and the Qur'an states elsewhere the literacy; together they are the chapter's claim about its own origin. The two statements work as one argument. A narrative containing details unavailable to any living source, delivered by a person without access to written accounts, leaves the audience with a question the chapter does not need to answer because it has already framed it. What is asserted is not the truth of the story but the impossibility of its having been assembled.

**Why This Verse Belongs to This Chapter**

Verse 102 could have closed any of the Qur'an's narrative sūrahs, and it closes this one. The reason is that no other chapter contains a comparable concentration of private, unwitnessed material: a family council, a concealed plot, a false report, a woman's locked room, a prison conversation, a search conducted in a calculated order, a cup planted in a bag, a disclosure made to one brother and withheld from nine. Almost nothing in the story was public at the time it happened. The chapter therefore has the strongest internal reason to make the argument in verse 102, and it makes it once, at the end, after the reader has encountered the private material a hundred times.

**Story and Claim Held Together**

The verse does not detach the narrative from its purpose. It says *that* — referring back to the account just completed — is from the accounts of the unseen. The demonstration and the claim occupy the same verse, which means the chapter expects the story it has told to function as the evidence for the claim it now makes. The sūrah's method is not to argue for its origin in the abstract but to supply a narrative and then ask where it came from. The method distinguishes the chapter's argument from a doctrinal one. No premise is stated in advance, no authority is invoked at the opening, and no conclusion is drawn until the narrative has run its full length.

## Verse 12:103

> And most people will not believe—no matter how keen you are—

**Guidance Is Not the Messenger's to Give**

The Qur'an states the principle explicitly elsewhere: *You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills, and He knows best who are ˹fit to be˺ guided* (28:56). Verse 103 is the narrative application of that ruling. The chapter has shown a prophet with every advantage — divine knowledge, a verified forecast, a country's gratitude, a family's confession, a public exoneration — and it now states that none of these produces belief in most of those who encounter them. The verse separates the messenger's task from the messenger's outcome, and it does so at the point in the chapter where the task has been most visibly successful. That placement keeps the statement safe from a cynical reading. Following a story of failure it would have sounded like an excuse; following a story of total success it can only be a description of how the success occurred. Joseph did not guide his brothers — he fed them, detained them, disclosed himself and forgave them — and the guidance arrived as something other than his doing.

**The Same Claim Runs Through the Qur'an**

The statement that most people will not believe appears in several places, and the repetition is deliberate. It is a claim about a proportion rather than about a person, and it functions as a standing correction to the expectation that clear evidence produces conviction. The chapter's own story supplies an instance in miniature: a torn shirt examined by a witness inside a household established Joseph's innocence (12:26–28), and the household imprisoned him anyway (12:35). A public confession before a king established the same thing (12:51), and the years already spent were not returned. Evidence and belief are not treated in this sūrah as cause and effect. The pattern continues past the story: the brothers saw a cup produced from a bag they had watched loaded, and their first response was to slander a brother they believed dead (12:77).

**Why This Follows a Story of Success**

The placement of verse 103 is the point. Verses 1 to 101 have described a prophetic life that ends in complete vindication: the dream fulfilled, the family reconciled, the country fed, the injury forgiven, the prayer answered in advance. Verse 103 states that most people will not believe. The two statements are held together without reconciliation, and the effect is to detach the value of the story from its outcome. Joseph's success did not depend on general belief, and the messenger receiving this account is being told that his own will not either. The chapter is describing what prophetic work consists of when the numbers are against it. The placement refuses the reader an easy inference. Joseph's story ends with a family saved, a country fed and a dream fulfilled, and the verse that follows says most people will not believe however much the messenger desires it. The chapter will not let a successful narrative become a promise about success, and it corrects the inference in the same breath as it completes the story.

**What the Verse Is Not Saying**

Three misreadings are excluded by the wording. The verse does not say belief is impossible, only that most will not arrive at it; the quantifier leaves the remainder open. It does not say the messenger's effort is wasted, since the effort is described as keen and is not criticised. And it does not say the message is deficient, since verse 104 immediately states that no reward is asked for it and that it functions as a reminder addressed to the whole world. What remains is a claim about an audience and nothing more.

**"Most People" Leaves Room for the Rest**

The quantifier is not universal. Most will not believe; the verse does not say all, and the chapter itself is full of the exceptions. A prisoner believed enough to carry a message, eventually (12:45). A king recognised competence and trusted it (12:54). A woman confessed before her sovereign (12:51). Ten brothers admitted their fault twice (12:91, 12:97). A father knew from God what his household did not (12:86). The verse's claim about proportions is compatible with a story in which individuals are repeatedly moved by evidence they cannot dismiss, and the chapter keeps both facts in view without reconciling them. The exceptions are not marginal either: they include the ruler of the country, the accuser who prosecuted Joseph, and the entire family that injured him.

## Verse 12:104

> even though you are not asking them for a reward for this ˹Quran˺. It is only a reminder to the whole world

**No Payment Is Requested**

The verse completes the sentence begun in verse 103, and the completion supplies an argument. Most people will not believe, however keen the messenger is — even though he asks them for nothing in return. The Qur'an states the same claim in the Prophet's own voice elsewhere, listing the prophets who were rightly guided and instructing him to follow their guidance: *“I ask no reward of you for this ˹Quran˺—it is a reminder to the whole world”* (6:90). The formulation is therefore not local to this chapter. It belongs to the Qur'an's standing account of itself: a message delivered without charge, by a person with no financial interest in its acceptance. The absence of charge is also a claim about the messenger's position. A man who earns nothing from belief has no incentive to manufacture it, and the verse puts that fact in his own mouth as a disclaimer. The chapter offers it immediately after a story in which its hero was sold for money and later distributed grain for nothing.

**"A Reminder to the Whole World"**

The phrase describes the message's scope and its function in a single clause, and the Qur'an uses it of itself elsewhere: *Surely this ˹Quran˺ is only a reminder to the whole world* (81:27). The noun denotes a reminder, a mention, something that calls to mind what is already known rather than introducing a wholly new object. The scope is universal — not a tribe, not a city, not a generation. Verse 104 states the Qur'an's own self-description at the end of its longest continuous narrative.

**Reminder as the Chapter's Own Method**

The word describes what the preceding hundred verses have been doing. The sūrah does not argue for God's existence, establish a legal code, or refute a named opponent. It tells a story in which a series of propositions are enacted rather than asserted: that concealed planning does not reach its destination (12:52), that mercy is given according to a will (12:56), that the soul commands evil unless mercy intervenes (12:53), that the reward of the mindful and patient is not lost (12:90). A reader who accepts none of these as doctrines can still recognise them in the events. That is what a reminder requires — not assent to a premise but recognition of a pattern, and the chapter supplies the pattern in narrative form precisely because recognition works differently from argument.

**What a Reminder Does and Does Not Do**

The category explains verse 103. A reminder can be refused without contradiction, since it does not compel assent; it presents what is already available to be recognised. That is why eagerness does not produce belief and why the absence of a fee does not secure acceptance. The chapter's own story illustrates the mechanism repeatedly: a torn shirt reminds a household of a fact it can see (12:26–28), a confession reminds a court of a fact it has heard (12:51), a question reminds ten brothers of a crime they committed (12:89). In each case the reminder supplies nothing new. It re-presents what the hearers already have, and the hearing is the variable. That is why the chapter's argument is narrative rather than demonstrative. A proof addresses someone who lacks information; a reminder addresses someone who has it and is not attending. The hundred verses behind this one supply no new premise and are not intended to: they are a story arranged so that what the hearer already knows becomes unavoidable while he reads it.

**Contrast With the Chapter's Only Sale**

The sūrah contains one transaction in which a payment is made for a person. Joseph is sold for a few counted dirhams by men who set no store by him (12:20), and the chapter uses the smallness of the price to measure the smallness of their regard. Verse 104 states that no price is asked for the message. The contrast is not drawn by the text and does not need to be: a chapter whose central injury was a sale ends by declaring that what is being offered to the world is offered free. The same money is later returned to the brothers unasked (12:62), and the pattern of gratuitous giving runs from that act to this verse. The pattern gives the disclaimer its weight. Joseph was priced at twenty dirhams and ended up distributing grain without charge to the family that priced him; the message he is now associated with asks for nothing either. The chapter has spent a hundred verses describing gifts that nobody paid for, and this verse describes the message in the same terms.

## Verse 12:105

> How many signs in the heavens and the earth do they pass by with indifference

**Two Locations Named**

The signs are in the heavens and in the earth, and the pairing covers the whole of a person's ordinary experience. Above: the sun, the moon, the stars, the alternation of night and day, the rain that ends a famine. Below: the crops, the soil, the animals, the ruins of previous peoples, the bodies people inhabit. The chapter's own narrative has depended on both categories. Joseph's forecast turned on rainfall and harvest (12:47–49); his vision concerned eleven stars, a sun and a moon (12:4); his deliverance from famine turned on grain stored in the earth. The verse names the same two domains and states that they are full of signs that are passed by. Nothing in the two domains requires travel, education or revelation to encounter; the heavens are above every person and the earth beneath every person, and both are visible daily. The verse describes not rare phenomena missed through ignorance but constant ones missed through inattention, which is a different and more indictable failure.

**Turning Away From What Is Seen**

The commentators explain the verse in reflection: people continuously encounter signs indicating the being of their Creator together with His wisdom, power and oneness, and they do not consider what the signs mean or take a lesson from them. The Qur'an puts the same point as a question elsewhere: *Who then does more wrong than those who deny Allah's revelations and turn away from them?* (6:157). The wrongdoing identified is not ignorance but avoidance — a person who has the evidence in front of him and declines to look at it. Verse 105 describes the avoidance as habitual: passing by is something people do repeatedly, without effort and without noticing. The habituality is what makes the diagnosis serious. A person who rejects a sign after examining it has performed an act; a person who walks past it has performed nothing, and the verse treats that nothing as the commoner and more dangerous condition. Most of the signs in this chapter's own story were missed by the people nearest them, including a shirt and a dream.

**The Historical Reading**

One line of commentary connects the verse specifically to past civilisations and the traces they leave behind. On that reading the signs in the earth include the ruins, the roads, the abandoned settlements and the recorded ends of peoples who were more powerful than those now walking among their remains. The reading gives the verse a concrete object and links it to verse 109, which asks whether the deniers have not travelled through the land to see the end of those destroyed before them. The story of Joseph is itself a sign in the earth — an account of a real country, a real famine and a real family, preserved and reported — and verse 109 will ask whether people have travelled through the land and seen how earlier generations ended.

**Why the Verse Follows the Story**

The placement is deliberate and it changes how the story is to be read. Verse 102 claimed that the account of Joseph is among the reports of the unseen revealed to the Prophet ﷺ. Verses 103 and 104 stated that most people will not believe and that the message is a reminder requiring no payment. Verse 105 says that people pass by signs indifferently. The chapter is therefore presenting itself as a sign and asking whether it will be treated like the others. The narrative that occupied a hundred verses is not an illustration appended to a doctrine; on the chapter's own account it is one of the things being passed by. That placement turns the narrative into an instance of its own teaching. The reader who has just spent a hundred verses attending closely to a shirt, a cup and a dream has demonstrated the opposite of passing by; the verse then asks how most people behave toward the signs in the heavens and the earth. The story stands beside the diagnosis as its counter-example.

## Verse 12:106

> And most of them do not believe in Allah without associating others with Him ˹in worship˺

**Association Is Not Limited to Carved Objects**

The commentators extend the category considerably. What is set alongside God is not restricted to physical idols; it includes whatever a person treats as ultimate — desire, power, an idea, one's own judgement. The Qur'an makes the same extension elsewhere, describing the person who takes his own desire as his god and is left astray by God on account of his knowledge. The broadening is significant for how the verse is read after a hundred verses of narrative. Joseph's brothers acted on a preference of their own and described it as fair (12:18); a woman pursued a desire until it governed a household and then a city's gossip (12:30); a prisoner chose his own release over a promise made to a fellow inmate (12:42). None of these is idolatry in the ritual sense, and the commentators' reading of this verse covers all of them.

**The Verse Follows Directly From the Previous One**

Verse 105 described people passing by signs in the heavens and the earth with indifference. Verse 106 states what their belief looks like as a result of that habit. The connection is causal in the chapter's logic: inattention to evidence does not usually produce denial, it produces a mixed conviction in which God is acknowledged and other things are admitted alongside Him because nothing has been sorted out. The sūrah's diagnosis is therefore not that its audience lacks belief but that the belief it has is unsorted, and the sorting requires an attention the audience has not given. Nothing in the verse proposes that additional evidence would resolve this; the signs are already abundant, and the problem identified is what happens to them.

**"Most of Them" Continues the Quantifier**

The proportion stated in verse 103 returns here and narrows the claim. Most people will not believe; most of them do not believe without association. The repetition of the quantifier is deliberate. Verse 103 concerned belief in the message; verse 106 concerns the character of the belief that does exist. The chapter is describing an audience in which outright rejection is not the dominant category. The dominant category is partial belief, and the verse treats partial belief as a condition requiring correction rather than as a stage on the way to full belief that will resolve itself. That treatment explains why the following verses turn to security and invitation rather than to further argument about God's existence. The turn is significant. Having established that most people will not believe, the chapter neither withdraws its address nor lowers its claim; it moves to what a person can actually rely on — security from an unexpected punishment, and an invitation stated in the first person by a messenger asking for nothing. The quantifier limits the outcome, not the offer.

**A Diagnosis Rather Than a Verdict**

Nothing in verse 106 assigns a punishment, closes a door or declares anyone beyond reach. It describes a religious condition in the present tense, and the verses that follow move immediately to questions about security and then to an invitation to a way declared publicly (12:107–108). The chapter's method in its closing section is consistent: state the condition, ask whether the audience feels safe inside it, and then declare what is being offered. Verse 106 belongs to the first of those three movements. It tells the reader what the obstacle is, and it does so in terms that most of its audience would recognise in themselves rather than in their neighbours — which is what makes it a diagnosis rather than an accusation. Locating it in themselves is the verse's purpose. A verdict would identify a group and close the matter; a diagnosis describes a condition the reader can test for in the ordinary business of a day during which signs are constantly present and constantly unfelt. The chapter ends its argument by asking the reader to examine his own attention rather than somebody else's belief.

## Verse 12:107

> Do they feel secure that an overwhelming torment from Allah will not overtake them, or that the Hour will not take them by surprise when they least expect ˹it˺

**Two Questions About a Sense of Safety**

The verse does not ask whether people are right or wrong in what they believe. It asks whether they feel safe. The rhetorical form is characteristic of the Qur'an's closing arguments, and the two questions cover two different kinds of ending: a punishment that overtakes a people within this world, and an Hour that arrives without warning and finds them unprepared. The verse's target is not ignorance but composure — the settled assumption that nothing will interrupt the present arrangement. After diagnosing inattention in verse 105 and mixed belief in verse 106, the chapter identifies the emotional condition that sustains both, and it identifies that condition as a feeling rather than as a position.

**"An Overwhelming Torment"**

The commentators gloss the word in two directions. Some take it as a thunderbolt, a sudden destructive stroke from above. Others read it more generally as an enveloping punishment, something that covers a people entirely so that no part of them escapes and no one is left outside it. Both readings preserve the same essential feature: the punishment is not proportionate to human defence. It is not an event that can be prepared for, negotiated with, insured against or survived by resources. The chapter's own narrative contains an instance of a different kind — a famine that no household could survive without central planning across fifteen years (12:48) — and verse 107 describes something that no planning addresses at all.

**Security as the Verse's Central Word**

The chapter has used the same noun twice in the immediately preceding verses, and both uses point in the opposite direction. Joseph invited his family to enter Egypt in security, with the divine exception attached to the promise (12:99), and the security he offered was given rather than assumed. Verse 107 asks whether an audience that has received no such invitation still feels secure. The contrast is between a safety conferred by God on a family that had lived for decades in fear and under a false report, and a safety claimed for itself by people who have passed by the signs. The chapter does not draw the comparison explicitly; it places the two uses of the word eleven verses apart.

**Why the Question Follows the Story**

The narrative has just demonstrated, over a hundred verses, that nothing in it went as its participants planned. The brothers intended an ending and got another. Zulaykhā intended a seduction and produced a confession before her sovereign. A cupbearer forgot a message and remembered it years later. A king's dream produced a government. Joseph asked to die submitting rather than to keep any of what he had been given (12:101). Verse 107 turns that demonstration outward and asks whether the reader has drawn any conclusion about his own arrangements. The question is not supported by argument inside the verse; it is supported by the preceding story, which is the chapter's chosen method of making it. The support is empirical on the chapter's own terms. Every reversal in this narrative reached the people affected without warning: the brothers did not expect Joseph in Egypt, the cupbearer did not expect to remember him, Jacob did not expect the caravan, and the household did not expect the shirt to work. The verse asks whether its readers are safe from the same pattern.

## Verse 12:108

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “This is my way. I invite to Allah with insight—I and those who follow me. Glory be to Allah, and I am not one of the polytheists.”

**The Command to Declare**

The verse opens with *qul*, say — the imperative the Qur'an uses whenever a statement is to be made publicly in the Prophet's own name rather than in the narrator's. The command is significant after three verses of diagnosis. Verses 105 to 107 described an audience in the third person: they pass by signs, they associate others with God, they feel secure from an overwhelming punishment and from the Hour. Verse 108 changes the grammatical person and puts words into the messenger's mouth. The chapter's closing argument therefore ends not with an analysis of its audience but with a declaration by the person addressing it, and the declaration is ordered before the analysis has been resolved or answered by anyone. The order of the two acts is deliberate. A messenger who waited until his message had been accepted would never declare it, and the verse places the declaration first, unconditionally, with the results described afterwards and left where they belong. The chapter has just watched Joseph do exactly this: state the plan, request the office, and let the outcome rest with God.

**"With Insight"**

The commentators gloss the term as that by which a person distinguishes with certainty between truth and falsehood — insight in the sense of settled conviction rather than rhetorical skill or persuasive technique. The specification answers verses 105 and 106 directly. People pass by signs without reflecting on them; belief remains mixed because nothing has been sorted out and no distinction has been drawn. The invitation declared here is made on the basis of certainty about which is which, and it offers the hearer the same clarity rather than a competing claim requiring adjudication. The chapter has just shown what insight looks like in practice: a prisoner reading a dream nobody else in Egypt could read, and reading it as a fifteen-year agricultural programme with a year of relief attached.

**The Verse Answers the Whole Closing Section**

Verses 102 to 108 form a single movement: an account of where the story came from, a statement that most people will not believe however keen the messenger is, a declaration that no reward is asked and that the message is a reminder to the whole world, an exclamation about signs passed by, a diagnosis of mixed belief, two questions about false security, and finally a way declared publicly in the first person. Verse 108 is the response to all of it, and it is the only verse in the sequence that offers something rather than describing something. The chapter's structure at the end mirrors its narrative: a long account of what happened to one prophet, followed by a short statement of what is now being offered by another.

## Verse 12:109

> We only sent before you ˹O Prophet˺ men inspired by Us from among the people of each society. Have the deniers not travelled through the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them? And surely the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter is far better for those mindful ˹of Allah˺. Will you not then understand

**Messengers Were Men From Their Own People**

The verse states a fact about the whole history of revelation, and the fact answers an objection the Qur'an records elsewhere: why has no angel been sent down to this messenger. The answer is that messengers have always been human beings drawn from the populations they were sent to, receiving inspiration rather than possessing a different nature. The Qur'an makes the same point repeatedly — a messenger from among yourselves, speaking the language of his people, eating food and walking in the markets. Verse 109 belongs to that series, and its placement at the end of a narrative sūrah gives it an illustration ready to hand: Joseph was a man, sent among a people who did not know him, whose knowledge came by revelation rather than by status or training.

**The Verse Restates the Chapter's Own Case**

Read against the story just completed, verse 109 describes the account the reader has been given. A man was sent among a people not his own, received knowledge by inspiration rather than by study, and was recognised by a ruler with no prior acquaintance with him. The ends of those who plotted against him are recorded in the same narrative: a household disgraced by a public confession, a plot that produced the opposite of its intention, a prison that produced an office. And the ranking of the two abodes was stated by the narrator in verse 57 and enacted by Joseph in verse 101, when he asked to die submitting rather than to keep any of what he had been given. That prayer is the chapter's own demonstration of the preference. A man holding the granaries of Egypt, reunited with his family and vindicated before a court, asks to die in submission and to be joined with the righteous rather than to keep any of it. Verse 109 states the ranking; verse 101 has already enacted it, in the words of the person with most to lose by it.

**Why the Verse Belongs at the End**

The chapter could have closed with verse 108's declaration. Verse 109 continues past it into a general statement about revelation, its messengers and their outcomes, and the continuation serves a purpose: it places Joseph's story inside a series rather than leaving it as a single case that might be dismissed as unusual. The reader who has just been told that this account came from revelation is now told that every account of the same kind has had the same shape — a human messenger, a people, delay, refusal, an end. Verse 111 will draw the conclusion from that series in explicit terms. Verse 109 supplies the series, and supplies it in the plural so that the rule covers cases the reader has not been shown.

## Verse 12:110

> And when the messengers despaired and their people thought the messengers had been denied help, Our help came to them ˹at last˺. We then saved whoever We willed, and Our punishment is never averted from the wicked people

**Despair as a Stage in Prophetic Work**

The verse begins at the lowest point. Messengers are described as having despaired — reaching the end of expectation, after prolonged effort and without result. The Qur'an treats this state as part of the prophets' experience rather than as a failure of faith in them, and it describes their human reactions to trial elsewhere in similar terms. Verse 110 generalises what this chapter has narrated in detail. Jacob hoped against every available fact and was called deluded for it (12:83, 12:95). Joseph spent years in a prison after being declared innocent, forgotten by the one man who owed him a message (12:42). The chapter has already supplied the instance; the verse supplies the pattern. Naming despair as a stage rather than as a failure is the verse's comfort. The messengers described here are not reproached for reaching a point where they expected nothing; that point is presented as part of the sequence, followed by help. Jacob reached it and was told not to; Joseph reached it in a prison and was released from it, and the chapter records both.

**"Then Our Help Came to Them"**

The turning is abrupt and it is attributed entirely to God. Nothing in the verse describes a change in circumstances, a shift in the people's attitude, or an improvement in the messengers' position that produced the help. The help arrives, and the verse marks its arrival with a particle of suddenness. The chapter has narrated the same structure twice: a cupbearer's memory after years of forgetting (12:45), and a shirt arriving at the moment a father was called deluded (12:96). In both cases the help came without any antecedent that the participants could have pointed to, and in both cases it came after the expectation had been exhausted. That timing is the chapter's recurring structure. Help reached Joseph after years in a prison where he had been forgotten; it reached Jacob after his sight had gone and his household had sworn he was senile; it reached the messengers after their own expectation of a response had been used up. Each arrival is reported as sudden, and each was preceded by a long wait.

**"Whoever We Willed Was Saved"**

The salvation is selective and the selection is not explained. The verse does not say that the saved were the righteous or that the destroyed were the worst; it says that whoever God willed was saved. The formulation is consistent with the chapter's theology throughout: mercy falls on whom God wills (12:56), elevation is given to whom God wills (12:76), and the reward of the good-doers is not discounted (12:56, 12:90). The three statements are held together in the sūrah without being harmonised, and verse 110 adds the fourth — deliverance arrives according to a will that the verse does not analyse. Refusing to analyse it is consistent with the chapter's treatment of everything else. Elevation was willed rather than calculated, healing was attached to a command rather than to a property of cloth, and a fifteen-year forecast was fulfilled without being narrated. Deliverance belongs to the same category, and the verse reports the fact of a will without offering the reader its reasons.

**"Our Punishment Is Not Turned Back From the Guilty"**

The final clause states the other half of the pattern. Help comes, some are saved, and the punishment is not diverted from the guilty. The Qur'an makes the same point elsewhere when asking who is more unjust than the one reminded of his Lord's signs and then turns away from them, adding that He will exact retribution. The clause prevents verse 110 from being read as an unconditional promise of rescue. Deliverance is real and it arrives, and it does not arrive for everyone. The chapter's own story contains the distinction: a household was disgraced by a confession, a prisoner was executed on the interpretation of his own dream (12:41), and a family was reconciled.

**The Timing of the Help Is Not Negotiable**

Nothing in the verse allows the messengers to bring the help forward. It comes when it comes, and the only marker given is that it arrives after despair — that is, after expectation has been exhausted rather than while it is still intact. The chapter's narrative observes the same rule with precision. Joseph's release required a cupbearer's memory, and the memory came years late (12:42, 12:45). Jacob's sight required a shirt from Egypt, and the shirt arrived after he had been called deluded for hoping (12:95–96). Neither man could have accelerated either event, and neither is shown trying. Verse 110 states the rule that the narrative has already obeyed: help is timed by the one who sends it, and the despair that precedes it is part of the timing rather than an obstacle to it.

## Verse 12:111

> In their stories there is truly a lesson for people of reason. This message cannot be a fabrication, rather ˹it is˺ a confirmation of previous revelation, a detailed explanation of all things, a guide, and a mercy for people of faith

**"A Lesson for People of Reason"**

The noun denotes an admonition, a lesson, something that moves a person from one state to another by way of an example. Those addressed are people of intellect — a phrase the Qur'an uses for the audience capable of drawing an inference from what is presented to them. The specification is important after verse 103, which stated that most people will not believe however keen the messenger is. The lesson exists and is available; the verse names the capacity required to take it. The chapter's own story supplies the exercise: a dream reported in verse 4 and fulfilled in verse 100, with a hundred verses of arrangement between them, is a lesson of exactly this kind.

**"It Is Not a Fabricated Account"**

The denial answers the charge the Qur'an records elsewhere, that its narratives are legends written down and recited. The verse's argument is not that fabrication is impossible but that this account cannot be one, and the ground is given in the next clause: it confirms what came before. Verse 102 made the related claim from the other direction — the recipient was not present at the events and had no access to them — and verse 111 makes it from the side of the text's relation to prior scripture. Verse 102 argued from the messenger's lack of access — he was not present at the council and could not have known what was said there; this verse argues from the text's agreement with what came before it.

**"A Guidance and a Mercy for People Who Believe"**

The final two terms are joined, and the joining has a condition attached. The guidance and the mercy belong to a people who believe, which does not mean the text withholds itself from others but that its effect is realised in them. Verse 104 described the same message as a reminder to the whole world, with no restriction; verse 111 describes its guidance and mercy as belonging to believers. The chapter holds both claims without reconciling them, and the same structure appears in verse 103: most people will not believe, and the message is addressed to everyone. Availability is universal; benefit is conditional. The condition is what makes the verse an address rather than a summary. A statement about a text describes its contents; a statement about belief describes the reader's position relative to them. The chapter ends by telling its audience that the hundred verses behind this one are capable of guiding and of showing mercy, and that the capability is realised on the reader's side.

**The Chapter's Verdict on Itself**

Verse 111 is the only place in the sūrah where the text evaluates its own content directly. Everything before it is narrative, address or diagnosis. The verdict has four parts: the stories contain a lesson, the account is not invented, it confirms prior revelation and elaborates all things, and it is guidance and mercy for believers. The four parts answer the four questions the closing section raised — where this came from (12:102), why most people refuse it (12:103), what is being asked in return (12:104), and what it is for (12:111). The chapter ends by stating its own purpose in a single verse, after a hundred verses of letting a story carry the argument. Stating the purpose at the end reverses the ordinary order of an argument, and the reversal is the point. A reader told in advance what the story meant would have read it as an illustration; a reader told afterwards has already lived through it, and the statement arrives as a description of something that has happened to him. The sūrah closes by naming what it has just done.

**From the Opening Verses to the Closing One**

The sūrah began with disconnected letters, an oath by a clear Book, and a declaration that it was sent down as an Arabic recitation so that people might understand (12:1–3). It ends with a lesson, a denial of fabrication, a confirmation, an elaboration, a guidance and a mercy. The frame is complete and the middle is a single continuous narrative with no interruption except the doctrinal verses placed inside it. The chapter's claim about itself is therefore not argued separately from its content; the content is the argument. A reader who has followed the story from the dream in verse 4 to the prostration in verse 100 has already been given what verse 111 describes, and the final verse names it.
