# Sūrah an-Naml (Chapter 27) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Language of Creation and the Throne of Sheba**

An-Naml, "The Ant," is the twenty-seventh chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 93 verses of mature Makkan revelation. It is the middle chapter of the *Ṭā-Sīn* tri-sūrah cluster and takes its title from the delightful and profound scene in verses 18–19, where an ordinary female ant (*namlah*), observing the advancing legions of Solomon, warns her colony to enter their subterranean dwellings lest they be crushed unawares by Solomon and his soldiers—prompting Solomon to smile in joy and offer a profound prayer of gratitude for the divine favors bestowed upon him and his parents. Sūrah an-Naml is also unique in scriptural codex geography as containing the second *Basmalah* of the Qur'anic text: appearing not as a chapter header, but inside verse 30 within the diplomatic letter sent by King Solomon to Bilqīs, the Queen of Sheba: *“Indeed, it is from Solomon, and indeed, it is: 'In the name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.'”* Sūrah an-Naml is a magnificent theological celebration of divine knowledge, illustrating how absolute worldly power, technological mastery, and knowledge of the natural world can be harmonized with supreme spiritual humility and surrender to the One God.

**The Structural Architecture and the Court of Solomon**

The architecture of Sūrah an-Naml moves from prophetic signs to imperial diplomacy, and from geological cosmology to Last-Day reckoning. The opening movement (1–14) proclaims the Qur'an as good tidings for the believers, recounts Moses at the burning bush where his staff becomes a serpent and his hand becomes radiantly white without disease, and depicts Pharaoh's obstinate rejection: *“And they rejected them unjustly and arrogantly, while their souls were convinced of them”* (27:14). The second and celebrated movement (15–44) presents the court of David and Solomon: Solomon inheriting the kingdom, marching with his hosts through the Valley of the Ants, missing the lapwing (*al-Hudhud*), and receiving the bird's extraordinary intelligence report about the wealthy Queen of Sheba (*Bilqīs*) who, along with her people, worshipped the sun instead of Allah. It traces the diplomatic exchange: Solomon's monotheistic letter, the Queen's gift rejected by Solomon, the miraculous transportation of the Queen's massive throne in the twinkling of an eye by a courtier endowed with knowledge of the Scripture (*ʿindahu ʿilmun min al-Kitāb*), the test of the modified throne, the palace courtyard paved with crystalline glass that the Queen mistakes for a pool of water, and her triumphant declaration of faith: *“My Lord, indeed I have wronged myself, and I submit with Solomon to Allah, Lord of the worlds”* (27:44). The third movement (45–58) reviews the fate of Thamūd, where nine conspirators who plotted to assassinate Ṣāliḥ and his family were wiped out overnight, followed by the rescue of Lot from his depraved town. The fourth movement (59–93) delivers the magnificent litany of cosmic rhetorical questions: *“Is not He [better] who created the heavens and the earth and sent down for you rain... Is there a deity with Allah?”* (repeated five times, 27:60–64), introduces the mysterious apocalyptic sign of the *Beast of the Earth* (*Dābbat al-Arḍ*, 27:82) who will speak to humanity when judgment is near, describes the mountains passing away like clouds on that Day (27:88), and concludes with the Prophet's personal manifesto: *“I have only been commanded to worship the Lord of this city [Makkah], who made it sacred, and to Him belongs all things, and I have been commanded to be of the Muslims, and to recite the Qur'an.”*

## Verse 27:1

> Ṭâ-Sĩn. These are the verses of the Quran; the clear Book

**The Same Two Letters, a Different Neighbour**

Ṭā Sīn does not stand alone in the Qur'an. The chapter immediately before it opens *Ṭâ-Sĩn-Mĩm* (26:1), and so does the chapter immediately after it (28:1). An-Naml sits in the middle of that run with one letter removed. The three sūrahs are usually treated as a set, and they share their subject matter: each tells the story of Mūsā at length, each answers the charge that the revelation is poetry or sorcery, and each builds toward the same claim about who sent it.

The variation in the opening letters therefore tracks something real rather than decorating the page. Ash-Shuʿarāʾ and al-Qaṣaṣ frame an-Naml, and an-Naml is the shortest of the three and the only one that carries the Solomon material. Some later commentators, including al-Rāzī, read the missing *mīm* as a signal that this chapter handles the shared theme in a compressed form. That reading cannot be proved, but the pattern it notices is plain on the page: the same signature, slightly altered, on three consecutive chapters that do related work.

**"The Verses of the Quran" — Signs Before They Are Sentences**

The word translated *verses* is *āyāt*, and its ordinary Arabic sense is signs or marks. The Qur'an uses the same word for the alternation of night and day, for rain that revives dead ground, and for the units of its own text. Calling a sentence an *āyah* therefore makes a claim about what kind of thing it is: not a line of composition but a piece of evidence, pointing past itself to the one who placed it there.

That distinction matters in a chapter which will shortly report Pharaoh's court calling the miracles of Mūsā *“This is pure magic.”* (27:13) — a response that treats a sign as a performance.

**Reciting and Writing: The Quran and the Book Are Two Descriptions**

The verse names the same revelation twice, using two words that are not synonyms. *Qur'ān* comes from the root *q-r-ʾ*, to recite or read aloud; it is revelation as sound, delivered by a voice, carried in memory. *Kitāb* comes from *k-t-b*, to write; it is revelation as a fixed and recorded text. The two names describe one thing from two sides, and the Muslim community's handling of the revelation followed both from the start: memorisation in the chest and transcription on whatever material was available.

Exegetes split on the reference of *the clear Book*. Some, including al-Rāzī, take it as the Preserved Tablet, the heavenly record described as *“a glorious Quran, ˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet.”* (85:21–22), so the verse would mean that these recited signs come from a written original above. Others, al-Qurṭubī among them, take it as the Qur'an itself, the two names stacked for emphasis. The second reading fits the sentence better: both phrases are predicates of the same *these*, and the natural sense of *these are the verses of X, the clear Book* is one object described twice. The first reading remains theologically sound but imports a referent the sentence does not supply.

**Ten Chapters, One Formula, Sharpened Here**

The phrase *these are the verses of* opens several chapters in almost identical form: *“Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom.”* (10:1), and again *“These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom.”* (31:2). Yūsuf, ar-Raʿd and al-Ḥijr use the same construction with their own adjectives attached. The formula is fixed; the descriptive word attached to it changes from chapter to chapter, and that change is where the meaning lies.

Here the chosen word is *mubīn*, clear, rather than *ḥakīm*, wise. Luqmān and Yūnus emphasise the wisdom folded into the text; an-Naml emphasises its legibility. That choice suits what follows, since this chapter's stories turn on whether evidence gets read correctly. Mūsā's signs arrive *as a clear sign* and are named sorcery. The hoopoe brings news that is tested before it is believed. Bilqīs examines a palace floor and revises her judgment. A Book that opens by calling itself clear is announcing the standard by which every character in it will be measured.

## Verse 27:2

> ˹It is˺ a guide and good news for the believers

**A Book Does Two Jobs at Once**

The revelation just named is now described by what it does. *Hudan* — guidance — is direction for someone who has to move: it tells a person which way to walk and what to avoid. *Bushrā* — good news — is the announcement of an outcome that has not yet arrived. Guidance addresses the road; good news addresses the destination. Put together, they cover the whole span of a religious life, the effort and the reward, and neither term can stand in for the other.

The order is not reversible. Guidance comes first because the good news is conditional on following it; a promise announced to someone with no path to walk would be cruelty rather than mercy. The Qur'an's habit is to pair these two words tightly and in this sequence. The same pairing appears when the revelation is defended against those who attacked its messenger angel: *“confirming what came before it—a guide and good news for the believers.”* (2:97), and again when the charge of forgery is answered: *“as a guide and good news for those who submit ˹to Allah˺.”* (16:102). Three passages, three different controversies, one formula — the Book defines itself by function each time, not by origin alone.

**The Believers Are Named Before They Are Described**

The verse stops at *the believers* and leaves the category unfilled. That gap is filled immediately afterward by a list of three practices. The sequence matters: faith is named first as an inward reality, then given outward content. Had the order been reversed, the Book would appear to reward behaviour alone; had the description been omitted, *believer* would be a word anyone could claim without cost.

Arabic grammar marks the join. The definite article on *al-muʾminīn* points forward to a group the sentence is about to identify, and the following verse opens with a relative pronoun that completes the reference. The two verses are one sentence divided across a pause. Reciters treat verse 2 as an incomplete stop for exactly this reason: the voice rests but the syntax leans forward. This construction — a claim, then its qualification — is the Qur'an's standard method for preventing a label from becoming a slogan. It happens at the start of al-Baqarah as well, where *“a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺,”* (2:2) is followed at once by a list of what such people actually do.

**Three Openings, Three Audiences**

Comparing this verse with its closest parallels shows how precisely the Qur'an adjusts a repeated formula. Luqmān opens with the same two verses in almost the same words, but with a different beneficiary: *“˹It is˺ a guide and mercy for the good-doers—”* (31:3). Here the second noun is *good news* and the recipients are *the believers*; there it is *mercy* and the recipients are *the good-doers*. Al-Baqarah opens with guidance directed to those who are mindful of God.

Three chapters, three descriptions, and none of them interchangeable. Mercy suits al-Luqmān, a chapter of a father's counsel and God's forbearance. Good news suits an-Naml, a chapter that will spend most of its length on rescues — Mūsā saved from fear, Solomon given a kingdom, Ṣāliḥ and Lūṭ delivered while their peoples are destroyed. The promise stated abstractly in the second verse is demonstrated as narrative for the rest of the chapter. Al-Rāzī observed that the Qur'an's openings function as headings for what follows, and this pairing supports him: the word chosen at the top is the word the stories will illustrate.

## Verse 27:3

> ˹those˺ who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and have sure faith in the Hereafter

**"Establish" Is a Stronger Verb Than "Perform"**

The Arabic does not say they pray; it says they *establish* prayer, *yuqīmūn al-ṣalāh*. The verb comes from the root *q-w-m*, to stand upright, and in this form it means to set something up straight, to make it stand and keep standing. A crooked wall can be built; it cannot be *established* in this sense. Applied to prayer, the word covers the physical uprightness of the worshipper, the correctness of the act, and its steady repetition over time.

Commentators have drawn out each of those layers. Ibn Kathīr takes establishment to include performing prayer at its proper times with its conditions met, not merely going through its motions. Others add constancy: a prayer offered once is performed, but a prayer offered through every season of a life is established. The distinction has a practical edge. Arabic has a perfectly ordinary verb for doing a prayer, and the Qur'an consistently avoids it in favour of this one. What is being described is not an event in a person's schedule but a fixed structure in a person's life, and the difference is exactly the difference between a tent and a building.

**Certainty Is a Higher Bar Than Belief**

The third item shifts vocabulary. The verse does not say they *believe* in the Hereafter; it says they are certain of it, *yūqinūn*, from *yaqīn*, a word for knowledge that has no doubt left in it. Arabic distinguishes the two states, and the Qur'an uses the distinction deliberately. Belief can coexist with a private hesitation. Certainty is what remains after the hesitation is gone.

Setting the bar there has consequences, because the Hereafter is the one article of faith with no sensory support at all. Prayer can be seen; poverty relieved by alms can be counted; the resurrection cannot be inspected. The Qur'an still asks for *certainty* on precisely this point, and the reason becomes visible in the next verse of this chapter, where disbelief in the Hereafter is identified as the root from which a whole distorted life grows. Ibn Kathīr understands the certainty here to cover the full set — resurrection, the reckoning, the Garden and the Fire — rather than a vague expectation of something after death. The definite content is what makes the conviction operational. A person who is sure there will be an accounting behaves differently from a person who suspects there might be.

**The Same Three Marks, Placed in a Different Argument**

This verse appears almost word for word at the opening of Sūrah Luqmān: *“those who establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and have sure faith in the Hereafter.”* (31:4). The repetition is exact, which makes the surrounding difference instructive. In Luqmān the Book is called a guide and mercy for *the good-doers*; here it is a guide and good news for *the believers*. Same description, two different labels attached to the same people.

Al-Baqarah performs a related move with a different list, marking its people by belief in the unseen, prayer, spending, acceptance of earlier revelation, and then closing on the same clause: *“and have sure faith in the Hereafter.”* (2:4). Certainty about the Hereafter is the fixed element across all three openings while the other items vary. That consistency is a claim in itself. Whatever else distinguishes the Qur'an's model believer, the non-negotiable is that he lives as though the accounting is real. The following verse turns to those who do not, and traces what happens to a life built on the opposite assumption.

## Verse 27:4

> As for those who do not believe in the Hereafter, We have certainly made their ˹evil˺ deeds appealing to them, so they wander blindly

**"We Have Made Their Deeds Appealing" — a Difficult Attribution**

The hard word in the verse is *We*. The beautification of evil deeds, *zuyyina*, appears many times in the Qur'an, and the agent changes. Sometimes it is Satan, as in this very chapter: *“For Satan has made their deeds appealing to them—hindering them from the ˹Right˺ Way and leaving them unguided—”* (27:24). Sometimes the verb is passive, with the agent left out: *“Their evil deeds have been made appealing to them.”* (9:37). And here, God states it of Himself.

The three formulations are not in conflict once the levels are distinguished. Satan is the immediate tempter, the one who presents the act as attractive. The human being is the one who accepts the presentation. God is the one in whose creation this sequence can occur at all, and who has made it a standing law that persistent refusal hardens into inability to see. That law is stated plainly elsewhere: *“We turn their hearts and eyes away ˹from the truth˺ as they refused to believe at first, leaving them to wander blindly in their defiance.”* (6:110) — where the turning away follows the refusal and does not precede it. The order is the whole answer. Divine beautification is the confirmation of a choice already made, not its cause.

**Beauty as the Instrument of Ruin**

The Qur'an's choice of verb deserves attention. Evil is not made easy or made permitted; it is made *beautiful*. A person walking into destruction because the path looks pleasant is a far more accurate picture of moral failure than one who walks in knowing exactly what he is doing. Almost nobody chooses harm under the description of harm. The choice is always made under some description that makes it look like gain, safety, loyalty or justice.

The root *z-y-n* is the ordinary Arabic word for adornment and decoration — the same root behind the word for jewellery. The Qur'an uses it for the honest beauty of the sky, *“We have indeed adorned the lowest heaven with the stars”* is the pattern, and it uses it for this fatal cosmetic effect on deeds. The overlap is the point. The faculty that responds to beauty is a good faculty. It is exactly the faculty that gets captured. Once it is captured, the person does not need to be argued into wrongdoing, and no argument will easily pull him out, because what he is defending is not a conclusion but a taste.

**Wandering Without Direction Is the Punishment, Not the Prelude**

The verse ends with *yaʿmahūn*, translated *they wander blindly*. The verb describes movement without a destination — a person in a trackless place, going back and forth, covering distance and arriving nowhere. It is not stupidity and it is not idleness. Considerable energy is expended in this state.

Placed as the verse's final clause, the wandering is the outcome of the beautified deeds, and this is where the verse shows what kind of punishment it is describing. There is no thunderbolt here, no seized city, no drowned army — all of which this chapter will supply later for other peoples. The penalty in this verse is purely internal and entirely invisible from outside: a life lived at full speed in no direction, felt by the one living it as freedom. Al-Qurṭubī describes the state as swinging between sin and confusion and treating serious matters as play. That is a description of a man who cannot take his own existence seriously, which is a reasonable outcome for someone who has concluded it leads nowhere.

## Verse 27:5

> It is they who will suffer a dreadful torment, and in the Hereafter they will ˹truly˺ be the greatest losers

**A Verdict Written in the Language of Trade**

*Al-akhsarūn* — the greatest losers — comes from *khusr*, a word that belongs to commerce before it belongs to theology. It names the merchant who returns from a venture with less capital than he set out with. Arabic used it for spoiled goods, short measures and failed expeditions, and the Qur'an borrows the term without softening it.

The metaphor runs through the Book and shapes how disbelief is described. Guidance is treated as a commodity a person can trade away: *“They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided.”* (2:16). The image is exact and unflattering. A man who denies the Hereafter has not made an intellectual error in this framing; he has made a bad deal. He handed over something of permanent value and received something that expires. Al-Ṭabarī reads the superlative here against that background, and the reading fits: calling them the *greatest* losers ranks them within a market where everyone is trading, which is what makes the label sting. They were not passive. They were active, confident, and wrong about the price.

**"It Is They" — a Construction That Excludes**

Both halves of the verse use an emphatic Arabic construction, *ulāʾika lahum* and *wa hum*, which the translation renders with *It is they* and *they will ˹truly˺ be*. This is not decoration. The form restricts the predicate to the subject named, in the way English does by stressing a pronoun: *they* are the ones, meaning the others are not.

The restriction is doing real work in a chapter about misread evidence. In the world the Qur'an's first audience lived in, the men who denied the Hereafter were not obviously losing. They held the trade routes, the sanctuary and the social power, and the believers were the ones visibly short of everything. Verse 5 reverses the apparent ledger and does so with grammar rather than argument, refusing to let the category of loser stay attached to where it visibly seemed to belong. Al-Zamakhsharī, who was attentive to these constructions, noted that the emphatic pronoun functions as a correction of an assumed opinion. Somebody had a list of losers. This verse hands back a different one.

## Verse 27:6

> And indeed, you ˹O Prophet˺ are receiving the Quran from the One ˹Who is˺ All-Wise, All-Knowing

**The Address Turns to One Man**

After five verses about groups — believers, deniers, losers — the sentence swings round to a single person. The Arabic verb is second-person singular, and the emphatic particle *inna* with the pronoun attached makes it insistent: you, specifically, are the one receiving this. Everything said so far about the Book has been in the third person. Now the Book's recipient is addressed directly and told where his material comes from.

The verb *tulaqqā* is worth pausing on, because it is passive. Muḥammad is not described as composing, gathering or even seeking the Qur'an. He is described as being met with it, having it handed to him. Arabic could have said he recites it or he brings it; the form chosen removes him from the position of author entirely. This matters because the accusation in circulation was exactly the opposite — that a man in Mecca was producing these verses himself, or having them produced for him. The grammar of the verse denies the charge before any argument is offered, simply by putting the Prophet in the receiving position and someone else in the giving one.

**A Hinge Between Two Halves of the Chapter**

Al-Rāzī and al-Qurṭubī both read this verse as a joint: it closes the opening section and opens the narrative one. Everything before it describes the Book; everything after it, from verse 7 to the end, mostly tells stories. Placed exactly at the seam, this verse explains why the stories are being told and on what authority.

The placement solves a real problem for the audience. Mūsā at the fire, Solomon and the ant, the destruction of Thamūd — these are events no Meccan witnessed and most had no reliable record of. A listener could reasonably ask how the man reciting them knows. Verse 6 supplies the answer in advance: he is receiving them from the One who was present and who knows how to tell them. This is not an incidental piece of framing. The Qur'an uses the same device in Sūrah Hūd and Sūrah Yūsuf, attaching a statement about the source of the report to the beginning of a long narrative sequence. The stories are presented as testimony, and testimony requires a witness to be named.

**Hearing the Book Through Its Source**

A strand of later Islamic spirituality reads this verse past its immediate context and applies it to every reciter. The argument runs that if the Qur'an is genuinely *from the presence* of the Wise and Knowing, then reciting it while thinking only of the words on the page misses what the sentence claims. Aḥmad al-ʿAlawī and others in that tradition held that nobody is counted among those firmly grounded in knowledge — the phrase comes from *“As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, “We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.””* (3:7) — until he recites the Book as though hearing it from its source rather than reading it from a text.

This is an extension of the verse, not its plain sense, and it should be labelled as such. The verse addresses the Prophet about his own situation. But the extension is a defensible inference rather than an imposition, because it takes the verse's own logic — that the Book's value derives from where it comes from — and asks what follows for the person holding it. What follows is a standard of attention. A message that arrives from the Wise and Knowing deserves to be received with the alertness one brings to words from someone who knows more than we do. The chapter's first narrative, beginning in the next verse, opens with a man who notices something on a dark horizon and goes to investigate.

## Verse 27:7

> ˹Remember˺ when Moses said to his family, “I have spotted a fire. I will either bring you some directions from there, or a burning torch so you may warm yourselves.”

**Two Requests, Both Modest, Both Answered Beyond Asking**

His sentence offers alternatives: *some directions* or *a burning torch*. The Arabic is *bi-khabarin*, literally a piece of news, and *bi-shihābin qabas*, a flame taken from a larger fire. Both are small requests, and the commentators observe that both were granted in a form vastly larger than the request.

He asked for news of the road. Ibn Kathīr and al-Rāzī agree that the family had lost their way in the dark, and news here means the information a local would give a stranger. What arrived was news of an entirely different order — the identity of the speaker, the fate of Pharaoh, the mission of the man himself. He asked for fire to warm his family. What he found was a fire that did not warm, that did not consume, and that gave him something he could not carry back. The structure is a pattern the Qur'an uses elsewhere: a servant asks within the limits of his situation, and the answer breaks the frame of the question. Mūsā's request was reasonable and his need was real. Neither is mocked. They are simply overtaken.

**The Parallel Accounts Fill Each Other In**

The same night is reported three times in the Qur'an, and the versions are not identical. Ṭā Hā gives the setting: *“When he saw a fire, he said to his family, “Wait here, ˹for˺ I have spotted a fire. Perhaps I can bring you a torch from it, or find some guidance at the fire.””* (20:10). Al-Qaṣaṣ supplies what neither of the others does — the timing and the place: *“When Moses had completed the term and was travelling with his family, he spotted a fire on the side of Mount Ṭûr.”* (28:29).

Reading them together gives the full scene: Mūsā has finished his years of service in Madyan and is on the road with his household when he sees the fire on the flank of the mountain. The three accounts also differ in emphasis. Ṭā Hā records that he told them to wait; an-Naml omits that and keeps only his plan; al-Qaṣaṣ keeps both but adds the geography. This is how the Qur'an handles repeated narrative — not by copying a passage but by selecting from it according to what each chapter is doing. An-Naml is building toward a series of encounters with the unseen, so it keeps the perception and the plan and drops the logistics.

**The Word for Family and Who Was Actually There**

*Ahl* is translated *his family*, and the word's range in Arabic is wide. It can mean a household, a clan, the people of a town, or a wife alone. Al-Rāzī records the view that only Mūsā's wife was present, which is one legitimate sense of the term and fits a man returning from Madyan with the woman he married there.

Later reports add children and servants to the party, but the Qur'an does not, and the reticence is probably deliberate. What the narrative needs is that Mūsā was not alone and that he had dependents who were cold. The exact headcount changes nothing. There is a further point in the word's ambiguity: the plural address in his speech — *bring you*, *so you may warm yourselves* — uses a form that Arabic also employs for respectful address to one person. The grammar permits either reading, which is why the commentators could not settle it from the text alone. Where the Qur'an leaves a detail open, the responsible position is to say so rather than to pick.

## Verse 27:8

> But when he came to it, he was called ˹by Allah˺, “Blessed is the one at the fire, and whoever is around it! Glory be to Allah, the Lord of all worlds

**Who Is Blessed — a Question the Grammar Leaves Open**

The sentence *būrika man fī al-nār wa man ḥawlahā* has occupied commentators for centuries, because Arabic permits several readings and the text supplies no object for the verb. Normally *bāraka* takes something to bless. Here it stands alone, in the passive, and the reader must decide who is meant.

Al-Ṭabarī records the main positions with their grounds. One reading takes *the one at the fire* as God Himself, whose light was present in it, so the blessing falls on the manifestation. A second takes it as Mūsā, the one who had come to the fire, with the angels around him — so the blessing falls on both. A third notes that the Arabic *man*, ordinarily *who*, can carry the wider sense of *what*, giving *blessed is what is in the fire*, meaning the fire itself. A fourth, reported by al-Thaʿlabī, supplies an elided word: blessed is the one *seeking* the fire. Al-Zamakhsharī connects the whole clause to the parallel in al-Qaṣaṣ, where the location is called *the sacred ground*, and concludes the blessing attaches to the place. None of these can be eliminated on grammatical grounds, and the honest summary is that the sentence blesses the whole scene — fire, ground, angels and prophet — without separating them.

**Fire or Light?**

A second problem is what Mūsā actually saw. He called it a fire, and the narrative accepts his word for it in the previous verse. Yet fire burns, and nothing in any account of this night is burned. Several commentators concluded that what he saw was light, *nūr*, and that he named it fire, *nār*, because that is what a light at night looks like from a distance to a man who needs one.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Thaʿlabī note that the Arabs sometimes used *nār* where *nūr* was meant, the two words differing by a single vowel and sharing the image of brightness. Ibn Kathīr transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās that it was not fire but a flickering light, and elsewhere that he called it the light of the Lord of the worlds. This is not an attempt to explain a miracle away. It sharpens the episode: Mūsā went out to fetch a burning brand and found something that gives light without consuming, which is the exact inversion of what he wanted it for. His request was for fire; what he received was illumination, and the two Arabic words sit one vowel apart.

**The Veil of Light**

Discussion of this verse regularly draws in a report about God's relation to light, and it is worth stating accurately. Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī reported that the Prophet ﷺ stood among them and told them five things: that God does not sleep and it does not befit Him to sleep; that He lowers the scale and raises it; that the deeds of the night are taken up to Him before the deeds of the day, and the deeds of the day before those of the night; and that His veil is light — and were He to remove it, the splendour of His face would burn away everything of His creation that His sight reached (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 179a). One transmitter of the same report gives *fire* in place of *light*.

The relevance is direct. If the veil is light and its removal would consume, then a prophet summoned to speak with God must be met by something screened. A light in a fire on a mountainside is exactly such a screening — enough to draw a man across the dark, not enough to destroy him. The report also explains why the accounts stay auditory. What Mūsā received was a voice, and the visible element was a covering rather than a disclosure.

## Verse 27:9

> O Moses! It is truly I. I am Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise

**How This Differs From "I Am That I Am"**

The obvious comparison is with the Hebrew scriptures, where Mūsā at the same mountain asks for a name and receives *ehyeh asher ehyeh*, variously rendered *I am that I am*, *I am who I am*, or *I will be what I will be* (Exodus 3:14). The Qur'anic version is not a variant of that exchange, and the difference is instructive rather than argumentative.

In the biblical passage, Mūsā asks the question and the answer is a phrase whose grammar has been debated for two millennia — whether it names, refuses to name, or promises presence. In the Qur'anic passage, Mūsā asks nothing and the answer is a name plainly given: *Allāh*. The ambiguity that exists in the Arabic is about sentence structure, not about whether an identity was disclosed. Where the two traditions converge is in the reading, held by many Jewish and Christian interpreters, that the Hebrew phrase is itself a name or contains one. On that reading both accounts report the same event in the same terms: at the fire, God told Mūsā His name. The Qur'an is simply more direct about it.

**Mighty and Wise: Attributes Fitted to a Mission Against Pharaoh**

Two names follow the self-identification, and their selection is precise. *Al-ʿAzīz* means the invincible, the one who cannot be overcome — the root carries strength, scarcity and inaccessibility together. *Al-Ḥakīm* means the wise, the one whose acts are placed correctly.

Consider what Mūsā is about to be sent to do. He must return to the country he fled as a wanted man and confront the most powerful ruler of his world with a demand. The two obvious failure modes are that the sender lacks the power to back him, or has the power but no plan. The names answer both, in order. Al-ʿAzīz addresses the question of whether Pharaoh can win; al-Ḥakīm addresses the question of whether this mission makes sense. The pairing is not generic. Just three verses earlier the Qur'an was named as coming from the All-Wise, All-Knowing (27:6), where knowledge and wisdom were the relevant attributes for a book. Here, at the start of a confrontation, might replaces knowledge. The Qur'an selects divine names to fit the work in front of them.

**A Voice Without a Body, and What the Silence Covers**

The verse reports speech and nothing else. No form is described, no direction is given for where the voice came from, no account of what the fire looked like at this moment. This restraint is consistent across the Qur'an's treatment of the incident in all three chapters, and it is theologically load-bearing rather than merely stylistic.

The immediately preceding verse declared *Glory be to Allah, the Lord of all worlds.* (27:8), which is a statement that God is beyond likeness to created things. Having said that, the narrative cannot then describe a shape in the flames without contradicting itself. What remains is speech — and speech, in Islamic theology, became the point on which this episode turns. Mūsā's traditional title, *kalīm Allāh*, the one God spoke to, derives from this night and from the further statement in an-Nisāʾ that God spoke to Mūsā directly. The encounter is remembered for what was heard, not for what was seen, and the verse's silence about everything visual is the reason. The first command follows in the next verse, and it concerns something Mūsā was already holding.

## Verse 27:10

> Now, throw down your staff!” But when he saw it slithering like a snake, he ran away without looking back. ˹Allah reassured him,˺ “O Moses! Do not be afraid! Messengers should have no fear in My presence

**The First Order Concerns Something Already in His Hand**

God's opening command to Mūsā is not to travel, preach or gather anyone. It is to drop what he is carrying. The staff was the most ordinary object in his possession — Ṭā Hā records his own description of it: *“It is my staff! I lean on it, and with it I beat down ˹branches˺ for my sheep, and have other uses for it.”* (20:18). A shepherd's tool, worn smooth by use, with no significance whatever.

That is precisely why it was chosen. The sign Mūsā will carry to Pharaoh is not a borrowed instrument or a manufactured wonder; it is the stick he walks with, transformed. Al-Rāzī observes that a miracle worked through an object the prophet already owns removes any suspicion of equipment being supplied. There is a further point in the command's structure. He is told to throw it down — to let go of the thing he leans on. The mission begins with a man giving up his support, and what happens next is that the support becomes terrifying. Before Mūsā is given power, he is shown that the source of it is not under his control.

**Running Without a Sideways Glance**

The staff moves *tahtazzu ka-annahā jānn* — quivering as though it were a serpent. The word *jānn* denotes a small, fast snake rather than a large one, and commentators noted the choice: the terror is in the speed and the wrongness of the movement, not in the size. Elsewhere the same transformation is described with a word for a large serpent, and the two descriptions are usually reconciled as the size of a great snake with the quickness of a small one.

Mūsā's reaction is reported without any softening. He turned and ran, *wa lam yuʿaqqib* — without coming back. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī gloss the phrase as running without even turning his head to the side, so complete was the flight that he did not risk a sideways glance. This is a prophet at the moment of his commissioning, and the Qur'an records him fleeing. The candour is characteristic. Nothing is gained by presenting the men God chose as incapable of ordinary fear, and something important is lost, because the reassurance that follows would be meaningless addressed to someone who was never afraid.

**Ṭā Hā Tells the Same Moment Differently**

The parallel accounts arrange this scene in their own ways, and the differences are informative. In Ṭā Hā the sequence is expanded into a conversation: God asks what is in his right hand, Mūsā answers at length about his sheep, and only then comes the instruction *“Throw it down, O Moses!”* (20:19), followed by the reassurance *“Take it, and have no fear. We will return it to its former state.”* (20:21). In al-Qaṣaṣ the reassurance is put as *“O Moses! Draw near, and have no fear. You are perfectly secure.”* (28:31).

Three versions of one event, each shaped to its chapter. Ṭā Hā, the longest, includes the exchange about the staff's uses and the promise of restoration. Al-Qaṣaṣ adds the command to come closer — the opposite of running — and a flat declaration of safety. An-Naml keeps neither, and instead supplies the general principle about messengers, which none of the others has. That principle is what this chapter needs, since it is building a series of prophets and will shortly place Solomon, Ṣāliḥ and Lūṭ alongside Mūsā. A statement about what is true of messengers as a class belongs in a chapter that surveys several of them.

## Verse 27:11

> ˹Fear is˺ only for those who do wrong. But if they later mend ˹their˺ evil ˹ways˺ with good, then I am certainly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**An Exception That Points at the Listener**

The previous sentence said messengers have no fear in God's presence. This one adds a qualification, and the qualification is aimed. Mūsā is standing at the fire as a man who left Egypt because he had killed someone. Al-Qaṣaṣ records the incident plainly: he intervened in a fight, struck an Egyptian, and the man died — *“So Moses punched him, causing his death.”* (28:15). He fled the country over it.

Al-Ṭabarī reads the clause as an oblique reference to that event, and the reading fits the psychology of the scene. Mūsā has just been told that messengers are safe here. A man with his history could reasonably wonder whether the reassurance covers him, or whether he has been listed under a category he no longer qualifies for. The verse answers the unasked question. It first admits that wrongdoing is a real ground for fear, then immediately supplies the route out of it. Nothing about this is abstract theology; it is a specific man being told, at the moment of his appointment, that the thing in his past has a remedy.

**Why Some Commentators Fought the Plain Reading**

The particle *illā*, rendered here as the exception introducing *those who do wrong*, normally means *except*. Read straightforwardly, the two verses together say: messengers have no fear before Me — except one who did wrong. That puts a wrongdoer inside the class of messengers, and a number of scholars resisted the implication.

Al-Ṭabarī preserves their alternative. They read *illā* in a connective sense, giving: messengers have no fear in My presence, and neither does one who did wrong and then replaced evil with good. On that reading the clause introduces a second, separate category of the unafraid rather than carving an exception out of the first. The grammar permits it; *illā* does have such uses. But the motive behind the preference is not grammatical. It is the doctrine of prophetic protection from sin, held by most Muslim theologians, which makes any attribution of wrongdoing to a prophet uncomfortable.

**Where the Doctrine Actually Stands**

Al-Qurṭubī, who was no minimiser of prophetic dignity, made the point that settles most of the difficulty: many scholars accept that a prophet could commit an error before receiving prophethood. Mūsā's blow fell years before this night at the fire. Nothing in the doctrine of ʿiṣmah as most theologians framed it requires that a man be faultless in the period before he was appointed.

There is a second observation in al-Qurṭubī's discussion that is more interesting than the first. Fearing that one's repentance has not been accepted is itself a mark of sincerity, not of deficient faith. The man confident that his account is settled has stopped examining it. The tafsīr literature attaches to this passage a blunter formulation, attributed to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, to the effect that prophets sin and are called to account for it. That attribution should not be leaned on. Sayings reach the commentaries under al-Ḥasan's name with no chain attached, and he is among the figures to whom material was most freely ascribed — the ḥadīth critics documented precisely this problem with reports running through him. The point does not need the attribution. It stands on the Qur'an's own testimony, which is that Mūsā asked forgiveness for the killing and received it: *“He pleaded, “My Lord! I have definitely wronged my soul, so forgive me.” So He forgave him”* (28:16).

## Verse 27:12

> Now put your hand through ˹the opening of˺ your collar, it will come out ˹shining˺ white, unblemished. ˹These are two˺ of nine signs for Pharaoh and his people. They have truly been a rebellious people.”

**Shown in Private, Performed in Public**

Mūsā sees this happen on the mountainside, alone. The same transformation occurs again much later, in Pharaoh's presence: *“Then he drew his hand ˹out of his collar˺ and it was ˹shining˺ white for all to see.”* (26:33). The pattern matches the staff, which also quivered first in the dark and then before the court.

The rehearsal principle is consistent. A prophet is shown his signs before he needs them, so that when the moment arrives he is demonstrating rather than discovering. There is also a difference between the two rehearsals worth marking. When the staff moved, Mūsā ran. When the hand shines, no fear is recorded at all. Between the two moments he has been told that messengers have no fear in this presence and that his own past has a remedy. The absence of a reported reaction to the second sign is the narrative's way of showing that the reassurance took hold. Al-Qaṣaṣ preserves an additional instruction at this point — to draw his arm to his side to steady himself — which suggests the residue of alarm was still being managed even as the second sign was given.

**Two of Nine: Counting the Signs Given to Mūsā**

The verse places these two miracles inside a larger set: *among nine signs*. The Qur'an states the number again elsewhere — *“We surely gave Moses nine clear signs.”* (17:101) — without listing them in either place, and the commentators assembled the list from other passages.

The standard reconstruction begins with the staff and the hand, then draws the rest from the plagues recorded in al-Aʿrāf: *“So We plagued them with floods, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood—all as clear signs”* (7:133). That gives seven, and the remaining two are usually supplied as the famine years and the shortage of crops, which al-Aʿrāf mentions immediately before. Other arrangements circulated; some counted the parting of the sea, others substituted differently. The disagreement is over which items fill the list, not over the number, which the Qur'an fixes twice. What the number itself asserts is abundance. Pharaoh's court was not asked to accept a claim on the strength of one ambiguous event. Nine separate demonstrations were delivered, and the chapter's next verse reports what was done with them.

**A Verdict Delivered Before the Mission Starts**

The verse closes with an assessment: *They have truly been a rebellious people.* The word is *fāsiqīn*, from a root meaning to break out — the image is of something bursting its casing, a date splitting its skin. A *fāsiq* is one who has broken out of the boundary he was inside.

Two things follow from the placement of this clause. First, the tense is past and continuous: they *have been* rebellious, a settled condition rather than a fresh accusation. Mūsā is being told what he is walking into before he walks into it, which is a kindness rather than a discouragement. Second, and more pointedly, God states the verdict before the signs are delivered. Foreknowledge of the outcome does not cancel the sending. The nine signs still go, the argument is still made, the case is still put — because the point of the mission is not to inform God of something He does not know, but to leave Pharaoh's court without an excuse. What that court did when the evidence arrived is stated in the following verse.

## Verse 27:13

> But when Our enlightening signs came to them, they said, “This is pure magic.”

**Sorcery Was the Only Available Category**

Their verdict — *siḥrun mubīn*, pure or manifest magic — is not a random insult. It was the one classification that let them handle what they had seen without changing anything else they believed. Egypt had an established professional class of practitioners whose work involved apparent transformation. To file Mūsā's staff under sorcery was to place it in a known bin, staffed by known men, answerable to the palace.

The move is intellectually cheap and psychologically effective, which is why it recurs. The Quraysh used the same word against the Qur'an. Naming something as a known phenomenon makes the question of its source disappear, and the label does the work an argument would otherwise have to do. There is a sharp irony in their choice of adjective. They called it *mubīn* — manifest, obvious, clear — the very word the Qur'an applies to itself in this chapter's opening verse. Both sides agree the thing is unmistakable. They disagree only about what it unmistakably is, and the court settles that question by asserting rather than testing.

**The Same Word, Three Peoples, One Mechanism**

This response is not unique to Egypt, and the Qur'an makes that point by repeating the vocabulary across centuries and communities. The people of ʿĀd, the Thamūd, the Meccans who heard Muḥammad — each produced a version of the same dismissal, and in several cases the same Arabic word.

The Qur'an records the Meccan version in almost these words, and the parallel would not have been lost on the first audience. They were listening to a chapter that described an ancient court dismissing evidence as sorcery while they themselves were dismissing the recitation in front of them as the same thing. The narrative is a mirror held up mid-argument, and it works without any explicit application, because the vocabulary does the pointing.

What makes the repetition significant is that these communities shared no culture, no religion and no historical contact. They arrived independently at the same verdict because the verdict solves the same problem for each of them. Accepting the sign means accepting its sender, and accepting the sender means accepting the demand that follows: release the Israelites, abandon the idols, change how the poor are treated. The label is not a conclusion about the evidence but a defence of an arrangement. Ibn Kathīr observes that the resort to *siḥr* appears wherever the alternative would be costly, and the observation holds across every instance. The next verse states what those who used the word actually believed while they were using it.

## Verse 27:14

> And, although their hearts were convinced the signs were true, they still denied them wrongfully and arrogantly. See then what was the end of the corruptors

**The Heart Knows and the Tongue Refuses**

The Qur'an's psychology here has a structure worth drawing out, because it treats knowledge and acknowledgment as separable faculties. What a person's *nafs* has accepted and what his mouth will concede can diverge indefinitely, and the divergence is stable — it does not resolve itself through the passage of time or the accumulation of further evidence.

There is a second implication, which concerns how the Qur'an can make this claim at all. No human reporter had access to what Pharaoh's officials privately believed; the statement is made on the authority named nine verses earlier, the One described as All-Wise, All-Knowing (27:6). The chapter established its source before it began asserting things about the insides of people, and this verse is the first place where that framing is doing real work.

Nine signs arrived in Egypt. Each one, on the model where disbelief is an information problem, should have narrowed the gap. The Qur'an reports the opposite: the court's position hardened as the demonstrations multiplied, because every new sign raised the cost of the reversal they were already refusing to make. A man who has publicly called something magic eight times cannot call the ninth a miracle without indicting his previous eight statements. Al-Rāzī noted that persistence in a denial becomes self-reinforcing, since each repetition adds to what must be surrendered. This is why the Qur'an so often frames faith as a moral act rather than an intellectual one. The obstacle is rarely the evidence.

**A Command to Look at the Ending**

The verse closes by turning to the audience with an imperative: *fa-anẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mufsidīn* — see then what was the end of the corruptors. The address shifts from the third-person report to a direct instruction, and the instruction is to examine an outcome.

This closing formula appears throughout the Qur'an at the end of destruction narratives, with the verb of looking and the word *ʿāqibah*, the end or upshot of a matter. Its function is historiographical. The past is presented as a set of completed experiments whose results are available for inspection, and the audience is told to inspect them rather than to take the warning on trust. What makes the formula effective in Mecca is that the evidence was partly physical: the ruins of ʿĀd and Thamūd stood on trade routes the Quraysh used. Egypt's case required no ruins, since the outcome was famous. The command is not to feel something about these peoples but to look at how their story finished, and to notice that the looking is being demanded of people currently in the middle of their own.

## Verse 27:15

> Indeed, We granted knowledge to David and Solomon. And they said ˹in acknowledgment˺, “All praise is for Allah Who has privileged us over many of His faithful servants.”

**What Kind of Knowledge Was Given?**

The verse says *ʿilm* without specifying, and the commentators disagree over the referent on reasonable grounds. Al-Qurṭubī takes it as the wisdom and understanding proper to prophethood — the knowledge of God and of law that makes a man fit to be sent. Al-Ṭabarī connects it forward to the particular gifts described in the verses immediately following, the language of birds and the marshalled hosts. A third view points to the crafts the Qur'an attributes to them elsewhere, especially the armour-making taught to Dāwūd: *“We taught him the art of making body armour to protect you in battle.”* (21:80).

The three readings need not compete, and the strongest position takes the unqualified noun as covering all of it. The Qur'an's habit with an indefinite *ʿilm* in a context of divine gift is expansive rather than restrictive. What matters more than the inventory is the verb: *ātaynā*, We gave. Every item on any of the three lists arrived from outside. Even the metalwork, which looks like a human skill acquired by practice, is described in the Qur'an as taught. This framing is what makes the kings' response the only coherent one.

**Gratitude Placed on the Knowledge, Not the Kingdom**

Both men held thrones. Sulaymān's is described in this chapter as a dominion extending over jinn and birds, and the Qur'an elsewhere records him asking for a kingdom unlike any other. Yet the praise recorded here is not for the crown. It is for the knowledge.

Al-Rāzī draws out the significance of that selection: their thanks name the gift they valued most, and they did not name the one that would impress an observer most. A king who thanks God for his territory is thanking Him for what he can see and count. A king who thanks Him for understanding is naming the thing that makes the territory survivable. The Qur'an reinforces the ranking elsewhere by pairing faith and knowledge as the two grounds of elevation: *“Allah will elevate those of you who are faithful, and ˹raise˺ those gifted with knowledge in rank.”* (58:11). Al-Qurṭubī cites that verse on this one, and the pairing is exact — the two prophets are described as raised above many servants, and the reason they themselves give is knowledge.

**Knowledge in Prophets Is Never Idle**

Al-Rāzī adds an observation about the character of this knowledge that deserves recording. Prophetic knowledge, he argues, is not a stock of information a man may set down when he is off duty; it includes a continuous awareness of God, which is why it cannot be separated from the conduct of the one who has it.

There is supporting evidence in how the Qur'an consistently describes these two. Dāwūd is given the Zabūr and a voice the mountains echo; Sulaymān is given command over wind and jinn. In neither case does the Book separate the man's abilities from his worship. The mountains join Dāwūd in praise rather than merely obeying him, and Sulaymān's response to every marvel in this chapter is a prayer.

The claim is tested immediately by the narrative that follows. Sulaymān will overhear an ant and respond with a prayer of thanks; he will notice one missing bird from a parade of hosts; he will receive a report about a distant kingdom and insist on verifying it before acting. Each of those is an exercise of attention rather than of power, and each is what al-Rāzī's description would predict. The knowledge given in this verse is what makes those episodes intelligible as a set. The chapter states the gift first and then spends twenty verses demonstrating what having it looks like in practice, beginning with the succession described next.

## Verse 27:16

> And David was succeeded by Solomon, who said, “O people! We have been taught the language of birds, and been given everything ˹we need˺. This is indeed a great privilege.”

**What a Prophet Leaves Behind**

Sulaymān inherited from Dāwūd, and the Qur'an's silence about what he inherited is the interesting part. Dāwūd was a king with a treasury and territory, and he had other sons. Nothing here mentions land, gold or armies. The two things Sulaymān names as his in the next breath are a form of knowledge and a comprehensive provision.

Al-Rāzī reads the inheritance as covering prophethood and knowledge rather than property, and the reading fits both the grammar and the context. The verse places the succession immediately after a verse about knowledge given to both men, and Sulaymān's own statement specifies what he received. There is a practical point embedded here as well. A kingdom passes to whoever holds it; prophethood does not pass by descent, and the Qur'an never treats it as hereditary. That these two happen to be father and son is a fact about them, not a rule about the office. What the verse reports is that in this particular case the gift ran in a line, and the son's first public act was to describe it accurately.

**The Language of the Birds**

The phrase *manṭiq al-ṭayr* is literally the speech or express utterance of birds. *Manṭiq* comes from the root for speaking and is the same word Arabic philosophers later used for logic, the discipline of ordered speech. Applying it to birds asserts that their sounds are not noise but communication with structure, and that Sulaymān was taught to parse it.

The gift has a precedent in his father. Dāwūd's relationship with birds is recorded repeatedly: *“We subjected the mountains as well as the birds to hymn ˹Our praises˺ along with David.”* (21:79), and again *“Indeed, We granted David a ˹great˺ privilege from Us, ˹commanding:˺ “O mountains! Echo his hymns! And the birds as well.””* (34:10). A third passage adds *“And ˹We subjected˺ the birds, flocking together. All turned to him ˹echoing his hymns˺.”* (38:19). Al-Rāzī connects these directly to the inheritance in this verse: what Dāwūd had as shared praise, Sulaymān had as understood speech. The father sang with them; the son could follow what they said. This is not a new gift dropped into the narrative but a development of one already in the family, which is exactly what the word *inherited* would lead a reader to expect.

**Announced in Public, Credited to God**

Sulaymān does not keep the gift private. He addresses *al-nās*, the people, and states what he has been given. On its face this sits oddly with the humility the chapter has been recommending, and the tension is worth resolving rather than ignoring.

The resolution is in the verbs. Both are passive: we *have been taught*, we *have been given*. Neither sentence has Sulaymān as its agent. He announces the gifts while removing himself from the account of how they arrived, and then labels the whole thing *faḍl mubīn*, a clear favour — the word *faḍl* meaning something granted over and above what is owed. A ruler who publicises his advantages while naming them as unearned is doing something different from boasting. There is also a governmental reason for the announcement. A king whose armies include jinn and birds needs his human subjects to understand the basis of that arrangement, and the alternative to his explanation is their speculation. Al-Qurṭubī notes that stating a divine favour openly is itself a form of thanks when the giver is named, and the Qur'an elsewhere commands exactly that.

## Verse 27:17

> Solomon’s forces of jinn, humans, and birds were rallied for him, perfectly organized

**"Marshalled" — a Word About Holding Lines Together**

The final word, *yūzaʿūn*, is the technical term in the verse and the commentators divided over it. The root *w-z-ʿ* carries the sense of restraining and of arranging. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī report the primary reading as corralled or kept in check — the ranks held together, the front prevented from outrunning the rear, the columns kept from spreading. Others took it to mean they were moved forward in order, and a further group understood it of a rearguard set against attack.

The readings are close enough that the general picture is not in dispute: this was a disciplined body, not a crowd. What the restraining sense adds is an image of active management. An army of birds and jinn would naturally travel at wildly different speeds; holding such a force in formation is a continuous effort rather than a one-off arrangement. Ibn Kathīr's gloss, that officers went ahead to keep the ranks from breaking, describes an organisation with structure below the king. The word also returns in a surprising place two verses later, in Sulaymān's own prayer, where the same root means something quite different, and the shift is worth watching for.

**Organisation as a Form of Gratitude**

Sulaymān had just announced that he was given a share of all things. This verse shows the first thing he did with it: he ordered it. The gift arrived as capacity; what he added was arrangement.

That is the substantive claim in *yūzaʿūn*, and it separates this king from the picture of a man simply indulging in miraculous resources. Power without organisation dissipates; an army of jinn and birds moving as it pleased would be a spectacle rather than an instrument. The Qur'an mentions the marshalling in the same breath as the assembling, which makes discipline part of the description of the favour rather than an afterthought. There is a moral implication that the chapter will press further. The ant's warning in the next verse is triggered precisely by the size and momentum of this force — an organised army is also a dangerous one, and the danger it poses to small things is a function of how well it moves. Sulaymān's competence sets up the problem the following episode solves.

**A Kingdom That Included Creatures With No Legal Standing**

The three categories named here are not equals in any human system. Humans have rights and obligations; jinn, in Islamic law, are addressed by revelation and accountable; birds are neither. Yet the verse lists all three as *junūd*, forces, under one command and one organising discipline.

Sulaymān's own request, recorded in Ṣād, was for a kingdom that would suit no one after him, and this verse describes what was granted. The unrepeatable element is not the size of the force but its membership. Any conqueror can assemble men; nobody else assembles species.

The inclusion says something about the scope of the authority described and, more quietly, about the responsibilities attached to it. Sulaymān is answerable for what he does with creatures that cannot petition him. The chapter tests this immediately. Within two verses the smallest possible constituent of his realm — an ant, outside all three named categories — will speak about the danger his army poses, and the king's response to that will be the measure of his kingship.

## Verse 27:18

> And when they came across a valley of ants, an ant warned, “O ants! Go quickly into your homes so Solomon and his armies do not crush you, unknowingly.”

**The Chapter's Title Comes From an Insect**

This is the verse that names the sūrah. Ninety-three verses covering Mūsā, Sulaymān, the queen of Sheba, Ṣāliḥ, Lūṭ and the Day of Resurrection are called *an-Naml*, the Ants, after a single episode lasting two verses. The naming is not arbitrary in a chapter whose subject is who notices what.

Everything else in the surrounding passage is large. An army of three species, a king given a share of all things, a throne carried across a desert. Set against that, an ant issuing a warning is nothing, and the chapter takes its name from it. Al-Qurṭubī observed that Sulaymān's greatness shows in his attention to the smallest member of his realm, and the title makes the same point structurally. A reader who remembers only the marvels of this chapter has remembered the wrong things; the Qur'an has labelled it after the insect.

**Were They Ants, and Did She Have a Name?**

Two side-questions occupied the classical commentators, and they are worth reporting with their grounds. Some, cited by al-Qurṭubī and al-Thaʿlabī, proposed that these ants were winged, which would classify them as *ṭayr* — flying things — and so bring them under the language of birds Sulaymān had been taught. The suggestion is a harmonisation, designed to explain how he understood a creature outside the one category the previous verse specified.

It is unnecessary. Sulaymān's gift is described in verse 16 as the speech of birds, but the chapter never says that was the limit of what he could hear, and a king given a share of all things is not obviously confined to one taxon. The second question is stranger: several commentators transmit a personal name for this ant. Al-Qurṭubī records the reports and also records the objection, that there is no conceivable route by which anyone could know such a detail. That objection is decisive and is the conclusion. The name occurs in no authenticated report and in no verse; it enters the commentaries as narrative colour and is traceable no further. The general point is worth holding for the rest of this chapter, because the same pattern recurs — the Qur'an withholds a proper name, and the tradition supplies one.

**An Ant Colony Is a Community With Standing**

Behind this episode lies a principle the Qur'an states elsewhere and the Prophet ﷺ applied directly to ants. Every animal group is described in the Qur'an as a community like human communities, and that description carries weight.

Ants in particular were given protection in Islamic law on this basis. The jurists list them among the creatures that may not be killed without cause, and the prohibition is derived from the incident below rather than from any general principle of kindness.

The clearest illustration is a report Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī both cite on this passage. Abū Hurayrah related that the Prophet ﷺ said an ant bit one of the prophets, and he ordered the ants' colony burned; God then revealed to him: *because one ant bit you, you have burned a nation from among the nations that glorify Me* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3019; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2241). Another sound narration of the same incident gives the rebuke as: could you not have burned one ant? The report is unambiguous about the principle. A colony is an *ummah* that praises God, and destroying it in retaliation for an insect's bite was a wrong serious enough to warrant direct revelation. Read against that, this verse's ant is not a charming detail. She is a member of a community with standing before God, warning her people about a danger she does not blame anyone for. What Sulaymān did when he heard her is reported next.

---

## Verse 27:19

> So Solomon smiled in amusement at her words, and prayed, “My Lord! Inspire me to ˹always˺ be thankful for Your favours which You have blessed me and my parents with, and to do good deeds that please you. Admit me, by Your mercy, into ˹the company of˺ Your righteous servants.”

**The Word He Uses for "Inspire Me"**

*Awziʿnī* is the verb, and it is the same root that appeared two verses earlier as *yūzaʿūn*, the marshalling of Sulaymān's hosts. Al-Ṭabarī glosses the request as *make me*, compel me, and the shared root makes the connection audible in Arabic in a way translation cannot carry.

The king who holds his armies in ranks is asking to be held in ranks himself. The same restraining, ordering force he applies to jinn and birds he now requests for his own gratitude, as though thankfulness were a formation liable to break without discipline. That is a precise piece of self-knowledge. Gratitude is not difficult to feel in the moment of a gift; it is difficult to maintain, and what fails is the keeping rather than the starting. Asking to be *marshalled* into thanks concedes that the natural drift runs the other way. The identical root carrying two such different senses in three verses is the kind of detail the classical commentators noticed and modern readers miss, and al-Ṭabarī's note that the difference comes from context is exactly right.

**Why He Names His Parents**

The prayer asks thanks for favours given *to me and to my parents*. Sulaymān's father was Dāwūd, a prophet and king; the previous verses described the son inheriting from him. Including the parents in a prayer about personal blessing is not a courtesy.

Al-Rāzī explains it on the grounds that Sulaymān counted blessings on his parents as blessings on himself, which is straightforwardly true in his case: the kingdom, the prophethood and the knowledge all reached him through Dāwūd. But the phrase also corrects a distortion that success invites. A man at the head of an unprecedented empire is uniquely positioned to believe he is self-made, and this one, at the peak of his powers, names the people the gift came through. The Qur'an pairs gratitude to God and to parents elsewhere in almost the same construction, and the pairing is not sentimental. Anyone who thinks clearly about what he has been given will find other people standing at the start of the chain.

**Two Requests Covering Two Worlds**

The prayer has two halves, and al-Rāzī's division of them is clean. *To do good deeds that please You* concerns this life — the work itself, done here, in time. *Admit me, by Your mercy, into the company of Your righteous servants* concerns the next, and the word for admission is a verb of entry into a place.

The second half contains the theologically weighted phrase. Sulaymān does not ask to be admitted because of the righteous deeds he has just asked to perform; he asks to be admitted *by Your mercy*. The man given a share of all things, who has just been told his kingdom includes creatures no human commands, declines to present any of it as a claim. There is also something exact in what he asks for. Not a rank, not a reward, not a station above others — the company of the righteous, membership among servants. A king asks to be admitted to a group. That is the same instinct as verse 15, where he and his father placed themselves inside the community of believing servants before noting any distinction, and it is the settled posture from which the rest of his story proceeds.

---

## Verse 27:20

> ˹One day˺ he inspected the birds, and wondered, “Why is it that I cannot see the hoopoe? Or could he be absent

**A King Who Counts**

Sulaymān reviews his birds and finds one missing. That is the whole event, and its force lies entirely in the scale. His forces include jinn, humans and every kind of bird; the previous verses have described them as a marshalled host filling a valley. Out of that assembly he identifies the absence of a single individual.

Al-Qurṭubī draws the governing lesson from exactly this: a great leader knows the condition of those he leads, down to one bird. The observation is not sentimental, because the alternative is a real failure mode. A ruler of a large organisation who knows only aggregates cannot notice anything that happens to a single member of it, and a system in which individuals can vanish unremarked is precisely the system the ant feared two verses earlier — one where creatures are crushed *unknowingly*. Sulaymān's inspection is the answer to that fear. The same man who heard one ant in a valley notices one bird missing from a parade.

**The Verb Is About Careful Looking**

*Tafaqqada* is the word, and it is stronger than *looked at*. The root *f-q-d* concerns loss and missing, and this verbal form means to seek out what is absent — to inspect with the specific intention of finding what is not there. English has no single word for it; the idea is closest to taking a roll call.

That the Qur'an uses this verb rather than a general verb of seeing tells us the absence was found rather than stumbled upon. Sulaymān was checking. This turns the episode from a lucky noticing into a routine of administration, which is a more demanding claim about his kingship. Anyone might spot a gap; maintaining a practice that reliably surfaces gaps is the work of governance. The verb also explains the tone of his question. He is not startled. He asks what has happened in the measured manner of someone who has just found a discrepancy in an inspection and wants it accounted for.

---

## Verse 27:21

> I will surely subject him to a severe punishment, or ˹even˺ slaughter him, unless he brings me a compelling excuse.”

**"A Compelling Excuse" — the Word Is About Proof**

The Arabic for the escape clause is *sulṭān mubīn*. *Sulṭān* is usually the word for authority or dominion, and it is the same root behind a ruler's title. Al-Ṭabarī glosses it here as clear evidence or a plain justification for the absence, and the shift of sense is natural in Arabic: an argument that has authority over the hearer is one he cannot resist.

The choice of word sets a high bar. Sulaymān does not ask for an apology, an explanation, or a reason. He asks for something with the force of proof — a justification that compels rather than requests. Note also the adjective, *mubīn*, clear, which by now has run through this chapter three times: the clear Book in verse 1, the manifest sorcery of Pharaoh's court in verse 13, the clear favour Sulaymān named in verse 16. The standard being applied to the bird is the same standard the chapter applies to everything else. Bring something unambiguous, or the claim fails.

**The King Does Not Judge Before He Hears**

Read this verse against the one before it and a procedure emerges. Verse 20 had Sulaymān questioning his own perception first — perhaps I am not seeing him — before entertaining the possibility of absence. Verse 21 states the penalty, and attaches to it the condition that the accused may speak.

Announcing a penalty is not the same as imposing one, and the distinction is the difference between law and violence.

No sentence is carried out in this verse. Nothing is done at all; a rule is announced. The hoopoe, when he returns, is not seized, and the verses that follow show him speaking first and at length, without interruption, before any response from the king. Whatever the harshness of the language here, the practice that follows it is a hearing. Al-Qurṭubī, discussing Sulaymān's conduct in this episode, treats the whole sequence as an illustration of governance rather than of severity, and the text supports that reading: the threat exists to make the inquiry serious, and the inquiry actually happens.

**Anger Is Recorded, Not Edited Out**

The Qur'an could have reported this scene without the threat. It would have lost nothing narratively — the bird returns, delivers his news, and the story proceeds to Sheba. Instead the text preserves a prophet-king saying he will punish or kill a subordinate, in words strong enough to make later readers uncomfortable.

The pattern holds across the Book. Yūnus left his post in frustration and the Qur'an says so; Yaʿqūb wept until his sight failed; Ayyūb complained of what had touched him. None of these moments is softened, and none of them is held against the man.

This candour is characteristic and worth naming. The same chapter recorded Mūsā running away from a staff. Both details make their subjects less impressive and both are kept. The Qur'an's prophets are shown reacting — with fear, with anger, with impatience — and the reactions are neither praised nor censured but simply reported as part of what happened. What is consistently marked is what they do next. Mūsā was reassured and went to Pharaoh. Sulaymān, having threatened, listened. There is a further point in the specific offence that provoked him: the king is angry about a breach of order in the ranks, which is the one thing he has responsibility for. The bird's answer arrives immediately in the following verse. The threat is stated in full and then immediately qualified, which is the pattern a legal proceeding follows rather than the pattern of an outburst.

---

## Verse 27:22

> It was not long before the bird came and said, “I have found out something you do not know. I have just come to you from Sheba with sure news

**A Bird Tells a Prophet He Has Missed Something**

The first sentence out of the hoopoe's mouth is the boldest line anyone speaks in this chapter: *aḥaṭtu bimā lam tuḥiṭ bih* — I have encompassed what you have not encompassed. He is addressing a prophet who has just threatened to kill him, and he opens by claiming superior knowledge.

The verb is precise and strong. *Aḥāṭa* means to surround, to enclose completely, and in the language of knowledge it means to grasp something in full rather than in part. The Qur'an uses it of God's knowledge repeatedly. For a bird to use it of himself in front of Sulaymān, to whom God had given knowledge and a share of all things, is a considerable claim. It is also true, and the chapter does not undercut it. Verse 16 had Sulaymān announce that he was given *everything ˹we need˺* — and the partitive sense of that phrase is confirmed right here, by a hoopoe who knows something the king does not. The limit was always in the grammar. The bird supplies the demonstration.

**"Sure News" — the Bird Answers the Exact Demand**

Sulaymān had demanded *sulṭān mubīn*, a compelling proof. The hoopoe offers *naba' yaqīn*, sure news, and the match is deliberate. *Naba'* in the Qur'an is not ordinary information; it is weighty news, the kind that changes a situation, and the Book uses it for revelation and for reports of great consequence. *Yaqīn* is the same word for certainty that verse 3 used of the believers' conviction about the Hereafter and verse 14 used of Pharaoh's court knowing the signs were true.

The pairing of the two words is itself deliberate. News can be weighty and false, or certain and trivial; joining them claims both weight and reliability at once.

So the bird is not saying he has heard a rumour. He is claiming firsthand, certain knowledge of something consequential, in the vocabulary the chapter has been using for its highest grade of conviction. That is a precise response to a demand for proof. It is also a testable one, which the king will immediately exploit: a claim of certainty invites verification in a way that a claim of impression does not. The hoopoe has, in one sentence, both defended himself and staked his life on the report being accurate.

**Sheba Was a Real Place**

*Sabaʾ* is not a fable. It was a kingdom in the southwest of the Arabian peninsula, in what is now Yemen, known to ancient Near Eastern sources and to the Arabs themselves, famous for the incense trade and for large-scale irrigation works. The Qur'an devotes part of a whole chapter to its later fate, describing the land at its height: *“Indeed, there was a sign for ˹the tribe of˺ Sheba in their homeland: two orchards—one to the right and the other to the left.”* (34:15), and then the flood that destroyed the gardens when the people turned away.

The distance from Jerusalem to Sheba is roughly two thousand kilometres of desert and mountain, which is what makes the hoopoe's errand remarkable and also what makes it plausible for this particular bird, a migratory species that crosses continents. Islamic tradition names the queen Bilqīs and supplies her a father, but the name is not scriptural and its provenance is traceable. The Qur'an never uses it; neither does the biblical account. Al-Ṭabarī, notably, tells most of the story before the name appears at all, referring to her simply as the woman who rules Sabaʾ. Where the genealogies and the accession stories do surface — in ath-Thaʿlabī, and through him in Ibn Kathīr — they arrive without prophetic chains and in mutually inconsistent versions. Nor does the epigraphic record help: the South Arabian inscriptions document Sabaʾ in detail and preserve no queen answering to this description. The name is a convenience of the tradition, useful for referring to her and carrying no authority. What the bird brings back is a description of that kingdom, and he begins it in the next verse.

## Verse 27:23

> Indeed, I found a woman ruling over them, who has been given everything ˹she needs˺, and who has a magnificent throne

**The Same Phrase Applied to Two Rulers**

The words used of her provision — *ūtiyat min kulli shayʾ*, given of all things — are the same words Sulaymān used of himself seven verses earlier when he told his people they had been given everything they needed. The repetition is exact in the Arabic and cannot be accidental.

Al-Qurṭubī reads her portion as everything her kingdom requires, and the same partitive grammar governs both statements: a share of every kind of thing, not the totality of things. Setting the two rulers side by side with identical language invites the comparison the chapter wants. Both are well-supplied monarchs with impressive courts. What will distinguish them is not resources but what each does with the knowledge that arrives. Sulaymān responded to a report by testing it; she will respond to a letter by consulting her council, then by sending a gift to probe the sender's motive, and finally by revising her position when the evidence requires it. Two rulers given all they need, and the chapter measures them by their handling of information rather than by their holdings.

**"A Magnificent Throne" and Where the Phrase Leads**

The third item is *ʿarsh ʿaẓīm*, a mighty throne. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī and al-Thaʿlabī all take the description to cover both senses at once: the physical object was genuinely enormous and richly made, and it also stood for the power of the one seated on it. Arabic allows the throne to mean the reign, as English does, and the commentators saw no need to choose.

A seat of state in the ancient world was a political instrument as much as a piece of furniture, built to be seen and to settle questions of rank before anyone spoke.

The detail is not decorative, because this throne will return. Sulaymān will later have it brought to his court before she arrives, and its transportation becomes the chapter's most dramatic demonstration of what his kingdom could do. The hoopoe's passing mention plants the object that the narrative will pick up twenty verses later. There is also a deliberate echo waiting at the end of the bird's speech. He has just called her throne mighty; three verses on he will call God *the Lord of the Mighty Throne*, using the same adjective. The bird sets up his own comparison, and it is the comparison his whole report exists to make.

---

## Verse 27:24

> I found her and her people prostrating to the sun instead of Allah. For Satan has made their deeds appealing to them—hindering them from the ˹Right˺ Way and leaving them unguided—

**Why the Sun Is the Hardest Case**

Sun worship is the most defensible form of idolatry available, and it is worth saying why rather than treating it as obvious folly. The sun genuinely does sustain life. It is visible to everyone, reliable in its movements, indifferent to human politics, and the source of heat, light and every crop. A community that oriented its rituals toward it would not be worshipping a fiction; it would be worshipping the most powerful thing it could see.

Sun cults existed across the ancient world for exactly these reasons, from Egypt to Mesopotamia, and they were the religions of advanced states rather than of primitive ones. Sheba was a wealthy trading kingdom with engineering works that outlasted it by centuries. Its people were not simple.

That is the Qur'an's actual complaint. The error is not a mistake about the sun's importance but a failure to ask what the sun depends on. The Book makes the argument explicitly elsewhere through Ibrāhīm, who watches a star, then the moon, then the sun, and rejects each one as they set — the reasoning being that whatever sets is governed, and what is governed is not the governor. Sheba had noticed the greatest visible power and had stopped there. Their worship was not irrational. It was insufficiently curious, which is a different and more common failing.

---

## Verse 27:25

> so they do not prostrate to Allah, Who brings forth what is hidden in the heavens and the earth, and knows what you ˹all˺ conceal and what you reveal

**Two Ways to Read the Opening Word**

The verse begins with a word the reciters read in two ways, and both are canonical. Read as *allā*, it continues the previous verse: Satan turned them from the way, so that they do not prostrate. Read as *alā*, it becomes an exclamation and a rhetorical question: will they not prostrate to God?

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both record the two readings and treat them as accepted. The difference is one of grammar, not of doctrine. Under the first, the failure to prostrate is the consequence of the adornment described in verse 24, and the sentence is a diagnosis. Under the second, the hoopoe breaks off reporting and addresses an appeal to the court — a call, not a description. Each reading has support in the flow of the passage. The first keeps the syntax continuous with what precedes; the second explains the shift to direct address in the verse's final clause, where the pronoun turns to *you*. Neither reading can be eliminated, and Arabic manuscripts of the period, written without short vowels, would have carried both.

**"What Is Hidden" — Rain, Growth, or Everything?**

The phrase *al-khab'* means the concealed thing, and the commentators offered a range. Al-Ṭabarī reports rain among the interpretations — *ghayth*, the water held in the sky until it is released — and also the produce of the earth, the seed that lies under soil until it comes up. A broader reading takes it as everything in the heavens and earth known only to God, and al-Qurṭubī allows mysteries in general.

The concrete readings are the more powerful ones in context. Rain and crops are the two things a sun-worshipping agricultural kingdom most depends on, and they are precisely the two things the sun does not produce. Sheba was famous for irrigation; its later ruin came when a great dam failed and the water went. A people whose survival rested on water from the sky and growth from the soil were bowing to the one visible power that supplies neither. The hoopoe's argument lands on their own economy. Whatever they thought they were thanking the sun for, the two inputs that kept them alive came out of hiding, and something else was bringing them out.

---

## Verse 27:26

> ˹He is˺ Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the Lord of the Mighty Throne.”

**Two Thrones, Two Meanings of "Mighty"**

The final phrase, *rabb al-ʿarsh al-ʿaẓīm*, deliberately reuses the adjective the bird applied to Bilqīs three verses earlier when he called her throne magnificent. The same word, *ʿaẓīm*, is now attached to something else, and the repetition is the argument.

Al-Qurṭubī draws the distinction cleanly. Her throne is called mighty relative to the other thrones of earthly sovereigns — it is the largest and richest among a class of comparable objects. God's Throne is mighty in a different sense altogether, since what is said of it is that it encompasses the heavens and the earth. One is the top of a scale; the other is not on the scale. That difference disposes of a misreading the report could otherwise invite. A hoopoe impressed by a great throne might be understood as recommending an alliance between two powers. His last sentence blocks it. The throne he ends on is not a larger version of hers, and the relation between them is not competition.

**Naming God as Lord of a Throne, Not as a Throne**

The construction is careful in a way that matters for the doctrine. God is called the *Lord of* the Mighty Throne, not identified with it. The Throne is a created thing under His authority; He is not seated on it in any sense the verse licenses us to picture.

This is the same restraint the chapter showed at the burning fire, where the call was immediately followed by *Glory be to Allah, the Lord of all worlds.* (27:8) — a declaration of transcendence attached to the moment God's voice came from a specific place. Islamic theology has argued for centuries about how to read the Throne verses, and the mainstream positions all share one commitment: whatever the Throne is, God is not contained by it. The grammar here supports that without needing the argument. A lord of something stands over it. Placed at the end of a speech about people worshipping a visible object, the phrasing does one more piece of work — it names God's relation to the greatest created thing, so that the sun, a lesser created thing, is placed at once.

**A Hoopoe Delivers Correct Theology**

Step back and look at who has been speaking. A bird has crossed a continent, observed a foreign state, diagnosed its religious error, identified the divine attribute that refutes that error, and concluded with a statement of pure monotheism and a description of God's relation to the created order.

The Qur'an presents this without any note of the comical, and the chapter has prepared for it. The ant spoke with foresight and charity; the hoopoe speaks with accuracy and doctrine. What both episodes establish is that the creatures around Sulaymān are not props for his miracles but participants with their own awareness. This connects to a claim the Qur'an makes generally, that all things glorify God whether or not human beings understand how. The bird here is not doing anything the Qur'an says other creatures fail to do; he is doing it in a language the king was given the means to hear. What Sulaymān does with the report — and he does not simply accept it — comes next.

---

## Verse 27:27

> Solomon said, “We will see whether you are telling the truth or lying

**The King Does Not Believe the Bird**

After a report that ended in a declaration of God's oneness, Sulaymān's reply is a single flat sentence: we shall look into whether you are honest. No thanks, no praise for the theology, no immediate action against Sheba. The claim is received and set aside pending verification.

Al-Ṭabarī identifies the addressee plainly — Sulaymān is speaking to the hoopoe — and al-Qurṭubī explains the sharpness by pointing back to the bird's opening line. He had said he grasped what the king had not grasped, which is a categorical claim about a prophet's knowledge made by one of his own troops. A statement that bold invites exactly this response. What the king says is not that the bird is lying; it is that the question is open and will be settled. That is the correct posture toward an unverified report from a single source, however impressive its content.

**Why Good Content Does Not Excuse a Missing Check**

The hoopoe's report was, as it turns out, entirely true, and its theology was sound. It would have been easy for Sulaymān to treat the correctness of the doctrine as evidence for the accuracy of the intelligence. He does not, and the separation is the point.

A report can be pious and false. If a story flatters a listener's convictions, the listener is less likely to test it, which is precisely why such stories travel furthest. The hoopoe told a monotheist king that a distant kingdom had fallen into idolatry and needed correcting — a report calculated, whether the bird intended it or not, to be welcome. Sulaymān applies the test anyway. The Qur'an elsewhere commands this directly, instructing believers to investigate when someone of uncertain reliability brings news, lest they harm people out of ignorance and then regret it. The principle is stated there as law; here it is shown as practice, in a case where the news happened to be true and the verification cost nothing but a flight.

**The Stakes of Getting It Wrong**

Consider what would have followed an unverified acceptance. Sulaymān would have acted against a sovereign state on the word of one witness with a motive to justify his own absence. Whatever form that action took — a letter, a demand, a campaign — it would have been launched on testimony that had not been examined.

This is why the verse belongs in a chapter preoccupied with how people handle evidence. Pharaoh's court saw enlightening signs and refused them. Sheba's people accepted an adorned appearance without asking what lay behind it. Sulaymān, offered a report that confirmed everything he believed, asks whether it is true. Three responses to incoming information, and only one of them is a method. Al-Qurṭubī reads the whole episode as instruction in leadership, and the reading holds: the same king who noticed one absent bird now declines to act on one bird's word. Attention and scepticism are the same discipline pointed in two directions.

---

## Verse 27:28

> Go with this letter of mine and deliver it to them, then stand aside and see how they will respond.”

**A Letter, Not an Army**

The most consequential word in this verse is the one that is absent. Sulaymān commands a force of jinn, humans and birds, arranged in marshalled ranks. He has just been told that a foreign kingdom worships the sun. What he sends is a piece of writing.

The choice sets the terms of everything that follows. The entire Sheba narrative — the queen's consultation with her chiefs, the gift she sends, her journey, her recognition in the palace — proceeds as an exchange rather than a conquest, and it proceeds that way because the first move was a letter. Nothing in the text says an army was ruled out on principle, and Sulaymān will later speak firmly about what would follow refusal.

**The Excuse Is Accepted in Practice**

Al-Qurṭubī draws from this exchange the principle that a leader should accept the legitimate excuses of those under him, and the text bears him out in action rather than in words. Sulaymān never announces that the hoopoe is forgiven. He simply gives him a mission.

That is the more convincing form of acceptance. A pardon can be announced and then quietly withdrawn in the assignment of duties; entrusting a diplomatic errand to a bird suspected of desertion is a statement that cannot be taken back. Al-Qurṭubī cites in this connection a report that none loves excuses more than God, and that this is why He sent the messengers. The ḥadīth is authentic and can be placed exactly: it is narrated from al-Mughīrah ibn Shuʿbah in **Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7416** and from Ibn Masʿūd in **Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2760**, with a further version from Saʿd ibn ʿUbādah at **Muslim 1499a** — "no one is fonder of accepting an excuse than God, and on account of that He sent the bearers of good news and the warners." The wording is worth pressing. It does not say God accepts excuses; it says He *loves* to, and that the sending of prophets is the consequence. Revelation, on that reading, functions as the acceptance of an excuse in advance: a warned people can no longer plead ignorance, and a people given the means to correct themselves have been handed the route out rather than the verdict.

**Watching the Reply Is the Real Intelligence**

The final clause reveals what Sulaymān is actually after. He does not ask the bird to bring back an answer; he asks him to observe *how* they respond. The content of the reply matters less than the manner of it.

That distinction is sophisticated. A ruler who receives a demanding letter from a more powerful neighbour can react in several ways — defiance, submission, silence, delay, negotiation — and each reveals something the words of the reply may conceal. As it happens, the queen's reaction is to summon her council and ask for advice, which is precisely the kind of information a formal answer would not have carried. Sulaymān's instruction anticipates that a response is a behaviour before it is a text. What his letter said, and how she received it, are reported next.

**Writing as an Instrument of Government**

The verse assumes something easy to pass over: Sulaymān has a letter, already composed, and a means of sealing and sending it. His kingdom has a chancery. Alongside the jinn and the birds, the apparatus described here is the ordinary machinery of a literate state.

This matters for how the Qur'an presents prophetic kingship. Dāwūd is credited with metalwork, Sulaymān with a written diplomacy, Yūsuf with the administration of a granary during famine. None of these is magical, and the Book records them alongside the miracles without distinguishing in tone. A prophet's competence in the ordinary crafts of government is treated as part of what God gave him, which is consistent with verse 15's unqualified *knowledge* and verse 16's share of all things. The letter is the least spectacular thing in this passage and the most consequential: it is what actually changes Sheba. Two thousand kilometres away, a wind-commanding king with jinn at his disposal achieves his result with ink.

---

## Verse 27:29

> The Queen ˹later˺ announced, “O chiefs! Indeed, a noble letter has been delivered to me

**The Scene Changes Without a Word of Transition**

The narrative jumps. One verse ends with Sulaymān instructing the hoopoe; the next opens with the queen already addressing her court about a letter that has arrived. The flight of two thousand kilometres, the delivery, the withdrawal — all of it is skipped.

This is the Qur'an's usual narrative economy, and it produces a specific effect here. By omitting the journey, the text places the reader in the same position as Sulaymān: he sent a letter and now learns what happened at the other end. The hoopoe's errand is not dramatised because the story is not about the errand. What matters is the reception, which is what the king asked to be observed. The ellipsis also confirms, silently, that the hoopoe's report was accurate. There is a queen, there is a court, and the letter reached her. The verification Sulaymān ordered has already succeeded by the time this verse begins.

**"Noble" Is a Strange Word for a Demand**

The adjective she chooses is *karīm* — noble, generous, honourable. Applied to a letter from a foreign power containing an instruction not to be arrogant, it is unexpected, and the commentators offered three explanations for it.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī record all three. It may refer to the seal: a sealed letter in the ancient Near East was a mark of authority and of respect for the recipient, since sealing protected the contents from being read en route. It may refer to the sender: a letter from Sulaymān is noble because he is. Or it may refer to the contents, which open with the name of God and proceed without insult or boast. Each reading is defensible, and they are not exclusive. What all three share is that she formed a favourable judgment of the letter before disclosing its demand, and she says so publicly. That framing shapes how her council will hear what follows, and it was a choice she did not have to make.

**Addressing the Chiefs**

The word she uses for her council is *al-mala'*, which the Qur'an applies to the ruling elite around a monarch — the notables, the men whose agreement a throne requires. The same term is used of Pharaoh's court, and of the chiefs who opposed Nūḥ and Hūd.

Its appearances elsewhere in the Book are almost uniformly negative. The *mala'* are typically the party of resistance: they advise against the prophet, they warn the king about the danger of conceding, they protect their own standing by protecting the existing order. That is what makes their behaviour in this episode notable. Sheba's chiefs will answer their queen by stating their military strength and then, remarkably, leaving the decision to her. They do not push for war. Whatever the failings of this kingdom's religion, its politics are functional in a way the Qur'an rarely shows, and this verse is where that becomes visible: a monarch who consults, addressing a council she expects to be heard by. She now reads them the letter's opening line.

---

## Verse 27:30

> It is from Solomon, and it reads: ‘In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful

**A Letter to an Idolater Begins With the Name of God**

The recipient of this letter prostrates to the sun. Sulaymān knows it; the hoopoe told him. He opens by invoking the God she does not acknowledge, and he does it before saying anything else.

The move is not tactless, and it is not a failure to consider his audience. It establishes the frame of the entire communication in five words. Whatever the letter goes on to demand, the demand is made in the name of a specific reality, and the writer is not concealing his premise to make his request more palatable. The alternative — an opening in Sulaymān's own name and authority — would have turned the letter into an assertion of one king's will over another's, which is exactly what he is not claiming.

**Two Names of Mercy, Chosen Over Every Alternative**

The formula names God twice by mercy: *al-raḥmān* and *al-raḥīm*, both from the root *r-ḥ-m*. Arabic grammarians distinguish them by form. *Raḥmān* is an intensive pattern denoting mercy in its fullest extent, understood as the mercy that covers all creation without distinction; *raḥīm* is a pattern denoting a settled, continuing quality, understood as the mercy specifically directed toward those who turn toward it.

That the letter opens with these two and not with, say, the Mighty or the Overpowering is a substantive choice. Sulaymān could have invoked attributes suited to a demand from a superior power. He invokes the two that describe God's disposition toward creatures who have not yet submitted — which is the queen's exact position as she reads it. The sequence also matters: the universal mercy is named first. Before she is told anything about what she must do, she is told what the one making the claim on her is like.

**A Letter That Becomes Scripture**

There is a curious layering in this verse. A queen is reading aloud, to her council, a document written by a prophet, and the Qur'an quotes her quoting him. Three voices are stacked, and the innermost of them is a formula that Muslims would later recite at the start of meals, journeys and every chapter of their scripture.

The layering explains why the verse carries the weight it does in Islamic practice. Sulaymān's letter became the model for prophetic correspondence: the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have sent letters to foreign rulers opening with the same formula, and the practice of beginning any written communication with the basmalah is traced to this precedent. A form used for a diplomatic dispatch three thousand years ago, preserved in a quotation inside a quotation, became the standard opening of Muslim writing. The letter's actual demand follows in the next verse, and it is two sentences long.

## Verse 27:31

> Do not be arrogant with me, but come to me, fully submitting ˹to Allah˺.’”

**"Against Me" — the Phrase That Creates the Difficulty**

The awkward word is *ʿalayya*, against me. Sulaymān tells her not to exalt herself over *him*, personally, which reads at first like an assertion of rank between two monarchs.

Ibn Kathīr takes the clause as forbidding her to approach him as tyrants approach, with the posture of a sovereign who concedes nothing. On that reading the personal reference is unavoidable, because the letter is from one ruler to another and the relation between them has to be settled before anything else can proceed. But the letter has already framed itself in the name of God rather than in Sulaymān's own, which limits what the phrase can mean. He is not claiming personal superiority; he is refusing the posture in which she would negotiate as an equal power over a matter that is not negotiable between powers. Al-Ṭabarī's reading of the second clause reinforces this, since the submission he identifies is to God rather than to the writer. The *me* in the first clause is the messenger, not the object.

**Submitting to Whom?**

The second clause contains the real interpretive question. *Wa 'tūnī muslimīn* — come to me submitting. Al-Ṭabarī reads the state of submission as submission to God, in obedience and in acknowledgment of His oneness. Al-Rāzī records the alternative, that the queen and her notables should approach Sulaymān in obedience — a political surrender rather than a religious one.

The stronger reading is al-Ṭabarī's, and the grounds are in the letter itself. It opens by invoking God, not Sulaymān's throne. The problem the hoopoe reported was worship directed at the sun, not a territorial dispute. And the outcome of the whole narrative is a declaration of faith rather than a treaty: she ends by saying she submits, with Sulaymān, to the Lord of all worlds. Nothing in the episode records a transfer of territory or tribute. Al-Rāzī's alternative is not baseless, since the Arabic word can carry the sense of yielding, but it fits neither the letter's opening nor the story's end.

**Arrogance Is the Chapter's Recurring Diagnosis**

The word for arrogance here, from the root *ʿ-l-w*, has appeared before in this chapter. Pharaoh's court denied the signs *wrongfully and arrogantly* (27:14), and the second term there is from the same root: *ʿuluww*, self-exaltation. The letter to Sheba prohibits exactly what destroyed Egypt.

That repetition organises the chapter's two royal narratives. Both begin with a ruler who has every material reason for confidence and an established religion that is wrong. Both are presented with a demand backed by evidence. Pharaoh's court knew the signs were true and refused anyway, held in place by the posture the root describes. Sheba's queen is warned against that posture in advance, in writing, before she has taken it. The letter is thus not only a demand but a diagnosis offered to someone still able to act on it — which is the difference between the two stories, and the reason one ends in drowning and the other in a declaration of faith. Her first move on reading it is reported next.

**The Word Behind "Submitting"**

*Muslimīn* is the participle of *aslama*, to hand over, to give up a thing entirely into another's keeping. The root carries safety and wholeness — the same root behind *salām*, peace, and behind the greeting Muslims exchange. Handing yourself over completely and being secure are, in this vocabulary, the same condition described from two sides.

That etymology bears directly on what Sulaymān is asking. The queen, when she finally uses the word, uses it in exactly this sense — she declares that she submits along with Sulaymān to the Lord of all worlds, placing herself beside him rather than beneath him, which is what the letter's careful wording always permitted.

## Verse 27:32

> She said, “O chiefs! Advise me in this matter of mine, for I would never make any decision without you.”

**A Council Is Not a Formality Here**

What follows in the next verses shows that the consultation was genuine, and the evidence is in the shape of the exchange. Her chiefs answer with a real position — they state their military capacity — and she then argues against the implication of that position, giving her reasons. A rubber-stamp council does not get argued with.

The Qur'an's other courts do not work this way. Pharaoh consults his *mala'* and they tell him what he wants to hear, urging him to act against Mūsā. The chiefs of Nūḥ's people and of Hūd's speak to intimidate rather than to advise. What is described here is deliberation: a question posed, an answer given, and a counter-argument offered before a decision is reached. The Qur'an itself commands consultation of believers in their affairs and praises those whose matters are decided by mutual counsel. That a sun-worshipping kingdom is shown practising it, in a chapter that elsewhere condemns that kingdom's religion, is a distinction the text makes without comment and expects the reader to notice.

**The Question Is Left Genuinely Open**

Notice what she does not tell them. She does not say whether she is inclined to comply or resist, does not characterise Sulaymān as an enemy or a threat, and does not tell them what she thinks the letter means. She called it noble in verse 29 and stops there.

Withholding her inclination is the technical core of good consultation, and the reason is practical rather than moral. A council that knows the ruler's preference will supply reasons for it; advisors reason toward what they expect is wanted. By keeping her position unstated she preserves the possibility of learning something she did not already believe. Whether she was consciously applying a method or simply behaving as she always had, the effect is the same, and the answer she gets is one she is able to disagree with. Her disagreement, when it comes two verses later, is substantive and turns on a reading of history rather than of Sulaymān's character.

**A Model of Rule the Chapter Endorses by Depiction**

Ibn ʿArabī, commenting on this episode in his *Fuṣūṣ al-ḥikam*, argued that the queen's handling of Sulaymān's letter demonstrated her fitness to rule. The judgment is sound on the text as given: she disclosed the letter, judged it fairly, consulted, reasoned from precedent, tested the sender, and revised her position when the evidence required it.

Setting that record beside the chapter's other monarch sharpens it further. Pharaoh's court had certainty and suppressed it. This queen had an error and corrected it. The Qur'an never comments on her being a woman, and the silence is eloquent in a text that comments freely on everything it finds objectionable. What the narrative endorses, by showing rather than stating, is a manner of governing — consultative, evidence-driven, capable of changing its mind — and it locates that manner in a ruler whose religion it condemns and whose eventual submission it records. The answer her chiefs give comes immediately.

## Verse 27:33

> They responded, “We are a people of strength and great ˹military˺ might, but the decision is yours, so decide what you will command.”

**They Do Not Push for War**

The restraint here is easy to overlook and is genuinely unusual. A military council informing its ruler that it has great might, in response to a foreign demand for submission, is one sentence away from recommending that the might be used. They do not take the step.

Compare the councils elsewhere in the Qur'an. Pharaoh's chiefs press him to act against Mūsā, warning that the land will be ruined if he does not. The notables of earlier peoples consistently harden their rulers against the prophets sent to them. Sheba's *mala'* break the pattern. The phrase *al-amru ilayki* — the matter is yours — hands back a decision they could have shaped, in circumstances where the shaping would have favoured their own standing. Generals who argue for war argue for their own importance; these ones report and stand down. Whatever this court's failings in religion, its chiefs do not pursue their institutional interest at the cost of their state.

**Strength Is Stated as Fact, Not as Boast**

The Arabic construction is worth noticing: *naḥnu ulū quwwatin wa ulū ba'sin shadīd* — we are possessors of strength and possessors of severe force. The form is descriptive. They claim possession of a capacity rather than asserting they would prevail.

That distinction is the difference between an inventory and a prediction. They do not say they can defeat Sulaymān, that his forces are overrated, or that the kingdom has nothing to fear. They say what they have. This is the correct form for advice given to someone who must weigh it against information the advisors lack, and it leaves room for the queen's counter-argument two verses on, which concerns what happens when kings enter a land rather than which army is larger. Had they claimed victory, she would have had to contradict them. Because they claimed only capacity, she can accept their report in full and still reject the course it seems to point toward.

---

## Verse 27:34

> She reasoned, “Indeed, when kings invade a land, they ruin it and debase its nobles. They really do so

**A Political Law Stated as a General Rule**

Her chiefs offered strength. Her reply does not dispute it. Instead she states a rule about what war produces, regardless of who wins: when kings enter a town, they wreck it, and they take the honoured people of the place and make them the lowest.

The claim is general and has no proper names in it. She does not say Sulaymān would do this; she says kings do. Al-Qurṭubī reads the sentence as her answer to the council's implication that they could resist — meaning that if Sulaymān entered their land, destruction would follow. That is the practical force of it. But the wording covers her own side too. A queen whose army marches out is also a monarch entering a land, and she is describing what such entries do. This is a ruler who has generalised about her own profession and drawn an unflattering conclusion from it.

**Two Specific Damages, Not a Vague Lament**

She names two effects, and they are precise. *Afsadūhā* — they corrupt or spoil the place. The root is the same one the chapter used of Pharaoh's court in verse 14, where they were called *corruptors*. And *jaʿalū aʿizzata ahlihā adhillah* — they make the honoured of its people abased.

The second is the sharper observation. Conquest does physical damage, which anyone can see. What she identifies is the social inversion: the people who held standing in a place lose it, and the loss is not incidental but instrumental, since a conqueror who leaves the local hierarchy intact has not conquered anything. The Arabic sets *aʿizzah* against *adhillah*, two words from roots that are direct opposites, so the sentence performs the reversal it describes. She is not describing destruction in general terms. She is describing what happens to a court exactly like the one she is addressing, which is a pointed thing to say to chiefs who have just volunteered for battle.

**A Sentence With a Long Afterlife**

This verse acquired a second life in Islamic spiritual writing, and the reading is worth recording alongside the plain one. Ibn ʿAṭāʾillāh al-Iskandarī, in his *Kitāb al-Ḥikam*, took the kings as an image of the inspirations that arrive in the heart from above. When such an inspiration is genuine, it enters the town of the heart the way a king enters a city — it overturns the standing order, topples the desires that had held the highest positions there, and leaves them abased.

The reading inverts the verse's valuation while keeping its structure, since what is destruction in politics becomes necessary demolition in the soul. It is allegory, not exegesis, and it should not be mistaken for what the queen meant. She was discussing armies. But the survival of her sentence in that literature indicates how exact the original observation was: a formula precise enough to describe what conquest does to a city was found precise enough to describe what truth does to a self. Her actual next move is considerably more practical.

## Verse 27:35

> But I will certainly send him a gift, and see what ˹response˺ my envoys will return with.”

**What a Gift Tests For**

Consider the possible outcomes she is buying information about. If Sulaymān accepts the gift and drops the demand, he is a king like other kings, wanting wealth, and his letter was an extortion dressed in religious language. If he accepts and keeps the demand, he is a conqueror and the matter is settled. If he refuses it outright, then the demand was not about wealth at all, and whatever he wants is something a gift cannot satisfy.

Only the third outcome is consistent with his letter being what it claims to be. She has designed a test where the sender's sincerity is the one hypothesis that survives a refusal, and she has designed it without committing herself to anything. The gift costs her treasure, not standing. Sulaymān's actual reaction, when it comes, is the third option delivered with some force, and it therefore gives her precisely the finding she needed: this is not a man who can be paid. Everything she does afterwards follows from that single result.

**The Commentators' Inventories of the Gift**

Classical commentaries supply extensive detail about what she sent — the metals, the quantities, the slaves, sometimes elaborate puzzles designed to test Sulaymān's wisdom. Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī transmit such accounts, sometimes at length.

Al-Rāzī dismissed the whole category of detail as irrelevant to the story, and he was right to. The Qur'an says *hadiyyah*, a gift, and stops. There is no evident route by which the contents of a three-thousand-year-old diplomatic package could have reached an eighth-century commentator, and the accounts do not agree with each other — which is the ordinary signature of material generated rather than transmitted. Genuine reports converge as they multiply; invented detail diverges. The inventories are best classified with the other narrative expansions the story attracted, and al-Rāzī's refusal to engage them is the soundest handling in the classical literature. What the narrative needs is that something valuable was sent and refused, and the text supplies exactly that and no more. The reticence is characteristic. The Qur'an withholds her name, her city, her dates and the contents of her gift, and keeps every detail that bears on how she thought.

**Her Reasoning Has Moved in Three Steps**

Track the queen across four verses. She received a letter and judged it noble. She asked her council for a ruling and got a capability report. She reasoned from a general law about what war does to a place. Now she acts, and the action is designed to produce knowledge rather than to settle the question.

Each step is conditioned by the one before and none of them skips ahead. She has not yet decided anything about Sulaymān's God, has not conceded a point of religion, and has not committed her kingdom to a course. What she has done is refuse to decide while uninformed — three times, in three different ways. The chapter's opening verses described people whose deeds were made to look good to them so that they wander blindly, and she is the counter-example built at length: someone who keeps checking. Her envoys reach Sulaymān's court in the next verse, and the reception they get is not what she expected to purchase.

## Verse 27:36

> When the chief-envoy came to him, Solomon said, “Do you offer me wealth? What Allah has granted me is far greater than what He has granted you. No! It is you who rejoice in ˹receiving˺ gifts

**"What God Has Given Me Is Better"**

His comparison is exact in its wording and easily misread. He does not say *what I have* is greater than *what you have*. He says *what God has given me* is better than *what He has given you*. Both sides of the comparison are described as gifts from the same giver.

That construction removes the boast from a sentence that would otherwise be one. Al-Ṭabarī's reading, that Sheba's wealth was no match for Sulaymān's, is true as far as it goes, but the grammar is saying something more careful. If both parties hold what they hold by grant, then the comparison is not between two men's achievements but between two allotments, and neither party has any standing to be proud of his. What makes his portion better is not its size. It includes prophethood, the knowledge named in verse 15, and command over creatures nobody commands — none of which can be added to by anything a treasury can send. Al-Qurṭubī's point is the practical one: a prophet is not in the market the gift assumed he was in.

**Wealth Is Not Condemned, Only Its Use as Currency Here**

It would be easy to read this verse as a general denunciation of riches, and the chapter does not support that reading. Sulaymān himself announced in verse 16 that he had been given a share of all things, and the queen's provision is described in identical terms in verse 23. Neither description carries criticism.

What the verse rejects is a specific transaction: the attempt to settle a question about worship by means of property. The gift was offered as a substitute for a decision, and it is refused because the decision cannot be substituted for. The Qur'an states the general principle elsewhere, that neither wealth nor children will avail on the Day when only a sound heart counts, and Sulaymān is applying it in a diplomatic setting. There is also a detail of his own conduct worth noting. He turns down a fortune while sitting on a throne of his own, and the refusal costs him nothing precisely because he does not regard what he has as his. The message he sends back with the envoys is considerably less gentle.

**The Envoy Is Addressed as a Party, Not a Messenger**

The Arabic pronouns in this speech shift in a way translation blurs. Sulaymān asks *do you offer me wealth* using a singular, addressing the chief envoy in front of him, then closes with a plural: *it is you who rejoice in your gift*. The rebuke starts with the man and ends with the court that sent him.

That widening is appropriate to what he is objecting to. An ambassador does not decide what his queen sends; the misjudgment being named belongs to the whole apparatus that designed the gift, and Sulaymān makes sure the plural reaches back to it. There is a practical function too. Envoys report what was said to them, and a rebuke framed in the plural is one the envoy must carry home as addressed to his masters rather than absorbed as a personal insult. Sulaymān is speaking past the man in the room to the council in Sheba, which is exactly what the queen intended when she sent people to watch rather than merely to deliver.

---

## Verse 27:37

> Go back to them, for we will certainly mobilize against them forces which they can never resist, and we will drive them out from there in disgrace, fully humbled.”

**The Threat Is Stated Plainly**

Having refused the gift, Sulaymān sends the envoy home with a message that contains no diplomacy at all. Forces they cannot face, expulsion from their own land, humiliation. Al-Ṭabarī identifies the addressee as the envoy from Sheba, which is the plain reading — the verb is singular and the delegation is being dismissed.

There is no point softening this. The letter had been restrained, the demand brief, the tone measured. This is not. What changed between the letter and this speech is that the demand was answered with treasure, and treating a claim about God as a matter for purchase is what draws the sharpness. The escalation is a response to the form of the reply rather than to its content. She had not refused; she had tried to convert the question into a different kind of question, and he refuses the conversion in the strongest terms available.

**Her Own Words Turned Around**

The most striking feature of this verse is that Sulaymān uses her vocabulary. She had said that kings entering a land debase its nobles — *jaʿalū aʿizzata ahlihā adhillah*, making the honoured into the abased. He now says he will drive them out *adhillah*, abased, and they will be *ṣāghirūn*, humbled.

Whether or not the envoy reported her council speech to him, the Qur'an places the same root in both mouths within four verses, and the effect on the reader is deliberate. Her general law about what kings do is quoted back at her as a specific forecast. She had used the observation as a reason to avoid war; he uses it as a description of what war would bring. Neither of them disagrees about the facts. The difference is that she thought the prospect was an argument for delay, and he is telling her it is an argument for decision. The echo is the most efficient possible reply to her reasoning, because it concedes her premise entirely.

**"We" Rather Than "I"**

Every verb in the threat is first-person plural: we shall come upon them, we shall drive them out. In the previous verse Sulaymān spoke of himself in the singular — what God has given *me*. The shift is not stylistic grandeur.

Arabic royal speech did use the plural for a single sovereign, but the reading available here is more concrete, since Sulaymān genuinely commands a plurality. The forces described in verse 17 were jinn, humans and birds marshalled in ranks, and the threat concerns exactly those forces. When he speaks of what God gave him he uses the singular, because the gift was personal; when he speaks of what will arrive at Sheba's border he uses the plural, because an army is a collective. The grammar tracks the difference between a man and a state. The envoy hearing it would have drawn the intended conclusion: what is being promised is not a royal temper but a mobilisation, and the speaker has the apparatus to mean it.

---

## Verse 27:38

> Solomon asked, “O chiefs! Which of you can bring me her throne before they come to me in ˹full˺ submission?”

**The Timing Clause Assumes an Outcome**

The most informative words in the verse are *qabla an ya'tūnī muslimīn* — before they come to me submitting. Sulaymān speaks as though the arrival is settled. No message has reached him announcing her journey; the last communication in either direction was his threat to the envoy.

Commentators split over what the participle means here, and the split matters. Al-Qurṭubī reports the reading that it means in obedience to Sulaymān, a political surrender. Al-Ṭabarī takes it as Muslims in the religious sense, submitted to the one God. The narrative's ending favours al-Ṭabarī: she arrives and declares faith in the Lord of all worlds, and no political settlement is ever mentioned. Reading the clause that way makes Sulaymān's confidence more remarkable rather than less. He is preparing a demonstration for a woman he expects to arrive as a believer, before she has left her capital, and the preparation is designed to help her get there.

**A King Asks Instead of Commanding**

The form of the request repays attention. He does not order the throne brought; he asks which of his chiefs can do it. *Ayyukum ya'tīnī bi-ʿarshihā* — which of you will bring me her throne.

This is the same man who commands jinn and marshals armies in ranks, and he opens a demand for the impossible by inviting volunteers. The effect is to make the response a matter of capability rather than obedience, and the two answers that follow are both offers rather than compliance. Each volunteer states what he can do and how fast, which is information Sulaymān would not have obtained by issuing an order. There is a continuity here with his conduct throughout the chapter: he inspected rather than assumed, verified rather than accepted, and now asks rather than commands. A ruler who wants to know what his people are actually capable of has to leave them room to tell him.

**The Throne Was the Right Object to Choose**

Of everything in Sheba, Sulaymān asks for the throne. The choice is precise. The hoopoe had named three things about that kingdom — a woman ruling, full provision, and a magnificent throne — and the throne is the one that stands for the other two.

A throne in the ancient world was not furniture. It was the physical assertion of a right to rule, made deliberately large and costly so that it would settle questions of rank before a word was spoken, and hers had impressed a bird flying over a wealthy kingdom. Removing it accomplishes something no message could. It demonstrates, without violence and without a single casualty, that the foundation of her authority is not beyond reach — and it does so before she arrives, so that the demonstration is available to her as evidence rather than as a humiliation after the fact. Whatever Sulaymān intended by the request, the object selected was the one that carried the argument. Who answered him, and what they offered, comes next.

## Verse 27:39

> One mighty jinn responded, “I can bring it to you before you rise from this council of yours. And I am quite strong and trustworthy for this ˹task˺.”

**What an ʿIfrīt Is**

The word is specific and the commentators do not agree on it. Al-Ṭabarī reports that an *ʿifrīt* is a giant among the jinn, and Ibn Kathīr transmits a description of one as large as a mountain. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī also record a different sense: a jinn notable for cunning and mischief rather than size. Al-Rāzī mentions a third usage in which the word can apply to a human being who is coarse and audacious.

Al-Qurṭubī cites in support of the mischief sense a report in which the Prophet ﷺ was confronted during prayer by an *ʿifrīt* attempting to disrupt him, and overpowered it. That incident is well attested in the collections, where the Prophet describes seizing the creature and says he intended to tie it to a pillar of the mosque so the children of Madīnah could look at it in the morning, before recalling Sulaymān's prayer for a kingdom belonging to no one after him and letting it go. The detail is revealing in this context: the Prophet's reason for releasing it is a recollection of this very king's unique dominion over jinn.

**"Strong and Trustworthy" — Two Different Assurances**

The *ʿifrīt* offers two guarantees, *qawiyy* and *amīn*, and they answer two distinct worries. Strength addresses whether he can carry the thing. Trustworthiness addresses whether he will bring it back intact, without taking what is on it or diverting to his own purposes.

The second assurance is the one a jinn would need to give. Arab tradition regarded these beings as powerful and unreliable in roughly equal measure, and Sulaymān's command over them is presented in this chapter as a marvel precisely because they are not ordinarily controllable. A volunteer from that class knows what his listener will be weighing. The pairing of the two words also appears elsewhere in the Qur'an as the standard formula for a good agent — the daughter of the man in Madyan recommends Mūsā for hire on exactly these grounds, that the best person to employ is the strong and the trustworthy. The *ʿifrīt* is applying for a job using the correct references.

**Raw Power Is Not the Highest Thing in This Court**

The structure of the two offers delivers the argument of the whole episode. First the strongest creature present states what brute capability can achieve. Then someone described only by knowledge does it faster.

This ranking runs through the chapter from its fifteenth verse, where the gift given to Dāwūd and Sulaymān was knowledge and their gratitude was for knowledge rather than for kingdom. It runs through the ant, who understood a situation, and the hoopoe, who understood a kingdom, and now through a contest in which the mountain-sized volunteer loses to someone holding a book. The Qur'an is not disparaging strength; the *ʿifrīt*'s offer is real and would have worked. It is placing strength below something else, and doing it by demonstration rather than by assertion. The demonstration follows immediately.

## Verse 27:40

> But the one who had knowledge of the Scripture said, “I can bring it to you in the blink of an eye.” So when Solomon saw it placed before him, he exclaimed, “This is by the grace of my Lord to test me whether I am grateful or ungrateful. And whoever is grateful, it is only for their own good. But whoever is ungrateful, surely my Lord is Self-Sufficient, Most Generous.”

**Who Was He?**

The commentators filled the gap in three different ways, and the disagreement is instructive. Al-Ṭabarī takes him to be a man, and the name usually supplied is Āṣif ibn Barkhiyā, said to have known a name of God by which supplications are answered. Ibn Masʿūd is reported to have identified him as Khiḍr, the figure who later becomes Mūsā's teacher. Al-Rāzī records and prefers a third possibility: that the speaker was Sulaymān himself, addressing the *ʿifrīt*.

Al-Rāzī's grounds are contextual and they are the strongest of the three. A prophet would be the person in the room most knowledgeable about the Book. And the gratitude that immediately follows — *this is by the grace of my Lord to test me* — reads most naturally as the words of the one who performed the act rather than of one who watched someone else perform it. Against this, the verse's own grammar introduces the knower and Sulaymān as apparently distinct, with Sulaymān *seeing* the throne set before him. None of the three identifications can be established, and the first can be traced to its source. The name Āṣif ibn Barkhiyā enters the commentaries through Ibn Isḥāq, the biographer, who reports him as a companion of Sulaymān — which places it in the same body of transmitted narrative material as the other Israelite detail in this story, not in the ḥadīth corpus. The Khiḍr identification is reported from Ibn Masʿūd and is likewise an opinion rather than a prophetic report. The Qur'an gives no name, and the disagreement among the commentators is itself the evidence that none of them had one.

**Which Book, and What the Knowledge Did**

*ʿIlmun min al-kitāb* is a portion of knowledge from the Book, and the partitive again matters — not the whole of it, a share. Al-Rāzī canvasses what the Book might be: the Preserved Tablet described in *“in a Preserved Tablet.”* (85:22), the scriptures of the prophets generally, or some revelation particular to Sulaymān, though what that would be he does not specify.

The mechanism is left equally open. Al-Ṭabarī's transmitted accounts have the man invoking a divine name, and some reports have the throne rising out of the ground rather than travelling. What the text establishes is only the relation: knowledge from a revealed source produced an effect that a mountain-sized jinn could not match for speed. That is a claim about where power actually comes from, and it disposes of the reading in which this scene is a magic contest. Nothing here is a technique anyone could acquire by practice. Both volunteers act by permission; the difference is that one of them acts through what God has disclosed.

**"Before Your Glance Returns to You"**

The phrase for the promised speed is *qabla an yartadda ilayka ṭarfuk*, literally before your gaze comes back to you. It means immediately, but the image is worth unpacking because the commentators drew different pictures from it.

Al-Ṭabarī reports one reading in which the sense is: in less time than it would take a man within your sight to walk over to you. Another takes it as the time required for your eye to travel out to the limit of its range. A third abandons the visual measure entirely and has the throne spring up from the ground where Sulaymān sat. The *ʿifrīt* measured his offer in the duration of a council session.

**The Throne Arrives and the King Suspects Himself**

Then comes the reaction, and it is the most important thing in the verse. Sulaymān sees the throne in front of him and does not celebrate. He calls it a grace from his Lord given to test him — *li-yabluwanī a-ashkuru am akfur* — whether I will be thankful or ungrateful.

Al-Ṭabarī's gloss is that Sulaymān understood such astonishing blessings to carry a trial of sincerity inside them. That is the insight, and it inverts the ordinary reading of good fortune. Most people examine hardship for a divine purpose and accept success without inspection. He does the reverse. Having just watched a throne cross a continent at his word, his first thought is that this is the more dangerous of the two conditions, because nothing about it feels like a test. The same recognition shaped his prayer after the ant, where he asked to be marshalled into gratitude rather than assuming he would feel it. A man who has already identified his own weak point does not need a second warning.

---

## Verse 27:41

> ˹Then˺ Solomon said, “Disguise her throne for her so we may see whether she will recognize ˹it˺ or she will not be able to.”

**The Throne Is Altered Before She Arrives**

Having obtained the throne, Sulaymān does not display it. He orders it changed. The verb is *nakkirū*, from the root for making something unrecognisable — the same root that gives Arabic its word for the unknown and for denial.

Al-Ṭabarī explains the instruction as changing the throne by adding some things and removing others. Al-Qurṭubī records a simpler version: turning it upside down. Either way the object remains hers and stops looking like hers, which is the precise condition a test of recognition requires. Too small a change and the question is trivial; too large and it is unanswerable. What is being prepared is an examination with a correct answer, and the design of it tells us something about the examiner. Sulaymān is not staging a spectacle. He has taken the most impressive object he could get and deliberately made it less impressive to learn something.

**A Test Designed to Be Passed**

There is a reading of this episode in which Sulaymān is humiliating her — seizing the seat of her power and then playing games with it. The design of the test argues against it.

He does not ask her to identify the throne cold. He asks whether it is *like* hers, which supplies the comparison and invites a judgment rather than demanding certainty. He alters it partially rather than beyond recognition. And he asks before revealing what he has done, so that her answer is her own. Every choice here lowers the difficulty and preserves her standing. A ruler intending to shame a rival would have unveiled the throne in its original state and let the silence do the work. What Sulaymān constructs is closer to a question a good teacher asks: hard enough to be informative, fair enough to be answerable, and phrased so that a wrong answer costs the answerer little.

**The King Keeps Testing**

This is the third time in the chapter that Sulaymān responds to a situation by devising a test. He tested the hoopoe's report by sending a letter and watching the reply. He tested his court's capability by asking who could bring the throne. Now he tests the queen's judgment by altering it.

The chapter opened by describing people whose deeds are made to look good to them so that they wander blindly, and every one of Sulaymān's tests is a defence against exactly that condition — the condition of a person whose beliefs are never checked against anything outside himself.

---

## Verse 27:42

> So when she arrived, it was said ˹to her˺, “Is your throne like this?” She replied, “It looks to be the same. We have ˹already˺ received knowledge ˹of Solomon’s prophethood˺ before this ˹miracle˺, and have submitted ˹to Allah˺.”

**Who Speaks the Second Sentence?**

The verse's second half is disputed among the commentators, and the two readings change its meaning considerably. On the reading this translation follows, the queen continues speaking: knowledge came to her before this, and she had already submitted. Al-Zamakhsharī records this as a minority position — that she is acknowledging the power of God and the truth of Sulaymān's prophethood before the throne and the pavilion were shown to her.

The majority took the words as Sulaymān's, spoken to his court: we were given knowledge before her and we were submitters. Al-Ṭabarī reads the following verse as continuing that speech. Both are grammatically available, since Arabic supplies no speech markers here. The minority reading has a real advantage: it explains why she is described as arriving at all, since a woman travelling to a king who demanded her submission has already conceded something, and it makes her later declaration in the palace a completion rather than a reversal. The majority reading has the advantage of the following verse, which speaks of her in the third person as one hindered by what she worshipped — an awkward thing for her to say of herself.

**"It Was Said" — the Questioner Goes Unnamed**

The verse uses a passive: *qīla*, it was said. Sulaymān gave the order to alter the throne in the previous verse, but the question itself is attributed to nobody in particular.

The construction recurs in this passage — she is told to enter the palace in the same impersonal form two verses later — and its effect is to take the king out of the frame at the moments that matter most for her. She is not being interrogated by a rival monarch; she is being presented with a question and a doorway. Whatever she concludes, she concludes without a face to concede to. That anonymity is consistent with how the whole encounter has been managed, from a letter that put God's name before Sulaymān's to a correction about the floor delivered as a flat statement of fact. The Qur'an is arranging a sequence in which nothing she does can be read as deference to the man in the room.

---

## Verse 27:43

> But she had been hindered by what she used to worship instead of Allah, for she was indeed from a disbelieving people

**What Was Blocking Her**

The verse names the obstacle precisely: *mā kānat taʿbudu min dūni Allāh* — what she used to worship instead of God. Not her pride, not her throne, not her advisors. The sun.

Al-Ṭabarī's reading is the plain one: her worship of the sun prevented her from worshipping God properly. The verb *ṣaddahā*, she was barred, is the same verb the hoopoe used in verse 24 when he said Satan had barred them from the way. The chapter is consistent about the mechanism. An existing object of worship does not leave a vacancy; it occupies the position, and the occupation is what has to be cleared before anything else can be placed there. This is why Sulaymān's letter demanded submission rather than argument, and why the hoopoe's speech began by denying before it affirmed.

**Two Ways to Read This Verse's Speaker**

If the previous verse's second half was Sulaymān speaking, this verse continues him, and al-Ṭabarī reads it that way. If the queen was speaking there, then this verse is the Qur'an's own narration, breaking in to explain her position.

The second option is the more natural for this particular sentence. The description is in the third person, it assesses her from outside, and it supplies a causal account of her past that a speaker would not ordinarily produce about herself in a foreign court. Al-Zamakhsharī's minority reading of the previous verse and this narratorial reading of the present one fit together into a coherent whole: she states what she has come to know, and the text explains what had kept her from it. Either way the content is the same, and the narrative is now positioned for its final move. The obstacle has been named. What remains is the moment that removes it, and it arrives in the next verse through something as ordinary as a floor.

---

## Verse 27:44

> Then she was told, “Enter the palace.” But when she saw the hall, she thought it was a body of water, so she bared her legs. Solomon said. “It is just a palace paved with crystal.” ˹At last˺ she declared, “My Lord! I have certainly wronged my soul. Now I ˹fully˺ submit myself along with Solomon to Allah, the Lord of all worlds.”

**The Folklore Around This Verse, and Why It Should Go**

A body of legend attached itself to this scene. The stories hold that Sulaymān's jinn told him the queen's mother was a jinn and that her legs were deformed, and that the glass floor was contrived to expose them; further tales have him marrying her afterwards. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī all record versions of this material.

Ibn Kathīr's judgment on it is the correct one. He sees little value in the folklore and traces it to Arab legend and to oral traditions carried in by converts from Judaism and Christianity — the category the scholars call *isrāʾīliyyāt*. Nothing in the Qur'an supports any of it. The text says she saw a surface, judged it water, and acted; it says nothing about her legs beyond the ordinary act of lifting a garment to cross water, and it reports no marriage. Ibn Kathīr's own handling confirms the classification. Where he transmits the jinn-mother material he names ath-Thaʿlabī as its channel, and where a report is attached to the Prophet he flags the weakness of the narrator; on the marriage he records a version from Wahb ibn Munabbih and states that it is not authenticated. A body of narrative whose most sympathetic transmitter marks it as unauthenticated at each point where it touches a chain is not a competing account of the episode. The episode as the Qur'an tells it is about perception and it ends in a declaration of faith.

**"I Have Wronged My Soul"**

Her confession uses a formula the Qur'an gives to others at their turning points. *Rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī* — my Lord, I have wronged myself. Mūsā says almost exactly this after the killing in Egypt: *“My Lord! I have definitely wronged my soul, so forgive me.”* (28:16).

The construction places the injury on herself. She does not say she was deceived by her people, misled by her advisors, or trapped by an inherited religion — all of which the previous verse had, in practice, said on her behalf. The Qur'an supplied her excuse and she declines to use it. That refusal is what makes the confession a real one. There is also something exact in the verb: *ẓulm* is putting a thing where it does not belong, and misdirected worship is precisely that, a right given to the wrong recipient. She names her error by its correct technical description without any prompting.

**Submitting "Along With Solomon," Not to Him**

Her final words are the most carefully constructed sentence anyone speaks in the chapter: *aslamtu maʿa Sulaymān li-Llāhi rabb al-ʿālamīn* — I submit, with Sulaymān, to God, the Lord of all worlds.

Three things are settled at once. She places herself beside him rather than beneath him, which the letter's wording had always permitted. She names the recipient of her submission as God, not the king who summoned her. And she uses the widest possible title, Lord of all worlds — the same phrase spoken at the fire in verse 8. Ibn ʿArabī drew the comparison that makes this land: her submission was superior to Pharaoh's, because she submitted to the Lord of all worlds without qualification, whereas he, at the moment the sea closed over him, submitted to the Lord of Mūsā and Hārūn. Pharaoh conceded a God attached to two men. She names God as He is. The chapter's two royal narratives end in the same act performed at two entirely different depths, and Sheba's story closes here, with the last word hers.

---

## Verse 27:45

> And We certainly sent to the people of Thamûd their brother Ṣâliḥ, proclaiming, “Worship Allah,” but they suddenly split into two opposing groups

**A New People, a Familiar Opening**

Sheba's story ended in a declaration of faith. The chapter now moves to Thamūd, and the shift in register is immediate. Where the Sulaymān narrative ran for thirty verses with dialogue, deliberation and tests, this one opens with a formula the Qur'an uses again and again: We sent to them their brother, saying worship God.

The compression signals what kind of story this is. Thamūd's account appears in several chapters — al-Aʿrāf, Hūd, ash-Shuʿarāʾ, al-Qamar — and readers of the Qur'an knew it. An-Naml is not retelling it but selecting from it, and what this chapter takes is the part about a plot and its unravelling. The people were an ancient Arabian nation, remembered in the Qur'an for carving dwellings out of rock, and their ruins in northwestern Arabia stood on routes the first audience used. This is not distant legend for them. It is a place they could visit.

**"Their Brother" Is a Deliberate Word**

Ṣāliḥ is called *akhāhum*, their brother. The Qur'an applies the same term to Hūd with ʿĀd, to Lūṭ with his people, to Shuʿayb with Madyan. The word means one of them by descent, sharing their lineage and their speech.

This detail forecloses a standard objection. A messenger who arrives from outside can be dismissed as a foreigner who does not understand the community, its history or its arrangements. Ṣāliḥ has no such distance available to him, and neither do his people. He is their kinsman, raised among them, speaking their dialect, and whatever he is telling them cannot be filed under the customs of some other nation. The Qur'an makes the same point about Muḥammad ﷺ to the Quraysh, who had known him for forty years before he recited anything. Sending a brother removes the easiest excuse before the message is delivered.

**Where Thamūd's Story Stands in the Chapter**

The chapter's four narratives are arranged with care. Mūsā before a court that knew and refused; Sulaymān and a queen who did not know and accepted; now Thamūd, and after them the people of Lūṭ. Two prophets sent to foreign powers, then two sent to their own kin.

That second pair changes the terms. Mūsā walked into Egypt as an outsider with a demand, and the hostility he met is at least intelligible. Ṣāliḥ speaks to his own nation, in their language, about the God they nominally acknowledged, and the response is a plot to kill him in the night. The chapter is closing off the explanations that let a reader keep the lesson at a distance. It is not foreignness that produces the refusal, and it is not the strangeness of the message. The Quraysh hearing this sūrah had a brother of their own reciting it to them, and the parallel needs no drawing.

---

## Verse 27:46

> He urged ˹the disbelieving group˺, “O my people! Why do you ˹seek to˺ hasten the torment rather than grace? If only you sought Allah’s forgiveness so you may be shown mercy!”

**He Still Calls Them His People**

The nation has split into two hostile factions and Ṣāliḥ addresses the hostile one as *yā qawmi* — O my people. The possessive is not a formality. It is the same word a man uses of the tribe he belongs to, and he keeps using it after they have declared against him.

The Qur'an gives this vocative to every prophet sent to his own kin, and it survives escalation in each case. Nūḥ uses it through centuries of rejection; Shuʿayb uses it while being threatened with stoning. What the address preserves is the relationship the message is meant to save. A messenger who began saying *you people* would have conceded the separation his opponents were trying to create, and the separation is the thing he is arguing against. Within three verses a group of them will be plotting to kill him and his household in the dark, and he is at this moment still claiming them as his own.

**Forgiveness Is Offered With No Preconditions**

The second half of his speech is the alternative: *lawlā tastaghfirūna Allāh laʿallakum turḥamūn* — why not seek God's forgiveness, so that you may be shown mercy. Nothing is attached to the offer. No compensation, no penance, no probationary term.

The construction is worth noticing. *Lawlā* with a present verb functions in Arabic as an urging rather than a question — closer to *why not simply* than to a demand for an explanation. And the outcome clause uses *laʿalla*, a particle of hope rather than guarantee, which keeps the promise honest: seeking forgiveness is what opens the possibility, and the granting remains God's. Ṣāliḥ is not selling a transaction. He is telling people standing at one end of a road that the other end is still reachable, and the only thing required to start is to ask. Set against the length of the punishment they are demanding, the cost of the alternative is nothing at all.

---

## Verse 27:47

> They replied, “You and your followers are a bad omen for us.” He responded, “Your omens are destined by Allah. In fact, you are ˹only˺ a people being tested.”

**"Your Omens Are With God"**

Ṣāliḥ's reply is compressed: *ṭā'irukum ʿinda Allāh* — your bird, your omen, is with God. The commentators unfold it in slightly different ways that add up to one position.

Al-Qurṭubī takes it to mean that the things they predict from omens are with God; al-Ṭabarī, that such matters are known to Him alone; al-Rāzī, that whatever good or evil reaches them originates with God rather than with any omen. The common claim is that causation has been misassigned. Something does determine what befalls them, and it is not the direction a startled bird flies, and it is not the presence of a preacher in their town. The answer is economical because the error is simple. They have taken a real question — why are things going badly — and attached it to a mechanism that cannot answer it.

**What They Actually Accused Him Of**

Their charge is worth stating precisely, because it is not a claim that he is lying. They do not say his message is false, his God does not exist, or his warnings are invented. They say he brings bad luck — *iṭṭayyarnā bika wa biman maʿak*, we take you and those with you as an evil sign.

That formulation concedes a great deal. It accepts that something has changed in their situation since he began speaking and that the change is real enough to need explaining. What it denies is only the explanation he offers. In place of a test they substitute a curse, which keeps the facts and reverses the responsibility. They also extend the charge to his followers, which is how factional thinking works: the believing party from verse 45 is now a category of person rather than a set of positions, and misfortune is attributed to their presence rather than to anything they have done. Ṣāliḥ's reply refuses both halves in one sentence.

---

## Verse 27:48

> And there were in the city nine ˹elite˺ men who spread corruption in the land, never doing what is right

**A Precise Count**

The Qur'an gives a number: nine. Not a faction, not a majority, not the people of the city — nine men, in one settlement, named by their count and their conduct.

The specificity narrows the story considerably. Thamūd had split into two parties in verse 45, and the disputing group was presumably large. Yet what follows — the conspiracy, the oath, the plan to murder Ṣāliḥ's household by night — is the work of this small number. Al-Ṭabarī identifies them as the ones who acted together to hamstring the she-camel and then conspired against the prophet. The Qur'an is describing how a nation's destruction is actually organised, and the answer is that it does not require many people. It requires nine, in the right positions, and a population that does not stop them.

**Corruption and Repair Are the Qur'an's Standard Pair**

The verse defines them twice, positively and negatively: they work *fasād* in the land and do not *iṣlāḥ*. The two roots are paired throughout the Qur'an as opposites — spoiling and setting right, breaking and mending.

The negative half is not filler. It closes an excuse that would otherwise be available, since a man who causes damage can always point to something he also built. These nine are described as doing no repair at all, which turns a description of acts into a description of a settled direction. The pairing also defines corruption usefully. *Fasād* in the Qur'an is not primarily personal vice; the root refers to something going rotten, a functioning thing being spoiled. A society is a working arrangement, and corruption is what makes it stop working — which is exactly what predatory lending and routine humiliation do to the bonds that let people live together.

---

## Verse 27:49

> They vowed, “Let us swear by Allah that we will take him and his family down by night. Then we will certainly say to his ˹closest˺ heirs, ‘We did not witness the murder of his family. We are definitely telling the truth.’”

**Swearing by God to Commit Murder**

The conspirators bind themselves with an oath, and the oath is *taqāsamū bi-Llāh* — by God. They invoke the name of the deity whose messenger they are planning to kill, as the guarantee of their agreement with one another.

This is the sharpest irony in the chapter and it is not accidental. Thamūd were not atheists. They acknowledged God in the way their society acknowledged many things, as a name to swear by, and Ṣāliḥ's demand that they worship Him is what they are conspiring against. An oath in His name is the strongest bond available in their culture, and they reach for it instinctively to secure a murder. What the detail exposes is the difference between recognising a name and submitting to what it refers to. Verse 14 described a court that knew and refused; this is a smaller version, where the knowledge has been reduced to a formula for making promises binding.

**"We Are Definitely Telling the Truth"**

The rehearsed speech ends with an assertion of honesty: *wa innā la-ṣādiqūn*. They are composing, in advance, the sentence with which they will vouch for a lie, and they are adding the emphatic particles that Arabic reserves for insistence.

There is a dark precision in this. The most heavily emphasised claim to truthfulness in the chapter comes from men constructing a perjury, and the emphasis is itself the tell — a truthful witness does not usually need to certify himself. The Qur'an has been building a vocabulary around truth and certainty since verse 3, where the believers were marked by *yaqīn*, and verse 14, where Pharaoh's court was certain and denied anyway, and verse 27, where Sulaymān said he would see whether the hoopoe was truthful. Here the word arrives in the mouths of conspirators as decoration on a fabrication. The verse leaves them at the moment the plan is complete, and the next verse says what happened to it.

**The Timing in the Wider Account**

The Qur'an's other chapters supply the surrounding events. Thamūd had demanded a sign, been given a she-camel, and hamstrung it; Ṣāliḥ then told them they had a fixed number of days left. The number of days is not left to the commentators. Hūd states it: *“But they killed her, so he warned ˹them˺, ‘You have ˹only˺ three ˹more˺ days to enjoy life in your homes— this is an unfailing promise!’”* (11:65). Several commentators accordingly place this conspiracy inside that three-day window.

If that placement is right, it makes the plot considerably stranger. These men had been told a deadline and are using the time inside it to arrange a murder — hastening the evil before the good, exactly as verse 46 described. They are also attempting to kill a man whose prediction of their destruction has not yet been falsified, which is a poor use of a countdown. Two things should be kept apart here. The three days are the Qur'an's own datum, stated in Hūd and not an inference. What the commentators add is the placement of *this particular meeting* inside those days, which an-Naml does not date; the sūrah reports the plot without a calendar. The reconstruction is reasonable and the deadline behind it is scriptural, but only the deadline is.

---

## Verse 27:50

> And ˹so˺ they made a plan, but We too made a plan, while they were unaware

**Does God Plot?**

The word *makr* in Arabic normally carries deception, and applying it to God raises an obvious difficulty. The Qur'an still uses it, here and elsewhere, and the solution the commentators reached is a linguistic one rather than an evasion.

*Makr* at root means a covert stratagem — a plan whose workings are hidden from the one it concerns. Deceit is a frequent feature of human stratagems, not the meaning of the word itself. When the Qur'an says God devised a plot, the hidden quality is what transfers: the conspirators could not see what was being arranged. Nothing false was told to them, and no promise was broken. The Book elsewhere calls God the best of planners and states that all planning belongs to Him. Read together, these passages describe sovereignty over outcomes rather than a divine talent for lying. The rhetorical repetition here is what forces the reading — the word is chosen because they chose it.

**The Response Is Simultaneous, Not Subsequent**

Both verbs are in the past tense and the sentence presents them as concurrent. God's plan is not described as a reaction arriving after theirs matured. It was running while theirs was being sworn.

That simultaneity is what makes the closing clause devastating. The nine had spent their meeting arranging a night attack and rehearsing an alibi, confident that the only parties to the matter were themselves, Ṣāliḥ, and an heir they intended to deceive. The verse says a third party was present throughout. Their secrecy was real about the city and worthless about what actually determined the outcome. The hoopoe's speech in verse 25 had already named the attribute at work — God knows what is concealed and what is disclosed — and here the abstract claim is cashed out in a specific conspiracy.

---

## Verse 27:51

> See then what the consequences of their plan were: We ˹utterly˺ destroyed them and their people all together

**The Command to Look, Used a Second Time**

The verse opens with the same imperative the chapter applied to Pharaoh's court in verse 14: *fa-anẓur kayfa kāna* — see then how it was. Thirty-seven verses apart, two nations are handed to the reader with the same instruction.

The formula recurs throughout the Qur'an at the close of destruction narratives, and its function is to convert a story into evidence. What the reader is told to examine is not the wickedness but the *ʿāqibah*, the end, the upshot. A story judged by its ending is being treated as a completed experiment, and experiments can be compared. The repetition within a single chapter turns two separate accounts into a series, and a series with two matching results is the beginning of a pattern. That is the argument an-Naml has been assembling since its opening verses, and here is where it starts to close.

**A Warning Placed Between Two Rescues**

Sequence is doing work here. Verse 44 ended with a queen declaring faith. Verse 53 will record the deliverance of those who believed. This verse sits between them, and it is the only one of the three that ends badly.

The arrangement prevents either extreme reading. A chapter of rescues would make the warnings decorative; a chapter of destructions would make the invitations empty. An-Naml alternates, and the alternation is the argument: the same message reached Sheba and Thamūd, and the outcomes diverged entirely according to what was done with it. Nothing in the two situations made the results inevitable. A sun-worshipping queen with every political reason to resist submitted; a nation warned by its own brother, with a believing faction already among them, ended as empty stone. The next verse tells the reader where those stones are.

**What the Nine Achieved**

Trace the conspirators' intentions against their result. They wanted Ṣāliḥ silenced, their standing in the city preserved, the she-camel affair closed, and an heir satisfied with a coordinated lie. Every one of those objectives concerned the continuation of normal life in their city.

What the plan produced was the end of the city. Not a setback, not an exposure, not a reversal of fortune for the nine alone — the removal of the entire community whose arrangements they were protecting. That is the specific irony the imperative points at. The plot was rational on its own terms, and its terms were wrong, because they had left out the only factor that would decide the outcome. The chapter had already named that omission in verse 25, where the hoopoe described God as knowing what is concealed and what is disclosed, and again in verse 50, where the plot they could not see was running alongside the one they made. This verse is where the two threads meet and the account is settled.

---

## Verse 27:52

> So their homes are there, ˹but completely˺ ruined because of their wrongdoing. Surely in this is a lesson for people of knowledge

**The Evidence Is Still Standing**

The demonstrative is present tense and pointing: *fa-tilka buyūtuhum* — those are their houses. Not *those were*. The Qur'an directs its first audience toward structures they could go and look at.

Thamūd's dwellings survive in northwestern Arabia, monumental façades cut directly into sandstone outcrops, and the site lies on the caravan route between Mecca and Syria that the Quraysh used for trade. Merchants passing north saw them. That geography converts the preceding verse's claim from a story into a checkable assertion, which is exactly what the imperative in verse 51 demanded — look at the outcome. The chapter is not asking for trust in a report about ancient peoples. It is pointing out of the window at a ruin whose emptiness is the whole argument.

**"Because of Their Wrongdoing"**

The verse assigns a cause: *bimā ẓalamū*, on account of the wrong they did. Al-Ṭabarī identifies the wrong as their idolatry and their denial of their prophet.

Naming the cause blocks the natural alternative explanation. Ruins invite the reading that a civilisation failed for the reasons civilisations usually fail — drought, invasion, trade routes shifting, a dynasty exhausting itself. The verse forecloses that reading for this case and states a moral cause instead. Whether a modern reader accepts the claim is a separate question from what the text is doing, which is refusing to let the evidence be absorbed into a purely material account. The word chosen is *ẓulm*, putting something where it does not belong, which covers both halves of al-Ṭabarī's gloss precisely: worship given to the wrong recipient, and a messenger given the wrong reception.

---

## Verse 27:53

> And We delivered those who were faithful and were mindful ˹of Allah˺

**The Exception That Completes the Accounting**

Verse 51 said the destruction covered them and their people all together. This verse states the exception, and without it the previous claim would describe an indiscriminate catastrophe. The believers were taken out.

The placement is deliberate. The Qur'an states the destruction first, in the strongest available terms, and supplies the qualification two verses later — long enough for the weight of the ruin to land, close enough that the accounting is completed within the same passage. Every destruction narrative in the Book follows this structure: a people ends, and those who believed among them do not. The pattern is what distinguishes these accounts from stories of divine temper. A punishment that fell on everyone present would be a disaster; one that sorts is a judgment.

**Two Conditions, Not One**

The deliverance is granted to those who *believed* and who *were mindful* — *alladhīna āmanū wa kānū yattaqūn*. Two verbs, and the second is not a restatement of the first.

*Īmān* is the inward assent: accepting that what Ṣāliḥ brought was true. *Taqwā* is the resulting carefulness, the conduct of someone who lives as though the assent were real. Arabic builds the word from a root meaning to guard oneself, and the Qur'an uses it for a practical vigilance rather than a feeling. Pairing them here does the same work the chapter's third verse did, where believers were identified by prayer, alms and certainty. A claim about the inside is given an outside. In a city run by nine notables whose business was corrupt lending and public humiliation, *taqwā* would have had an obvious daily content, and the people who practised it would have been visible.

**The Verb of Rescue**

*Najjaynā* comes from a root meaning to pull something clear, to bring it out of a place where it would have been lost. The same verb is used of Nūḥ on the ark, of Lūṭ leaving his city, and of Mūsā's people crossing the sea.

What the word describes is extraction rather than protection. The believers were not made immune where they stood; they were removed from where the destruction fell. That distinction matters for how the Qur'an presents divine rescue throughout. It generally involves motion, timing and a warning acted upon — Lūṭ's household leaves at night, Nūḥ builds for years before the water. Deliverance in these accounts is something people participate in, and the participation is the evidence that the belief was real. The verse's two conditions and its verb of extraction fit together: those who believed and were careful were the ones in a position to be pulled clear.

## Verse 27:54

> And ˹remember˺ Lot, when he rebuked ˹the men of˺ his people, “Do you commit that shameful deed while you can see ˹one another˺

**The Fourth and Shortest Narrative**

Lūṭ's story occupies five verses. Mūsā received eight, Sulaymān thirty, Ṣāliḥ nine. The chapter is closing its historical section, and the compression is a sign that the pattern is by now established rather than being built.

What an-Naml keeps is minimal: a question, a second question, the people's reply, the rescue, the destruction. No negotiation, no angels at the door, no bargaining over the city — all of which appear in Hūd and al-Ḥijr. The selection tells us what this chapter wants from the episode. It wants the exchange in which a prophet names a practice and its practitioners answer by expelling the people who object. That exchange is the one that matches what has come before, and everything else is left to the other accounts.

**A Rebuke Framed as a Question**

Lūṭ does not open with a prohibition or a threat. He asks. Both this verse and the next put his objection in interrogative form, and the form is a choice — Arabic had every resource for a flat command.

A question addressed to people about their own conduct invites them to supply the answer, which means it assumes they have the materials to see the problem. That assumption is the substance of Lūṭ's approach, and it fits his position: the Qur'an elsewhere calls him their brother in the same sense as Ṣāliḥ, one of the community speaking to his own. Rhetorical questions of this kind run through the Qur'an's prophetic speech from Ibrāhīm asking his father why he worships what cannot hear to Mūsā before Pharaoh, and their function is consistent. They locate the argument inside the listener rather than between the speaker and the listener. His second question, in the next verse, sharpens the first by naming what the practice displaces.

**"And Lot" — the Formula That Opens Each Account**

The verse begins *wa Lūṭan idh qāla li-qawmih* — and Lot, when he said to his people. The construction matches the chapter's other narrative openings: *wa laqad arsalnā ilā Thamūd*, and *idh qāla Mūsā li-ahlih*. Each prophet is introduced by a name and a moment of speech.

The pattern tells the reader what kind of history this is. These are not biographies; they are records of what was said and how it was answered. Mūsā is introduced speaking to his family about a fire, Sulaymān addressing his people about a gift, Ṣāliḥ proclaiming two words, Lūṭ asking a question. In every case the narrative begins at the point where a message enters a community, and it ends at the point where the community's answer has produced its result. Everything in between is the exchange. An-Naml is a chapter about how claims are received, and its narrative formula is built to isolate exactly that, stripping away everything that would turn an exchange into an adventure. The question is put to them directly rather than reported about them, which keeps the exchange in the present tense of a confrontation.

---

## Verse 27:55

> Do you really lust after men instead of women? In fact, you are ˹only˺ a people acting ignorantly.”

**The Objection Is Stated Plainly**

Where the previous verse used a definite article as a euphemism, this one names the matter directly: approaching men with desire *instead of* women. Lūṭ has moved from pointing at the practice to describing it.

The phrase that carries the argument is *min dūni al-nisāʾ* — to the exclusion of women, in place of women. The same construction appeared in verse 24, where Sheba's people prostrated to the sun *min dūni Allāh*, instead of God. The Qur'an uses this formula for substitution: something has been put in the position belonging to something else. That is a consistent way of framing what the Book objects to, and it recurs across quite different subjects. The complaint is not that a desire exists but that an arrangement has been displaced.

**"A People Acting Ignorantly"**

The verdict is *qawmun tajhalūn* — a people who are ignorant, or who behave ignorantly. The root *j-h-l* is important in Qur'anic vocabulary and it does not mean a simple lack of information.

*Jahl* in Arabic usage is the opposite of *ḥilm*, forbearance and self-possession, as much as it is the opposite of knowledge. The pre-Islamic period is called the *Jāhiliyyah* not because the Arabs of that era lacked learning — they had poetry, genealogy and astronomy — but because their conduct was governed by impulse, pride and unchecked appetite. Applied here, the word names a way of acting rather than a gap in what is known, a failure of self-government rather than of education, which is consistent with the previous verse's charge that they did this *while seeing*. They are not uninformed. They are operating without the restraint that information should produce.

**Two Charges That Fit Together**

Put the two verses side by side and the accusation has a shape. Verse 54 said they did it openly, in each other's sight, knowing. This verse says they are ignorant. Those look contradictory and are not.

Knowing something and being governed by it are different capacities, and the Qur'an keeps them apart throughout this chapter. Pharaoh's court was certain the signs were true and denied them anyway. Thamūd's nine swore by God while planning murder. Lūṭ's people know the practice is indecent — that is what *while you see* asserts — and the knowledge does not reach their conduct. *Jahl* names precisely that disconnection. It is the condition of a person whose information and whose behaviour have come apart, which is a more accurate description of most human failure than simple error is.

---

## Verse 27:56

> But his people’s only response was to say, “Expel Lot’s followers from your land! They are a people who wish to remain chaste!”

**Purity Turned Into an Accusation**

The charge is astonishing when read closely. They call for the expulsion of Lūṭ's household on the grounds that *innahum unāsun yataṭahharūn* — they are people who keep themselves pure.

The verb is *taṭahhara*, and the Qur'an uses it elsewhere as unambiguous praise. God is said to love those who purify themselves, and a mosque is commended because men in it love to be purified. Here the same word is used as an indictment. Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī both gloss the sense as a sneer: they are trying to keep themselves clean of what we do. The people of Lūṭ have not disputed that his household is pure; they have agreed, and made the purity the offence. That inversion is the most complete moral reversal recorded in this chapter, and it is delivered in a single word. The Qur'an reports the same sneer from these people in another chapter, in almost identical wording, which indicates it was their settled formulation rather than a remark made once in anger.

**A Response With No Content**

Strip the reply to its components and nothing remains but a motion and a justification. The motion is expulsion. The justification is that the people to be expelled are clean. There is no denial, no counter-claim about the practice, no defence of it, and no attack on Lūṭ's standing to speak.

That emptiness is what the restrictive opening formula was marking. The Qur'an could have reported an argument and refuted it; what it reports is that no argument was offered. This matters for the chapter's larger case, which has been measuring communities by the quality of their response to evidence. Sheba's queen received a demand and built a test around it. Sulaymān received a report and verified it. Pharaoh's court produced at least a classification, however dishonest — they called the signs sorcery, which is a claim capable of being wrong. Lūṭ's people produce nothing that can be assessed at all, which places them at the bottom of the chapter's scale. The reply is recorded in their own words, so the assessment that follows rests on what they said rather than on a characterisation of it.

---

## Verse 27:57

> So We delivered him and his family, except his wife. We had destined her to be one of the doomed

**Marriage to a Prophet Saved Nobody**

The verse is the Qur'an's clearest statement that religious standing is not inherited or acquired by association. The woman lived in the house of a messenger of God, shared his table, and was part of the household the rescue covered.

The Book presses this point elsewhere with the same example. Sūrah at-Taḥrīm sets the wives of Nūḥ and Lūṭ side by side as a parable for those who disbelieve — both married to righteous servants, both of no avail to their husbands — and then places against them the wife of Pharaoh, who believed inside the house of the chapter's worst tyrant. The pairing is deliberate and it cuts both ways. Neither the best nor the worst household determines what the person inside it becomes. Lūṭ's wife appears in five other chapters of the Qur'an, and in every one of them the same exception is recorded. That consistency across passages of very different length and emphasis indicates how central the point was held to be.

**The Household Is Rescued as a Household**

The rescue covers *him and his family*, a unit rather than a list of qualifying individuals. That framing is worth holding alongside the exception, because the two together describe how the Qur'an handles collective and individual fate.

Ordinarily the household travels as one. Nūḥ's family boards the ark, Ibrāhīm's leaves with him, Lūṭ's is brought out. The Book does not treat faith as a purely private matter with no social shape; a believing household is a real thing and its members are extracted together. Sharing a roof with people who believe exposes a person to the argument daily and makes the choice easier than it would otherwise be, and the Qur'an counts that as a genuine advantage. What the exception establishes is that the unit is not a container that protects whatever is inside it. Membership is the normal channel of rescue and it is not a guarantee, and the difference between those two statements is the whole content of this verse. Nūḥ's son, refused a place on the ark in another chapter, makes the same point from the other side.

## Verse 27:58

> And We poured upon them a rain ˹of brimstone˺. How evil was the rain of those who had been warned

**"How Evil Was the Rain"**

The second clause is an exclamation of the kind Arabic reserves for emphasis: *fa-sā'a maṭaru al-mundharīn* — evil was the rain of those who had been warned. The construction is compressed and the genitive is unusual.

The rain is called *theirs* — the rain of the warned. It belongs to them in the way a sentence belongs to a convict, produced by their situation rather than arriving from outside it. And the group is identified not as the sinners or the deniers but as *al-mundharīn*, those who had received warning. That is the description the verse chooses at the moment of destruction, and it is the one that makes the destruction intelligible. What fell on them fell on people who had been told.

**The Heading in the Text**

The translation line carries an editorial heading that belongs to the printed edition rather than to the verse: a label announcing the section of questions addressed to the pagans that begins immediately afterwards. It is reproduced here because the translation is quoted exactly as it stands.

The label is not part of the revelation, but it does identify something real about the chapter's structure. An editor reading straight through felt the need to mark a break at precisely this point, which confirms what the passage itself indicates: verse 58 closes the narrative half and verse 59 opens something formally different. Modern printed Qur'ans frequently carry such headings, added by translators and publishers to help readers navigate; they vary between editions and carry no authority. The sentence is short and the brevity is functional, since nothing further needs to be established once the outcome has been named.

## Verse 27:59

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Praise be to Allah, and peace be upon the servants He has chosen.” ˹Ask the disbelievers,˺ “Which is better: Allah or whatever ˹gods˺ they associate ˹with Him˺?”

**Praise and Peace Before the Challenge**

Before the question is put, two things are said. Praise belongs to God, and peace be upon the servants He has chosen. Only then does the interrogation begin.

The order is not decorative. A argumentative question that opens with praise of God establishes that the speaker's position is not primarily against anything, and the salutation on the chosen servants ties the challenge to the line of messengers the chapter has just recounted — Mūsā, Dāwūd, Sulaymān, Ṣāliḥ, Lūṭ. Those five were the *chosen servants* of the preceding narratives, and greeting them here links the history to the argument that follows. There is also a formal effect. The section that is about to ask five times whether there is another god beside God begins by giving God what is owed to Him, which is the practical answer to its own question.

**A Section Built on Repetition**

What begins here runs for several verses with a fixed shape. Each states something God does — creating the heavens and earth, sending water, fixing the earth, answering distress, guiding through darkness, originating creation — and each ends with the same refrain asking whether there is a god beside Him.

The repetition is the method. A single instance could be argued with; six instances covering cosmology, agriculture, geography, prayer, navigation and provision leave no domain in which the associated deities have work to do. The construction is also grammatically pointed. Each question begins with a relative clause — *He who created*, *He who made* — of a kind that sets up a comparison and then does not complete it, arriving instead at the refrain. The implication carried by that incompleteness is that no explicit comparison should be offered at all. The first of the six arrives in the next verse and begins with the largest claim available.

## Verse 27:60

> Or ˹ask them,˺ “Who created the heavens and the earth, and sends down rain for you from the sky, by which We cause delightful gardens to grow? You could never cause their trees to grow. Was it another god besides Allah?” Absolutely not! But they are a people who set up equals ˹to Allah˺

**"Delightful Gardens" and What the Words Mean**

The Arabic is *ḥadā'iqa dhāta bahjah*. A *ḥadīqah* is not a garden in the general sense; al-Ṭabarī defines it as an enclosed, walled garden, and al-Qurṭubī specifies gardens of date palms. The word denotes cultivated ground with a boundary around it — a property.

*Bahjah* is beauty of the kind that gladdens. Al-Ṭabarī connects it here to what is edible, the flowering and fruiting from which people and animals eat, so the delight is not purely visual. Taken together the phrase names something an Arabian audience knew as the highest form of agricultural wealth: a walled palm garden in fruit. The verse's argument is then addressed to the owner of exactly such a property. He built the wall, dug the channels, planted the offshoots and pruned the crowns, and the verse is about to tell him what he did not do.

**"Was It Another God?" — the Refrain Begins**

The question closes with *a-ilāhun maʿa Allāh* — is there a god along with God? This is the refrain that will end each of the questions in this section, and its form is worth attention.

The preceding clause set up a comparison and never delivers one. The grammar leads the listener to expect something placed against God and then arrives at a question about whether anything can be placed there at all. That structural refusal is itself the argument: the power and generosity just described are of an order that should not be offered a comparison. The answer follows immediately as *bal*, a particle that cancels what came before — no, rather, they are a people who set up equals. And the verb chosen, *yaʿdilūn*, is ambiguous in a way the commentators noticed. Without a preposition to fix it, it can mean to make equal or to swerve away. Both fit. A person who assigns God an equal has, in the same act, turned aside from Him.

## Verse 27:61

> Or ˹ask them,˺ “Who made the earth a place of settlement, caused rivers to flow through it, placed firm mountains upon it, and set a barrier between ˹fresh and salt˺ bodies of water? Was it another god besides Allah?” Absolutely not! But most of them do not know

**The Barrier Between Two Waters**

The fourth item is the strangest: a barrier set between two bodies of water. The Qur'an returns to this image in several places, sometimes calling the divider *ḥājiz* and sometimes *barzakh*, and the pairing is usually understood as fresh water and salt.

The phenomenon is observable. Where a large river meets the sea, the two waters remain visibly distinct for a considerable distance, differing in colour, density and salinity, and they mix slowly rather than at once. Estuaries maintain a measurable boundary layer between outflowing fresh water and intruding salt. What the verse names is therefore something a traveller could see at any major river mouth on the coasts the Arabs traded along.

**"Most of Them Do Not Know"**

The refrain is answered here with a statement about knowledge: *bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn* — rather, most of them do not know. The previous verse ended by calling them people who set up equals. This one is gentler and more diagnostic.

Two things follow from the wording. The first is *most*, not all, which keeps the judgment honest — the Qur'an's argument against Meccan paganism regularly leaves room for exceptions, and this chapter has just spent thirty verses on a queen who was wrong and correctable. The second is that ignorance is named rather than malice. Pharaoh's court in verse 14 knew and refused, which is the worst case; these are people who have not put the question to themselves. That diagnosis explains the form of this whole section. You do not answer ignorance with a threat. You answer it with a question, repeated in enough different domains that the answer becomes hard to avoid, which is exactly what the chapter is doing.

## Verse 27:62

> Or ˹ask them,˺ “Who responds to the distressed when they cry to Him, relieving ˹their˺ affliction, and ˹Who˺ makes you successors in the earth? Is it another god besides Allah? Yet you are hardly mindful!”

**Who the "Distressed" Are**

The word is *al-muḍṭarr*, a passive form meaning one who has been driven to necessity — pushed into a corner by circumstances rather than having walked into one. Al-Qurṭubī glosses it as the helpless and powerless; al-Rāzī specifies someone in a bad situation not of their choosing, afflicted by poverty or illness.

The grammar is the point. The person described did not choose distress and cannot choose their way out; the form of the word denies them agency. That is a specific and unflattering condition, and the verse makes it the one God is named as responding to. There is no mention of the righteous, the deserving, or those with a record. The qualification for the response described here is having nothing left, which is the opposite of how access to a patron normally works in any human system.

**The Supplication With No Veil**

Al-Qurṭubī connects this verse to a report about the prayer of the wronged, and the report is well attested. When the Prophet ﷺ sent Muʿādh ibn Jabal to Yemen he instructed him at length and ended with a warning: beware the supplication of the oppressed, for there is no veil between it and God (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1496; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 19).

The context of that instruction sharpens its application here. Muʿādh was being sent as a collector of the obligatory charity, with authority over people's property, and the warning was against taking the best of their wealth. The prayer that passes without a veil is therefore the prayer of the person your own power has placed in distress. Read alongside this verse, the two texts describe the same access from two angles: the Qur'an says God answers the one driven to necessity, and the report tells those with power that this is a fact about their own position. The chapter has already shown nine notables in a city who did not think so.

**"Hardly Mindful"**

The refrain closes with *qalīlan mā tadhakkarūn* — little do you remember, or reflect. The verb is from *dhikr*, recollection, and it implies the material was already possessed.

That is the accurate charge for what this verse describes. Nobody needs to be informed that they called out in their worst hour; they need to remember it. The experience of being answered is one of the most common in human life and one of the most quickly filed away once the crisis passes, because acknowledging it has consequences for the rest of the week. The verse's complaint is therefore not against an absence of evidence but against the handling of evidence already held — the same complaint the chapter made against Pharaoh's court, who knew, and against Sheba, whose people were barred by what they already worshipped. Note also the switch to the second person: the previous verse said *most of them do not know*, and this one says *you* hardly reflect. The audience has stopped being discussed and started being addressed.

## Verse 27:63

> Or ˹ask them,˺ “Who guides you in the darkness of the land and sea, and sends the winds ushering in His mercy? Is it another god besides Allah? Exalted is Allah above what they associate ˹with Him˺

**The Refrain Answered With Exaltation**

Each of these questions closes differently. Verse 60 answered with a charge of setting up equals; verse 61 with ignorance; verse 62 with failure to reflect. This one answers with *taʿālā Allāhu ʿammā yushrikūn* — exalted is God above what they associate.

The escalation is worth following. The first three answers describe the people; this one describes God, and it does so by distance. *Taʿālā* means to be high above, and the preposition sets Him above the comparison itself rather than at the top of it. This is the section's most direct refusal of the framing its own questions employ. Having invited a comparison five times, the refrain here states that the comparison was never available. That is not a contradiction. The questions were addressed to people who believed a comparison was possible, and they work by allowing the attempt until the attempt collapses.

**Guidance Is the Chapter's Oldest Theme**

The verb in this question, *yahdī*, has been running through an-Naml from the beginning. Verse 2 called the Book guidance; verse 4 said the deniers wander blindly; verse 24 said Sheba's people were left unguided; verse 41 asked whether the queen would be guided.

Placing the same verb here, in a question about crossing a desert at night, is the chapter's most economical connection between its two halves. The narratives were about people finding or failing to find their way with evidence in front of them. This question asks about people finding their way with stars in front of them. Both are cases of a traveller who cannot generate direction from inside himself and must read something placed outside. The Qur'an does not argue the analogy; it uses one word for both operations and lets a reader who has come this far make the link.

## Verse 27:64

> Or ˹ask them,˺ “Who originates the creation then resurrects it, and gives you provisions from the heavens and the earth? Is it another god besides Allah?” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Show ˹me˺ your proof, if what you say is true.”

**"Show Me Your Proof"**

The refrain is followed by a demand that has not appeared in the previous questions: *qul hātū burhānakum in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — bring your proof, if you are truthful. The burden shifts.

This is the section's sharpest move. Five questions have put the case for one position; now the other side is asked to produce an argument of its own. The word *burhān* means a demonstrative proof, evidence that compels rather than persuades, and it is the same register as the *sulṭān mubīn* Sulaymān demanded of the hoopoe in verse 21. The Qur'an uses this demand repeatedly against those who assert partners with God, and the demand is always unanswered in the text, because the position it addresses was never argued for. Meccan paganism was inherited practice, not a set of claims defended with reasons, and asking for proof exposes that in one sentence. The Qur'an records their actual justification in other places, and it is always the same: we found our fathers doing this.

**The Condition Attached to the Demand**

The clause *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — if you are truthful — is doing more than adding emphasis. It ties the request for evidence to the speaker's own honesty rather than to the strength of his case.

That connection has been running through the chapter. Sulaymān told the hoopoe he would see whether he was truthful or among the liars, and the bird answered with sure news from Sheba. Thamūd's nine rehearsed a false statement and certified themselves as truthful. Here the same standard is applied to the audience: a person who asserts something is claiming to know it, and a claim to know carries an obligation to be able to say how. The chapter has consistently treated the willingness to be tested as the mark of an honest position. What cannot be known by anyone at all, including the Prophet, is the subject of the verse that follows.

---

## Verse 27:65

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “None in the heavens and the earth has knowledge of the unseen except Allah. Nor do they know when they will be resurrected

**Knower of the Absent and the Present**

The Qur'an pairs *ghayb* with *shahādah* in a formula it uses about a dozen times, describing God as Knower of the unseen and the witnessed. The pair could equally be rendered as what is absent and what is present.

Setting the two together is what makes the attribute meaningful. A being who knew only hidden things would be a specialist; the formula asserts that the same knowledge covers both categories without difference, so that the distinction between hidden and apparent is a fact about creatures rather than about God. This chapter already used the claim in the hoopoe's argument to Sheba, where God was described as knowing what is concealed and what is disclosed. Verse 50 cashed it out in narrative when nine conspirators made a plan they believed nobody could see. The abstract attribute, the historical example and this general statement are three presentations of one claim.

**Even the Angels Are Included**

The scope is *man fī al-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — whoever is in the heavens and the earth. The heavens are where the Qur'an locates the angels, and they are therefore covered by the denial.

That inclusion closes off a route the surrounding argument needs closed. Meccan religion treated certain beings as intermediaries with privileged access, and the jinn in particular were credited by soothsayers with information from above. This chapter has already shown a jinn of extraordinary power volunteering in Sulaymān's court, and it will not have escaped the audience that such a creature could carry a throne across a continent. Power and knowledge are separated cleanly: an *ʿifrīt* can move a throne and cannot tell you when the Hour comes. Sūrah Sabaʾ makes the same point about Sulaymān's jinn labourers, who kept working after his death because they had no way of perceiving that he had died.

**A Claim That Cost Something to Make**

Consider the position this statement puts its speaker in. The Prophet is being publicly asked for a date, the questioners are hostile, and the answer he is instructed to give is that nobody knows and he is included in nobody.

An inventor of a religion does not write that sentence. The whole value of a claimed channel to the unseen lies in what it can produce on demand, and a movement's credibility in seventh-century Arabia was measured against soothsayers who did produce, however unreliably. Declining the question forfeits that ground entirely. The Qur'an makes the Prophet decline it repeatedly and in the most exposed circumstances, and it does the same with other things he was asked and could not answer. The pattern is one of the internal features of the text that later Muslim scholars pointed to when arguing that its author was not the man reciting it.

---

## Verse 27:66

> No! Their knowledge of the Hereafter amounts to ignorance. In fact, they are in doubt about it. In truth, they are ˹totally˺ blind to it

**A Verb the Reciters Read Differently**

The opening phrase is *bal iddāraka ʿilmuhum fī al-ākhirah*, and the classical readers vocalised the consonants in more than one way, producing genuinely different senses. Al-Rāzī sets out the options.

Read as a question, it becomes a reproach: has their knowledge reached to the Hereafter? — meaning of course it has not. Read as a statement, al-Ṭabarī reports the sense that their knowledge *will* be complete in the Hereafter, when the thing they denied is in front of them and knowing it is worth nothing. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī note a third possibility, taking the verb as a contracted form of *tadāraka*, which yields the sense that their knowledge is completed there. The readings converge on one point and differ in tone. Nobody holds that these people currently know anything about the matter; the question is whether the verse mocks the claim now or predicts the moment it collapses.

**Knowledge Arriving Too Late**

One of the readings al-Ṭabarī transmits deserves separate attention, because it says something the other readings do not. On that vocalisation the clause is a statement rather than a question: their knowledge *does* reach the Hereafter — meaning it arrives there, at the point where knowing is useless.

The picture is grim and the Qur'an draws it elsewhere at length. The Book describes the deniers at the resurrection asking to be sent back, admitting what they had refused, seeing clearly at the one moment when clear sight purchases nothing. Knowledge in that setting is no longer a basis for decision; the decisions are over. What the reading adds to this verse is a completion of its three-stage description. Empty now, doubtful now, blind now — and then, at the end, full knowledge delivered to people who have nothing left to do with it. The value of understanding something depends entirely on when it arrives, and the chapter has been arguing since its opening verses that the interval available for acting on it is the thing being spent.

---

## Verse 27:67

> The disbelievers ask, “When we and our fathers are reduced to dust, will we really be brought forth ˹alive˺

**The Objection Is About Physical Bodies**

What they find incredible is specific. Not that God exists, not that He created them, but that a person reduced to *turāb*, dust, could be brought out again as himself. The verb they use, *mukhrajūn*, means taken out — the image is of extraction from a grave.

That specificity tells us the dispute was never theological in the broad sense. The Meccans conceded a creator; the Qur'an says so elsewhere. What they could not accept was a bodily resurrection, and the reason is visible in their phrasing. They are reasoning from an observation: decomposition is total, the material disperses, and nothing in ordinary experience reverses it. As an inference from available evidence it is entirely sound. It is also, the chapter has just argued, an inference drawn from the wrong evidence base, since the question is not what usually happens but what the one who began the process can do.

**Why This Objection Recurs Across the Qur'an**

The same question appears in nearly identical wording in ar-Raʿd, al-Isrāʾ, al-Muʾminūn, aṣ-Ṣāffāt and al-Wāqiʿah. Five chapters, one sentence, barely altered — which tells us the Qur'an was answering a fixed formulation rather than a shifting set of doubts.

A repeated objection usually indicates a settled talking point, something a community has reduced to a single line that closes the conversation. The Meccan version had all the marks of one: short, concrete, framed as a question, and apparently unanswerable by observation. The Qur'an's treatment of it is correspondingly consistent. It never disputes the premise that bodies decompose, and it never offers a mechanism for reassembly. What it does, every time, is relocate the question from what has been observed to who is being asked, which is the only place the argument can actually be settled. The objection is about the limits of nature; the reply is about who set them. That is why none of the answers in the Qur'an is technical. Each one asks the questioner to compare the difficulty of the second act with the difficulty of the first, and the comparison always runs the wrong way for the objector.

---

## Verse 27:68

> We have already been promised this, as well as our forefathers earlier. This is nothing but ancient fables!”

**"Ancient Fables"**

The dismissal is *asāṭīr al-awwalīn*, the tales of the ancients. The phrase appears repeatedly in the Qur'an as the standard Meccan verdict on the recitation, and it is worth taking seriously as a classification rather than an insult.

Every culture has a category for stories that are told, valued, and not believed — legends, old wives' tales, the things people say. Filing the Qur'an in that category solves the problem of what to do with a text that is manifestly impressive and inconveniently demanding. It concedes the artistry and removes the obligation. The move is identical in structure to the one Pharaoh's court made in verse 13 of this chapter, when they called the signs *pure magic*: assign the phenomenon to a known and harmless category, and the question of its source disappears. Two peoples, separated by a thousand years, reaching for the same manoeuvre. Neither of them examined the classification before applying it, which is what makes the two cases parallel rather than merely similar. A label adopted because it settles a problem is not a judgment about evidence.

**What "Promised" Concedes**

Their complaint contains an admission. They say they *were promised* this, and their fathers before them — which acknowledges that the warning has a history, was delivered to earlier generations, and has been consistent.

That is a considerable thing to grant while dismissing the content. A teaching invented recently by one man in Mecca would not have reached their forefathers, and they are not claiming it did. They are claiming that a long-standing, repeated promise has not been kept. The Qur'an's reply to that complaint is distributed across the chapter rather than stated here: the promise has no date attached, the peoples who treated the delay as a refutation are the ones whose ruins now stand on the trade roads, and the delay itself is described elsewhere as a space given for return. Their own phrasing establishes the continuity of the message, which is one of the things the chapter has been arguing. An objection that concedes its opponent's main historical claim is not a strong objection. The objection is given in full before it is answered, which is how this chapter consistently handles the positions it rejects.

---

## Verse 27:69

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Travel throughout the land and see the fate of the wicked.”

**The Reply Is an Instruction, Not an Argument**

They called the message ancient fables. The answer is: go and look. *Sīrū fī al-arḍ fa-anẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mujrimīn* — travel in the land and see how the guilty ended.

Nothing in the reply engages the philosophical objection about dust and bodies at all. The reply changes the mode of the dispute from assertion to inspection, which is the move the whole chapter has been preparing. An-Naml has twice already issued this command about a specific people: at Pharaoh's court in verse 14 and at Thamūd in verse 51. Here the same imperative is generalised and attached to a journey. What was a rhetorical instruction in those two places becomes a practical one here.

**Looking With More Than the Eyes**

Al-Qurṭubī records the reading that *fa-anẓurū* here means to observe with the heart and with insight rather than merely with the eye. The gloss is not a spiritualising of a plain command; it identifies a real requirement.

Caravans had passed those ruins for centuries, and the passing produced no conclusions. Seeing a collapsed city and drawing an inference from it are separate operations, and the second requires a willingness to treat the ruin as a case rather than as scenery. This is the same restriction verse 52 attached to the empty houses of Thamūd, where the sign was said to be for a people who know. The evidence is public and the reading of it is not automatic, which is why the Qur'an issues an imperative about looking rather than simply stating what is there.

---

## Verse 27:70

> Do not grieve for them, nor be distressed by their schemes

**Grief for Them, Not About Them**

The Arabic is *lā taḥzan ʿalayhim* — do not grieve *over* them. The preposition matters. He is not being told to stop being upset by their behaviour; he is being told to stop mourning for them.

That is a different emotion and a more specific one. The men mocking him were his kin, his neighbours, his clan. He had grown up among them and knew what was coming to people who persisted, having just recited the fate of Thamūd and Lūṭ's people. What he is described as feeling is the sorrow of someone watching people he cares about walk toward something. The Qur'an elsewhere puts this in stronger terms, telling him he might destroy himself with grief over their refusal to believe — an image of a man being consumed by anxiety about others. The instruction here is a limit on that, not a rebuke for having it.

**The Qur'an Records a Prophet's Emotional Life**

Set this verse alongside the others in this chapter that report interior states. Mūsā ran from a staff in fear. Sulaymān smiled at an ant and worried that his own blessings were a test. Now Muḥammad ﷺ is grieving and constricted, and the Book says so.

None of these is presented as a failing, and none is edited out. The pattern is consistent enough to count as a position: the men God chose are shown feeling what people feel, and the feeling is addressed rather than denied. That has a practical consequence for how the text has been read. A community whose founder is recorded as needing to be told not to grieve has scriptural warrant for its own grief, which is a different inheritance from one whose exemplars are depicted as serene.

---

## Verse 27:71

> They ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?”

**Why the Question Cannot Be Answered**

Verse 65 had already closed this off. Nobody in the heavens or the earth knows the unseen except God, and the resurrection was named there as the specific case. The demand in this verse asks for exactly what was just declared unavailable.

That makes the question a trap rather than an inquiry, and the questioners presumably knew it. If the Prophet names a date he either proves himself false when it passes or contradicts what he has already said. If he declines, he can be presented as evasive. The Qur'an's reply, in the next verse, does neither. It gives a partial answer that concedes the ignorance and removes the comfort the ignorance was supposed to provide, which is the only move available that is both honest and effective.

**The Same Demand Across Several Chapters**

This question recurs in Yūnus, al-Anbiyāʾ, Sabaʾ, Yā Sīn and al-Mulk in almost identical wording. Five other chapters carry it, and in each the reply is a variation on what an-Naml gives here: a refusal to name a time, coupled with a statement that the thing is nearer than assumed.

The consistency of both the question and the answer is informative. It indicates that this was the settled shape of the dispute in Mecca over a period of years — not an argument that developed, but a challenge repeated and a reply repeated. It also means the Qur'an never broke its own rule under pressure. Across every instance of the demand, no date is ever given, no approximation offered, and no sign specified by which the timing could be calculated. A text produced under that much provocation, by an author who wanted to convince, would have been sorely tempted at some point. This one holds the line in all six places.

---

## Verse 27:72

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Perhaps some of what you seek to hasten is close at hand.”

**"Close Behind You"**

The verb is *radifa*, and al-Ṭabarī glosses it as having come near. Al-Qurṭubī adds the root's more specific sense: to follow directly in someone's footsteps, the way a second rider sits behind the first on the same mount.

That image is exact and it is unnerving. What follows in your footsteps is not visible from where you stand, is not in front of you where it could be watched, and is closer than anything you can see. The word does not say the punishment is coming; it says it may already be behind them, moving at their own pace. A demand for a date assumes the threatened event is somewhere ahead on a road, at a measurable distance. The reply places it in the one position that cannot be measured at all.

**Why "Perhaps" Is the Honest Word**

*ʿAsā* in the Qur'an, spoken by God, is not the hedge of someone uncertain. Classical commentators generally read the particle in divine speech as indicating something realised, softened in expression rather than in content.

But the softening is not empty here, and the reason returns to verse 65. The Prophet has been told, and has publicly said, that nobody knows the unseen. A flat prediction in his mouth would contradict that statement four verses later. *ʿAsā* is therefore the precise word: it lets him convey the nearness without claiming the knowledge, and it keeps the two verses consistent. The Qur'an is careful about this in a way a careless text would not be, and the care is itself evidence of how seriously the limit announced in verse 65 was meant.

**Answering a Taunt Without Adopting Its Terms**

The reply is a model of how the chapter handles hostile questions. It does not accept the premise that a date is owed, does not concede that the absence of a date is a weakness, and does not counter-attack.

What it does is supply a possibility the questioner had not budgeted for. Their challenge assumed two outcomes, a date they could test or an admission they could mock. The answer produces a third, and the third is the one that costs them something to hear. Sulaymān used the same technique with the envoys from Sheba, refusing the gift rather than negotiating over it, and the queen used it herself when she declined both war and surrender and sent a gift instead. An-Naml is full of people who decline the options they are offered and construct a better one, and the reply here is the last instance of it in the chapter.

---

## Verse 27:73

> Surely your Lord is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most of them are ungrateful

**Ingratitude as the Standard Condition**

The second half is a demographic claim: *wa lākinna aktharahum lā yashkurūn* — but most of them are not grateful. The Qur'an repeats this observation in a dozen places, always with the same word for the majority.

The consistency indicates it is meant as a description rather than a complaint. What makes ingratitude the default is not wickedness but familiarity: a benefit that arrives every day stops registering as a benefit, and nothing in ordinary experience corrects the drift. The chapter has already shown the alternative in a man far better positioned to be ungrateful than most. Sulaymān, given a share of all things, asked to be marshalled into thankfulness, as though gratitude were a formation that breaks without discipline. He identified in himself exactly the tendency this verse names in the majority, and he treated it as something requiring active management rather than assuming his own good character would supply it.

**Why Ingratitude Is Grouped With Disbelief**

The root of *yashkurūn* is *sh-k-r*, thanks, and its opposite in the Qur'an is *kufr* — the same word the Book uses for disbelief. The overlap is not loose usage.

*Kufr* at root means to cover over, and the image behind it is concealment of something that is there. A farmer covering a seed with soil uses the same root in classical Arabic. Applied to gifts, it names the act of covering over a benefit received so that it no longer shows; applied to God, it names covering over Him. Sulaymān's speech at the arrival of the throne used both terms in a single sentence, asking whether he would be grateful or *akfur*. The chapter therefore has a precise vocabulary at work: the deniers of verse 4 and the ungrateful of this verse are described with one root, because concealing a giver and concealing a gift are the same operation performed on different objects.

**A Verse Placed Between Warning and Surveillance**

Look at the neighbours. Verse 72 warns that the reckoning may be near. Verse 74 says God knows what their hearts conceal and what they disclose. Between the threat and the surveillance sits a statement about generosity.

The placement prevents the passage from reading as intimidation. A God who watches and warns and gives nothing would produce fear; the sequence here inserts the bounty in the middle, so that the knowing and the warning are attributes of someone already described as generous to the very people being warned. This is the chapter's method in miniature, and it matches what it did at the start: verse 2 called the Book guidance and good news, and only in verse 4 turned to those who refuse. Threat is never the first thing said in this chapter, and it is never the only thing said.

---

## Verse 27:74

> And surely your Lord knows what their hearts conceal and what they reveal

**The Chapter's Recurring Claim, Stated Plainly**

This assertion has appeared three times in an-Naml now. The hoopoe used it against Sheba's idolatry, describing God as knowing what is concealed and what is disclosed. Verse 50 demonstrated it in narrative, with nine conspirators inside a plan they could not see. Here it is stated flatly about the present audience.

The repetition tracks the chapter's structure. First the principle is voiced by a character, then it is shown operating in history, and now it is applied to the people listening. That is the same movement the whole sūrah makes — argument, demonstration, application — and this verse is where the third stage arrives for this particular claim. Nothing new is being asserted. What changes is who is being described.

**Concealment Is Where Sincerity Is Decided**

Why does the Qur'an return to this attribute so often? Because the gap between what is held and what is shown is where the moral reality of a person is located.

Public conduct can be produced by pressure, habit or calculation, and a community can be full of people performing correctly for reasons that have nothing to do with belief. The Qur'an addresses this repeatedly, warning about those who say with their mouths what is not in their hearts. What this verse asserts is that the performance is not the record. It also cuts in the other direction, and the chapter has shown that side too — Sheba's queen worshipped the sun in public while, on one reading of verse 42, already knowing something she had not yet declared. The attribute is not only a threat.

**Placed Immediately After a Statement of Bounty**

The previous verse said God is bountiful to mankind and most are ungrateful. This one says He knows what is hidden and what is shown. The order matters.

Ingratitude is often an interior matter. A person can receive a benefit, say the right thing about it, and privately regard it as his due — and no observer could tell the difference between him and someone genuinely thankful. Verse 73 names the failure; verse 74 says it is visible. Together they close the gap that gratitude most easily slips through. Sulaymān's reaction to the throne is the counter-example the chapter has already supplied: his first words on receiving an extraordinary gift concerned whether he would be thankful, which is a man examining exactly the interior this verse describes.

---

## Verse 27:75

> For there is nothing hidden in the heavens or the earth without being ˹written˺ in a perfect Record

**The Word for Hidden**

The verse says *ghā'ibah*, from the same root as *ghayb*, the unseen category the chapter discussed at verse 65. The connection is direct and the two verses should be read together.

There the point was a limit: nobody in the heavens or the earth knows the unseen except God, and the resurrection was named as the case in hand. Here the same category is described from the other side — everything in it is written down. Put together, the two verses describe a single arrangement. There is a domain creatures cannot reach, and it is not chaotic or unformed; it is registered. The ignorance announced in the earlier verse is therefore not a statement about the world being partly indeterminate. It is a statement about who has access to a record that exists.

**"A Perfect Record"**

The phrase is *kitābin mubīn*, a clear or manifest Book. Al-Ṭabarī identifies it with what the Qur'an elsewhere calls the Mother of the Book — the archetypal record mentioned in Āl ʿImrān and az-Zukhruf, and connected to the Preserved Tablet described at the end of al-Burūj.

The adjective is the chapter's own. Verse 1 called the Qur'an *al-kitāb al-mubīn*, the clear Book, and here the same two words name something else. The overlap is not confusion; it is a claim about relation. What is recited in Arabic and what is written in the heavenly register are given one description, and the reader who has come from the chapter's opening will hear the echo. A Book called clear at the start of the sūrah is matched by a Book called clear at its close, and the sūrah's whole argument runs between them.

**Why Writing Rather Than Knowing**

Knowledge can be complete and still private. A written record is the form knowledge takes when it is going to be produced — entered, kept, and brought out later. The Qur'an uses this image throughout its account of the judgment, where deeds are described as recorded, books are handed to their owners, and nothing small or large is left uncounted. What this verse establishes is the existence of the register before any of that machinery is described. The accounting the mockers of verse 71 were demanding a date for is, on this claim, already documented; only the session has not been convened.

---

## Verse 27:76

> Indeed, this Quran clarifies for the Children of Israel most of what they differ over

**What They Actually Differed Over**

Al-Rāzī and al-Ṭabarī identify the disputes as internal quarrels among the Israelites, pursued to the point of mutual denunciation. Al-Rāzī specifies matters connected to what he calls the distortion of the Torah and the Gospel; al-Qurṭubī adds disagreements over the obligations of the divine law.

Both categories are recognisable. Communities with a long scriptural history generate disputes about the text itself — what belongs, how it was transmitted, what was altered — and disputes about practice, which obligations remain binding and which were provisional. The Qur'an engages both kinds in other chapters, correcting the account of ʿĪsā, addressing what was permitted and then forbidden to the Israelites, and adjudicating the status of Ibrāhīm. This verse is the general statement of which those are instances. A broader reading also circulated among the commentators, taking the disputes as questions about the nature of God, which the Qur'an answers universally.

**"Most of What They Differ Over" — Not All**

The limitation is explicit and it is unusual. *Akthara alladhī hum fīhi yakhtalifūn* — most of what they differ about, not the whole of it.

That qualification is easy to pass over and hard to explain away, since an expansive claim would have been rhetorically stronger. What it indicates is a selection principle: the Qur'an addresses the disputes that matter for the relationship between a person and God, and leaves alone the ones that do not. Questions of ritual detail, historical minutiae and legal particulars internal to another community's law are not the Book's subject. The restriction is consistent with how the Qur'an actually behaves. It says a great deal about the identity of ʿĪsā and nothing about the genealogies that occupy so much of the earlier scripture.

**Why the Children of Israel Appear at This Point**

The placement can look arbitrary in a chapter aimed at Mecca, and it is not. An-Naml has just spent fifty verses recounting the histories of Mūsā, Dāwūd, Sulaymān, Ṣāliḥ and Lūṭ — four of whom belong to the Israelite tradition and its prehistory.

The chapter has therefore been doing, in narrative, exactly what this verse describes in summary. It told the story of Mūsā at the fire, of Sulaymān and the queen, of the household of Lūṭ, and in each case it gave an account that differs in emphasis and sometimes in substance from what circulated elsewhere. Verse 9 of this chapter set the divine self-disclosure at the fire beside its biblical counterpart; verse 44 recorded a queen's submission that no other tradition preserves. This verse names what the chapter has been doing and identifies its second audience after the fact.

---

## Verse 27:77

> And it is truly a guide and mercy for the believers

**Mercy in Place of Good News**

The substituted word repays attention. *Bushrā* is an announcement about the future: something good is coming. *Raḥmah* is a present quality of the thing itself: the Book is mercy, now, in the hands of whoever holds it.

The change tracks where the chapter has arrived. Verse 2 was addressed to people at the start of a text, being told what the Book promises. Verse 77 comes after the accounts of Thamūd's emptied houses, the rain that fell on Lūṭ's people, and Pharaoh's court refusing what it knew. A reader who has been through that material is not in a position to hear about good news in the abstract; what he has just been shown is what happens to communities without a guide. Against that, the Book's presence is itself the mercy. The pairing appears elsewhere in the Qur'an for both this revelation and the Torah, always in the same order.

**Guidance Named Twice**

The one element that does not change across the two verses is *hudan*, guidance. It is the first thing said about the Book at the chapter's opening and the first thing said about it here.

That constancy identifies the primary function. Everything else the Qur'an is called in this chapter — clear, a criterion between disputing parties, good news, mercy — describes an aspect or an effect. Guidance is the thing itself. The chapter's whole vocabulary has been organised around this word: the deniers of verse 4 wander blindly, Sheba's people are left unguided, the queen's test in verse 41 is framed as whether she will be guided, and verse 63 asked who guides travellers through the darkness of land and sea. A Book described as guidance is being placed in the same category as the stars a caravan steers by.

**A Pairing Used of the Torah Too**

The two words appear together in several other chapters, and in some of those places they describe the Book given to Mūsā rather than the Qur'an. Al-Anʿām applies the phrase to the scripture of Mūsā twice in three verses; al-Aḥqāf does the same.

That shared description carries a claim about continuity. The Qur'an is not presenting itself as a new kind of thing but as the latest instance of a recognised kind, and it uses the identical vocabulary for its predecessor. The placement here reinforces the point, since the previous verse has just addressed the Children of Israel directly. A book that says it clarifies their disputes, and then describes itself with the same two words their own scripture is given, is making an argument about lineage rather than replacement. What follows in the next verse — that God will judge between the parties — assumes the same framework.

---

## Verse 27:78

> Your Lord will certainly judge between them by His justice, for He is the Almighty, All-Knowing

**The Verdict Is Deferred, Not Cancelled**

The previous verses described a Book that recounts to a disputing community most of what they differ over. This one says who will actually settle the matter, and when: *your Lord* will judge between them.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both read the judgment as occurring in the Hereafter — God will decide there who was right. That timing is the substance of the verse. The Qur'an has just claimed to clarify the disputes, and it now distinguishes clarification from adjudication. Making the truth available and enforcing a verdict are two different acts, and only the first has happened. The disputes will continue in this world, and the Book's role is to inform them rather than to end them.

**Two Names Fitted to Judgment**

The verse closes with *al-ʿazīz al-ʿalīm*, the Almighty and the All-Knowing. The pairing is chosen for the task described, and the chapter has used the same technique before.

A judge needs two things: the knowledge to determine what actually happened, and the power to impose the finding. Human courts fail at one or the other constantly — they reach correct verdicts they cannot enforce, or enforce verdicts reached on incomplete information. The two names answer both failures in order. Note also the echo. These are the same two attributes named at the fire in verse 9, when God identified Himself to Mūsā as the Almighty, the All-Wise, and in verse 6, where the Qur'an was described as coming from the All-Wise, All-Knowing. The chapter's beginning and end are held together by the same small set of names.

**The Chapter's Fourth Deferral**

An-Naml has now declined four times to bring matters to a conclusion in this world. The Hour's timing was withheld at verse 65. The date of the reckoning was refused at verse 72. The Prophet was told not to grieve over the refusers at verse 70. Now the adjudication of religious dispute is placed beyond death.

Together these establish the position the chapter leaves its reader in. Nothing will be settled here; the evidence is available and the verdict is not. That is an uncomfortable arrangement and the chapter does not soften it. What it offers instead is the record of communities that were in exactly the same position and what they did with it — Sheba's queen, who investigated, and three nations that did not. The verse that follows tells the Prophet what to do while the judgment is pending.

---

## Verse 27:79

> So put your trust in Allah, for you are surely upon the ˹Path of˺ clear truth

**Trust Is Not Passivity**

The word could be misread as an instruction to stop working, and the chapter itself blocks that reading. Everything an-Naml admires involves action. Sulaymān verified a report, wrote a letter, and altered a throne. The queen consulted, tested and travelled. The hoopoe flew two thousand kilometres and came back with evidence.

None of that is quietism, and none of it is presented as a failure of trust. What the Qur'an pairs *tawakkul* with elsewhere makes the balance explicit: the Prophet is told to consult his companions on the matter and then, having resolved, to rely on God. Consultation first, decision second, reliance third — in that order. Reliance is what a person does about the part of the outcome that was never his, and it becomes an excuse only when it is applied to the part that was.

**"You Are Upon Clear Truth"**

The second half gives the ground for the first. *Innaka ʿalā al-ḥaqq al-mubīn* — you are upon the manifest truth. The preposition is spatial: he is *on* it, standing on it, as one stands on solid ground.

The commentators note that the phrase can also be rendered as following it or being based upon it, and all three senses share the image of support from below. That is the reason reliance is possible. A man is told to trust not because trusting is a virtue in itself but because what he is standing on will hold. The adjective is the chapter's own signature word, *mubīn*, clear — the same one applied to the Book in verse 1, to the sorcery charge in verse 13, to the favour Sulaymān named in verse 16, and to the record of hidden things in verse 75. Each time it marks something unambiguous.

**Consolation Addressed to a Specific Pressure**

This verse belongs with verse 70, where the Prophet was told not to grieve and not to feel constricted by plots. The two together form the chapter's treatment of his position, and they work differently.

Verse 70 named the feelings and set a limit on them. This one supplies what replaces them. A man told only to stop grieving has been given a prohibition; a man told to rely on God because he stands on clear truth has been given something to do instead. The order is also right. The chapter first records the distress, then argues at length — six questions, a command to travel and look, a statement about the record of hidden things — and only then instructs him to trust. The reliance is placed after the evidence rather than in place of it.

---

## Verse 27:80

> You certainly cannot make the dead hear ˹the truth˺. Nor can you make the deaf hear the call when they turn their backs and walk away

**Two Images, Two Different Failures**

The verse gives two comparisons and they are not interchangeable. The dead cannot hear because they have no capacity left. The deaf described here cannot hear because they are deaf *and* walking away.

That second clause is doing the work. A deaf man standing still might yet be reached by gesture, by writing, by someone catching his eye. The image the verse constructs removes even that: the hearing is gone and the back is turned. What is being described is not a disability but a combination of incapacity and motion, and the motion is the part the person is responsible for. Al-Ṭabarī's reading of similar passages treats the deafness as acquired through refusal, which fits the chapter's account in verse 4 of hearts and eyes turned away after an initial rejection.

**The Speaker Is Not at Fault**

The verse's grammar keeps the responsibility away from the Prophet. He *cannot* make them hear; the incapacity is in them and the emphatic particle *innaka* marks the statement as a correction of something he may have believed.

That correction matters for how he was to understand the preceding years. A messenger watching his own clan refuse him has an obvious temptation to search his own conduct for the failure, and the chapter has already recorded him grieving. What this verse supplies is a diagnosis that locates the problem correctly. It is the same service Ṣāliḥ performed for his people in reverse, when they blamed their misfortunes on his presence and he told them they were being tested. Both cases involve someone attributing a cause wrongly and being corrected.

---

## Verse 27:81

> Nor can you lead the blind out of their misguidance. You can make none hear ˹the truth˺ except those who believe in Our revelations, ˹fully˺ submitting ˹to Allah˺

**"Submitting" as the Second Condition**

Belief is not the only qualifier. Those who hear are described as *muslimūn* — submitting, surrendered. The pairing of belief with submission adds something to the description.

A person can accept that a claim is true and decline to act on it, and the Qur'an is attentive to that gap. Pharaoh's court is the chapter's own example: they were certain in their souls and rejected all the same. Assent without surrender is precisely the position the chapter has been documenting in its opponents. The verse therefore names two things — the inner recognition and the practical yielding that follows — and describes hearing as belonging only to those in whom both are present. The word is also the one Sheba's queen used of herself, twice, when she declared her submission along with Sulaymān.

**Al-Rāzī on What This Does for the Prophet**

Al-Rāzī reads verses 80 and 81 as directed at the Prophet's own condition rather than at the deniers. His argument is that hope of getting something from people weakens one's ability to oppose them, and that absolving the Prophet of responsibility for their belief strengthens his capacity to declare the truth.

The observation is shrewd and it is about the psychology of a person who depends on an audience. A preacher who believes the outcome rests on his performance will begin, without deciding to, adjusting the message toward what his listeners can accept. Removing the outcome from his account removes the pressure that produces the adjustment. On al-Rāzī's reading the passage is not a verdict on Mecca at all but an instruction to the man addressing it, and the instruction to rely on God two verses earlier is what these verses exist to make possible.

---

## Verse 27:82

> And when the decree ˹of the Hour˺ comes to pass against them, We will bring forth for them a beast from the earth, telling them that the people had no sure faith in Our revelations

**"When the Word Comes Upon Them"**

The phrase is *idhā waqaʿa al-qawlu ʿalayhim*, and al-Ṭabarī connects it to similar language elsewhere about a word or decree falling due. The sense is of a sentence that has been pronounced and now takes effect.

Others among the commentators read it as the point at which the divine wrath becomes necessary against them. Both readings agree on the structure: something already determined arrives at its appointed moment. The Qur'an uses the same construction in several chapters, always for the transition from warning to execution. What the verse adds to that familiar formula is the creature, which appears in the Qur'an only here and which the tradition identifies as one of the signs preceding the end.

**The Commentators' Descriptions and al-Rāzī's Caution**

The tafsīr literature carries a large and colourful body of material. Some give the creature a length of sixty or seventy cubits. Others say it emerges carrying the staff of Mūsā and the seal of Sulaymān, marking believers with the staff and stamping deniers with the seal so that each is recognisable on sight. One composite description assembles it from the parts of a dozen animals — a bull's head, an elephant's trunk, a stag's horns, a camel's legs. There is disagreement, too, about where it appears, with Mecca, Minā, Yemen and the desert all named.

Al-Rāzī's response to this abundance is the soundest position available. Since the Qur'an gives no indication of any of these details, a report should be accepted only so far as it can be authenticated from the Prophet, and otherwise disregarded. The methodological point is worth keeping: the density of a tradition is not evidence for it, and a text that says little about something was probably not intending to say more. Applying his test resolves the question cleanly. There *is* authenticated material about this creature, and it is strikingly thin — the report naming it among three signs after which faith no longer benefits is sound, recorded from Abū Hurayrah at **Jāmiʿ at-Tirmidhī 3072** and graded *ṣaḥīḥ*, and it says nothing whatever about the creature's appearance. The cubits, the composite anatomy, the staff and the seal and the marked faces belong to a separate stratum, traceable largely to ath-Thaʿlabī and the storytellers, with no prophetic chain behind any of it. What survives the test is a sign, a function, and an announcement.

**A Reading Difference in the Verb**

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both note an alternative vocalisation. Read as *tukallimuhum* the creature speaks to them; read as *taklimuhum* it wounds them. The consonantal text supports either.

The variant matters more than it looks. On the first reading the event is an announcement — the creature declares that people had no certainty about the signs. On the second it is a physical marking, which fits the reports about the staff and the seal. Most commentators, including az-Zamakhsharī, take the declaration as the creature's own speech. The chapter's own concerns favour that reading too, since an-Naml is a sūrah in which animals speak and are understood: an ant warns her colony, a hoopoe delivers a report, and the last creature in the chapter announces a verdict.

**The Content of the Announcement**

What the creature says is short and specific: *anna al-nāsa kānū bi-āyātinā lā yūqinūn* — that people had no certainty about Our signs. Not that they disbelieved, and not that they disobeyed. That they were not certain.

The word is *yūqinūn*, from *yaqīn*, and it takes the chapter back to its third verse, where the believers were described as being certain about the Hereafter. Certainty is the quality the sūrah named first and returns to last. It also connects to Pharaoh's court in verse 14, whose souls were certain while their tongues denied — the one case in the chapter where certainty existed and made no difference. The final sign in the Qur'anic chapter about signs announces that the problem was never the availability of evidence.

## Verse 27:83

> ˹Watch for˺ the Day We will gather from every faith-community a group of those who denied Our revelations, and they will be driven in ranks

**"Driven in Ranks"**

The verb is *yūzaʿūn*, and readers of this chapter have met it before. It was used in verse 17 for Sulaymān's hosts of jinn, men and birds, who were marshalled into ordered ranks before him.

Al-Ṭabarī glosses it in both places as corralling or grouping together, holding the front back so the rear can catch up. The reuse of a rare word across one chapter is unlikely to be accidental, and the effect is a deliberate parallel with the difference left visible. Sulaymān's ranks were an army assembled in glory, marching under a king who thanked God for his share of all things. These ranks are assembled for interrogation. The same formation, the same word, and two entirely different meanings depending on who is doing the marshalling and where the column is headed.

**The Contingent Is Drawn From Their Own People**

There is a detail in the phrasing that would have landed sharply on the first audience. The group is taken *from* a community — pulled out of it, separated from the rest of their nation.

Tribal Mecca understood identity as collective. A man's standing, protection and fate ran with his clan, and the worst thing that could happen to him was to be cut off from it. The chapter has already played on this at verse 56, where Lūṭ's opponents proposed expelling his household from the town, and at verse 57, where the rescue was of a household as a unit. This verse describes a separation that runs the other way and that no clan can prevent. Belonging, which was the ultimate security in that society, does not determine which contingent a person is counted into.

## Verse 27:84

> When they ˹finally˺ come before their Lord, He will ask ˹them˺, “Did you deny My revelations without ˹even˺ comprehending them? Or what ˹exactly˺ did you do?”

**"Encompassed in Knowledge"**

The verb is *aḥāṭa*, to encompass or surround, and this chapter has used it once before at a memorable moment. The hoopoe returned to Sulaymān and said *aḥaṭtu bi-mā lam tuḥiṭ bih* — I have encompassed what you have not encompassed.

That earlier use sets the standard the question here applies. A bird flew a long distance, observed a nation, identified its religion and its ruler, and returned with a report it described as certain news. The chapter presents that as the model of how a claim is established. Against it, the contingents at the judgment are asked whether they encompassed anything at all before rejecting. The word is the same and the comparison is unflattering. A hoopoe did the work; they did not.

**The Second Question**

After the charge comes an odd addition: *ammā dhā kuntum taʿmalūn* — or what was it that you used to do? The question is left open, and its openness is the point.

It functions as an invitation to supply an alternative account. If you did not reject out of ignorance, then tell us what you were doing. This is how a competent interrogation proceeds — the charge is stated and the accused is given room to answer it. What makes the moment devastating is not the severity of the question but its fairness. Nobody is being convicted by assertion. They are being asked to describe their own conduct, and the following verse records that they cannot speak.

---

## Verse 27:85

> And the decree ˹of torment˺ will be justified against them for their wrongdoing, leaving them speechless

**"For Their Having Done Wrong"**

The cause is named: *bimā ẓalamū*, because they did wrong. The chapter used this exact phrase once before, at verse 52, where the emptied houses of Thamūd stood as a consequence of the same thing.

The repetition ties the two scenes together. What happened to a nation in history and what happens to the contingents at the judgment are given the same explanation in the same words, which is the chapter's method throughout — the pattern visible in the ruins is the pattern that operates at the end. Al-Ṭabarī identifies the wrong specifically as their denial of the truth and rejection of what was revealed. *Ẓulm* in Qur'anic usage means putting something where it does not belong, and the chapter has already applied it to the self: Sulaymān's confession and the queen's both used it of themselves.

**They Do Not Speak**

*Fa-hum lā yanṭiqūn* — and they do not speak. Al-Ṭabarī reads it as an inability to defend themselves: nothing remains that could be offered as a case.

The silence is not imposed by force. Nothing in the verse describes them being prevented; the natural reading is that the question of verse 84 admitted no answer. A man asked whether he condemned something without examining it, when he did, has only two options, and confession is not going to help him. The verse describes the position where argument has run out. Elsewhere the Qur'an depicts the deniers at the judgment talking a great deal — blaming their leaders, asking to be returned, disputing among themselves. Here, at the point where the specific charge is put, there is nothing.

**The Verb Chosen for the Silence**

*Nuṭq* is not the general word for speech. Its sense is express utterance, the forming of intelligible speech, and the chapter opened one of its most famous scenes with the related noun.

When Sulaymān said he had been taught *manṭiq al-ṭayr*, the speech of birds, it was this root. The chapter that began with a king given the power to understand express utterance in creatures that supposedly have none ends by describing human beings who have lost it. An ant addressed her colony in intelligible speech and was understood; a bird made a report and was believed; and the deniers, at the scene where speech would finally matter, produce none. The word is rare enough in the Qur'an that the echo across a single chapter is unlikely to be coincidence.

---

## Verse 27:86

> Do they not see that We made the night for them to rest in and the day bright? Surely in this are signs for those who believe

**The Question Form Returns**

*A lam yaraw* — have they not seen? The chapter used a sequence of such questions in verses 60 to 64, each beginning with the same challenge and each ending by asking whether there is a god besides God.

That series stopped at verse 64 and the chapter turned to the Hour, the mockers, the record and the judgment. Now the interrogative returns, which signals that the argument from creation has not been abandoned but resumed. What has changed is the ending. The earlier questions closed by asking whether another god could do this; this one closes by saying there are signs here for people who believe. The rhetorical pressure has shifted from disputing about deities to identifying who actually reads the evidence.

**"For a People Who Believe"**

The closing restriction is the same one the chapter has used repeatedly. The signs are for those who believe — not because the phenomenon is hidden from anyone else, but because it registers as a sign only for those prepared to read it that way.

This is the third time the chapter has attached a qualifier of this kind to its evidence. Verse 52 said the ruins are a sign for a people who know. Verse 86 says the night and day are signs for a people who believe. The recurring structure carries the chapter's central claim about evidence: the material is public and the reading is not automatic. Every person in Mecca had seen the ruins and every one of them had slept through a night. The chapter has never argued that the deniers were short of data.

---

## Verse 27:87

> And ˹beware of˺ the Day the Trumpet will be blown, and all those in the heavens and all those on the earth will be horrified ˹to the point of death˺, except those Allah wills ˹to spare˺. And all will come before Him, fully humbled

**What the Trumpet Might Be**

Most take *ṣūr* to mean a horn, and the word is used that way in Arabic. Al-Rāzī records two other possibilities and both are worth hearing.

On one, the image is military: a trumpeter mustering an army, with the raising of the dead pictured as troops being called into formation. That reading fits this chapter unusually well, since the previous judgment scene described the deniers being driven in ranks using the word the sūrah applied to Sulaymān's marshalled hosts. On the other, *ṣūr* is read as *ṣuwar*, the plural of *ṣūrah*, form — so that what is blown is spirit into bodies. Al-Rāzī reports both without deciding, and the second is the more speculative, depending as it does on an alternative vocalisation.

**A Warning Placed After an Invitation**

Notice the arrangement. The previous verse pointed at the night and the day and called them signs. This one describes the blast. The chapter alternates between the gentle evidence and the severe outcome rather than grouping them.

That alternation runs through the whole sūrah. Verse 2 promised guidance and good news, verse 4 described those who wander blindly. Verse 73 called God bountiful, verse 74 said He knows what is concealed. Here the pattern appears one last time, and in the same order: the invitation first, the warning second. What follows in the next verses is the return, with the mountains moving and the deeds weighed, before the chapter ends on a command to recite.

---

## Verse 27:88

> Now you see the mountains, thinking they are firmly fixed, but they are travelling ˹just˺ like clouds. ˹That is˺ the design of Allah, Who has perfected everything. Surely He is All-Aware of what you do

**The Chapter's Most Contested Verse**

Few verses in an-Naml have produced more disagreement, and the disagreement is not about the words but about when the scene takes place. Is the mountains' movement a present fact we fail to perceive, or an event at the end of the world?

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both report the first reading: the mountains move now, and we do not see it because large objects viewed from a distance appear stationary even when travelling fast. Al-Rāzī also records the second, connecting the verse to the many passages describing mountains scattered, levelled or turned to carded wool at the Hour. The surrounding verses support the Last-Day reading, since the trumpet has just sounded. The verb tense and the address to a present "you" support the other. Both camps are working from the same four words.

**Three Allegorical Readings, and Whose They Are**

Al-Qurṭubī closes his treatment by reporting, on the authority of al-Māwardī, that the verse has been taken as a similitude, and he gives three versions of what it is a similitude for. The attributions can be stated precisely.

The first is Sahl ibn ʿAbdullāh's: the world itself is the mountain, and the man looking at it supposes it fixed while it is taking its share of passing away, like a cloud. The second applies the image to faith — you suppose it settled in the heart while its work is ascending. The third takes it of the soul at death, departing the body and rising toward the Throne. All three use the perceptual argument in the same way, by insisting that the appearance of stillness is a statement about the observer rather than the observed, and the first is the most exact use of it. A popular anecdote attaches this verse to Junayd al-Baghdādī, who is said to have recited it when asked why he showed none of the visible ecstasies of his contemporaries. The reading is apt — a mountain in motion looks still, and composure is no evidence of stillness — but the story is not in al-Qurṭubī's discussion and does not belong to this chain. It circulates in the later Sufi literature and should not be attributed to the commentary.

**"Who Has Perfected Everything"**

The verb is *atqana*. Al-Ṭabarī glosses it as tying down or making firm; al-Rāzī takes it in this context as God tying things together according to His wisdom.

Both senses turn on fastening, and the word's placement is deliberate. The sentence has just said that the firmest things are moving, and it immediately describes God as the one who makes things firm. The apparent contradiction resolves into the chapter's point: firmness is not a property things have in themselves but a relation they stand in to their maker. *Itqān* in later Arabic came to mean craftsmanship, doing a thing thoroughly and well, and the word retains that sense here. What the verse claims is not that the world is stable but that it is made properly.

## Verse 27:89

> Whoever comes with a good deed will be rewarded with what is better, and they will be secure from the horror on that Day

**What the Good Deed Is**

Al-Qurṭubī identifies the *ḥasanah* specifically: testifying that there is no god but God, and believing in Him. He supports it with a report that this testimony is the best of good things.

The identification narrows the verse considerably and it fits the chapter. An-Naml's entire argument has been about the object of worship — the hoopoe's report concerned a nation prostrating to the sun, the six questions each ended by asking whether there is a god besides God, and the queen's turning point was the sentence in which she submitted with Sulaymān. If the sūrah has been driving at one deed, it is this one. The reading is not the only available one, since *ḥasanah* in Arabic covers any good act, but al-Qurṭubī's is well anchored in the chapter's own concerns.

**Security as the Named Reward**

Whatever else is included, the verse states one benefit outright: *hum min fazaʿin yawma'idhin āminūn* — they are secure from the terror of that Day. The word for terror is the one used two verses earlier for the reaction to the trumpet.

The connection is direct. Verse 87 said everyone in the heavens and the earth would be terrified except whom God wills, and left the exception unidentified. This verse supplies a criterion. The commentators who guessed at martyrs or named angels were filling a gap that the chapter closes three verses later with a general rule rather than a list of persons. Whoever comes bringing a good deed is secure — which is a more useful answer than a roster, because it is a category a reader can join.

**Whoever — With No Qualifier**

The pronoun is open: *man jā'a bi'l-ḥasanah*, whoever comes with a good deed. No nation is named, no lineage, no prior standing. The rule is stated in the most general terms Arabic allows.

That generality matters at this point in the chapter. The judgment scene of verses 83 and 84 emphasised that contingents are drawn from every community, which removed the protection of belonging. This verse removes the same barrier from the other direction: if membership does not condemn, neither does it save, and what counts is what a person arrives holding. Sheba's queen is the chapter's proof that the category is open. She came from a nation that worshipped the sun, ruled it, and ended by declaring her submission to the Lord of the worlds along with Sulaymān.

---

## Verse 27:90

> And whoever comes with an evil deed will be hurled face-first into the Fire. Are you rewarded except for what you used to do

**What the Evil Deed Is**

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Ṭabarī both identify the *sayyi'ah* here as *shirk* — associating partners with God. The identification is the mirror of the good deed in the previous verse, which they took as the testimony of God's oneness.

Reading the pair this way makes the two verses a summary of the chapter rather than a general statement about ethics. An-Naml opened by describing believers who pray, give alms and are certain of the Hereafter, and it has spent its length on one question: who is worshipped. Sheba prostrated to the sun. Pharaoh claimed a status he did not have. Thamūd and Lūṭ's people rejected the messengers sent to them. The final assessment scene sorts by the same criterion the whole sūrah has been applying.

**"Face-First"**

The phrase is *kubbat wujūhuhum fī al-nār* — their faces cast down into the Fire. The Qur'an uses similar imagery in several chapters, describing the guilty gathered upon their faces or dragged upon them.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both note that in Arabic idiom the face, like the head or the neck, can stand for the whole person, so that "their faces" may mean simply "they." On that reading the violence of the image is a figure of speech rather than a posture. The other reading takes the detail literally, matching the passages elsewhere that describe the guilty cast down inverted. Either way the connotation is the same in Arabic as in English: the face is where dignity resides, and to be put on it is to be humiliated rather than merely harmed.

**The Face in This Chapter**

There is a reason the detail lands where it does. The chapter has used the face for status throughout, and the contrast with its earlier scenes is sharp.

Sheba's queen entered a palace and was told to look at a floor; Sulaymān's response to the arrival of her throne was to lower himself in gratitude. The chapter's whole moral vocabulary has been about who bows and to what. A person who declined to lower his face voluntarily is described here as arriving at the same position involuntarily. That is not a flourish added for effect; it is the logic the sūrah has been building since the hoopoe reported a nation prostrating to the wrong object.

**The Closing Question**

The verse ends by turning to the reader: *hal tujzawna illā mā kuntum taʿmalūn* — are you repaid for anything other than what you used to do? The address shifts to the second person, and the shift is abrupt.

Up to this point the verse has described other people in the third person. The question breaks that frame and puts it to whoever is listening. Al-Rāzī records the alternative that the question is spoken to those being cast down, as they are cast down, which would keep it inside the scene. Both readings are available and the ambiguity may be intended: a question addressed to the condemned is overheard by the living, and its force is the same in either position.

## Verse 27:91

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I have only been commanded to worship the Lord of this city ˹of Mecca˺, Who has made it sacred, and to Him belongs everything. And I am commanded to be one of those who ˹fully˺ submit ˹to Him˺

**"The Lord of This City"**

God could have been named in any number of ways here, and the chapter has used many — the Almighty, the All-Knowing, the Lord of the Mighty Throne, the Lord of the worlds. The closing statement chooses a local title: the Lord of this city.

Al-Ṭabarī notes that the reference is to Mecca, while adding the necessary qualification that God is Lord of all lands. The specificity is chosen for the audience. This is the home of the idolaters being addressed, and the phrase reminds them of what they have by virtue of living there — a place where they are safe from attack in a peninsula where safety was scarce. The argument is the same one al-Quraysh makes at greater length elsewhere, pointing at the security and provision of the sanctuary and asking its beneficiaries to worship its Lord.

**"Who Has Made It Sacred"**

*Ḥarramahā* — He made it forbidden, inviolable. The word is the root behind *ḥaram*, the sacred precinct, and it names a status the Meccans already recognised and enforced.

The commentators describe what the sanctity involved. Certain acts were prohibited during the pilgrimage; the place served as a refuge for those seeking sanctuary; its trees were not to be cut and its game not hunted. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Rāzī both note that the Arabs of the time observed these prohibitions, out of a persisting recognition of the sanctity of the Kaʿbah as the House built by Ibrāhīm. That is the crucial point for the verse's rhetoric. The Prophet is not asking them to accept a new claim about their city. He is pointing at an institution they already maintained and naming the one who established it.

**Submission Named as a Command**

The second item in the instruction is that he be among the *muslimīn*, those who submit. It is put as a command rather than as a description, which is worth attending to.

The word has appeared twice before in this chapter, both times at decisive moments. Sulaymān's letter to Sheba ended by summoning her to come as submitters. Her declaration at the palace floor ended with her saying she submitted, with Sulaymān, to the Lord of the worlds. Now at the chapter's close the Prophet is commanded to be one of the same company. The term links the three figures across the sūrah — a king, a foreign queen and the man reciting — and puts them in the same category rather than in a hierarchy. He is not commanded to lead the submitters. He is commanded to be among them.

---

## Verse 27:92

> and to recite the Quran.” Then whoever chooses to be guided, it is only for their own good. But whoever chooses to stray, say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I am only a warner.”

**The Third Command**

The previous verse listed two instructions — worship and submission — and this one completes the set with a third: *wa an atluwa al-Qur'ān*, and to recite the Qur'an. The three together are the whole of what the Prophet reports being ordered.

The brevity of the list is its point. Nothing about building a community, defeating opponents, or securing acceptance appears in it. Two of the three items concern his relationship to God and the third concerns his relationship to the text. The chapter that opened by calling these the verses of the clear Book closes by identifying the reciting of that Book as the whole of what its reciter was told to do with it.

**Reciting, Not Enforcing**

The verb is *tilāwah*, reading aloud or reciting, and the absent verbs matter as much as the present one. He is not commanded to explain, to defend, to convince or to impose. He is commanded to say the words.

That narrowness follows directly from what verses 80 and 81 established. If there are people who cannot be made to hear, then the effectiveness of the delivery is not a variable the deliverer controls, and defining his duty by an outcome would make it undischargeable. Defining it by the act makes it something he can complete every day. Al-Rāzī's reading of the earlier verses, that relieving the Prophet of responsibility for belief strengthens his ability to declare the truth, has its practical expression here.

**One of the Warners**

The phrasing includes a detail worth drawing out. He does not say *I am a warner* but *I am one of the warners*, placing himself within a company.

This matches the previous verse, where he was commanded to be among those who submit rather than to be their head. Twice in two sentences the Prophet is located inside a group rather than above one, and the chapter has spent fifty verses populating that group: Mūsā at the fire, Dāwūd and Sulaymān given knowledge, Ṣāliḥ sent to his brothers, Lūṭ addressing his people. The claim being made at the chapter's close is one of continuity with them, which is the same claim verse 76 made about the Book and the disputes of the Children of Israel.

---

## Verse 27:93

> And say, “All praise is for Allah! He will show you His signs, and you will recognize them. And your Lord is never unaware of what you do.”

**The Last Instruction Is Praise**

After ninety-two verses of argument, narrative, warning and judgment, the chapter's final command to its Prophet is to say *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh*. Praise, not a summary and not an appeal.

The placement is deliberate and it completes a frame. Praise has appeared at the pivot of this chapter's greatest scenes: Dāwūd and Sulaymān said it when given knowledge, and Sulaymān said it again in a different form when the throne arrived. Both times it followed a gift. Here it follows something else — a long refusal by an audience that has not accepted the message. The chapter ends by putting the same words in the mouth of a man who has been given no visible success, which says something about what praise is anchored to.

**Not Unaware**

The final clause is *wa mā rabbuka bi-ghāfilin ʿammā taʿmalūn* — your Lord is not heedless of what you do. The chapter has said versions of this four times, and it ends on it.

Verse 74 said God knows what breasts conceal and what they disclose. Verse 75 said nothing hidden anywhere is outside a clear record. Verse 88 closed with the statement that He is aware of what you do. Now the last sentence of the sūrah says it once more, and in the negative form, which is the strongest of the four. A positive claim about knowledge can be heard as general; denying heedlessness answers the specific hope that something has gone unnoticed. The chapter's opponents included nine men who swore a night oath believing themselves unobserved, and its close forecloses on the assumption they were operating under.

**The Ant and the Last Verse**

It is worth returning, at the end, to the incident the chapter is named for. An ant saw an army approaching and warned her colony to get inside. Sulaymān heard her, smiled, and asked to be marshalled into gratitude for the favour.

Almost nothing else in an-Naml is as small as that scene, and the chapter takes its title from it rather than from the queen, the throne, or the judgment. What the ant did was notice something coming and speak about it in time. What Sulaymān did was hear a warning not addressed to him and respond with thanks. Between them they perform the two acts this final verse asks for — recognising signs and saying *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh* — and the chapter that closes with both commands was named, from the beginning, after the creature who managed them first.
