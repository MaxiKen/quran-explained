# Sūrah an-Najm (Chapter 53) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Descent of the Star and the Vision at the Lote Tree**

An-Najm, "The Star," is the fifty-third chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 62 verses of early Makkan revelation. It takes its name from its breathtaking opening oath: *“By the star when it descends! Your companion [Muḥammad] has not strayed, nor has he erred, nor does he speak from [his own] desire; it is not but a revelation revealed!”* (53:1–4). In the history of the prophetic mission, Sūrah an-Najm marks an extraordinary, legendary watershed: it was the very first chapter of the Qur'an that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ recited publicly, aloud, in the courtyard of the Kaʿbah before the gathered assembly of the Quraysh. Recorded by ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (1067), when the Prophet concluded the recitation with the thunderous final command of verse 62: *“So fall in prostration to Allah and worship Him!”*—the entire assembly, including the most hardened pagan chieftains (such as ʿUtbah, Shaybah, and Umayyah ibn Khalaf), was so completely overwhelmed by the volcanic linguistic majesty and spiritual power of the verses that they all spontaneously fell prostrate to the earth alongside the Muslims. Sūrah an-Najm is celebrated for containing the supreme scriptural eyewitness account of the *Miʿrāj*—the Prophet's celestial ascension and his unmediated vision of the Angel Gabriel at the *Sidrat al-Muntahā* (The Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary).

**The Structural Symphony and the Classical Pagan Idols**

The architecture of Sūrah an-Najm moves across three distinct, powerful movements. The opening movement (1–18) establishes the absolute purity of prophetic speech, free from personal ego or psychological distortion, detailing the two historic celestial visions of Gabriel during the *Miʿrāj*. The second movement (19–32) directly attacks the central trinity of the pagan Arabian pantheon: *al-Lāt* (the idol of al-Ṭā'if), *al-ʿUzzā* (the idol of Nakhlah), and *Manāt* (the idol of Qudayd). It exposes them as empty, manufactured names invented by ancestral custom without divine authority: *“They are not but [mere] names you have named, you and your forefathers, for which Allah has sent down no authority; they follow not except assumption and what [their] souls desire”* (53:23). It exposes the absurdity of their claim that angels are female daughters of God, and defines the true nature of divine justice: that God rewards those who avoid major sins and immoralities, even if they commit minor lapses (*al-lamam*), for *“indeed, your Lord is vast in forgiveness”* (53:32). The third movement (33–62) reviews the ancient theological scrolls of Abraham and Moses (*Ṣuḥuf Mūsā wa-Ibrāhīm*), setting forth the universal moral principles of sacred history:
- That no bearer of burdens shall bear the burden of another (*lā taziru wāziratun wizra ukhrā*).
- That humanity will have nothing except that for which it has actively striven (*wa-an laysa li-l-insāni illā mā saʿā*).
- That his effort will soon be seen and fully recompensed.
- That to your Lord is the final destination (*wa-anna ilā Rabbika al-muntahā*).
- That it is He who causes laughter and weeping, causes death and life, created the pairs of male and female from a drop of sperm, and that He is the Lord of the dog-star Sirius (*Rabb al-Shiʿrā*). The sūrah concludes with the destruction of ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Noah, and the overturned cities of Sodom, ending with the apocalyptic warning: *“The Approaching Day has approached! Of it, [none] besides Allah can dispel it. Do you then marvel at this statement, and you laugh and do not weep, while you amuse yourselves? So fall in prostration to Allah and worship Him!”* (53:57–62).

Gabriel was not an imaginary phantom; the Prophet saw him in his true celestial form endowed with six hundred wings: first on the clear horizon, drawing near until he was at a distance of two bow-lengths or nearer (*qāba qawsayni aw adnā*, 53:9), and a second time beside the *Sidrat al-Muntahā*, near the Garden of Abode (*Jannat al-Ma'wā*), when the tree was enveloped in unearthly divine light: *“The sight [of the Prophet] did not swerve, nor did it transgress [its limit]! He certainly saw of the greatest signs of his Lord”* (53:17–18).

## Verse 53:1

> By the stars when they fade away

**An Oath on a Star in the Act of Setting**

The sūrah opens with a single oath, and the oath is on a moving thing. *An-najm idhā hawā* — the star when it descends, sets, or falls. The temporal clause is the point: the star is not sworn by in general but at a moment.

That differs from the oath-series in the sūrah before it, which listed five objects. Here one object is named at one instant, and the instant is what the oath fastens on.

A setting star is a familiar sight in a desert sky, and its movement is quick enough to be missed.

**Al-Najm: One Star, or the Stars**

The word *najm* is singular in form and can be read as collective in sense, and both readings have been given. Some take it as a particular star, and the identification most often given is the Pleiades, whose setting marked a season in the Arabian calendar. Others take it as stars generally, in the act of setting.

A third reading connects the word to the Qur'an's own vocabulary. The same root appears in the phrase about the positions of the stars, an oath the Qur'an calls a great one (56:75–76), and the commentators link that phrase to the revelation coming down in instalments — *nujūm*, portions, from the same root.

The readings are not mutually exclusive. A word that means both a star and a portion can carry both senses in an oath about revelation, and the sūrah's subject is precisely a revelation delivered in portions to a man who is being accused of composing it.

**Hawā: To Fall, To Set, To Descend**

The verb *hawā* describes downward movement — a star falling, a bird dropping from the sky, a person collapsing. It is the same verb used for the fall of the stars at the end of the world: and when the stars fall down (81:2).

The word therefore carries both an ordinary and an Last-Day sense. A star sets every night; the stars fall on the day described in Sūrah at-Takwīr. The oath sits between the two meanings.

That double sense suits the sūrah's argument. What is being sworn to is an event that happened once, in time, and the verb chosen is one that describes both the ordinary movement of the heavens and their final movement.

**Why a Star and Not a Mountain**

The comparison with the preceding sūrah is instructive. Aṭ-Ṭūr swore by fixed objects: a mountain, a book, a house, a sky, a sea. This one swears by a star in the act of setting.

The difference matches the different subjects. Aṭ-Ṭūr was establishing the certainty of a future event, and permanence was the right register. An-Najm is establishing the reality of a moment that has passed, and the right register is a moment.

The star setting is also an image of descent, and descent is the sūrah's central movement: a revelation coming down, an angel descending, a servant being taught. The oath names the direction before the sūrah describes it.

**The Oath and the Dispute It Answers**

The sūrah returns to its opening later, and the return shows what the oath was for. After describing the vision and the nearness, the sūrah asks: how can you then dispute with him about what he saw? (53:12).

The question treats the dispute as unreasonable in light of what has been described. An oath at the head of the passage and a challenge at its end frame the account between them.

The dispute being answered was real and is recorded elsewhere: they say this is a set of confused dreams, no he has fabricated it, no he must be a poet (21:5). Each of those descriptions is a theory about the origin of the speech, and the sūrah answers all of them with one account of where it came from.

That is the function of the opening oath. It does not prove the vision; it stakes the truth of the account that follows on something the hearer can see.

## Verse 53:2

> Your fellow man is neither misguided nor astray

**“Your Fellow Man” — The Word of Familiarity**

The verse refers to the Prophet ﷺ as *ṣāḥibukum*, your companion, your fellow. The word implies acquaintance: someone you have dealt with, travelled with, sat beside.

The choice is rhetorical and precise. The people being addressed had known him for forty years before the revelation. They had traded with him, married into his clan, and called him trustworthy. The verse appeals to that knowledge rather than to argument.

The same word returns later in the sūrah in a related context: and your fellow man is not insane (81:22). Both uses address people who could verify the claim from their own experience, and both deny a charge about his mind.

**The Charge in Its Meccan Setting**

The accusation of madness was not an insult thrown in anger; it was a considered classification with consequences. A *majnūn* in that society was a person whose speech could be dismissed without examination, since it was attributed to a jinn rather than to a mind.

The classification was therefore useful to those who made it. It removed the need to answer the Qur'an's arguments, and it explained the extraordinary quality of the language without conceding anything. The same function was served by the charge of soothsaying and by the charge of poetry.

The Qur'an records the charge as a general pattern rather than a local event: no messenger came to those before them without being told, a magician or a madman (51:52). The pairing is standard, and the verse in an-Najm answers it with the plainest possible denial.

**Straying and Guidance in the Qur'an's Own Usage**

The word *ḍalāl* is used elsewhere of the Prophet ﷺ himself, in a verse that needs careful reading. Did He not find you unguided and then guide you? (93:7). The verse describes a state before guidance, and a guidance given.

That statement is not in tension with the denial in this verse; it defines it. The denial is about the present condition — he is not astray now — and 93:7 explains how he came to be otherwise. The guidance was received, not self-produced, which is exactly what the following verses of this sūrah describe.

The pairing of the two verses closes the charge completely. A person who was guided by another is not deluded, and a person who is not astray cannot be the source of his own guidance. Both statements point to the same conclusion: the direction came from outside him.

**What the Denial Is About**

The verse is sometimes read as a claim about the Prophet's general character, and it is more precise than that. It is a claim about direction — that the path he is on is a path, and that he is on it correctly.

The two halves of the denial answer the two ways a religious claim can fail. A person can be sincere and lost, or guided in direction and deluded in execution. The verse denies both, and the denial is what allows the sūrah to move on to the question of source.

Nothing in the verse claims perfection in matters outside the message, and the Qur'an elsewhere corrects the Prophet ﷺ in such matters openly (80:1–10). The scope is the religion he brought, and within that scope the denial is absolute.

**What the Verse Does Not Claim**

The denial is specific, and its limits matter. The verse does not say the Prophet ﷺ knows everything, or that he is beyond error in ordinary human matters, or that he has independent access to the unseen.

The Qur'an states those limits explicitly elsewhere. Say: I do not say to you that I have Allah's treasuries or know the unseen, nor do I claim to be an angel — I only follow what is revealed to me (6:50). The same disclaimer appears in the sūrah that follows this one, where he is told to say that he has no power to benefit or harm himself except as God wills (7:188).

The verse therefore claims guidance in the matter at issue — the message — and nothing beyond it. That restraint is what makes the claim credible to the people it addresses.

**Why an Oath Was Needed**

The verse is the statement guaranteed by the oath in the first verse, and the oath indicates how seriously the charge was being pressed.

A plain assertion can be met with a counter-assertion. An oath stakes something on the claim, and the Qur'an uses the form when the denial is not only asserted but sworn to.

The charge being answered was public and persistent. The accusations of madness and possession are recorded in at least six chapters, and the sūrah of al-Qalam opens by denying the charge under oath: by the grace of your Lord, you are not insane (68:2).

## Verse 53:3

> Nor does he speak of his own whims

**Hawā: The Qur'an's Word for Whim**

The word *hawā* means an inclination of the self — a desire that pulls a person toward what he wants rather than toward what is true. The Qur'an treats it as the opposite of guidance, and the opposition is stated as an either/or.

Have you seen those who have taken their own desires as their god? Allah left them to stray knowingly, sealed their hearing and their hearts, and placed a cover on their sight (45:23). The description is of a person whose desire has taken the place that God occupies, and the result is a closed perception.

The same word appears later in this sūrah, describing those who follow nothing but inherited assumptions and whatever their souls desire, although guidance has come to them (53:23). Both verses place *hawā* on the side opposite revelation.

**The Denial and Its Scope**

The verse denies that the Prophet ﷺ speaks from *hawā*, and the commentators differ on how wide the denial runs. The narrower reading limits it to the Qur'an: what he delivers as revelation is not his own composition. The wider reading covers his speech generally: his own words are also guided.

Both readings have support. The narrower one fits the immediate context, which is about the source of the revelation. The wider one fits the description of him elsewhere as one who does not speak from whim in any matter touching the religion.

**The Answer to the Charge of Invention**

The previous sūrah recorded the accusation directly: or do they say he made this Qur'an up? — and answered it with the diagnosis that they do not believe (52:33) and with the challenge to produce something like it (52:34).

This verse answers the same accusation from the other side. Not by challenging the accusers to imitate it, but by stating what the thing is: not a product of his desire.

The two answers are complementary. The challenge addresses the possibility of imitation; this verse addresses the possibility of origin. Together they close the accusation.

**Why Desire Is the Specific Thing Denied**

Of all the human motives that could be named — ambition, fear, calculation, habit — the verse names *hawā*, and the choice is exact.

The accusation against the Prophet ﷺ was not that he was careless but that he was self-serving: that the Qur'an advanced his position, answered his critics, and served his interests. That is an accusation about desire, and the verse denies it in the same vocabulary.

The denial is also verifiable in a limited way. A message composed to serve a man's desires would be expected to flatter him, and the Qur'an contains passages that correct and rebuke the Prophet ﷺ (80:1–10; 33:37). A self-serving author does not include those.

**Desire and the Two Directions**

The Qur'an presents the religious life as a choice between two sources of direction, and *hawā* names one of them. A person either follows what has been revealed or follows what he wants, and there is no third source.

The verse in al-Jāthiyah describes the second option in its extreme form: a person who takes his own desire as his god (45:23). The formulation is severe because the substitution is complete — the desire occupies the place where the command should be.

The denial in this verse places the Prophet ﷺ on the other side of that division. Whatever he delivers as revelation is not his desire speaking, and the claim is verifiable in part by the content: a message that corrects its own deliverer is not the product of his inclination.

## Verse 53:4

> It is only a revelation sent down ˹to him˺

**Revelation Is Not Inspiration**

The distinction the verse draws is between revelation and the inner movements of a gifted mind. *Waḥy* in the Qur'an is a communication from outside the person receiving it, with a sender, a content, and a moment of arrival.

The Qur'an classifies its own modes of communication: it is not possible for a human being to have Allah communicate with them except through inspiration, or from behind a veil, or by sending a messenger-angel to reveal whatever He wills by His permission (42:51). Three modes are named, and all three originate outside the recipient.

That taxonomy matters for this verse. The claim being made is not that the Prophet ﷺ had unusual insight but that he received something, and the following verses describe the receiving.

**The Same Claim in Ash-Shuʿarāʾ**

The statement is not unique to this sūrah, and the parallel passage gives it in fuller form. This is certainly a revelation from the Lord of all worlds, which the trustworthy spirit brought down into your heart, so that you may be one of the warners, in a clear Arabic tongue (26:192–195).

The four elements in that passage correspond to the four verses here: the source, the agent, the recipient, and the language. Both accounts are given as statements of fact rather than as arguments, and both use the same vocabulary of descent.

The correspondence across two Meccan chapters shows that this was the standard answer to the charge of invention. It is not an improvisation for one occasion but a settled account, repeated in the same terms.

**Why the Restriction Matters Practically**

The claim that the Qur'an is nothing but revelation has an immediate consequence, and it is the one the Meccan audience cared about.

If the speech is a man's, its commands are advice and its warnings are opinions. If it is revelation, its commands bind and its warnings describe what will happen. The whole of the dispute about the Prophet's authority reduces to this question.

The Qur'an states the consequence plainly elsewhere. Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed Allah (4:80) — a statement that only holds if the messenger's message is not his own. The restriction in this verse is therefore not a metaphysical claim but the foundation of an obligation, and the sūrah's insistence on it is insistence on that foundation.

**The Verse Answers a Specific Alternative**

The restriction *nothing but* has an identifiable target, and the target was not vague. The alternatives in circulation were that the Qur'an was poetry, that it was the speech of a soothsayer receiving from jinn, that it was the product of a disturbed mind, and that it was composed by the Prophet ﷺ with help from others (25:4).

Each of those is a description of origin, and the verse excludes all four in a single phrase. It does not rank them or answer them individually; it removes them.

That method is efficient and deliberate. A verse that argued against each charge in turn would be long and would keep the charges in circulation. A verse that states what the thing is, exclusively, leaves nothing to argue with.

**Why the Source Matters So Much**

The whole of the Meccan dispute turned on the source. If the Qur'an came from God, then its commands bind and its warnings are true. If it came from a man, it is one opinion among others.

The Qur'an knows this and addresses it constantly. The challenge to produce a sūrah like it (2:23), the statement that it is a reminder to the worlds (81:27), the denials of invention (10:38; 11:13) — all are statements about origin.

This verse is the shortest of them and the most absolute. It does not argue about the source; it excludes the alternatives.

## Verse 53:5

> He has been taught by one ˹angel˺ of mighty power

**Dhū Quwwa: Possessor of Power**

The verse describes the teacher with a single attribute: *dhū quwwa*, one of mighty power. The construction — possessor of a quality — is used in the Qur'an for beings defined by an attribute rather than named.

Power in an angel is not metaphorical. The Qur'an describes the angels as harsh and mighty, not disobeying what they are commanded (66:6), and it describes a single angel's act in catastrophic force: the cities of Lot were overturned (11:82).

The attribute is chosen for this context because the account that follows involves a form, an appearance, and an approach — all acts requiring capacity.

**Gabriel Identified**

The majority of commentators identify the one of mighty power as the angel Gabriel, the messenger through whom the Qur'an was delivered. The identification is supported by other passages.

Sūrah at-Takwīr gives the same description in almost identical terms: this is the word of a noble messenger angel, full of power, held in honour by the Lord of the Throne, obeyed there in heaven, and trustworthy (81:19–21). The correspondence of attributes — power, honour, trustworthiness — makes the identification straightforward.

Sūrah al-Baqarah names the agent directly: the trustworthy spirit brought it down upon your heart (2:97 in the sense of the revelation's descent), and Gabriel is named elsewhere as the one who brought the Qur'an down.

**The Minority Reading**

Some commentators take the one of mighty power to be God Himself, reading the following verses as a description of divine disclosure rather than angelic mediation.

The reading is not baseless, since the verses that follow describe an approach and a nearness that some have understood as between the Prophet ﷺ and God.

It fits the Qur'an's consistent description of revelation's route through 42:51, and it accounts for the spatial details that the divine reading has to treat as metaphor.

**The Trustworthy Spirit and the Heart**

The Qur'an describes the mode of delivery in ash-Shuʿarāʾ: the trustworthy spirit brought it down into your heart, so that you may be one of the warners (26:193–194). The heart is named as the place of reception.

That detail matters for the account in this sūrah. Teaching implies a recipient, and the recipient here is not the memory alone but the heart, which in Qur'anic usage is the organ of understanding and conviction.

The phrase *ar-rūḥ al-amīn*, the trustworthy spirit, adds the quality that matters for transmission. Trustworthiness in a messenger means the message arrives unaltered, and the Qur'an pairs it with power in at-Takwīr — full of power, held in honour, obeyed, and trustworthy (81:20–21).

Both attributes are needed. A trustworthy messenger who is weak can be prevented; a powerful messenger who is not trustworthy can distort. The description here supplies both.

**Power as the Guarantee of Fidelity**

The attribute of power is not incidental to the account. A messenger who is weak can be interfered with; a messenger who is powerful cannot.

The Qur'an makes the connection explicit in at-Takwīr, where the messenger angel is described as obeyed in heaven and trustworthy (81:20–21), and where the Qur'an is distinguished from the word of an outcast devil (81:25). The contrast is between a transmission that can be corrupted and one that cannot.

That is the function of the attribute here. The one who taught him is powerful, and the teaching is therefore intact — which is the claim the sūrah opened by swearing to.

## Verse 53:6

> and great perfection, who once rose to ˹his˺ true form

**Dhū Mirā: Soundness, Vigour, Completeness**

The second attribute is *dhū mirā*, and the word carries a cluster of related senses: physical soundness, vigour, and — in one reading — a beautiful stature. The root is connected to completeness of form.

The pairing with mighty power in the previous verse produces a description of capacity in two dimensions: strength and integrity. A being can be strong and unstable; this one is described as both powerful and sound.

The choice of a second attribute rather than a repetition of the first suggests the description is being built up deliberately, since the verses that follow describe an appearance that requires both.

**Istawā: He Rose and Stood Firm**

The verb *istawā* means to rise to a level, to stand upright, to become established. It is used in the Qur'an for the Ark coming to rest on the mountain (11:44), for a person mounting (43:13), and — in a different register — for God's establishment on the Throne (20:5).

Applied here it describes a change of posture or state: the one who taught him rose, straightened, presented himself. The commentators read it either as the manner of his appearance on the horizon or as the manner of his teaching.

The verb's sense of settling into a position is important. It is not a movement toward something but an arrival at a stance, which prepares the spatial description in the next verse.

**The Two Appearances in True Form**

The tradition records that the Prophet ﷺ saw the angel in his created form on two occasions. The first was at the beginning of the revelation, when the angel appeared filling the horizon. The second was during the ascension.

This passage is generally read as describing the first, and the later verses of the same sūrah (53:13–14) describe the second, near the Lote-Tree of the Utmost Boundary. The two accounts differ in setting and in what is seen, which is why the commentators treat them as distinct events.

The distinction matters for interpretation. The vision described here is located on the horizon — a place in the world — while the second is located at the boundary of the created order.

**The Rising and the Teaching**

The verb *istawā* has been read in two ways, and both bear on the account. One reading takes it as describing the manner of the appearance: he rose and stood firm on the horizon. The other takes it as describing the manner of the teaching: he established himself to deliver the revelation.

The two readings are not far apart. In both, the angel is presented as settled rather than passing, which is what the following verses require — an approach and a nearness presuppose a presence that lasts.

**A Form Recognised Rather Than Invented**

The verse's claim is that the appearance was in the angel's own nature, and that claim has a consequence for the report. A vision of an invented image tells us about the one who sees it; a vision of a being in its own form tells us about the being.

The Qur'an is careful on this point elsewhere. It distinguishes the Qur'an from the word of an outcast devil (81:25) and describes the angel as obeyed in heaven and trustworthy (81:21). The distinction depends on the reality of the one appearing: an image projected into a mind has no such standing.

That is why the sūrah bothers with the form at all. The accusation it answers is that the experience was interior, and the answer is that what appeared was not interior to anyone.

**The Vision and the Night Journey**

The tradition connects the second appearance in true form with the night journey and ascension, which the Qur'an records separately: glory be to the One Who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, so that We may show him some of Our signs (17:1).

The two passages belong together without being the same account. The sūrah of the Star describes an appearance and an approach; the sūrah of the Night Journey describes a journey with a stated purpose — to show him signs.

This sūrah's later verses locate a second vision at the Lote-Tree of the utmost limit, which is the ascension setting. The verse here is generally read as the earlier of the two, at the beginning of the revelation, which is why the commentators treat the chapter as containing two accounts rather than one repeated.

## Verse 53:7

> while on the highest point above the horizon

**Al-Ufuq al-Aʿlā**

The verse gives a location, and the phrase is *al-ufuq al-aʿlā*, the highest horizon — the upper edge of the visible field, where the sky meets the line of sight.

The word *ufuq* is ordinary Arabic for the horizon, used for the eastern and western edges of the sky. Adding *aʿlā*, highest, specifies which part: not the horizon at eye level but the upper reach of it.

The specificity is unusual in an account of the unseen, and it is deliberate. The sūrah is describing something seen, and a seen thing has a place.

**The Horizon and the First Revelation**

The tradition places the appearance at the beginning of the revelation, in the setting the Qur'an itself alludes to elsewhere. The reports describe the angel appearing in a form that filled the horizon, and the Prophet ﷺ seeing him wherever he turned.

The detail is consistent with the verse's language. A horizon-filling appearance is what the phrase *al-ufuq al-aʿlā* describes from the observer's side, and it explains why the location is given in the visual field rather than in a place on a map.

The reading is supported by the parallel verse in at-Takwīr, which uses the same construction — he saw him on the clear horizon — and by the fact that the sūrah later records a second occasion in a different setting. Two occasions, two locations, one account of each.

**The Same Phrase in At-Takwīr**

Sūrah at-Takwīr uses the same description in a passage that parallels this one closely: and he certainly saw that angel on the clear horizon (81:23). The two verses describe the same event with different adjectives — the highest horizon here, the clear horizon there.

The correspondence supports the reading that this passage is about the angel rather than about God. Both chapters use the language of seeing an object at a location, and neither uses the language of interior experience.

The pairing also fixes the event. The commentators read both passages as describing the appearance at the beginning of the revelation, when the angel appeared filling the horizon.

## Verse 53:8

> then he approached ˹the Prophet˺, coming so close

**Two Verbs of Nearness**

The verse uses two words for approaching, and the doubling is emphatic. *Danā* means he drew near; *tadallā* is an intensive or reflexive form from the same root, meaning he lowered or inclined himself, coming down toward something.

The combination describes an approach that is both movement and condescension. The one approaching does not merely close distance; he comes down.

That sense matters for the reading of the passage. The subject of the two verbs, on the majority reading, is the angel, and the object is the Prophet ﷺ. A being of the description given in the previous verses inclined toward a human being, and the verse records the act.

**Descent as the Qur'an's Verb for Revelation**

The verb of approach fits a larger pattern in the Qur'an's language about revelation, which is consistently a language of descent. The trustworthy spirit brought it down into your heart (26:193–194). We sent this Qur'an down on the Night of Glory (97:1). Blessed is the One Who sent down the Standard to His servant (25:1).

The direction is always downward and outward: from God, through the angel, to the recipient. Nothing in that vocabulary describes an ascent achieved by the recipient's own effort.

The verse here is part of that pattern. The one who approaches is the one sent, and the approach is the delivery. That is why the majority reading takes the angel as the subject — it keeps the direction of the whole account consistent.

**Approach Without Overwhelming**

The nearness described is close but survivable, and the distinction matters. The Qur'an records what happens when a creature asks for direct disclosure: Mūsā asked to see God, the mountain was levelled to dust, and Mūsā collapsed unconscious (7:143).

Nothing of that kind is reported here. The approach is measured, the distance is given, and the next verse describes the recipient's heart as steady rather than shaken.

The contrast shows that the event described belongs to a different category from a theophany. It is a delivery, and deliveries happen at a range where the recipient can function. The verse records the range, and the following verse records the steadiness that the range made possible.

**Nearness as a Category**

Nearness between God and a servant is a theme the Qur'an develops elsewhere. The famous ḥadīth qudsī in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī describes it: a servant draws near to God through the obligatory acts, and continues drawing near through supererogatory acts until God loves him.

The nearness in this verse is of a different order. It is not the nearness of a servant approaching through worship but the nearness of a messenger arriving with a message, and it is measured in the next verse.

That distinction keeps the passage within its subject. The sūrah is describing the delivery of a revelation, not the ascent of a soul, and the nearness described is the nearness of delivery.

**What the Approach Excludes**

The description removes two possible characterisations of the event. It was not a distant communication — an inspiration received without contact. And it was not an overwhelming manifestation that destroyed the recipient.

The Qur'an records the second possibility elsewhere. The event described here has none of that: an approach, a measured distance, and a revelation received with the heart unshaken.

That contrast is important for the verse that follows. The recipient's composure is reported as a fact, and the composure is possible because the approach was ordered rather than overwhelming.

---

## Verse 53:9

> that he was only two arms-lengths away or even less

**Qāba Qawsayn: The Measure**

The phrase *qāba qawsayn* means the length of two bows. A *qaws* is an Arab bow, and *qāb* is the measure of its length or of the curve it describes. The unit was familiar to the audience: bows were everyday objects, and their length was a standard rough measure.

Using a customary measure for an event of this kind is a striking choice. The verse does not say the distance was beyond description or beyond measure; it gives a figure.

The figure is small. Two bow-lengths is the distance at which two people can speak without raising their voices, and the verse describes the delivery of a revelation at that range.

**The Particle Aw**

The word rendered as or is *aw*, and in this position the commentators read it in three ways: as expressing approximation (about two bow-lengths), as expressing a choice between alternatives (two bow-lengths, or less), and as intensification (nay, closer).

The readings converge on the same point: the distance was not more than two bow-lengths, and may have been less. The particle functions to reduce rather than to expand.

That direction is significant. A description that approximates upward would suggest majesty through distance; this one approximates downward, toward intimacy.

**Nearness Measured Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The Qur'an uses two other measures of nearness, and comparing them clarifies this one. God is described as closer to a person than his jugular vein (50:16), and as truly near to those who call on Him (2:186).

Both of those are statements about God's relation to creatures in general, and neither is spatial in the way this verse is. They describe knowledge and responsiveness rather than position.

The measurement here belongs to a narrative. A distance is given between two described parties at a described moment, and the units are physical. The difference in kind between the two uses of nearness is part of why the commentators disagree about the subject — a physical distance fits an angel more readily than it fits God.

**The Two Arcs**

Some commentators read *qawsayn* as two arcs rather than two bows, and see in them the two halves of a circle — the arc of descent by which creation comes from God, and the arc of ascent by which it returns.

The reading is elegant and has a long history in the mystical commentaries. It is not required by the Arabic, which most naturally denotes bows, and it turns a measurement into a symbol.

Weighing them, the plain reading is stronger: the verse is describing a distance in a narrative, and the narrative's other details are spatial rather than symbolic. The symbolic reading can be held as an additional layer without displacing the plain sense.

**What the Distance Does Not Say**

The measurement describes proximity and nothing else. It does not describe contact, union, or identity, and the sūrah does not draw any of those inferences from it.

That restraint matters, because the verse has been read in traditions that go considerably further than the text does. A distance given in a narrative is a fact about a narrative; it is not a statement about the nature of God or the possibility of union with Him.

The Qur'an's own statements on those matters are separate and clear: no vision encompasses Him (6:103), and He does not speak to a human being except by revelation, from behind a veil, or through a messenger (42:51). The verse here does not override either, and reading it as though it did imports a claim the text does not make.

---

## Verse 53:10

> Then Allah revealed to His servant what He revealed ˹through Gabriel˺

**Why the Ambiguity Is Tolerated**

The Qur'an does not resolve the ambiguity here, and the unresolved state has a function. What matters for the sūrah's argument is that the revelation came from God and reached the Prophet ﷺ. The precise mechanics of the transmission are secondary.

That priority is consistent with the rest of the Book. The Qur'an describes revelation's modes in general terms (42:51) without providing a theory of how each mode operates.

**“His Servant”**

The recipient is called *ʿabd*, servant, and the word is chosen with care. It is the same word used in the opening of Sūrah al-Isrāʾ: glory be to the One Who took His servant by night (17:1), and in the description of the revelation's descent in Sūrah al-Baqarah and al-Furqān.

The title establishes the relationship on which the whole account depends. A servant does not originate; he receives and delivers. The accusation of invention assumed a man speaking for himself, and the title removes that assumption in one word.

It also answers the charge from the other direction. A person who claims to be God's partner would not be described by God as His servant, and the description is given by God rather than claimed by the man.

**The Servant Title Across the Qur'an**

The description of the Prophet ﷺ as God's servant is not local to this verse; it is a standing title used at the most important moments of the account.

Blessed is the One Who sent down the Standard to His servant, so that he may be a warner to the whole world (25:1). Glory be to the One Who took His servant by night (17:1). The revelation is described as sent down to your heart (26:194), addressed to the same servant.

The consistency of the title across chapters and occasions suggests it is doing doctrinal work. It fixes the Prophet's position as recipient rather than originator, and it does so in the very verses that make the largest claims about what he received.

That is why the verse uses it here. The claim being established is the highest in the passage, and the title attached to the recipient keeps the claim from being misread.

**Revelation Given at a Point in Time**

The verse describes an act with a beginning: then He revealed. The particle *fa* marks sequence, and the sequence is temporal — after the approach, the giving.

The Qur'an insists on this temporality elsewhere. The revelation came down on a named night (97:1; 44:3), in a named language, to a named person, over a period of years. It is not an eternal truth grasped by a mind but an event that occurred in history.

The temporality is part of the answer to the charge of invention. A man's thoughts have no arrival time; a revelation does, and the sūrah gives one. The verse in question is the moment of arrival, described in a single clause.

**What Was Revealed**

Although the verse does not name the content, the sūrah identifies it later: he certainly saw some of his Lord's greatest signs (53:18). The revelation included both the message and the signs shown to the one who received it.

That distinction matters for understanding the event. The Prophet ﷺ received a text to deliver and a vision to testify to, and the sūrah treats both as parts of the same giving.

The vision is what the later verses of the chapter dispute, and the text is what the earlier sūrah defended. The verse here covers both without specifying either.

---

## Verse 53:11

> The ˹Prophet’s˺ heart did not doubt what he saw

**Mā Kadhaba: He Did Not Lie, and He Did Not Falter**

The verb allows two readings, both supported. The first takes it as *kadhaba*, he did not lie — the heart did not falsify what it perceived. The second takes it as *kadhdhaba*, it did not falter or waver — the perception was steady.

The two readings describe the same reliability from different angles. A heart that does not lie reports accurately; a heart that does not falter perceives without wavering.

**The Disagreement About What Was Seen**

The commentators differ on the object of the vision. Some read the passage as describing the Prophet's seeing of the angel, which fits the preceding verses. Others read it as a vision of God, and support the reading with reports from Ibn ʿAbbās that the Prophet ﷺ saw his Lord.

The disagreement is old and it is genuine. Both positions are attributed to Companions, and both have been defended by later scholars.

The verses that follow describe a second occasion at the Lote-Tree, and some read that occasion as the one in which the vision occurred, which allows both readings to stand: the angel on the horizon, and something greater at the boundary.

**The Heart in the Qur'an's Account of Perception**

The verse's attribution of seeing to the heart follows the Qur'an's general account of how people perceive truth. It is not the eyes that are blind, but the hearts in the chests that grow blind (22:46). Elsewhere the failure is described in the same terms: they have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with (7:179).

In that account the organs of sense supply data and the heart supplies recognition. A person can see and not recognise, which is the condition the Qur'an attributes to those who reject the signs.

This verse describes the opposite condition. The perception arrived and the recognition followed without a gap. That is what the negation states, and it is the highest claim the Qur'an makes about the reliability of its recipient.

**ʿĀʾishah's Position and Its Grounds**

ʿĀʾishah denied the vision of God in explicit terms. When Masrūq asked her whether the Prophet ﷺ had seen his Lord, she replied that his hair stood on end at the question, and she named three claims that constitute a great lie — among them that Muhammad ﷺ saw his Lord (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 177).

Her grounds were scriptural, and she cited them: no vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision (6:103). She also cited the verse stating that God does not speak to a human being except by revelation, from behind a veil, or through a messenger-angel (42:51).

The argument is not a denial of the event described in this sūrah. It is a denial that the event was a vision of the divine essence with the physical eye, and she located the vision in the angel instead.

**Abū Dharr's Question**

The other side of the record comes from Abū Dharr, who asked the Prophet ﷺ directly: did you see your Lord? The answer given was: He is light — how could I see Him? In another wording: I saw light (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 178).

The report is significant because it comes from the Prophet ﷺ himself and is not a later inference. The answer does not affirm a vision of the essence; it describes an obstacle of light.

The commentators who hold that the Prophet ﷺ saw his Lord read the report as describing a vision veiled by light rather than its absence, and they cite the saying that His veil is light. The report itself is consistent with either reading, which is why the disagreement persists.

---

## Verse 53:12

> How can you ˹O pagans˺ then dispute with him regarding what he saw

**The Question Turns on the Disputants**

The verse changes direction. Eleven verses have described an event; this one addresses the people arguing about it. *Afa-tumārūnahu ʿalā mā yarā* — will you dispute with him about what he sees?

The verb *tumārūn* carries more than disagreement. It denotes resisting, contesting, pushing back against something presented. The word choice describes an active opposition rather than a puzzled enquiry.

The shift from narrative to address is abrupt and deliberate. The sūrah has given its account, and now it asks what the account is being met with — not evidence, but contest.

The structure of the question exposes its own problem. The disputants were not present at the event. They had no observation of their own to set against the report, only a refusal to accept it.

That asymmetry is the point. A dispute between two witnesses can be resolved by comparing testimony; a dispute between a witness and someone who was absent can only be resolved by assessing the witness. The verse reduces the matter to that.

The question therefore is not asking them to produce a better account of the event. It is asking what their dispute consists of, given that they were nowhere near it.

**The Dispute Over the Night Journey**

The commentators connect this verse either to the vision of the angel or to the night journey and ascension, and the second connection has a documented history.

The Qur'an records the journey in a single verse: glory be to the One Who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, so that We may show him some of Our signs (17:1). The reports describe the Meccan response as immediate and hostile — a journey described as taking a month was said to have taken a night, and some who had accepted the message withdrew.

The dispute was therefore not private or theoretical. It was public, it cost the community members, and this verse answers it in the same register: you are contesting what he saw.

The verse implies a standard for legitimate contest. A person who disputes a report should have either a contrary observation or a defect in the report — an inconsistency, an impossibility, a motive to lie.

The sūrah has already removed the third: it established that the man is neither astray nor deluded, and that he does not speak from whim. The second is not available, since the account is internally consistent and matches the parallel description in at-Takwīr. The first was never offered.

What remains is bare refusal, and the verse names it as such by asking the question rather than answering it.

---

## Verse 53:13

> And he certainly saw that ˹angel descend˺ a second time

**Nazla Ukhrā: A Second Descent**

The verse states that the seeing happened twice. *Nazlah ukhrā* — another descent, a second time. The noun is from the root of descending, and it describes an occasion of coming down rather than a movement in progress.

The emphasis particle *laqad*, certainly, marks the statement as confirmed. The sūrah is not adding a detail casually; it is asserting a second occasion against anyone who might treat the first as a single unusual event.

Two occasions of the same kind establish a pattern. A single vision can be explained away as an aberration; a repeated one requires a different account.

The earlier verses of this sūrah describe the first — the horizon, the approach, the measured distance. This verse introduces the second, and the following verses give its location at the Lote-Tree of the utmost limit.

The division is what makes the chapter's structure intelligible. It contains two accounts, and readers who treat it as one narrative find the sequence confusing.

**The Interval Between the Two Sightings**

The two occasions named in this passage are separated by years. The first belongs to the beginning of the prophetic mission at Mount Ḥirāʾ, when the revelation started; the second belongs to the journey recorded in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ, which the tradition places in the later Meccan period.

The interval matters for the argument the sūrah is making. Two appearances separated by a long stretch of ordinary life are not a single sustained trance or a continuous state of exaltation. Between them the Prophet lived in Mecca, was opposed by his own tribe, lost his wife Khadījah and his uncle Abū Ṭālib, and continued delivering a message that brought him nothing but hardship.

That context is what makes the second sighting credible as a report rather than as a psychological episode. It came to a man whose circumstances had given him every reason to abandon the claim, and it did not change his conduct or his message.

**Seeing in the Created Form**

The commentators are agreed on one point about both occasions: what was seen was the angel in the form in which he was created, not in the form in which he usually came with revelation.

The distinction is important. The Qur'an describes revelation as coming through a spirit sent down upon the heart (26:193–194), and it describes the angel appearing to Maryam in the likeness of a well-proportioned man (19:17). The ordinary mode of contact did not involve a visible appearance at all.

What this sūrah reports is the exception to that mode — two occasions on which the angel appeared as he is, and the appearance is described in observational language: a horizon, a form, a distance, a location. The verse's assertion of a second occasion therefore asserts a second exception, and exceptions of that kind are what the audience found hardest to accept.

---

## Verse 53:14

> at the Lote Tree of the most extreme limit ˹in the seventh heaven˺—

**Sidrat al-Muntahā**

The location of the second vision is named, and the name is one of the most discussed in the Qur'an. *Sidrat al-muntahā*, the Lote-Tree of the utmost boundary.

The name has two parts. The first identifies a tree; the second identifies a limit. Together they describe a specific object at a specific edge, and the combination is unusual enough that the commentators have debated it for centuries.

The naming follows the method of the preceding verses. A location is given rather than a description, and the location is stated in terms that imply a geography of the unseen.

The *sidr* is a real tree, the lote or buckthorn, well known in Arabia. It grows in dry ground, gives dense shade, and is valued in a landscape where shade is scarce. The Qur'an mentions it elsewhere: the people of the garden in Sūrah Sabaʾ were given a land with two gardens and the lote-tree was among what they had (34:16), and the people of the right are described as being among lote-trees without thorns (56:28).

The choice of a familiar tree for a location at the edge of creation is deliberate. The Qur'an consistently describes the unseen using objects its audience knew, and the lote-tree is one of the most ordinary trees in the Arabian landscape.

An ordinary tree at the boundary of everything is an image that resists allegory. It is not a symbol of growth or life; it is a marker.

**The Connection to the Night Journey**

The verse belongs to the account of the ascension, which the Qur'an records separately: glory be to the One Who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, so that We may show him some of Our signs (17:1).

The stated purpose in that verse — to show him signs — corresponds to what this sūrah reports at the end of the passage: he certainly saw some of his Lord's greatest signs. The two chapters describe the same event from different angles.

That correspondence supports the reading that this verse locates the second of the two occasions mentioned in the verse before it.

A boundary could have been marked by a wall, a gate, or a line. The Qur'an marks it with a tree, and the choice is worth considering.

A tree is a living thing that grows where it is planted and stays there. It is the opposite of a barrier: it does not exclude, it occupies. A boundary marked by a tree is a boundary reached rather than imposed.

That fits the account. The vision occurred at the limit of the created order, and the limit is described as a place where something grows — the last living thing before what lies beyond.

---

## Verse 53:15

> near which is the Garden of ˹Eternal˺ Residence—

**Jannat al-Maʾwā**

The verse names a second location and gives its relation to the first: near the Lote-Tree is the Garden of Refuge. *Jannat al-maʾwā* — the garden of shelter, of dwelling, of the place one returns to for safety.

The word *maʾwā* is formed from a root meaning to take shelter or to give shelter. It is used in the Qur'an for a place of lodging and for refuge in danger.

Applying it to the Garden defines the Garden by its function rather than by its contents. Paradise here is not described as a reward but as a shelter.

The choice of name is significant given how the Qur'an describes paradise elsewhere. It is a place of rivers, fruit, thrones, and companions, and those descriptions emphasise abundance.

This name emphasises safety. A refuge is what a person needs when something is pursuing him, and the Qur'an's descriptions of the other destination are consistently about exposure — no shelter, no shade, no escape.

The verse in Sūrah al-Mursalāt makes the contrast explicit: proceed into a shade of smoke rising in three columns, providing neither coolness nor shelter from the flames (77:30–31). The word for shelter there is the same family as *maʾwā*. What is refused there is named here.

**The Spatial Relation**

The verse places the Garden near the Lote-Tree, and the nearness has been read in two ways. On the majority reading, the tree is in the seventh heaven and the Garden is at that limit, so the two are adjacent features of the same region.

On the alternative reading, the tree marks the boundary between two heavens and the Garden of Refuge is the seventh heaven itself, which would make the nearness one of containment rather than adjacency.

Neither reading changes the point. The boundary of the created order is located beside the place of shelter, and the verse states the proximity as a fact about the geography.

The Qur'an uses the same phrase for a specific company. Whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be with those Allah has blessed — the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous — and what excellent companions they are (4:69). The description in that verse is of a company rather than a place, and the word for the righteous there, *ṣiddīqūn* and *ṣāliḥūn*, matches the categories the Qur'an uses for the people of the Garden.

The commentators connect the Garden of Refuge with the place where the spirits of the prophets, the righteous, and the witnesses take refuge, which is consistent with the name.

That connection gives the location a population. The boundary of creation is not empty; beside it is a garden, and the garden is occupied.

---

## Verse 53:16

> while the Lote Tree was overwhelmed with ˹heavenly˺ splendours

**Yaghshā Mā Yaghshā**

The verse is built from a verb and its own undefined object: *idh yaghshā as-sidrata mā yaghshā* — when there covered the Lote-Tree that which covered it.

The construction refuses to name the covering. The verb is repeated, the object is left as a relative pronoun, and the sentence ends without saying what was there.

That is not a gap in the text. It is a deliberate rhetorical form, and the commentators have recognised it as such: the structure indicates something that cannot be adequately described, and the withholding is the description.

The Qur'an is not generally reticent. It describes the Fire in detail, the Garden in detail, and the day of judgment in detail. Its descriptions of the unseen are usually concrete enough to picture.

Here the description stops. The reason given by the commentators is reverence: what covered the tree was of a kind that language would diminish by naming.

The effect on the reader is specific. A named covering could be imagined and then judged inadequate; an unnamed one leaves the imagination without material. The verse produces awe by subtraction rather than by addition.

**The Readings of What Covered It**

The commentators supply what the text does not. Some describe the covering as light and brilliance, beauty and purity beyond description. Others report that it was covered with angels, and some add that they were like butterflies of gold circling it.

These are reports of varying strength, and the honest way to cite them is as interpretations rather than as facts established by the verse. The verse itself names nothing.

The variety of the readings is itself evidence for the reticence. Where a text withholds, the tradition fills, and the fillings differ. The verse's refusal to name is what kept the matter open, and the openness is arguably its point.

The construction has a parallel in the Qur'an's treatment of other overwhelming events. When Mūsā's people asked to see God, the mountain was levelled to dust and Mūsā collapsed unconscious (7:143) — the description there is of the effect on the observer rather than of the thing observed.

The same method appears here in the next verse, which describes the Prophet's sight as neither wandering nor overreaching. The focus is on the observer's steadiness, and the object is left unnamed in both cases.

That is the Qur'an's standard procedure for theophany and near-theophany: describe the recipient, not the source.

---

## Verse 53:17

> The ˹Prophet’s˺ sight never wandered, nor did it overreach

**Two Negations, One Claim**

The pair covers both directions in which a vision could be faulty. A gaze can fail by losing its object or by grasping at more than its object. Both failures are excluded.

The result is a statement about the reliability of the report. The witness was neither distracted nor presumptuous; he saw what was shown and reported what he saw.

That is the claim the sūrah needs. The dispute recorded earlier in the chapter was about the accuracy of the account, and this verse addresses accuracy directly rather than arguing for it.

The verse follows immediately after the statement that the Lote-Tree was covered by something unnamed. The contrast is the point: whatever was there, the observer did not flinch and did not strain.

The Qur'an elsewhere describes the opposite reaction to overwhelming disclosure. When the mountain was levelled to dust, Mūsā fell down unconscious, and when he came to he said glory be to You, I turn to You in repentance (7:143). That is the human response to direct divine presence.

What is described here is different. The observer remains composed and attentive, which suggests a vision of something other than the divine essence — consistent with the reading that the object was the angel in his created form.

**The Steady Gaze in the Qur'an's Language**

Steadiness of sight is used elsewhere in the Qur'an as a marker of inner state. The eyes of those who disbelieve will not return to them on the day of judgment, and their hearts will be empty (14:43); the sight returns twice, humbled and wearied, when a person tries to find a flaw in the creation and fails (67:3–4).

The pattern associates a wandering gaze with disturbance and a held gaze with certainty. This verse assigns the held gaze to the witness at the moment of the greatest disclosure reported in the Qur'an.

The assignment is a claim about his state, not merely about his eyes.

The verse's function in the argument is to characterise the reporter. A report is only as good as the reporter, and the reporter is here described as attentive, undistracted, and unambitious.

Those are the qualities a court would look for in a witness. The sūrah is making a case, and this verse supplies the character evidence.

It is also the strongest thing the sūrah says about the Prophet in this passage. He is not praised for his wisdom or his virtue here; he is described as having kept his eyes where they were told to be, which is a narrower and more useful claim.

**The Gaze as a Measure of the Heart**

The Qur'an treats the direction of the eye as an index of the inner state, and this verse uses that convention deliberately.

Where the heart is disturbed, the gaze wanders. The description of the day of judgment includes eyes that do not return to their owners and hearts that are empty (14:43), and the description of the hypocrites includes a turning away that begins in the look. Where the heart is settled, the gaze holds, and the Qur'an asks the reader to look again and again at the creation until the eye returns wearied and defeated (67:4).

Applying that convention here, the verse is making a statement about the Prophet's inner condition at the moment of the vision. He was neither agitated nor grasping. The composure attributed to him is not a physical detail but a claim about his state, and it is the claim that supports the reliability of what he reported.

---

## Verse 53:18

> He certainly saw some of his Lord’s greatest signs

The account closes with a summary of what was seen: he certainly saw one of the greatest signs of his Lord. *Min āyāti rabbih al-kubrā* — from the signs of his Lord, the greatest.

The superlative *al-kubrā* is feminine, agreeing with *āyah*, a sign. The verse states that what he saw belonged to the highest category of signs, without listing what else is in that category.

The emphasis particle *laqad*, certainly, appears again, as it did in the verse recording the second sighting.

*Al-kubrā* is singular, but the phrase *min āyāt*, from among the signs, implies plurality, and some read the superlative as a plural, making the meaning that he saw the greatest signs.

**What the Sign Was**

The commentators offer two main identifications. The first is the angel in his created form — the figure with the six hundred wings that filled the horizon, seen twice. The second is a specific object of the vision, described in some reports as a curtain or veil of light.

Both readings agree that the sign was a created thing. Neither identifies the sign as God Himself, which is consistent with the account's spatial language and with the Prophet's own reported statement that his Lord is light and that he did not see Him.

The verse's silence on the identification is consistent with the previous one's silence about the covering. The category is given; the content is not.

The word *āyah* has a defined range in the Qur'an. It covers natural phenomena presented as evidence — the alternation of night and day, the ships on the sea, the rain — and it covers miraculous events given to prophets as credentials.

The superlative form is used for the greatest of these. The Qur'an tells Mūsā to go to Pharaoh with Our greatest sign (79:20–21), and it describes the splitting of the moon as a sign (54:1).

Placing the vision in that category classifies it. It was not a private experience but a sign of the kind the Qur'an presents as evidence for its claims.

**The Parallel in al-Isrāʾ**

The verse corresponds closely to the other chapter's account of the same event: glory be to the One Who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, so that We may show him some of Our signs (17:1).

The two verses share the vocabulary of showing and of signs. Al-Isrāʾ states the purpose of the journey; an-Najm reports that the purpose was fulfilled.

The correspondence is one of the strongest indications that the two passages describe one event, and it supports the reading that this sūrah's account of the second descent is the ascension.

Eleven verses have described the vision, and this one summarises it. The summary is deliberately modest: a sign, of the greatest kind, belonging to his Lord.

There is no claim of union, no description of divine presence, no statement of what was learned. The account ends where it began, with an event witnessed and reported.

That modesty is the account's defence. The charge was that the Prophet invented the revelation, and the response is a spare report of an external event, closed with a claim that stops short of anything the audience could call extravagant.

**The Turn to the Idols**

The verse that follows opens with a question about al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā, and the abruptness of the turn has puzzled readers.

The connection is the dispute. The people contesting the vision are the same people who worship three named deities, and the sūrah now examines those deities on their own terms — what they are, what they can do, and how they came to be assigned a gender and a rank.

The argument moves from what cannot be verified to what can. The vision is beyond the audience's reach; their own idols are not.

---

## Verse 53:19

> Now, have you considered ˹the idols of˺ Lât and ’Uzza

**The Abrupt Turn**

The verse changes subject without transition. The Lote-Tree and the Garden give way to two stones in the Arabian desert, and the address turns from narrative to question.

*Afaraʾaytum* — have you considered, have you given thought? The formula is used in the Qur'an to introduce an examination of something the audience takes for granted. It invites reflection rather than belief.

The turn is the sūrah's central move. Having described a vision the audience cannot check, it now turns to a belief system the audience practices every day, and asks whether that system survives reflection.

Al-Lāt was a white stone venerated at Ṭāʾif, the town of the Thaqīf tribe in the mountains east of Mecca.

The reports of its destruction are specific: after the conquest of Mecca the Prophet sent al-Mughīrah ibn Shuʿbah, with Abū Sufyān ibn Ḥarb, to demolish it, and a mosque was built on the site afterwards. The account is recorded in the early histories and is cited by Ibn Kathīr in his commentary on these verses.

**Al-ʿUzzā**

Al-ʿUzzā was venerated at Nakhlah, a valley on the road between Mecca and Ṭāʾif, and it was associated particularly with the Quraysh, who considered it the most important of their deities.

Its name is built from the root of might and honour — *al-ʿizzah* — with the feminine form, so that it means the mighty one or the most honoured. The Quraysh gave personal names derived from it, including ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā, the name of the Prophet's uncle Abū Lahab.

The reports describe it as three trees or a house containing three trees, and its destruction is attributed to Khālid ibn al-Walīd, who was sent to demolish it after the conquest of Mecca.

Both destructions are recorded in the early histories, and the accounts share a feature: the idols did nothing when they were struck.

That detail matters for the argument the sūrah is making. A deity that cannot defend its own shrine cannot protect a worshipper, and the reports were well known to the audience within a few years of this revelation.

The sūrah does not need to argue the point from theory. It asks the audience to consider two objects that everyone knew, and the question does the work.

**The Report Attached to These Verses**

The early histories transmit a report that during the recitation of this passage, words were added praising these three deities, and that the addition was later withdrawn.

The report has been much discussed. Its chains are widely judged defective — al-Qāḍī ʿIyāḍ states that no trustworthy narrator related it with a sound uninterrupted chain, and al-Albānī concluded that it was fabricated — though Ibn Ḥajar held that the number of chains indicates the story has some basis. A chronological difficulty also arises, since the sūrah is dated to the tenth year of the mission while the event described belongs to the fifth.

The text of the Qur'an contains no such words, and the verse as it stands asks a question about three idols.

---

## Verse 53:20

> and the third one, Manât, as well

**Manāt: The Name of Fate**

The third deity is named, and the name is the most revealing of the three. *Manāt* is derived from the root m-n-y, which carries the meanings of allotting, apportioning, and measuring out — the same root as *manīyah*, fate or death.

The deity's name therefore means something like the one who allots or the goddess of destiny. The commentators connect it with the idea that the worshippers attributed their fortunes to her allocation.

The name is the clearest evidence in the triad of what the worship was for. Al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā are named for divinity and for might; Manāt is named for the distribution of outcomes, which is the thing people actually want from a god.

Manāt was venerated at al-Mushallal, a coastal place near Qudayd between Mecca and Madīnah, and it was particularly associated with the tribes of Khuzāʿah and with the Aws and Khazraj of Yathrib.

That location is significant for the audience of this sūrah. The Aws and Khazraj were the two tribes of Madīnah who would later receive the Prophet, and the idol was part of their pre-Islamic practice.

The verse names a deity whose worship extended beyond Mecca into the region that would become the centre of Islam. The three idols named in these two verses were the most widely venerated in western Arabia.

**The Third of the Other Two**

The phrase *ath-thālithat al-ukhrā* — the third of the other two — has been read in different ways. Some take it as marking Manāt's rank as the least of the three, the one added to complete the set.

Others connect it with the practice of the Arabs, who would speak of a person as the third of two when describing someone who joins a pair and is by this defined by inferiority to it.

Either way the phrase marks a hierarchy within the triad. The deities are not equals; they are ranked, and the ranking was assigned by the worshippers without any basis in the deities themselves.

Manāt's destruction is recorded in the early histories, though the reports differ over who was sent: some name Saʿd ibn Zayd al-Ashhalī, others Abū Sufyān ibn Ḥarb or ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib. All agree that the shrine was demolished after the conquest of Mecca.

The variation in the reports is worth noting rather than smoothing over. What is consistent across them is the fact of the destruction and the fact that the idol offered no resistance, which is the point the sūrah's question depends on.

The shrine had a custodian and accumulated offerings, which means it had an economy — staff, income, and a stake in its own continuation. That is what the sūrah is examining when it asks what these deities actually are.

They were institutions with property and function, and the question posed in the preceding verse is about the reality behind the institution.

**The Economies of the Three Shrines**

Each of the three deities named in these two verses was supported by an institution, and the institutions are documented in the early histories.

Al-Lāt had a house at Ṭāʾif with a custodian who collected the offerings of pilgrims. Al-ʿUzzā had a structure at Nakhlah with attendants and a treasury. Manāt had a shrine at al-Mushallal with its own custodian, and the Aws and Khazraj did not consider their pilgrimage complete until they had visited it and shaved their heads there — a practice ʿĀʾishah described and al-Bukhārī recorded.

The detail matters for the argument. These were not private superstitions but public establishments with income, staff, and a stake in their own continuation. The sūrah's question about what the deities are is therefore also a question about who benefits from the arrangement, and the audience could answer it without reference to theology.

## Verse 53:21

> Do you ˹prefer to˺ have sons while ˹you attribute˺ to Him daughters

**The Reversal Stated**

The question is short and cutting. *A-lakum adh-dhakaru wa lahu al-unthā* — is the male for you and the female for Him?

It restates the worshippers' own arrangement and holds it up. They had assigned daughters to God and kept sons for themselves, and the verse asks whether that distribution makes sense.

The question does not argue. It restates, and the restatement is the argument, because the arrangement is indefensible once it is put plainly.

The force of the question depends on a fact about its audience: they valued sons and considered daughters a diminishment. The Qur'an records the attitude directly elsewhere: when news of a daughter is brought to one of them, his face darkens and he is filled with inward grief (16:58).

Given that attitude, assigning the female sex to God was not an honour. It was assigning to the deity what the assigner would not accept for himself, and the verse points at the inconsistency without comment.

That is the whole of the argument, and it requires no premise the audience could dispute. Their own conduct supplies it.

**The Parallel in an-Naḥl**

The same argument appears at greater length in Sūrah an-Naḥl: do you prefer males for yourselves while He takes females? That is truly an unfair division. They attribute to Allah what they themselves dislike, and their tongues utter the lie that they will have the best (16:57–62).

The phrase *qismatun ḍīzā*, an unfair division, is the term used there for exactly the arrangement this verse describes.

The repetition across sūrahs shows that this was a standing argument rather than a one-off rhetorical device. The charge of an unfair distribution appears in a Meccan sūrah here and in another Meccan sūrah there, addressed to the same practice.

The method is worth isolating because it recurs throughout the chapter. The sūrah does not argue from revelation, which the audience rejected, or from metaphysics, which they had no framework for.

It argues from behaviour the audience could observe in themselves. They prefer sons; they assign daughters to God; therefore their theology contradicts their preferences, and a belief that contradicts its holder's own preferences has no evidential basis.

That is a form of argument available to anyone, believer or not, and it is why the sūrah uses it at the point where its other arguments have run out.

**The Preference Behind the Theology**

The verse works because it identifies a preference that the audience would not have denied. Sons were valued: they carried the name, contributed to the household's strength, and were the reason a man's line continued. Daughters were a burden in a society where marriage required a dowry and where tribal honour was thought to be at risk through them.

The preference was not hidden. It shaped inheritance, it shaped the language of congratulation and condolence, and it shaped the practice the Qur'an condemns elsewhere of burying infant daughters alive and then asking them for what sin they were killed (81:8–9).

Given a preference that strong, the theological arrangement is transparent. The audience assigned to God what they did not want, and the verse holds the assignment up without commentary, because no commentary is needed once the preference is acknowledged.

---

## Verse 53:22

> Then this is ˹truly˺ a biased distribution

**Qismatun Ḍīzā**

The verse is four words in Arabic and delivers a verdict. *Tilka idhan qismatun ḍīzā* — that, then, is a division that is unjust, skewed, biased.

*Ḍīzā* is a rare word. It denotes a distribution in which one party is shorted, a division that departs from equity. Its rarity is part of its effect: the sūrah reaches for a word it does not use often, and uses it for this arrangement.

The demonstrative *tilka*, that, points back at the arrangement described in the previous verse. The verdict is delivered on something already established rather than argued for here.

A division is unfair when the party making it takes the better share and assigns the worse to another, without grounds.

That is precisely the arrangement. The worshippers kept the male children for themselves and assigned the female to God, and they did so while considering the female the lesser portion. There is no principle behind the assignment other than their own preference.

The verse names the arrangement for what it is: a distribution made by an interested party in his own favour, presented as theology.

**The Judgment Announced**

The structure of the two verses is worth noting. The first asks a question; the second announces a judgment. No premises are stated between them, because none are needed.

That economy is a feature of the sūrah's method. Where the audience's position is self-evidently indefensible, the sūrah states it and judges it, and does not argue.

The particle *idhan*, then or in that case, marks the judgment as following from what was just said. Given the arrangement you have described, this is what it is.

The identical term appears in another Meccan sūrah: do you prefer males for yourselves while He takes females? That is truly an unfair division (16:57–58).

The recurrence shows that the charge was a standing one in the Meccan revelation. The same word, *ḍīzā*, applied to the same practice, in two sūrahs separated in revelation.

It also shows that the argument did not depend on the audience accepting the Qur'an. The charge is that their own practice is internally inconsistent, which is a claim they could assess on their own terms.

## Verse 53:23

> These ˹idols˺ are mere names that you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized. They follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions and whatever ˹their˺ souls desire, although ˹true˺ guidance has already come to them from their Lord

**Names Without Referents**

The verse moves from the arrangement to the objects of the arrangement. *In hiya illā asmāʾ sammaytumūhā* — they are nothing but names you have named.

The formulation is precise. The verse does not say the stones do not exist; it says the names have no referent. Al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā exist as words, as stones, and as institutions, but not as the deities the names are supposed to denote.

That distinction is the core of the Qur'an's critique of idolatry. The error is not the existence of the object but the projection of divinity onto it, and the projection has no source.

The verse names the agents of the naming: you and your fathers. The addition of the ancestors is significant.

It identifies the mechanism by which the names persist. No living person chose them; they were inherited, and their authority consists in the fact that they are old.

The Qur'an treats this mechanism as an argument against the practice rather than for it. Inheritance explains how a belief survives; it does not supply evidence for it. Several passages make the point directly, asking whether the believers would follow their forefathers even if their forefathers understood nothing and were not guided (2:170).

**No Authority Sent Down**

The verse states what is missing: *mā anzala allāhu bihā min sulṭān* — God has sent down no authority for them.

*Ānzala*, sent down, is the Qur'an's word for revelation, and *sulṭān* is proof, warrant, or authorising evidence. The phrase sets a standard: a claim about the unseen requires a warrant that comes from outside the claimant.

The standard is the same one the sūrah applied to the Prophet in its fourth verse — he does not speak from whim, it is nothing but revelation revealed. The two verses are mirror images: one establishes that the Prophet's claims have a source, the other that the idols' do not.

The verse identifies the actual sources of the belief: *in yattabiʿūna illa aẓ-ẓanna wa mā tahwā al-anfus* — they follow nothing but conjecture and what the souls desire.

**Guidance Has Already Come**

The verse ends with the fact that removes any excuse: *wa laqad jāʾahum min rabbihim al-hudā* — and guidance has certainly come to them from their Lord.

The emphasis particle *laqad* appears again, marking the statement as established. The audience is not in a position of ignorance; they have received a message and are following conjecture anyway.

That changes the character of the critique. It is not addressed to people who have never heard an argument but to people who have heard one and preferred their inherited practice. The verse's charge is not ignorance but preference.

Both halves of the verse have close parallels elsewhere. On the names: you worship instead of Allah only idols, and you invent a lie (29:17); the names you have named have no basis in anything sent down.

On conjecture and desire: have you seen the one who takes his own desire as his god (45:23)? And: those who do wrong follow their own desires without knowledge (30:29).

The recurrence across Meccan sūrahs shows that this was a developed critique rather than an improvised one. The same two diagnoses — conjecture and desire — are applied to the same practice in several places.

**How Inherited Beliefs Persist**

The verse names the mechanism by which the names survived: you and your fathers. The addition of the ancestors is the most informative part of the statement.

An inherited belief does not require evidence to persist. It requires repetition, and repetition is supplied by the household, the festival, the shrine, and the name given to a child at birth. The audience's children were called ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā and ʿAbd Manāt, and every use of the name reinforced the deity's standing without anyone having to argue for it.

The verse identifies this as the actual foundation of the practice, and the identification is a criticism. Age explains survival; it does not supply warrant.

## Verse 53:24

> Or should every person ˹simply˺ have whatever ˹intercessors˺ they desire

**The Rhetorical Question**

The verse is a single question, and it expects the answer no. *Am li-l-insāni mā tamannā* — or does man have whatever he wishes?

*Tamannā* is from a root connected with wishing, hoping, and constructing scenarios in the mind. It is not the neutral word for wanting; it carries the sense of imagining a situation one would like to be true.

The question therefore is not about ordinary desire. It is about the desire that shapes what a person believes, and it follows directly from the previous verse's statement that the worshippers follow what their souls desire.

The verse makes a claim about how religions are formed. Where a belief corresponds to what the believer wants, its origin is suspect.

The application to the audience is specific. They wanted intercessors who would secure their position with God, and they had three named deities who supplied exactly that function. The correspondence between the want and the belief is the evidence.

That is not an argument that the belief is false. It is an argument that the belief is unexamined, and the sūrah has already established that the examination was never carried out.

**The Parallel Claim in al-Baqarah**

The same challenge appears in the Madinan sūrah: they say none will enter Paradise except the Jews or the Christians. That is their wishful thinking. Say, produce your proof if you are truthful (2:111).

The phrase there, *dhālika amānīyuhum*, that is their wishful thinking, uses the same root as *tamannā* here. The charge is identical: a belief about the afterlife that matches the believer's hopes, offered without evidence.

The recurrence across a Meccan and a Madinan sūrah, addressed to different groups, shows that the Qur'an treats this as a general pattern rather than a local accusation.

The question denies a principle rather than a particular belief. It is not saying that this specific desire will go unfulfilled; it is saying that desire does not determine reality.

The principle has consequences for the audience's whole system. Their deities were assigned functions — intercession, protection, allocation of fortune — that corresponded to their needs. If desire does not determine what exists, then none of those functions is established.

That is why the question is placed here, immediately after the verse about conjecture and desire. It generalises the diagnosis into a principle.

---

## Verse 53:25

> In fact, to Allah ˹alone˺ belongs this world and the next

**Ownership of Both Worlds**

The verse answers the question of the previous one with a statement of ownership. *Wa li-llāhi mā fī al-ākhirati wa al-ūlā* — and to Allah belongs whatever is in the Hereafter and the first.

The word *al-ūlā*, the first, denotes this world in relation to the one that follows. The pairing covers the whole of existence, present and to come, and leaves nothing outside the statement.

The form of the sentence is significant. The prepositional phrase to Allah comes first, which in Arabic places the emphasis on the owner rather than on the owned. The sentence asserts not that God has possessions but that possession, as such, belongs to Him.

The Hereafter is named before this world, which reverses the natural order of experience.

The reversal is deliberate and appears elsewhere in the Qur'an. Where the point at issue concerns the afterlife, the afterlife is named first, and the point at issue here is precisely the afterlife — the audience's belief that their deities would intercede for them in it.

The ordering also carries a valuation. What is named first is what matters in the argument, and the argument concerns what happens after death.

**Ownership Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The claim is made repeatedly and in similar terms. To Allah belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth and whatever is in them (5:120); and to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth (2:284); blessed is He to Whom belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth and everything between them (43:85).

The formula is one of the most frequent in the Qur'an, and it functions as a premise for many arguments — about judgment, about provision, about the authority of revelation.

Its use here is continuous with those uses. Ownership entails authority, and authority entails that no arrangement in either world can be made without the owner's permission.

Eleven words in Arabic, and the sūrah moves on to intercession. The brevity reflects the audience's position: they did not deny God's ownership, so the point needed stating rather than proving.

That economy is characteristic of Meccan argument. Where a premise is shared, it is stated in a clause and used; where it is disputed, it is argued for at length.

The reader should not mistake the brevity for a minor point. The whole of the sūrah's treatment of intercession in the next several verses depends on this statement.

**The Shared Premise**

The verse makes a claim the audience accepted, and its persuasive force depends on that acceptance. The Meccan opponents of the Prophet did not deny God's existence or His role as creator; the Qur'an records them affirming both when asked who created the heavens and the earth (31:25).

Their error lay in the additions: partners, offspring, intercessors who held standing independently of God's permission. The verse appeals past those additions to the belief underneath them.

That is the shape of much Meccan argument. It does not begin by establishing what the audience denies; it begins from what the audience affirms and draws out the consequences. Here the consequence is immediate: if God owns both worlds, no arrangement in either can be made by anyone else, and no share of authority can be held apart from Him.

## Verse 53:26

> ˹Imagine˺ how many ˹noble˺ angels are in the heavens! ˹Even˺ their intercession would be of no benefit whatsoever, until Allah gives permission to whoever He wills and ˹only for the people He˺ approves

**Kam Min Malak**

The verse opens with a quantitative challenge. *Wa kam min malakin fī as-samāwāt* — and how many angels are there in the heavens?

*Kam* in this construction is exclamatory rather than interrogative: how many there are. The verse points at a multitude, and the multitude is the point.

The angels are the highest beings the audience acknowledged as intermediaries. If intercession were a function that beings could hold in their own right, the angels would hold it first.

The verse states that their intercession will not benefit at all — *lā tughnī shafāʿatuhum shayʾan*. The negation is emphatic: not any benefit, not in any degree.

The subject of the intercession is the angels, who are real, who inhabit the heavens, and who stand before God. The verse is not about imaginary beings; it is about the highest beings in the created order.

That is what makes the argument effective against the audience's position. If the angels' intercession is subject to permission, then the intercession of stones named by human beings is not only subject to permission but without standing at all.

**The Condition of Permission**

The exception is stated in two parts. The first: except after Allah gives permission to whoever He wills — *illā min baʿdi an yaʾdhana allāhu liman yashāʾ*.

Permission is before intercession. The intercessor does not approach and then seek approval; the approval comes first and makes the approach possible.

The order reverses the audience's model. In their model, the worshipper approached the deity and the deity approached God. In the verse's model, God authorises and the intercessor then acts, and the authorisation is not owed to anyone.

The second part restricts the beneficiaries: and only for those He is pleased with — *wa yarḍā*. The permission is not general; it is directed at specific people.

That closes the arrangement the audience had constructed. Their deities were supposed to intercede for their worshippers on the basis of the worship, and the verse replaces the basis: the intercession, where it occurs, rests on God's pleasure with the person interceded for.

The two conditions together mean that intercession is not a resource a worshipper can accumulate. It is not purchased by offerings or secured by allegiance to a particular deity.

**The Parallel Verses**

The doctrine is stated in several places, and the formulation is consistent. Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission (2:255)? Intercession will not avail on that Day except from one to whom the Most Merciful has given permission and whose word He approves (20:109).

The verse in Sūrah Sabaʾ adds the scene: intercession with Him will not avail except for those He permits, until when terror is banished from their hearts they will say, what did your Lord say? They will say, the truth (34:23).

The consistency across sūrahs shows that this was a settled doctrine rather than a argumentative device, and the verse here applies it to the angels specifically.

The verse's argument is all the more — from the greater to the lesser. The angels are real, they dwell in the heavens, and they stand in God's presence; their intercession still requires permission and applies only to those God is pleased with.

The audience's deities are stones with names assigned by human beings, located in three places in western Arabia, destroyed within a decade of this revelation. Their claim to intercede does not reach the level at which the angels' claim is examined.

The verse does not need to say this. It states the position of the angels, and the comparison is left to the reader.

They do not intercede except for whom He approves, and they are in awe of Him (21:28).

## Verse 53:27

> Indeed, those who do not believe in the Hereafter label angels as female

**The Female Names**

The verse turns to the second half of the audience's arrangement. *Inna alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-al-ākhirati la-yusammūna al-malāʾikata tasmīyat al-unthā* — those who do not believe in the Hereafter name the angels with the naming of the female. They had called the angels daughters of God, and the verse states the practice without offering a refutation of it. The naming is presented as a fact about them, and the fact is left for the hearer to assess.

The phrase *tasmīyat al-unthā*, the naming of the female, describes a category of naming rather than a single label. It is a way of classifying the angels, not merely a word applied to them, and that is why the verse uses the noun of naming rather than simply saying that they called the angels female. The error lies in the classification, in the decision that these beings belong to a sexed category at all.

The emphatic particle *inna* combined with the *la* of the verb gives the sentence a double emphasis, marking it as a statement of fact that the audience would not have disputed. Everyone in Mecca knew what the angels had been called.

**The Parallel in az-Zukhruf**

The same charge appears at greater length in another Meccan sūrah: they have made the angels, who are servants of the Most Merciful, females. Were they witnesses to their creation? Their testimony will be recorded and they will be questioned (43:19).

The question in that verse is the sharpest available form of the charge: were they present? The answer is no, and the absence of presence removes any possible basis for the claim. The verse then adds that the testimony will be written down and the witnesses questioned, which converts the matter from an intellectual error into an accountable statement.

The recurrence of the charge in two Meccan sūrahs shows that the practice was well known and widely held, and that the Qur'an treated it as a specific, checkable error rather than a general failing of character. It is presented as something a person could be asked about and could not answer.

## Verse 53:28

> although they have no knowledge ˹in support˺ of this. They follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions. And surely assumptions can in no way replace the truth

**Mā Lahum Bihi Min ʿIlm**

The verse states the ground of the charge plainly. *Wa mā lahum bihi min ʿilm* — and they have no knowledge of it whatsoever.

The construction is emphatic in three ways: the negation *mā*, the prepositional phrase placing the object before the verb, and the particle *min* that intensifies the negation. Together they leave no degree of knowledge in the statement.

The object, *bihi*, refers back to the naming described in the previous verse. The claim is not that they know little about the angels but that they know nothing about the matter they are asserting.

The verse returns to the word the sūrah used several verses earlier: *in yattabiʿūna illā aẓ-ẓann* — they follow nothing but conjecture.

The repetition is deliberate. The first occurrence diagnosed the worship of the idols; this one diagnoses the naming of the angels. The same failure underlies both, and the sūrah applies the same word to both.

That consistency is worth noting. The sūrah does not treat idolatry and the angelic naming as separate errors requiring separate refutations. It treats them as one error appearing in two places.

**Conjecture Does Not Avail Against Truth**

The final clause generalises the diagnosis into a principle. *Wa inna aẓ-ẓanna lā yughnī min al-ḥaqqi shayʾan* — and conjecture does not avail against the truth in anything.

The principle is not that conjecture is worthless. In ordinary matters, estimation and probability are the best available guides, and the Qur'an does not require certainty for everyday decisions.

The principle is narrower and stronger: where the truth is known, conjecture cannot displace it. The audience had received a message stating the facts of the unseen, and they opposed it with guesswork. The verse says that guesswork does not defeat a known fact.

The Qur'an makes the same distinction repeatedly. Say, do you have any knowledge that you can produce before us? You follow nothing but conjecture, and you are only guessing (6:148).

The verse in Sūrah Yūnus is a direct challenge: bring forward your proof, if you are truthful (2:111 makes the same demand about Paradise). The demand for proof is the standard the Qur'an sets for claims about the unseen.

The verses in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ add the prohibition: do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge (17:36). The claim about the angels' sex falls under that prohibition directly.

**The Report on Conjecture**

The commentaries cite a saying of the Prophet warning against conjecture, describing it as the most lying form of speech, and the report is transmitted in the collections of al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

The saying belongs to the same conceptual field as the verse: conjecture is unreliable, and relying on it in matters that can be known is a form of falsehood.

The verse itself makes the point without the report, and the report's function in the commentary tradition is to extend it from theology to ordinary speech, where the same caution applies.

This verse closes the examination of the audience's beliefs. Seven verses have taken the system apart: the unfair division, the empty names, the futility of desire, the ownership of both worlds, the conditions of intercession, the naming of the angels, and now the absence of knowledge underlying all of it.

The diagnosis is complete and it is a single one. Every element of the system rests on conjecture, and conjecture does not avail against a truth that has been stated.

What follows is not further argument but instruction, addressed to the Prophet rather than to the audience.

**The Charge Applied Beyond Theology**

The diagnosis in this verse has a range wider than the angels, and the commentaries have always applied it that way.

The warning against conjecture covers ordinary speech: reporting what one has not verified, attributing motives one has not observed, and repeating an account because it is plausible rather than because it is known. The saying cited in the tradition, describing conjecture as the most mendacious form of speech, extends the verse into that territory.

It also covers religious claims. Every assertion about the unseen that has no revealed source falls under the same charge, whichever community makes it, and the standard applies to the believer as strictly as to the idolater.

That is the verse's lasting force. It is not a argumentative point scored against a particular group in seventh-century Arabia but a rule about evidence, and the rule is stated once, briefly, and without qualification: conjecture does not avail against the truth in anything.

## Verse 53:29

> So turn away ˹O Prophet˺ from whoever has shunned Our Reminder, only seeking the ˹fleeting˺ life of this world

**Desiring Nothing But This Life**

The verse adds a description of the person's aim: *wa lam yurid illā al-ḥayāt ad-dunyā* — and he desires nothing but the life of this world.

The word *illā*, nothing but, restricts the desire to this world alone. The person is not described as loving this world, which everyone does to some degree, but as having no object of desire beyond it.

That restriction connects to the previous verses. The denial of the Hereafter, the naming of the angels, and the worship of stones are all consistent with a life whose horizon ends at death, and the verse identifies that horizon as the underlying condition.

The verse does not authorise withdrawal from the mission. The Qur'an continues elsewhere to command the delivery of the message and to describe the Prophet's persistence with those who rejected him.

The instruction is best read as governing the Prophet's emotional investment rather than his conduct. He is told not to exhaust himself over people who have made their decision, and the same theme appears in the verse telling him not to grieve over those who turn away (16:127) and in the verse asking whether he will destroy himself with grief because they do not believe (18:6).

That reading is supported by the surrounding verses, which shift to statements about God's knowledge and His ownership of both worlds — consolations rather than commands to withdraw.

**The Parallel Verses**

The theme recurs. So remind, for you are only a reminder; you are not a controller over them (88:21–22). And: if Allah had willed, they would not have associated others with Him, and We have not appointed you a guardian over them (6:107).

The verses in Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt add the scope of the task: you are only a warner for those who fear it (79:45).

The consistent element across them is the limitation of the messenger's role. He delivers and warns; the outcome is not in his hands, and the verse here releases him from responsibility for it.

The verse marks the sūrah's turn from argument to consolation. Eighteen verses of argument have preceded it, and the argument has reached its conclusion: the system rests on conjecture and will not be argued out of it.

What follows is addressed to the Prophet and to those with him. The remaining verses describe God's knowledge, His ownership, His justice in rewarding, and the fate of those who turn away, and they function as reassurance rather than as further proof.

The turn is a structural feature of Meccan sūrahs, and it usually comes at the point where the argument has been completed.

**What Turning Away Means**

The word *aʿriḍ* describes a turning of the face, and its use here has been read in two ways that differ in scope.

On the narrower reading, the instruction concerns the Prophet's inner investment: he is not to wear himself out over people who have made their decision.

On the broader reading, it concerns the direction of his effort: the message continues to be delivered, but the argument is not to be pressed on those who have already refused it. Both readings are consistent with the surrounding verses, which turn to God's knowledge and His justice rather than to any change in the mission.

---

## Verse 53:30

> This is the extent of their knowledge. Surely your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided

**The Extent of Their Knowledge**

The verse returns to the subject of knowledge with a verdict. *Dhālika mablaghuhum min al-ʿilm* — that is their reach of knowledge, the limit of what they attain.

*Mablagh* is from a root meaning to reach or to attain, and the word denotes the furthest point something extends to. Applied to knowledge, it describes a ceiling.

The verdict is that the conjecture described in the previous verses is not a step toward knowledge but the whole of it. They have not gone partway and stopped; the guesswork is the extent of what they have.

The same formulation appears in Sūrah al-Qalam: your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way, and He knows best who is guided (68:7).

The repetition of the second half of the verse, in nearly identical words, in a sūrah revealed at a different point in the Meccan period, indicates a set piece — a statement the revelation returns to when the subject of human claims about guidance arises.

The first half of the verse, about the extent of their knowledge, is unique to this sūrah, and it is what gives the repetition its particular force here.

**The Verse as Consolation**

The verse follows the instruction to turn away, and its function is to make the instruction bearable.

The Prophet has been told to withdraw his attention from people who have turned away, and the verse supplies the reason: the assessment of those people is not his task. God knows who has strayed, and the knowledge does not depend on the Prophet's continuing efforts.

That is the consolation the Meccan revelation offers repeatedly. The messenger is responsible for delivery and not for outcome, and the verses that state God's knowledge are the ground of that division.

The recurrence in three sūrahs — al-Anʿām, al-Qalam, and here — marks it as one of the Qur'an's standing responses to dispute about guidance. When the argument turns on who is right, the response is that the matter is known and will be settled by the One who knows it.

The response does not end the argument. It relocates it, from a contest between human claims to a matter held in divine knowledge.

**Why the Statement Is Repeated**

The second half of the verse appears in Sūrah al-Qalam in almost identical words, and a related form appears in Sūrah al-Anʿām. The repetition marks it as a set piece of the Meccan revelation, returned to whenever the argument turns on who is guided and who is not.

The reason for the repetition is that the question is unavoidable. Every dispute about revelation eventually becomes a dispute about who has understood correctly, and the parties to such a dispute are not neutral judges of it.

The statement relocates the question. It does not settle the argument in the sense of persuading the other side; it declares that the settlement belongs to a knowledge that neither party has, and it frees the believer from the need to win.

The same statement appears in another form: your Lord knows best who strays from His way, and He knows best those who are guided (6:117).

## Verse 53:31

> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth so that He may reward the evildoers according to what they did, and reward the good-doers with the finest reward—

**Reward According to the Deed**

The verse states the correspondence twice, and the two statements differ in a way that has been much discussed.

The evil is rewarded *bimā ʿamilū*, with what they did — the reward matches the deed. The good is rewarded *bi-al-ḥusnā*, with what is best — the reward exceeds the deed.

The asymmetry is the Qur'an's consistent position. Elsewhere it states that whoever brings a good deed will have ten times its like, and whoever brings an evil deed will be recompensed only with its like (6:160). The verse here states the same principle in compressed form.

The word *al-ḥusnā* is the feminine superlative of the root ḥ-s-n, goodness and beauty. It is used in the Qur'an for the finest outcome, and the same root appears in the description of those who do good.

The use of the superlative rather than a matching term is the point. The good deed is met not with its equivalent but with the best, and the word chosen makes the excess explicit.

The pairing of *asāʾū*, they did evil, with *aḥsanū*, they did good, in the same verse, sets the two categories against each other in the plainest possible terms.

**The Parallel Verses on Purpose**

The Qur'an states the purpose of creation in similar terms elsewhere: We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything between them except in truth, and for a specified term (46:3); and: We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything between them in play (21:16).

The verses in Sūrah an-Naḥl add the denial of purposelessness: He created the heavens and the earth in truth, exalted is He above what they associate (16:3).

The verse here belongs to that family. Ownership, purpose, and settlement are stated together, and the connection between them is that a world owned by God and created with purpose cannot end without accounting.

The verse consolidates the sūrah's argument. Ownership has been established, human knowledge has been shown to be limited, and the settlement has been announced with its terms — exact for evil, exceeding for good.

That consolidation matters for the sūrah's overall shape. It opened with an oath and a denial, described a vision, dismantled an idolatrous system, and has now arrived at the moral order that replaces it.

The remaining verses apply the moral order to specific cases, beginning with the people who turn away and the one who gives little and withholds.

**The Asymmetry of Reward**

The two halves of the verse are not symmetrical, and the asymmetry is the point.

The evil is met with its like: those who did evil are rewarded for what they did, and the correspondence is exact. The good is met with something better than its like: those who did good are rewarded with the best.

The verse here gives the principle without the figure, and the wording makes the direction of the excess unmistakable.

The asymmetry answers a question the sūrah has been circling. If the arrangement of both worlds belongs to God, then the arrangement is not arbitrary and not indifferent; it is weighted, and the weighting runs in favour of the one who does good.

## Verse 53:32

> those who avoid major sins and shameful deeds, despite ˹stumbling on˺ minor sins. Surely your Lord is infinite in forgiveness. He knew well what would become of you as He created you from the earth and while you were ˹still˺ fetuses in the wombs of your mothers. So do not ˹falsely˺ elevate yourselves. He knows best who is ˹truly˺ righteous

**Al-Lamam: The Word for the Minor Sin**

The verse describes a category of fault with a single word: *al-lamam*, the small slip, the thing a person brushes against without committing himself to it. The commentators gloss it as the minor sins from which a person repents, and the word itself suggests a touching rather than a dwelling.

There is a report that fixes the meaning precisely. Ibn ʿAbbās was asked about *al-lamam* in this verse, and he answered by citing what Abū Hurayrah had heard from the Prophet: God has written for the son of Adam his share of fornication, which he will inevitably reach; the fornication of the eye is the look, the fornication of the tongue is the speech, the heart wishes and desires, and the private part confirms all of it or denies it. Ibn ʿAbbās said he had seen nothing more closely resembling *al-lamam* than that. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6243, and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2657 gives the longer version that adds the ears, the hands, and the feet.

The point of the report is not to license the small sins but to define them: what stops short of the act is *lamam*, and the act itself is not.

**The Major Sins and the Absence of a List**

The verse pairs the major sins with shameful deeds, and the pairing has been discussed because the Qur'an nowhere settles on a single definition of the major category.

The commentators offer functional definitions rather than lists. The word translated as shameful deeds, *fawāḥish*, is often used for the most atrocious offences and is frequently associated with fornication specifically.

The Qur'an elsewhere makes the same distinction the basis of a promise: If you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of your ˹lesser˺ misdeeds and admit you into a place of honour (4:31).

**Forgiveness Described as Vast**

The middle of the verse makes a statement about God rather than about the believer: your Lord is of vast forgiveness. The word for vast denotes breadth and capacity, and the commentators explain the breadth in two directions.

He forgives the small sins by keeping a person away from the great ones, and He forgives the great ones through repentance. Both operations are acts of forgiveness, and neither depends on the person having been sinless beforehand.

Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins (39:53).

The commentators connect the first moment to the covenant taken from the descendants of Adam, when they testified to His lordship (7:172).

It is Allah who elevates whoever He wills (4:49).

## Verse 53:33

> Have you seen the one who turned away ˹from Islam,˺

**The Report of Its Occasion**

The commentators connect these verses with a specific incident, and the incident is recorded in the exegetical literature with a named chain of transmission.

Al-Ṭabarī reports it on the authority of Mujāhid and Ibn Zayd, both of them Successors, and it names al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah. He had inclined toward the Prophet's religion; an idolater rebuked him for abandoning the faith of his forefathers; and when al-Walīd said that he feared God's punishment, the man offered to carry that punishment in his place in exchange for part of his wealth, on condition that he returned to idolatry. Al-Walīd agreed, paid part of the sum, and then refused the rest.

Because the report comes from Successors rather than from a Companion, it is not a graded ḥadīth, and it is cited here as an exegetical report. The verses do not depend on it. They describe a pattern the report illustrates: a bargain in which a person's standing in the hereafter was bought with money, and the money was not paid in full. The pattern was familiar enough in Mecca for the question to be understood at once.

**Tawallā Compared With Other Words for Leaving**

Arabic has several verbs for departing from a message, and the Qur'an uses them with some discrimination.

*Aʿraḍa* describes turning the face away, a refusal to listen; it is used for those who will not engage at all. *Kadhdhaba* is the flat denial of a claim's truth. *Istakbara* names the pride that produces the refusal, and it is applied to those who consider themselves above the message and its bearer.

*Tawallā* is none of these. It describes a person who was oriented toward something and then reoriented himself, and the Qur'an uses it for people who had some contact with the message before the turning. That is why the word fits this verse: the man in question had approached, and the verse's question depends on the fact that he had.

The distinction matters for the reader. The verse is not about an opponent who never came near; it is about someone whose departure is more informative than his arrival, and the word chosen carries that meaning in itself.

## Verse 53:34

> and ˹initially˺ paid a little ˹for his salvation˺, and then stopped

**The Report Behind the Verse**

The occasion recorded in the commentaries fits the description exactly, and it identifies which side of the transaction the man in this verse occupies.

Al-Ṭabarī reports from Mujāhid and Ibn Zayd that the verses concern al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, who agreed to pay part of his wealth to an idolater who undertook to carry his punishment in the hereafter, on condition that he abandoned the Prophet's message. He paid a portion of the agreed sum and then refused the remainder. The report reaches no Companion, so it stands as an exegetical account rather than a graded tradition, and it is cited here on that footing.

What the report contributes is the identity of the two movements in the verse. The giving was the payment agreed for the guarantee; the stopping was the withheld remainder. On that reading the verse describes a bargain that was never completed, and the failure lay with the man who was buying, which is why the question in the following verse asks whether he has knowledge of the unseen.

**The Parallel in al-Qiyāmah**

The same figure appears in another Meccan sūrah describing a man who neither believed nor prayed, but persisted in denial and turned away (75:31–32).

The parallel is close in structure. Both passages describe a turning away, and both treat the turning as the revelation of a prior state rather than as a new decision. The man in al-Qiyāmah did not begin and then stop; he turned away after having approached, and the turning disclosed what had been there.

The recurrence shows that the Qur'an regarded this as a distinct category of person: not the outright opponent, but the one who came near and left, and whose leaving is more informative than his coming.

**Akdā Compared With Other Words for Withholding**

The Qur'an has a range of vocabulary for refusing to give, and the choice of *akdā* here is not the obvious one.

*Bakhila* names stinginess as a settled character trait; *amsaka* describes the simple act of holding back; *qatara* denotes meanness in spending. Each of these would have conveyed the refusal, and none of them is used.

*Akdā* is different because it describes an interrupted process rather than a disposition. The man is not called a miser; he is described as someone who began and did not finish, and the image is of a digger whose work stops at the rock.

The difference changes the diagnosis. A miser never starts, and his fault lies in the refusal. This man started, which means something in him responded, and his fault lies in the stopping. The verse's choice of word makes that distinction without stating it, which is why the commentators dwell on the verb's origin in the language of well-digging.

## Verse 53:35

> Does he have the knowledge of the unseen so that he sees ˹the Hereafter˺

**The Knowledge the Qur'an Reserves**

The verse asks whether the man has knowledge of the unseen, and the question is pointed because the Qur'an reserves that knowledge as a divine attribute.

The unseen, *al-ghayb*, covers everything beyond human observation: the future, the outcome of a life, the state of hearts, and the arrangement of the world to come. The Qur'an states repeatedly that its keys are with God alone, and it presents the claim to have them as the mark of a false prophet or an arrogant man.

Applied here, the question asks whether this man has been given what God has not given him. The answer is obvious, and the obviousness is the point: the man's conduct presupposes a knowledge he cannot have.

**What His Conduct Presupposed**

The commentators read the verse as addressed to a specific calculation. The man had stopped giving, and the stopping implies that he knew his past deeds would suffice, or that his wealth would be preserved if he kept it.

Neither claim is available to him. Whether his past deeds will suffice is a matter for the judgment he has not faced; whether his wealth will be preserved is a matter for a future he cannot see. Both are questions about the unseen, and he acted as though he had answers to them.

**The Parallel Verses**

The same question is put in two other chapters, in nearly identical words. Or do they have access to the Record in the unseen, so they copy it for all to see? (52:41, and the same sentence at 68:47).

The image in those verses is of a register that can be consulted and transcribed. The question asks whether the deniers have been admitted to it, and the absurdity of the suggestion is what carries the argument.

The recurrence of the question across three sūrahs marks it as one of the Qur'an's standing responses to confident claims about the future. Where a person acts on a certainty he cannot have, the response is to ask where the certainty came from.

**Seeing as Knowing**

The verb at the end of the verse is the verb of sight: *fa-huwa yarā* — so that he sees. The commentators take it as knowledge presented in the form of vision, which is the Qur'an's common idiom for certainty.

The choice of the visual verb is deliberate. The man does not merely believe that his calculation is correct; he sees it, in the sense that he treats it as established fact. The verse questions the seeing rather than the conclusion, because a conclusion drawn from nothing visible cannot be seen at all.

The same idiom runs through the earlier part of this sūrah, where the Prophet's vision is described in detail and his sight is said not to have wandered. There the seeing was real and its limits were respected; here the seeing is claimed and has no object.

**The Keys of the Unseen**

The Qur'an states what the unseen contains and who holds it, and the statement leaves nothing with human beings.

With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him (6:59). The verse in Sūrah Luqmān adds the specifics: the knowledge of the Hour, the sending down of rain, what is in the wombs, what any soul will earn tomorrow, and the land in which it will die (31:34).

The list is instructive because every item on it is something people want to know and none of them is available. The man who stopped giving was acting on exactly this kind of knowledge — whether his wealth would be preserved, whether his past deeds would suffice — and the verses deny that anyone outside God has it.

The Prophet himself is told to say that he does not have the treasuries of God, nor does he know the unseen (6:50). If the messenger does not hold it, the man who withheld his giving certainly does not.

## Verse 53:36

> Or has he not been informed of what is in the Scripture of Moses

**The Scrolls of Moses**

The verse names a written revelation, and the name is specific. *Ṣuḥuf Mūsā*, the scrolls of Moses — not the Torah as a whole but the written sheets given to him, which the Qur'an mentions elsewhere as a distinct thing.

The reference is to a text the audience recognised. The Meccans did not have the scrolls, but they knew of Moses and of the revelation given to him, and the Qur'an's frequent appeals to earlier prophets assume that familiarity.

The verse's appeal is therefore to something outside the Qur'an. It does not ask the man to accept the Prophet's word; it asks whether he has been informed of what an earlier revelation already stated.

**Nabba'a: The Verb of Being Informed**

The verb used is not the ordinary word for knowing. *Lam yunabbaʾ* — has he not been informed? — comes from a root carrying the sense of a significant report, news of consequence delivered to someone who did not have it.

The word implies that the information was available and that the man had access to it. The question is not whether the principles exist but whether he has been told them, and the expectation is that he has.

That is the force of the interrogative form. It presumes the informing has happened and asks why the conduct does not reflect it, which is a sharper question than a bare statement of the principles would have been.

**What the Scrolls of Moses Were**

The Qur'an distinguishes between the scrolls given to Moses and the Torah as a whole, and the distinction is worth setting out.

The word *ṣuḥuf* denotes sheets or written pages, and the Qur'an uses it for revelations given in written form. Sūrah al-Aʿlā refers to the scrolls of Abraham and Moses as containing a shared teaching (87:19), which is the same pairing this verse makes.

The scrolls are therefore presented as an early written record, distinct in form from the law given later at Sinai. What the verse claims about them is not their extent but their content: they stated the principles that the following verses set out.

That claim is modest and therefore hard to dispute. It does not require the audience to accept the Qur'an's account of the scrolls in full, only to accept that the prophets before Muhammad taught individual responsibility — a teaching that was common ground with the scripture-bearing communities of Arabia.

## Verse 53:37

> and ˹that of˺ Abraham, who ˹perfectly˺ fulfilled ˹his covenant˺

**Ibrāhīm Alladhī Waffā**

The verse attaches an epithet to Abraham and leaves it unexplained: Abraham, who fulfilled. The verb *waffā* means to complete, to discharge in full, to leave nothing outstanding.

The Qur'an uses the same root elsewhere for Abraham, describing him as a model of excellence, devoted to God, perfectly upright, not a polytheist, utterly grateful for God's favours (16:120–121). The epithet here is shorter and more open, and the openness has produced several readings of what exactly he fulfilled.

What all the readings share is the idea of completion. Abraham is named not for what he believed but for what he carried through to the end.

**The Readings of What He Fulfilled**

The commentators offer three main answers, and each has its ground in the text.

The first is that he fulfilled the trials he was given, enduring what no one else was made to endure. The second is that he fulfilled the command to deliver God's message, holding nothing back. The third is that he fulfilled the covenant God made with him.

The readings differ in object but agree in substance.

**The Fire and the Sacrifice**

Two events are usually cited as the content of the fulfilment, and both are recorded elsewhere in the Qur'an.

The first is the fire. His people concluded that he should be burned to avenge their gods, if they had to act at all (21:68), and the answer was given in a single command: O fire! Be cool and safe for Abraham! (21:69). The second is the sacrifice. Abraham said, O my dear son! Do as you are commanded (37:102), and the outcome was the call: O Abraham! You have already fulfilled the vision (37:104–105).

The verb used there is the same verb as in this verse. The Qur'an itself describes Abraham as having fulfilled, in the account of the sacrifice, which supports the reading that the fulfilment concerns the trials he endured.

**Why Abraham Is Named Here**

Abraham held a particular position in the audience's self-understanding. The Quraysh traced their descent to him through Ishmael, regarded the Kaʿbah as his foundation, and performed a pilgrimage they believed he had instituted.

Naming him here is therefore an appeal to their own claimed ancestor. The sūrah asks whether they have been informed of what is in the scrolls of Moses and of Abraham, and the second name is the one they claimed as theirs.

The appeal has an edge. A people who claim descent from a man who fulfilled are being asked to consider what they themselves completed, and the answer, in the case of the man who stopped giving, is that he completed nothing.

**Fulfilment in the Qur'an's Moral Vocabulary**

The root of *waffā* carries a specific sense in the Qur'an: giving in full, holding to a commitment without shortfall.

It is used for God as well as for human beings. The Qur'an states that every soul will be repaid in full for what it earned, using the same root, and it describes the repayment as complete and undiminished. It is also used for the keeping of covenants and the giving of full measure in trade, where the shortfall is the specific wrong condemned.

Applied to a person, the word describes someone who does not underdeliver. That is the quality the verse attributes to Abraham, and it is the quality the man described in the preceding verses lacked: he gave a little and stopped.

The contrast between the two figures is therefore built into the vocabulary. One fulfilled; the other fell short of his own beginning, and the sūrah sets the two within a few verses of each other.

## Verse 53:38

> ˹They state˺ that no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another

This verse is the earliest statement in the order of revelation of a principle the Qur'an repeats many times: no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another.

The formulation is compact. *Wizr*, a burden, is the word used for a load carried, and by extension for a sin carried by the person who committed it.

**The Parallel Verses**

The same sentence appears in several chapters, and the recurrence marks it as a fixed formula of the revelation.

No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another appears in Sūrah al-Anʿām within a statement about lordship and return (6:164), and in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ immediately before the statement that God does not punish until a messenger has been sent (17:15). Sūrah Fāṭir adds the case of a person who calls for help with his load and receives none, even from a close relative (35:18).

The consistency of the wording across sūrahs revealed at different times indicates a settled doctrine rather than a passing remark. The Qur'an returns to it whenever the question of who is answerable for whom arises.

**What the Axiom Does Not Exclude**

The commentators are careful to note the limit of the principle. A person is not punished for another's sin, but he is answerable for the influence he exerted over it.

The Prophet's saying on the point is recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1017: whoever sets an evil precedent in Islam bears its burden and the burden of those who acted on it afterwards, without anything being deducted from their burden. The same report gives the positive case, for the one who sets a good precedent.

The distinction is precise. The burden is not transferred — the followers carry their own — but the one who established the practice carries the weight of having established it. Influence is not vicarious guilt, and the verse does not exempt anyone from the consequences of what he made easy for others.

**The Double Punishment of Those Who Lead Others Astray**

The Qur'an states the same point in another form, describing those who misled others as receiving a double share of the punishment, and recording the followers asking that the torment of their leaders be multiplied, and the answer that it has already been multiplied for all (7:38).

The request is refused in the sense that both receive their share; neither is exempted by the other's greater guilt. That is the axiom operating in a scene: the burden stays with its bearer, and the one who caused the bearing carries his own additional weight.

Sūrah al-Aḥzāb records the plea of those in the Fire: Our Lord! Give them double ˹our˺ punishment, and condemn them tremendously (33:68). The consistency across chapters shows that the Qur'an treats influence as a distinct category of responsibility, not as an exception to the axiom.

**Burden as a Legal Image**

The word *wizr* carries a legal and physical weight at once, and both senses operate in the verse.

In its original sense it denotes a load carried on the back, the kind of burden a person takes up and transports. Applied to conduct, it denotes the sin as something the person has taken on himself and now carries, with the implication that the carrying is his and cannot be handed over.

The image is what makes the denial vivid. A load can in principle be transferred — one person can take up what another has set down — and the verse says that in the matter of sin this transfer does not happen. The load stays with the one who took it up.

The same image underlies the verse in Sūrah Fāṭir describing a person weighed down by his load, calling for help with it, and receiving none, even from a close relative (35:18). The calling is the natural human response; the refusal is the axiom operating.

## Verse 53:39

> and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards

**The Continuing Effect of Deeds**

The commentators add a qualification that keeps the verse from being read too narrowly. A person has what he strove for, and the effects of his striving can outlive him.

The Prophet's saying on the point is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1631: when a man dies his acts come to an end, except three — a recurring charity, knowledge from which people benefit, and a righteous child who prays for him. The same report is transmitted by al-Tirmidhī, Abū Dāwūd, and al-Nasāʾī.

The three exceptions are not contradictions of the verse. Each of them is something the deceased strove for while alive: the charity he established, the knowledge he taught, the child he raised. The reward continues because the striving occurred, not because another's effort is credited to him.

**Saʿā: The Word for Striving**

The verb in this verse denotes effort exerted with movement toward an end, and the choice of word is precise.

*Saʿā* is not the general word for doing. It describes a person setting out and working toward something, and it is used in the Qur'an for walking with purpose to the Friday prayer and for the running between the two hills in the pilgrimage rites. In each case the word carries the sense of directed exertion rather than mere activity.

That sense matters for the measure the verse establishes. A person is credited with what he strove for, and striving implies direction: the effort was aimed at something. Effort expended without direction, or aimed at what does not last, is still striving, and the verse's terms apply to it accordingly.

The word also excludes accident. What comes to a person without his exertion is not something he strove for, and it does not enter the account on his side. The measure is the exertion, and nothing else.

**The Verse and the Audience's Claim to Merit**

The verse removes a ground the audience relied on, and the removal is as significant as the promise it contains.

The Quraysh regarded themselves as the custodians of the sanctuary and the descendants of Abraham, and the standing that came with those positions was treated as a form of credit. A person could be secure because of his lineage, his office, or his tribe's relation to the sacred house.

The verse reduces the account to the individual's striving. Lineage supplies nothing; office supplies nothing; the standing of one's people supplies nothing. What a person has is what he exerted himself for, and the exertion is his alone.

That is the same blow the earlier verses struck at the idolatrous system, applied now to social position. The system promised security through affiliation; the verse states that the reckoning recognises no affiliation, and the statement would have been heard as a direct challenge to the audience's own sense of standing.

## Verse 53:40

> and that ˹the outcome of˺ their endeavours will be seen ˹in their record˺

**The Deeds Will Be Seen**

The verse states that the striving will be seen, and the passive form of the verb leaves the seeing open: it will be seen, without saying by whom.

The commentators supply the answer from elsewhere in the Qur'an. The deeds will be seen by God, by the person who performed them, and by others on the day of judgment. The seeing is not a private review but a disclosure.

That openness is the point of the verse. The striving described in the previous verse was performed in this world, often in private and often without witness; the verse says that its visibility is deferred rather than absent.

**The Verse in Sūrah al-Tawbah**

The Qur'an states the same disclosure in an explicit form. Tell them, O Prophet: do as you will. Your deeds will be observed by Allah, His Messenger, and the believers. And you will be returned to the Knower of the seen and unseen, then He will inform you of what you used to do (9:105).

The verse names three witnesses and adds the return, and its structure matches the one here: the deeds, the seeing, the return, the informing.

The difference is one of context. The verse in al-Tawbah is addressed to people whose conduct was under suspicion, and it functions as a warning. The verse here is part of a statement of the moral order recorded in the earlier scriptures, and it functions as a description. The same fact serves both purposes.

**The Deeds Made Visible to Their Doer**

The disclosure described in the Qur'an includes the person's own confrontation with his record, and that confrontation is the sharpest part of it.

The Qur'an describes the book being placed and the wicked crying, Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted? (18:49). The horror in that verse is not the punishment but the completeness of the record: nothing omitted, nothing forgotten, nothing mitigated by the passage of time.

The verse here points at the same event with a single verb. The striving will be seen, and the first person to see it fully will be the one who performed it, in a form he cannot dispute because it is his own.

That is why the seeing precedes the repayment in the sequence. The verdict follows the evidence, and the evidence is not supplied by an accuser but by the person's own history, presented to him.

## Verse 53:41

> then they will be fully rewarded

**Al-Jazāʾ al-Awfā**

The verse is three words in Arabic: *thumma yujzāhu al-jazāʾ al-awfā* — then it will be repaid with the fullest repayment.

*Awfā* is the superlative of a root meaning to complete and to give in full. It is the same root as the verb used of Abraham in the verse a few lines earlier, where he is described as the one who fulfilled. The word denotes a payment from which nothing has been held back.

The superlative form makes the statement stronger than a promise of fair treatment. The repayment is not only adequate; it is the fullest there is.

The word also carries a temporal sense. A full payment is one that is not deferred in part or paid in instalments, and the Qur'an's descriptions of that day exclude both. The account is settled once, in full, and nothing is carried over into a further period in which the person waits for the remainder.

**Repayment Without Deduction**

The word carries a specific implication about the reckoning. A full payment is one from which nothing has been subtracted, and the Qur'an's descriptions of judgment consistently emphasise the absence of deduction.

No person is wronged by the reduction of his reward, and no good deed is lost through the passage of time or the obscurity of its performance. The verse in Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ describes the setting up of the scales of justice, and states that no soul will be wronged in the least, and that even a deed the weight of a mustard seed will be brought forth (21:47).

That assurance is what the superlative form delivers here. The person who strove in secret, without witness and without reward in this world, receives the complete measure, and the completeness is the answer to the anonymity of his striving.

**Jazāʾ: The Word for Repayment**

The word used for the repayment is *jazāʾ*, and it carries the sense of a return given in correspondence to an act.

The root denotes requital, and the Qur'an uses it for both the reward of good and the recompense of evil. The word itself is neutral; what determines its sense is what is being repaid and how.

That neutrality is why the verse needs the superlative. A repayment could be partial, could be reduced, or could fall short of the deed; the addition of *al-awfā* excludes all three. The repayment will be the fullest form of itself, and the fullness is the assurance the verse gives.

The choice of a requital word rather than a gift word is deliberate. The verse is not describing a favour granted irrespective of conduct; it is describing a payment owed on the basis of striving, and the payment is described as complete. The earned character of the reward is preserved alongside its generosity.

## Verse 53:42

> and that to your Lord ˹alone˺ is the ultimate return ˹of all things˺

**Al-Muntahā: The Ultimate End**

The verse names the destination with a single word: *al-muntahā*, the furthest point, the place where movement stops. To your Lord is the ultimate end.

The word is formed from a root meaning to reach an end or to refrain, and as a noun of place it denotes the terminus of a journey. Everything that moves has a point at which it can go no further, and the verse identifies that point.

The identification is exclusive. The end is not to a tribe, a lineage, an intercessor, or a deity of one's own choosing. It is to the Lord, and the exclusivity is what the verse is asserting.

**The Same Word Used Twice in One Sūrah**

The word *al-muntahā* appears earlier in this sūrah, in the name of the Lote-Tree of the utmost limit beside which the Prophet's second vision occurred. The same term, the same form, applied first to a place in the created order and then to the destination of all things.

The repetition is unlikely to be accidental. The tree marks the limit of the created universe, the point beyond which nothing passes; the verse here marks the limit of every life, the point beyond which no one goes. The two uses of the word frame the sūrah's account of the unseen with the same idea of a boundary.

The first boundary was reached by one man on one occasion. The second will be reached by everyone, and the verse states it as the conclusion of the moral order given in the preceding verses.

**Ends That Differ**

The verse says that the end is to the Lord, and the commentators add that the ends of different groups are not the same.

The Qur'an states the difference plainly: The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that under it rivers flow; eternal is its fruit as well as its shade. That is the ˹ultimate˺ outcome for the righteous. But the outcome for the disbelievers is the Fire! (13:35).

The verse in Sūrah ʿAbasa describes the faces of that day: On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, laughing and rejoicing (80:38–39). The return is common to all; what follows the return is not.

That distinction preserves both halves of the statement. Everyone arrives at the same destination, and the destination is where the difference between them becomes final.

**The Return in the Qur'an's Vocabulary**

The Qur'an uses a small family of words for the return, and each carries a slightly different sense.

*Rujʿā* and *marjiʿ* denote the place or fact of returning; *maṣīr* denotes the destination arrived at; *munqalab* describes the turning back from one state to another. The verse here uses *al-muntahā*, the furthest point, which is the strongest of the set because it excludes any further movement.

The choice is significant. A return can be temporary — a person returns and then sets out again — and a destination can be a stopping place on the way to somewhere else. The furthest point is neither. It is where the journey ends, and the verse says that the ending is to the Lord.

That is the assurance and the warning in one word. Nothing lies beyond the destination, so nothing can be appealed to after it, and no further refuge exists for the one who arrives there unprepared.

## Verse 53:43

> Moreover, He is the One Who brings about joy and sadness

**Laughter and Weeping as Created Things**

The catalogue opens with the two states that seem most private and most one's own: laughing and weeping. The verse assigns both to God.

The assignment is not that God forces a person to laugh or to cry in a given moment. It is that the capacity for both, the occasions that produce them, and the outcomes that settle into one or the other are all within His decree.

The choice of these two as the catalogue's opening item is deliberate. They are the most immediate expressions of a human being's condition, and they are the ones a person would least think to attribute to anyone but himself.

**Three Readings of the Verse**

The commentators give three readings, and each locates the laughter and the weeping differently.

The first is that God creates the causes of both in this world: He brings joy and grief, and both come from Him. The second is that He makes the believers laugh in the Garden and the disbelievers weep in the Fire, which places the two states in the hereafter.

The third combines them: He causes the believers to weep in this world and to laugh in the next, while the disbelievers laugh here and weep there. The third reading is the one that gives the verse its edge, because it reverses the appearance of things — the weeping of this life is not a verdict, and the laughter of this life is not a certificate.

**The Faces of That Day**

The Qur'an describes the outcome of the reversal in concrete terms. On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, laughing and rejoicing (80:38–39), while ˹other˺ faces will be dusty, cast in gloom (80:40–41).

The description uses the same two states as this verse. The laughing belongs to one group and the weeping to the other, and the assignment is final.

The verse in Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn adds the detail that the believers will laugh at what the deniers used to laugh at, and that the reversal will be visible to both sides. The laughter of this world is thus not merely transient but reversible, and the verse here states the reversal's ground: both states are God's to give.

**Human Feeling and Divine Decree**

The verse raises a question about responsibility that the Qur'an answers elsewhere. If joy and grief are from God, what remains of human choice?

The answer the Qur'an gives is that the decree concerns the occurrence of things, not the justification of the responses to them. A person is not blamed for feeling grief; he is answerable for what he does with it — whether it drives him to patience or to complaint, to reliance or to despair.

The verse in Sūrah al-Ḥadīd states the principle: No calamity ˹or blessing˺ occurs on earth or in yourselves without being ˹written˺ in a Record before We bring it into being (57:22), and the purpose given is so that you neither grieve over what you have missed nor boast over what He has granted you (57:23). The decree is stated as the ground of composure rather than as its removal.

**Laughter and Weeping in the Qur'an's Scenes**

The Qur'an uses these two states in its descriptions of the hereafter, and the usage gives the verse its Last-Day edge.

The believers are described as laughing and rejoicing on that day, with bright faces; others are described as covered with dust and overcome by darkness.

The scenes show that the two states are not only psychological events but outcomes. The laughter of this world can be the laughter of contempt, and it can end; the weeping of this world can be the weeping of the oppressed, and it can end as well.

The verse here states the ground of all of it. Both states are God's to bring about, and the distribution between the two worlds is His arrangement rather than a continuation of this one.

## Verse 53:44

> And He is the One Who gives life and causes death

**The Order of the Two Words**

The Arabic places death before life: *amāta wa aḥyā*, He caused death and He gave life. The translation reverses the order to match the sequence of a life, and the reversal is worth noting because the original order carries a point.

Death first, then life. The order matches the Qur'an's argument elsewhere, where the fact of death is used to establish the possibility of a life that follows it. A God who brings the living from the dead can bring the dead to life again, and the sequence in the Arabic states the two acts in the order the argument requires.

The same pairing appears throughout the Qur'an — in the challenge of Abraham to the king who claimed the power of life and death (2:258), in the statement that it is Allah who gives life and causes death (3:156), and in the statement that to Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth and He gives life and causes death (57:2).

**The Argument From Life Against Denial**

The verse functions as evidence for the resurrection, which is the claim the sūrah's audience denied.

The argument is straightforward. A person who has been brought into existence from nonexistence has no ground for denying that he can be brought back. The first bringing was not something he contributed to or witnessed; the second requires no greater power than the first.

The Qur'an makes the argument explicitly elsewhere, asking whether the One who created the heavens and the earth cannot easily resurrect the deniers, and answering that He is the Master Creator, All-Knowing (36:81). The verse here states the premise without drawing the conclusion, because the conclusion was already drawn in the verses that preceded it.

**The Formula Across the Qur'an**

The pairing of life and death is one of the most frequently repeated statements in the Qur'an, and the repetition marks it as a foundational claim.

It appears in Abraham's answer to the king who boasted of the power to give life and to kill (2:258), where the challenge is to bring the sun from the west.

The consistency of the formula across Meccan and Madinan chapters shows that it was not an argument devised for a particular dispute but a settled description of God's action. Wherever the Qur'an needs to establish power over existence, this is the pair it names.

The verse here uses the formula in its shortest form, within a catalogue of such acts, and the brevity indicates that the audience did not dispute it. The disagreement was about the consequences of the pairing, not the pairing itself.

**Death as an Act, Not an Accident**

The verse's verb makes death an act performed by an agent, and that framing excludes several alternative descriptions.

Death is not presented as a natural process reaching its end, nor as the outcome of an enemy's success, nor as a random event. It is caused, and the causing belongs to one party.

The verse in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān addresses those who said of their fallen kin that they would not have been killed had they stayed at home, and the Qur'an states elsewhere that no soul can ever die without Allah's Will at the destined time (3:145).

The reading is not a rejection of the literal sense but an extension of it, and it has support in the Qur'an's own usage, where the one who was dead and was given life and light is contrasted with the one in darkness (6:122).

## Verse 53:45

> And He created the pairs—males and females—

The verse states the creation of the pair, using the dual form *az-zawjayn*, the two mates, and then specifies them: the male and the female.

The dual is significant.

The word *zawj* is used in the Qur'an for a spouse, for a mate among animals, and for one of any two corresponding things.

**Created From a Single Soul**

The Qur'an states the origin of the pair in terms that this verse assumes. He created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate (4:1; 7:189). The two sexes are not two separate creations but one creation extended.

That origin has a consequence for the argument of the sūrah. If the pair comes from a single source, then neither member of it is derived from the other in rank, and the arrangement the audience had made — assigning the female to God and keeping the male for themselves — has no basis in the order of creation.

The verse therefore does real work in the sūrah's case against the idolatrous system, and it does it without argument. The fact of a common origin is stated, and the ranking built on the difference collapses.

**The Pair as a Sign**

The Qur'an treats the pairing of the sexes as one of its signs, and the treatment is consistent across chapters.

He created spouses from among yourselves so that you may find comfort in them, and He placed between you compassion and mercy, and in that are signs for people who reflect (30:21). The pairing is presented not as a biological fact but as evidence of design, since a pairing that produces rest and affection is not the product of chance.

The verse here states the fact in its shortest form. No purpose is given and no description added, because the fact is already familiar to the audience and its status as a sign has been established elsewhere.

**Creation as Prerogative**

The verse's verb is the verb of creation, and the attribution is exclusive. He created the pair, and no one else is named as contributing.

The exclusivity matters for the sūrah's argument about the deities. A being that creates has a claim to worship; a being that does not create has none. The Qur'an makes the point directly, asking whether the one who creates is like the one who does not create (16:17).

The verse supplies the premise of that question in a single clause. The creation of the pair — the most basic fact of human existence, repeated in every generation — belongs to God alone, and the three stones named earlier in the sūrah created nothing.

**Pairing Beyond the Sexes**

The Qur'an extends the idea of the pair well beyond the human sexes, and the extension strengthens the claim made in this verse.

The word is used for the pairing of night and day, of the sweet and the bitter in what the earth produces, and of the kinds of plants whose pairs are known and unknown. Sūrah Yā Sīn states that Glory be to the One Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs —˹be it˺ what the earth produces, their genders, or what they do not know! (36:36).

The extension makes the pairing a feature of creation rather than a peculiarity of human beings. Wherever there is a pair, there is a maker of the pair, and the maker is one.

Applied here, the point is that the sexes are not an independent arrangement that arose and persisted on its own. They belong to a pattern that runs through the created order, and the pattern is the evidence for the single source the verse names.

## Verse 53:46

> from a sperm-drop when it is emitted

**Nutfah: The Word and Its Meaning**

The verse names the origin of the pair with a single word: *nutfah*, a drop, a small quantity of fluid. The word is used in Arabic for a mere amount, and its application here is deliberately unimpressive.

The addition of the participle — when it is emitted — fixes the moment. The verse is not describing a substance in the abstract but an event, and the event is the beginning of a human being.

The choice of so small a thing as the origin of the two sexes is the verse's method. It does not describe the complexity of what follows; it names the least of it, and leaves the distance between the beginning and the result to be measured by the reader.

**The Stages Described Elsewhere**

The Qur'an gives the sequence in several places, and the descriptions agree.

Sūrah al-Ḥajj sets it out at length: We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh —fully formed or unformed— (22:5). Sūrah al-Qiyāmah asks whether people were not once a sperm-drop emitted, then a clinging clot, then developed and perfected in form, producing from it both sexes (75:37–39).

The verse in Sūrah al-Ṭāriq adds the detail of the fluid's origin: ˹They were˺ created from a spurting fluid, stemming from between the backbone and the ribcage (86:6–7).

The consistency across chapters shows that the description was a settled part of the revelation's argument, repeated because it was the argument's strongest evidence.

**The Argument From the Origin**

The verse belongs to an argument the Qur'an presses repeatedly against the denial of resurrection, and the argument runs from the origin to the return.

Sūrah al-Qiyāmah draws the conclusion explicitly, Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life? (75:40). The reasoning is that the one who produced a formed human being from an emitted drop has demonstrated the capacity, and the second production requires nothing that the first did not already require.

The verse here supplies the premise without the conclusion, as the neighbouring verses do. The catalogue states what God does, and the inference is left to the reader — though the verse that follows makes it explicit by naming the re-creation.

**The Qur'an's Use of Embryonic Language**

The vocabulary of the stages appears repeatedly, and the repetition gives it a technical character within the revelation.

*Nuṭfah* is the drop; *ʿalaqah* is the clinging thing, described in the translations as a clot; *muḍghah* is the lump of flesh. Sūrah al-Muʾminūn sets out the sequence in order and adds the forming of bones and their clothing with flesh (23:14). Sūrah al-Ḥajj adds the qualification that the lump is sometimes formed and sometimes not (22:5).

The consistency of the terms across chapters indicates a settled description rather than an improvised one. The Qur'an returns to the sequence because it is the evidence it relies on most often for the claim that the human being is created rather than self-made.

The verse here names only the first stage, and the naming is enough. The audience knew the rest of the sequence from the other chapters in which it had already been recited to them.

**The Smallness That Carries the Argument**

The verse's force lies in the disproportion it presents. The smallest of beginnings produces the whole of a human being — the two sexes, the faculties, the life, the deeds, the reckoning described in the verses before this catalogue.

The Qur'an returns to that disproportion because it is the hardest thing in its argument to refuse. A person can dispute a report about the unseen; he cannot dispute the fact of his own origin, which he can verify in his own existence.

## Verse 53:47

> And it is upon Him to bring about re-creation

**An-Nashʾah al-Ukhrā: The Second Genesis**

The verse names a second bringing-into-being. *An-nashʾah al-ukhrā*, the other genesis, the second origination, and it assigns it to God with the preposition of obligation: it is upon Him.

The word *nashʾah* comes from a root meaning to bring something into existence and to raise it up. The Qur'an uses it for the first origination as well, and the addition of the other marks this one as distinct from the creation already described in the preceding verses.

**It Is Upon Him**

The preposition deserves attention. The phrase *wa anna ʿalayhi* — and that upon Him — uses the construction that denotes what someone has taken upon himself, not what is imposed on him by another.

The same construction appears elsewhere in the Qur'an: It is certainly upon Us ˹alone˺ to show ˹the way to˺ guidance (92:12), and It is upon Allah ˹alone˺ to ˹clearly˺ show the Straight Way (16:9). In each case the obligation is self-assumed, a commitment God has made rather than a duty owed to Him.

Read that way, the verse states that the re-creation is something God has committed Himself to. The promise is not contingent on a human claim or a bargain; it belongs to what He has undertaken, and the undertaking is the ground of its certainty.

**The Majority Reading: Resurrection**

Most commentators take the second genesis as the resurrection — the bringing back of the dead at the end of the world.

The reading follows directly from the context. The preceding verses describe the origin of a human being in an emitted drop, and the Qur'an's standard argument runs from that origin to the possibility of a return. The verse in Sūrah al-Qiyāmah draws the inference in the form of a question: is such a Creator unable to bring the dead back to life? (75:40).

On this reading the verse is the conclusion of the argument the catalogue has been building. The God who creates from a drop, who gives life and causes death, and who enriches and impoverishes is the same God who will create again.

**Al-Rāzī's Reading**

One commentator reads the second genesis differently, taking it as the breathing of the spirit into the human being rather than the resurrection.

The reasoning is that what distinguishes a human being from other animals is not the body but the spirit, so that the second origination — the one that makes him what he is — is the moment the spirit is breathed in. The Qur'an describes that moment in the account of Adam's creation, where God describes fashioning Adam and having a spirit of His own creation breathed into him (15:29).

The reading is coherent and it has textual support, but it fits the immediate context less well than the resurrection reading. The surrounding verses are about origins and ends, and the argument the passage is making concerns the return rather than the formation. The majority position is the stronger of the two on contextual grounds.

**The First Creation as the Evidence**

The Qur'an's argument for the re-creation rests consistently on the first creation, and this verse is the conclusion of that argument.

The reasoning is set out in several chapters. Sūrah Yā Sīn records the man who asked who could give life to bones that have crumbled, and the answer that they will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has perfect knowledge of every created being (36:78–79). Sūrah al-Rūm states that He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him (30:27).

The argument does not depend on the audience accepting the Qur'an's authority. It depends on a fact they could not dispute — that they exist, and that they did not make themselves — and it draws the only conclusion the fact supports.

The verse here states that conclusion in the catalogue's compressed form. The re-creation is upon Him, and the ground is the origination already named in the verses before it.

## Verse 53:48

> And He is the One Who enriches and impoverishes

**Aghnā wa Aqnā: The Two Verbs**

The verse pairs two verbs, and the second of them has been read in two ways that differ in meaning.

The first verb is clear: *aghnā*, He enriched, He made someone free of want. The second, *aqnā*, is read either as He granted possessions — giving a person property and means — or as He made content, giving a person sufficiency of heart.

The two readings produce different pairings. On the first, the verse describes two kinds of giving: wealth and property. On the second, it describes a contrast: He enriches some and leaves others with little, and the pairing is between the enriched and the impoverished.

The second reading fits the context better, since the verses before and after describe pairs of opposites — life and death, laughter and weeping. The translation follows it.

**Wealth as Given**

The verse's central claim is that both conditions come from the same source. Richness is not the achievement of the rich man alone, and poverty is not the failure of the poor man alone.

The Qur'an makes the same point repeatedly, stating that Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills (13:26). The restriction is not presented as a punishment or the expansion as a reward; both are described as distributions made by the One who owns what is distributed.

That is the ground of the verse's placement in this catalogue. The sūrah has established that everything belongs to God, and the distribution of wealth is one instance of that ownership in operation.

**The Test in Both Directions**

Both conditions are tests, and the Qur'an treats them as such rather than as verdicts.

The enriched are tested by the wealth: whether they give from it, whether they attribute it to themselves, whether they suppose it will last. The impoverished are tested by the want: whether they remain patient, whether they complain against the distribution, whether they keep the obligations that do not require money.

The verse in Sūrah al-Fajr describes the two errors directly: as for man, when his Lord tests him and honours him and favours him, he says, my Lord has honoured me; and when He tests him and restricts his provision, he says, my Lord has humiliated me (89:15–16). Both statements are corrected by the verses that follow, and the correction is that neither condition is the verdict the person takes it for.

## Verse 53:49

> And He alone is the Lord of Sirius

**Ash-Shiʿrā: The Star Named**

The verse names one star, and the naming is specific: *ash-Shiʿrā*, the star known in English as Sirius.

The identification is not in doubt. Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky, the chief star of the constellation Canis Major, and it is visible from Arabia for much of the year. Its brightness made it a marker for the seasons and a point of orientation for travellers.

The Qur'an names very few individual stars, and the naming of this one is not incidental. A star is named because something was being done with it.

**The Star and the Worship It Received**

The pre-Islamic Arabs venerated Sirius, and the veneration is recorded in the exegetical tradition as the reason this verse names it.

Some of the tribes regarded the star as a power in its own right, associated with the rising seasons and with the fortunes of those born under it. Al-Qurṭubī states in his commentary on this verse that the first person to worship Sirius was a man called Abū Kabshah, described in the reports as a noble of Khuzāʿah whose tribe followed him in the veneration, and that the Quraysh therefore used the name as a term of abuse for the Prophet, calling him the son of Abū Kabshah because he had opposed their idols as Abū Kabshah had opposed theirs.

The explanation of the nickname is recorded in the classical works on names and genealogy, including Ibn al-Athīr's al-Nihāyah and al-Dāraquṭnī's al-Muʾtalif wa-l-Mukhtalif. The identification of Abū Kabshah as a maternal ancestor is a report rather than settled genealogy, and it is cited here as such. What is not in doubt is that the verse names a star that was venerated, and that the naming denies the veneration.

**The Return to the Idol Passage**

The verse closes a circle that the sūrah opened many verses earlier. The examination of al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt established that the deities had names without authority and functions without power.

This verse extends the examination to the heavens. The stones were on the ground and could be seen; the star is above and cannot be reached, and the temptation to ascribe power to it is correspondingly greater. The verse meets the temptation by stating the star's Lord.

The commentators note this return explicitly: the verse comes back to the theme of the earlier passage, which was that God is the Lord of everything to which the pagans ascribe independent power. The list of such things included stones, angels, and stars, and the catalogue names all three.

## Verse 53:50

> And He destroyed the first ˹people of˺ ’Ȃd

**ʿĀd al-Ūlā: The Former**

The verse names a destroyed people and adds a qualification: ʿĀd, the first. The qualification implies that there was a second, and the commentators supply the distinction.

ʿĀd the former is identified with Iram, the tribe descended from a grandson of Noah of the same name, while the later ʿĀd was referred to simply as ʿĀd. The distinction matters for reading the Qur'an's references, since the tribe appears in several chapters and the descriptions vary in detail.

Sūrah al-Fajr names the first of them directly: Did you not see how your Lord dealt with 'Ȃd—the people of Iram—with their great stature, unmatched in any other land? (89:6–8). The description of stature and of columns is what distinguishes the earlier tribe in the accounts.

**The Prophet Sent to Them**

The Qur'an's account of ʿĀd is attached to a prophet named Hūd, and the account is given at length in several chapters.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf records the message and the refusal (7:65–72); Sūrah Hūd gives the exchange in fuller form, including the people's challenge and Hūd's answer (11:50–60); Sūrah al-Qamar describes the destruction in detail (54:18–21). Sūrah Fuṣṣilat adds the people's own boast, boasting, Who is superior to us in might? (41:15).

The consistency of the account across chapters indicates a single tradition with a fixed shape: a prophet sent, a boast of strength, a refusal, and a destruction that came through the very element the people trusted.

**The Destruction and Its Means**

The Qur'an describes the destruction of ʿĀd as a wind, and the description is unusually specific for an account of judgment.

Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah describes a furious, bitter wind (69:6) unleashed on them non-stop for seven nights and eight days, so that its people were seen lying dead like trunks of uprooted palm trees (69:7). Sūrah al-Aḥqāf records them saying, This is a cloud bringing us rain, and the reply that it is what they sought to hasten: a ˹fierce˺ wind carrying a painful punishment! (46:24).

The detail serves the argument. The destruction is not described as a vague catastrophe but as an event with a duration, a means, and a mistaken interpretation by the victims, and the specificity is what makes it usable as a warning.

**The Boast of Strength and Its Answer**

The Qur'an records the boast of ʿĀd, and the boast is the key to their destruction.

Sūrah Fuṣṣilat reports their question: who is greater than us in strength? (41:15). The people had built greatly, possessed physical power, and regarded themselves as beyond the reach of consequence. Their prophet answered by reminding them that the One who created them was greater than they, and the answer was rejected.

Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah describes the wind that levelled them, and the description of their bodies as hollow palm trunks is a deliberate reversal: the people who boasted of strength are described as empty husks.

**The Ruins as Evidence**

The Qur'an appeals to the remains of the destroyed peoples as evidence available to its audience, and the appeal is made in the language of travel.

The Quraysh were traders whose caravans passed through the country where these peoples had lived, and the Qur'an tells them that they pass by their ruins day and night (37:137–138). Sūrah al-Ḥijr names them as the residents of the Stone Valley, who were given God's signs and turned away from them (15:80–81).

The appeal converts the accounts from stories into observations. A story can be dismissed as an invention; a ruin passed on a familiar road cannot. The verse's naming of ʿĀd therefore points at something the audience had seen or could see.

That is the strongest form the warning takes. It does not ask for belief in a report but attention to a landscape, and the landscape carries the report in it.

## Verse 53:51

> and ˹then˺ Thamûd, sparing no one

**Thamūd: The Second Tribe**

The verse names the second of the two destroyed peoples, and the naming follows immediately on the first without a verb of its own. The destruction stated for ʿĀd is carried over to Thamūd, and the addition is the phrase that closes the verse.

Thamūd was an Arabian tribe dwelling in the region of al-Ḥijr, in the rocky country between the Ḥijāz and Syria, and their dwellings were cut into the rock. The Qur'an refers to their carving houses from the mountains as one of their achievements (26:149), and the ruins were known to the traders who passed through.

The tribe is mentioned in many chapters, which indicates that its story was among the most frequently used in the Meccan preaching.

**Ṣāliḥ and the Accounts**

The Qur'an's account of Thamūd is attached to a prophet named Ṣāliḥ, and the account has a fixed shape across the chapters in which it appears.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf records the message and the demand for a sign, and the sign given was a she-camel with a specified share of the water (7:73). Sūrah ash-Shuʿarāʾ gives the exchange at length, including the people's accusation that Ṣāliḥ was bewitched (26:141–158). Sūrah al-Qamar describes the hamstrung camel and the destruction that followed (54:23–31). Sūrah ash-Shams condenses the whole account into a few verses, ending with the statement that their Lord crushed them for their crime, levelling all to the ground, and that He has no fear of consequences (91:14–15).

The consistency across chapters shows that the account was a settled part of the preaching, told repeatedly because its elements were known to the audience.

**The She-Camel and the Sign**

The account of Thamūd turns on a sign, and the sign is what makes their destruction a case of refusal rather than of ignorance.

The people demanded a proof from Ṣāliḥ, and the proof was given in the form of a she-camel, with a specified arrangement for the water: one day for her and one day for them. The Qur'an records the command not to harm her, and it records the harm — they roused a companion of theirs, so he dared to kill her (54:29).

The sign matters for the argument of this verse. A people destroyed after receiving a visible sign cannot be described as having been uninformed, and the destruction that followed the killing was a response to a deliberate act rather than to a misunderstanding.

The verse states the outcome without the story, because the story was known. What it adds is the completeness: no one was spared. The sign was given to all of them, and the consequence reached all of them.

**The Houses Cut in the Rock**

The Qur'an mentions the dwellings of Thamūd as one of their achievements, and the mention is part of the argument.

Sūrah ash-Shuʿarāʾ records their boast that they carved houses from the mountains, and the boast is answered by the destruction that left the houses standing and the people gone (26:149). The dwellings outlasted their builders, and the outlasting is the point.

The detail also makes the ruins identifiable. A people who cut their houses into rock leave remains that endure, and the remains were on the trade route between the Ḥijāz and Syria, where the caravans of the audience's own people passed.

That is why the verse can state the destruction in a clause and move on. The evidence was not in the recitation but in the stone, and the audience had access to it. The completeness of the destruction — sparing no one — is confirmed by the empty houses the listeners could visit.

## Verse 53:52

> And before ˹that He destroyed˺ the people of Noah, who were truly far worse in wrongdoing and transgression

**Aẓlam and Aṭghā: Two Superlatives**

The verse describes Noah's people with two superlative adjectives, and the two are not the same word.

*Aẓlam*, more wrongdoing, comes from the root of ẓulm, which denotes placing a thing where it does not belong, and by extension injustice and wrongdoing. *Aṭghā*, more rebellious, comes from the root of ṭughyān, which denotes exceeding a limit, and it is the word used for Pharaoh and for those who overstep.

The pairing distinguishes two kinds of failure. Wrongdoing concerns what a person does to others and to what is owed; rebellion concerns the refusal of a limit. Noah's people are described as extreme in both, and the combination is what makes them the standard case in the Qur'an's account of destroyed nations.

**Why They Were the Worst**

The commentators give two reasons for the superlatives, and the two correspond to the two words.

They were the most wrongdoing because they established practices that others then imitated, particularly the worship of idols, so that their offence continued in the conduct of those who came after them. They were the most rebellious because the great majority of them refused the message, and refused it over a period the Qur'an describes as nine hundred and fifty years.

Both reasons concern scale rather than intensity. A single generation's sin ends with that generation; a practice established and inherited does not. The verse's superlatives measure the reach of the offence as well as its gravity, and on that measure the people of Noah stand first.

**The Accounts Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The story is told at length in several chapters, and the accounts agree on its essentials.

Sūrah Hūd gives the fullest version, beginning with Noah's statement that he has been sent with a clear warning, that they should worship none but Allah, and that he fears for them the torment of a painful day (11:25–26). Sūrah al-Muʾminūn records the people's rejection on the grounds that he was only a man like them (23:24). Sūrah ash-Shuʿarāʾ opens with the statement that the people of Noah rejected the messengers (26:105). Sūrah Nūḥ is given entirely to his preaching and their refusal.

The repetition across chapters indicates that the account served a fixed function: it was the first and clearest instance of the pattern the Qur'an sets out, in which a messenger is sent, refused, and answered by a destruction that vindicates him.

**The Argument From Precedent**

The verse's function in the sūrah is to establish that the pattern is not new, and the establishment is an argument.

An audience facing a warning can respond that the warning has no precedent, that things have always continued as they are, and that no generation has ever been cut off for what it believed. The verse removes that response by naming the oldest case, and the case is not presented as a rumour but as a matter the audience's own traditions acknowledged.

The Qur'an's use of destroyed peoples follows this logic throughout. It is not telling stories for their own sake; it is supplying precedents, and the precedents are cumulative. Three destroyed peoples in a single passage are harder to dismiss as coincidence than one.

**The Duration and Its Significance**

The Qur'an states the length of Noah's preaching among his people, and the figure is unusual in the revelation: he remained among them a thousand years less fifty (29:14).

The figure serves the argument of this verse. A refusal sustained over that period is not a misunderstanding that could have been cleared up with better explanation, and a messenger who persisted over that period is not one whose message could be dismissed as unclear.

The duration also answers an objection the audience might make — that they had not been given time. The first community was given the longest period recorded anywhere in the Qur'an, and the outcome was the destruction this verse names.

## Verse 53:53

> And ˹it was˺ He ˹Who˺ turned the cities ˹of Sodom and Gomorrah˺ upside down

**Al-Muʾtafikāt: The Overturned Cities**

The verse names its subject with a participle used as a name: *al-muʾtafikāt*, the overturned ones, the cities that were turned over. The word is formed from a root meaning to invert or to overturn, and in the Qur'an it functions as a designation rather than a description.

The designation is unusual. The cities are not named, and they are not described by location or by their people; they are named by what happened to them. The event became their identity, and the Qur'an uses it as their title.

That naming has an effect on the reader. A city is normally identified by its inhabitants, its position, or its history; this one is identified by its destruction, which tells the reader what the Qur'an considers the significant fact about it.

**The Cities of the People of Lot**

Most commentators identify the cities with those of the people of Lot, and the identification is supported by the Qur'an's own account.

Sūrah Hūd describes the event: when Our command came, We turned the cities upside down and rained down on them clustered stones of baked clay, marked by your Lord (11:82–83). Sūrah al-Ḥijr gives the same account in shorter form (15:74). The cities are associated with Lot's people throughout, and the destruction follows their refusal and their conduct.

The verse here does not name Lot or his people. It states the overturning, and leaves the identification to the accounts the audience already knew — which is consistent with the sūrah's compressed treatment of the whole list.

**The Act Attributed to God**

The verse's grammar puts God as the subject of the overturning. He turned the cities over; the act is His, and the verse does not describe it through intermediate causes.

That attribution is a feature of the Qur'an's descriptions of the destroyed peoples throughout. The wind that destroyed ʿĀd, the blast that took Thamūd, the stones that fell on the cities of Lot — each is presented as an act rather than a catastrophe, and the difference matters for what the events mean.

A catastrophe is an event in the world; an act is a statement. The verse's attribution makes the destruction a statement about the conduct that preceded it, and that is how the sūrah uses it in the argument that follows.

**The Inversion as a Sign**

The specific form of the destruction — the turning over of cities — is not incidental, and the Qur'an draws attention to it elsewhere.

The inversion reverses the most basic fact about a settlement: that it stands on the ground and its roofs are above. A city turned upside down is a settlement unmade, and the unmaking is total in a way that burning or flooding would not be.

Sūrah al-Ḥijr adds the detail of the stones of baked clay rained down afterwards, so that the account combines inversion from below and bombardment from above. The two together describe a destruction with no surviving structure and no surviving population, which is why the verse that follows can speak of what covered them without qualification.

**The Stones Rained Down Afterwards**

The overturning was not the whole of the event, and the parallel accounts add a second element that this verse leaves out.

Sūrah al-Ḥijr gives the same two elements in a single verse (15:74).

The addition of the marking is significant. Stones described as marked are not debris from a natural upheaval; they are presented as designated, and the designation is attributed to God. The detail answers the reading that would take the destruction as a geological event that happened to occur at the right moment.

The verse here does not repeat the detail, because the list it belongs to is compressed and the detail is available in the chapters that tell the story. What the verse supplies is the agent and the act, and the act is the overturning.

## Verse 53:54

> How overwhelming was what covered ˹them˺

**The Same Construction Used Twice in One Sūrah**

The verse uses a construction that appeared much earlier in this sūrah, and the repetition is one of the most striking features of the chapter.

In the account of the vision, the Lote-Tree was described as covered by that which covered it, with the covering left unnamed. Here the punishment of the cities is described in the same form: there covered them that which covered.

The identical construction applied to two opposite things — the splendour at the boundary of creation and the destruction of a condemned people — is not accidental. It marks both events as beyond description, and the inability to describe them is the same inability in both cases, though the reason differs.

**What Covered Them**

The Qur'an supplies elsewhere what this verse withholds, and the supply is specific.

Sūrah Hūd describes the stones of baked clay, clustered, rained down on the cities after they were overturned, and adds that they were marked by your Lord (11:82–83). Sūrah al-Ḥijr gives the same two elements: the inversion and the stones of baked clay (15:74).

So the covering is not a vague catastrophe. It is described in the parallel accounts with a material, a form, and a source. The verse here declines to repeat the description, not because it is unknown but because the description is available elsewhere and its repetition would serve no purpose in a list.

**Wonder and Terror in the Same Words**

The two uses of the construction produce opposite reactions, and the contrast is worth setting out.

The covering of the Lote-Tree produced an account that withheld its content out of reverence: something was there that language would diminish. The covering of the cities produced an account that withheld its content out of horror: something happened whose scale the language of the verse could not contain.

The commentators note the parallel explicitly, observing that the phrase conveys indescribable wonder in the earlier verses and indescribable terror here. The same words carry both, and the carrying is possible because in both cases the speaker is describing something he did not witness and cannot fully convey.

**Why the Description Is Withheld**

The withholding has an argumentative function that is easy to miss.

A detailed description of the punishment would invite the audience to compare it with punishments they knew and to judge whether it was proportionate. The verse removes that possibility by refusing the comparison: what covered them is presented as beyond measurement, and the only available response is the one the verse models.

That response is not despair but attention. The exclamation asks the reader to register the magnitude, and the magnitude is what makes the question in the following verse possible.

**Covering as the Qur'an's Word for Punishment**

The verb of covering is used elsewhere in the Qur'an for what overtakes a people, and the usage gives the word here its force.

Punishment in the Qur'an is frequently described as something that comes over a people from above or around them — a cloud, a blast, a wind, a covering — and the descriptions emphasise the inability to escape it. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt lists the forms the seizure took: against some a storm of stones, against some a mighty blast, some swallowed by the earth, and some drowned, with the closing statement that Allah would not have wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves (29:40). Sūrah al-Aḥqāf records the people of ʿĀd taking the approaching punishment for a rain cloud (46:24).

The covering is therefore not merely a description of scale but of inescapability. What covers a person cannot be stepped aside from, and the verse's choice of word makes that the point.

The audience's own case is the application. The warning has been delivered, and a warning that is not answered becomes, in the Qur'an's language, something that covers.

## Verse 53:55

> Now, which of your Lord’s favours will you dispute

**The Question and the Refrain**

The verse is a question, and the question has an echo that its first hearers would have recognised.

Sūrah ar-Raḥmān is built on a refrain that asks which of the favours of the Lord will be denied, and the refrain occurs thirty-one times in that chapter. The form is the same: a question about favours, expecting no answer, because no answer is possible.

The verse here is shorter and uses a different verb, but the shape is identical. A favour is named or implied, and the hearer is asked to dispute it. The impossibility of the answer is what makes the question an argument rather than a request.

**The Favours in Question**

The verse does not list the favours, and the omission is filled by the sūrah itself.

The chapter has described the creation of the human being from a drop, the giving of life and death, the provision of wealth and its withdrawal, the governance of the heavens, and the sending of messengers with warnings. Each of these is a favour in the Qur'an's usage, and each has been named in the preceding verses.

The question therefore refers to what the audience has just heard. It is not a general enquiry about blessings but a specific one about the blessings the sūrah has enumerated, and the audience cannot claim that the list was hidden from them.

## Verse 53:56

> This ˹Prophet˺ is a warner like earlier ones

**Two Readings of a Short Verse**

The verse is short enough that its grammar has been read in two ways, and the two readings differ in what the word refers to.

On the first reading, the subject is the Qur'an itself: this is a warning of the same kind as the earlier warnings, a message belonging to the continuous stream of revelation. On the second, the subject is the Prophet: this is a warner from among the warners of old, a man like the men sent to previous communities.

The readings differ in reference but agree in substance. Both place what the audience is hearing inside a series, and both deny that it is unprecedented. The commentators hold both without choosing, because the verse's function is the same either way.

**The Series of Warners**

The word used for the earlier ones is the plural of warner, and the series is one the Qur'an describes repeatedly.

Every community is said to have had a warner, and the warners are named across the chapters: Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, Shuʿayb, Moses. The verse in Sūrah Fāṭir states the pattern directly — there is no community to which a warner has not come (35:24) — and the verses in this sūrah name three of the communities whose warners were refused.

Placing the present message in that series does two things. It removes the claim of novelty, and it supplies the audience with the record of what happened to previous communities that made the same response.

**The Function of the Warning**

The Qur'an describes the function of a warner with some precision, and the precision matters here.

A warner is not a predictor of specific events and not a guarantor of outcomes. He delivers a message about what follows from conduct, and the delivery is his whole task. The verse in Sūrah al-Kahf makes the limit explicit: I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God (18:110).

The verse here places the Prophet in that role and no other. He is one of the warners, and the warners are messengers rather than miracle-workers, kings, or judges. The role is what the audience objected to, and the verse affirms it rather than answering the objection.

## Verse 53:57

> The approaching ˹Hour˺ has drawn near

**Al-Āzifah: The Approaching**

The verse names the Hour with a participle: *al-āzifah*, the approaching one, that which draws close. The word is used as a title, and the title is the verse's first claim.

The naming is significant. The Hour is not described as appointed, fixed, or coming at a distant time; it is named for its nearness. The quality chosen to identify it is the one most likely to be doubted, and the verse puts it in the name itself.

The same naming appears in Sūrah Ghāfir, where the warning of the approaching Day is announced and the Day is described as one when hearts will be at the throats (40:18). The participle functions there as it does here: the event is defined by its approach.

**The Hour Has Drawn Near**

The Qur'an states the nearness of the Hour in several places, and the statements are consistent.

Sūrah al-Qamar opens with the statement that the Hour has drawn near and the moon was split (54:1). Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ states that ˹The time of˺ people's judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away (21:1).

The consistency across chapters revealed at different times indicates that nearness was a standing claim of the Meccan preaching rather than a response to a particular situation. It was said to an audience that had heard it before, and its repetition was part of its force.

**Nearness in Divine Time**

The claim of nearness raises a question that the Qur'an's usage answers: near relative to what?

The revelation measures time differently from a human life. A thousand years in the sight of the Lord is like a day, and the commentators note that what is near in divine reckoning may be far in human terms. The nearness is therefore not a prediction about years but a statement about proportion.

That reading does not weaken the claim; it sharpens it. A person's life is short in any reckoning, and the Hour is near relative to the span in which a person must decide. The verse is addressed to individuals whose time is limited, and for them the nearness is exact.

**What the Nearness Does**

The effect of the claim is to remove the option of postponement.

A person who believes that a reckoning lies far ahead can defer his response indefinitely, and the deferral becomes a habit. The verse removes the ground of the habit by stating that the event is near, and the nearness converts the response from a future possibility into a present obligation.

The Qur'an states the difference in perception plainly: They truly see this ˹Day˺ as impossible, but We see it as inevitable (70:6–7). The difference is not information but attention, and the verse here is an appeal to attention.

**Suddenness and the Precedents**

The Qur'an describes the Hour as coming suddenly, and the description is supported by the precedents this sūrah has just named.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf states the pattern: it will only come upon you suddenly (7:187). The suddenness is not an additional feature of the Hour but the way it arrives, and the precedents show that the way it arrives is not altered by human expectation.

That is the practical content of the nearness. A person cannot prepare for a sudden event at the last moment; he can only be prepared in advance, and the advance is the interval the verse tells him is short.

## Verse 53:58

> None but Allah can disclose it

**Lā Yujallīhā: The Unveiling**

The verse uses a verb of disclosure: *yujallīhā*, he unveils it, he brings it into the open. The word comes from a root meaning to reveal or to make manifest, and it is used for the removal of a covering.

Applied to the Hour, the verb describes both the knowledge of it and its occurrence. To unveil an event can mean to disclose when it will happen, and it can mean to bring it about, since an event that has not occurred is covered and an event that has occurred is revealed.

The commentators hold both senses, and the verse's wording supports both. The exclusive claim — none but God — covers the knowledge and the act together, and the covering of both in a single verb is what makes the statement so compact.

**The Parallel Verse in al-Aʿrāf**

The Qur'an states the same position at length in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, in answer to a question the audience was asking.

They ask you, O Prophet, about the Hour, when will it be? Say, that knowledge is only with my Lord. He alone will reveal it when the time comes. It is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth and will only take you by surprise (7:187).

The verse there supplies what this one leaves out: the audience's question, the instruction to answer it, and the description of the event as sudden. The verse here gives the conclusion in six words, on the assumption that the fuller statement was already known.

**The Audience's Question About the Hour**

The Qur'an records that the audience asked about the Hour, and the asking is informative about the state of the discussion.

The question was not asked to prepare; it was asked to test. A person who wants a date is usually looking for a way to defer, and the deferral is what the question was meant to produce.

The answer given refused the date and stated the reason: the knowledge belongs to God alone. The refusal is not evasive but exact, because a date would have changed the nature of the warning from a call to readiness into a countdown.

The verse here closes the same question in six words. Nothing is added to the earlier answer, because nothing needed to be.

## Verse 53:59

> Do you find this revelation astonishing

**The Marvel at the Discourse**

The verse asks a question about a reaction, and the reaction is astonishment. *Afa-min hādhā al-ḥadīthi taʿjabūn* — do you marvel at this discourse?

The word for discourse, *ḥadīth*, denotes speech, a report, a thing spoken. Applied here it means the revelation being recited, and the word choice is significant: the audience's astonishment is directed at the fact that a speech exists at all.

The question does not deny that the discourse is astonishing. A message containing what this one contains — an account of a vision at the boundary of creation, a reckoning, a resurrection, an undated nearness — is astonishing. The question asks whether astonishment is the right response, and the following verses answer that it is not.

**The Parallel Questions in the Qur'an**

The same question is put in several chapters, and the parallels show what the astonishment consisted of.

Is it astonishing to people that We have sent revelation to a man from among themselves? (10:2). The deniers are astonished that a warner has come to them from among themselves, and the disbelievers say, this is an astonishing thing! (50:2).

The object of the astonishment is consistent in all of them: not the content of the message but the humanity of the messenger. A revelation was not astonishing; a revelation delivered by a man they knew was.

**The Messenger From Among Themselves**

The objection behind the astonishment is stated in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, where the people are asked whether they find it astonishing that a reminder should come to them from their Lord through one of their own (7:63).

The question implies the answer: a messenger from among the audience is what makes the message credible rather than what makes it doubtful. He speaks their language, they know his history, and they can assess his character from decades of acquaintance. The sūrah made the same appeal at its beginning, when it called him your companion.

The audience inverted the argument. Familiarity, which should have been evidence, became the ground of objection — a man we know cannot be a messenger. The verse's question exposes the inversion by asking whether the familiarity is really what astonishes them.

**The Word Ḥadīth and What It Claims**

The word used for the discourse carries a claim inside it, and the claim is part of what the audience found astonishing.

Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr describes the same refusal in another form: even if they were to see a deadly piece of the sky fall down upon them, they would still say it was a pile of clouds (52:44). The discourse has been delivered and heard, and the hearing has not produced acknowledgement.

The astonishment the verse asks about is thus an astonishment at the source, and the source is what the sūrah spent its opening verses establishing.

**Astonishment in the Qur'an's Other Uses**

The Qur'an uses astonishment in two directions, and the distinction matters for reading this verse.

There is the astonishment of wonder at a sign, which the revelation approves: the descriptions of the heavens, the alternation of night and day, and the origin of the human being are all presented as matters for wonder. That wonder leads to reflection and ends in acknowledgement.

There is also the astonishment of refusal, which the revelation rejects, and it is directed not at the evidence but at the fact that the evidence exists.

The verse here asks which kind the audience's astonishment is, and the following verses answer it by describing the laughter that follows. Wonder does not turn into mockery; refusal does.

The pagans are astonished that a warner has come to them from among themselves (38:4).

## Verse 53:60

> laughing ˹at it˺ and not weeping ˹in awe˺

**Weeping as the Fitting Response**

The verse contrasts the laughter with the weeping that is absent, and the contrast defines what the recitation calls for.

Weeping in the Qur'an is the response of those who understand what they are hearing. Sūrah as-Sajdah describes the true believers in the revelation as those who—when it is recited to them—fall into prostration and glorify the praises of their Lord and are not too proud (32:15). Sūrah Maryam states that whenever the revelations of the Most Compassionate were recited to them, they fell down, prostrating and weeping (19:58).

The response combines two movements: the body goes down and the eyes fill. Neither is performed for effect, and both are described as the natural outcome of understanding rather than as acts of devotion added to it.

**The Verified Saying on Laughter and Tears**

The Prophet's own statement on this contrast is recorded in several chains, and the wording is consistent.

If you knew that which I know, you would laugh little and weep much. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6485 from Abū Hurayrah and 6486 from Anas, and Bukhārī records a version addressed to his followers from ʿĀʾishah at 6631: by Allah, if you knew what I know, you would weep much and laugh little.

The saying is conditional, and the condition is knowledge. It does not condemn laughter as such; it states that the proportion between laughter and tears follows from what a person knows. The audience's laughter was therefore a statement about its knowledge rather than its temperament, and the verse's contrast makes the same point.

**The One Who Weeps Alone**

The value the tradition places on tears appears in one of the most widely transmitted sayings about the day of judgment.

Seven people will be shaded by God on the day when there is no shade but His, and among the seven is a person who remembers God in seclusion and whose eyes overflow with tears. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 660 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1031.

The detail that matters here is the seclusion. The tears counted are not those shed in company or in performance but those shed alone, where no audience exists to reward them. The verse's contrast between public laughter and absent weeping turns on the same distinction: one is performed for others, and the other is not performed at all.

**The Laughter Recorded in the Accounts**

The accounts of the Meccan period record the mockery this verse describes, and the records give it a specific form.

Sūrah al-Anfāl states that the prayer of the Quraysh at the Sacred Mosque was nothing but whistling and clapping (8:35), which the commentators connect with their conduct during the recitation. The reports describe people raising their voices to drown out the Qur'an, and the sūrah al-Fuṣṣilat records the instruction attributed to them: do not listen to this Qur'an and make noise during it, so that perhaps you will overcome (41:26).

The picture is of organised disruption rather than spontaneous amusement. The laughter this verse describes was a tactic, and the tactic was to prevent the recitation from being heard — by others as much as by the listeners themselves.

That context sharpens the verse's contrast. The weeping it asks about requires hearing, and the audience's conduct was arranged to make hearing impossible. The tradition preserves accounts of the response the verse calls for, and the accounts come from those who heard the recitation directly.

The reports describe Companions weeping at the recitation, and the sūrah of Maryam states the pattern for the prophets before them: when the verses of the Most Merciful were recited to them, they fell down, prostrating and weeping (19:58). The physical description is the same in both cases — the body descending and the eyes filling — and it is presented as a response rather than a performance.

And you used to laugh at them (23:110).

Those given knowledge before the revelation are described as falling on their faces in prostration when it is recited to them, saying that the promise of their Lord was fulfilled (17:107–108).

## Verse 53:61

> while persisting in heedlessness

**Sāmidūn: The Word and Its Readings**

The verse ends the description of the audience's reaction with a single word: *sāmidūn*, and the word has been read in three ways.

The commonest reading takes it as heedless, inattentive, holding the head high in indifference. A second reading takes it as diverted, turned away from the matter at hand. A third, reported from the Yemeni dialect, takes it to mean that they sang when they heard the recitation, which would make it a description of mockery through performance.

The three readings are not opposed. Heedlessness, diversion, and mockery through song describe one condition from three angles, and the commentators hold them together rather than choosing between them.

**Heedlessness as a Condition**

The word describes a state rather than an act, and the distinction matters for the charge the verse makes.

An act can be repented of and corrected; a condition persists and shapes the acts that follow. Heedlessness is not a decision made at a moment but a settled way of encountering things, in which the significance of what is heard is not registered.

The Qur'an treats heedlessness as the underlying condition of disbelief more often than it treats disbelief as a series of decisions. Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ opens with the statement that the time of people's judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away (21:1), and the pairing of nearness with heedlessness is the same pairing this sūrah makes.

**The Reading That Describes Singing**

The Yemeni reading deserves attention because it identifies a specific practice.

The reports describe the audience's response to the recitation as a kind of performance — whistling, clapping, or singing over the words so that they could not be heard. Sūrah al-Anfāl records the practice directly: Their prayer at the Sacred House was nothing but whistling and clapping (8:35).

On that reading the verse is not describing an inner state but a deliberate tactic. The recitation was drowned out, and the drowning out was organised. The charge would then be stronger than heedlessness: not inattention but the active prevention of attention, in themselves and in others. The tactic is recorded because it worked, and the verse's question asks whether it is still working.

**What Heedlessness Does**

The verse's charge is the most serious of the three reactions it describes, and the reason is what heedlessness prevents.

Astonishment can turn into enquiry, and mockery can turn into anger, and both remain engagements with the message. Heedlessness does not engage at all. It allows a person to hear the recitation repeatedly and remain unchanged, because nothing heard is allowed to reach the point where it must be answered.

That is why the verse ends the sequence with it. The descent described in the closing verses reaches its end not in hostility but in indifference, and indifference is the condition from which recovery is hardest.

## Verse 53:62

> Instead, prostrate to Allah and worship ˹Him alone˺

**A Verse of Prostration**

This verse is one of the verses at which the reciter prostrates, and its status in the tradition is specific.

The verses of prostration are counted at fifteen on the common reckoning, though the schools differ on the number and on which verses are included. Most jurists hold that the prostration after this verse is obligatory on the reciter and the listener; Imam Mālik held that in this instance it is recommended rather than required.

The reports indicate that the Prophet would prostrate on completing the recitation of this sūrah, and the commentators note that the practice may have been a habitual supererogatory act rather than the discharge of an obligation. The difference between the schools concerns the ruling, not the practice, and the practice itself is not in dispute.

**The Command and Its Two Parts**

The verse contains two imperatives, and the second is broader than the first.

Prostrate is a specific act with a specific form. Worship is the whole of what a person owes, of which prostration is one expression. The verse names the act first and the general obligation second, which is the order a recitation would suggest: the hearer is addressed in the moment of hearing.

The particle translated as instead carries a sense of turning away from what preceded. The audience was marvelling, laughing, and remaining heedless; the verse tells them what to do instead, and the instead is the whole of the answer.

**The Parallel Verses on Prostration**

The Qur'an describes prostration before revelation in several places, and the descriptions agree in treating it as a response rather than a ritual.

Sūrah al-Inshiqāq asks what is the matter with them that they do not believe, and when the Qur'an is recited to them, they do not bow down in submission (84:20–21). The question implies that bowing is what recitation calls for, and that its absence is a failure to respond.

Sūrah as-Sajdah describes the believers as those who fall into prostration when the revelation is recited to them and are not too proud (32:15). The phrase not too proud identifies the obstacle, and the obstacle is the same pride that produced the laughter in the verses before this one.

**Prostration in the Qur'an's Wider Usage**

Prostration is used throughout the Qur'an as the marker of acknowledgement, and the usage gives the command its weight.

Sūrah al-Ḥajj describes everything in the heavens and the earth bowing to God — the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, and the living creatures — along with many human beings (22:18). The prostration of a believer is therefore presented as joining a universal act rather than performing a private ritual.

The same chapter describes the angels, who do not disobey what they are commanded, and the sūrah's own account of the vision places the observer at the boundary of creation where those beings are. The command at the end of the chapter asks the hearer to do what the account has just described being done.

That is the sūrah's closing movement: from the vision at the boundary, through the examination of the stones and the stars, to the act that aligns the hearer with the worshippers described at the beginning.
