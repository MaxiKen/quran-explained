# Sūrah an-Najm (Chapter 53) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:1
> By the stars when they fade away!

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath on a Star in the Act of Setting**

The sūrah opens with a single oath, and the oath is on a moving thing. *An-najm idhā hawā* — the star when it descends, sets, or falls. The temporal clause is the point: the star is not sworn by in general but at a moment.

That differs from the oath-series in the sūrah before it, which listed five objects. Here one object is named at one instant, and the instant is what the oath fastens on.

A setting star is a familiar sight in a desert sky, and its movement is quick enough to be missed. The oath therefore invokes something seen and gone, which prepares the subject the sūrah is about to state: a vision granted at a moment, to one person, which those who were absent are now disputing.

**Al-Najm: One Star, or the Stars**

The word *najm* is singular in form and can be read as collective in sense, and both readings have been given. Some take it as a particular star, and the identification most often given is the Pleiades, whose setting marked a season in the Arabian calendar. Others take it as stars generally, in the act of setting.

A third reading connects the word to the Qur'an's own vocabulary. The same root appears in the phrase about the positions of the stars, an oath the Qur'an calls a great one (56:75–76), and the commentators link that phrase to the revelation coming down in instalments — *nujūm*, portions, from the same root.

The readings are not mutually exclusive. A word that means both a star and a portion can carry both senses in an oath about revelation, and the sūrah's subject is precisely a revelation delivered in portions to a man who is being accused of composing it.

**Hawā: To Fall, To Set, To Descend**

The verb *hawā* describes downward movement — a star falling, a bird dropping from the sky, a person collapsing. It is the same verb used for the fall of the stars at the end of the world: and when the stars fall down (81:2).

The word therefore carries both an ordinary and an eschatological sense. A star sets every night; the stars fall on the day described in Sūrah at-Takwīr. The oath sits between the two meanings.

That double sense suits the sūrah's argument. What is being sworn to is an event that happened once, in time, and the verb chosen is one that describes both the ordinary movement of the heavens and their final movement.

**The Oath Guarantees a Denial**

An oath in this Book is followed by the statement it guarantees, and here the statement is a defence: your fellow man is neither misguided nor astray. The oath exists to support a claim about a person.

That is unusual. Most of the Qur'an's oaths guarantee statements about God, the day, or the human condition. This one guarantees the sanity and guidance of a man, and the man is named only by relationship — your companion.

The choice tells us what the sūrah is answering. The accusations recorded in the previous chapter — soothsayer, madman, poet, inventor — were accusations about his mind and his source. The oath is placed at the head of the reply.

**Why a Star and Not a Mountain**

The comparison with the preceding sūrah is instructive. Aṭ-Ṭūr swore by fixed objects: a mountain, a book, a house, a sky, a sea. This one swears by a star in the act of setting.

The difference matches the different subjects. Aṭ-Ṭūr was establishing the certainty of a future event, and permanence was the right register. An-Najm is establishing the reality of a moment that has passed, and the right register is a moment.

The star setting is also an image of descent, and descent is the sūrah's central movement: a revelation coming down, an angel descending, a servant being taught. The oath names the direction before the sūrah describes it.

**The Oath and the Dispute It Answers**

The sūrah returns to its opening later, and the return shows what the oath was for. After describing the vision and the nearness, the sūrah asks: how can you then dispute with him regarding what he saw? (53:12).

The question treats the dispute as unreasonable in light of what has been described. An oath at the head of the passage and a challenge at its end frame the account between them.

The dispute being answered was real and is recorded elsewhere: they say this is a set of confused dreams, no he has fabricated it, no he must be a poet (21:5). Each of those descriptions is a theory about the origin of the speech, and the sūrah answers all of them with one account of where it came from.

That is the function of the opening oath. It does not prove the vision; it stakes the truth of the account that follows on something the hearer can see.

**The Vision the Sūrah Will Describe**

The verses that follow describe an approach, a nearness measured in bow-lengths, a revelation given, and a heart that did not falter at what it saw. The oath stands over all of it.

The structure is therefore argumentative rather than decorative. A claim about an unseen event needs a warrant, and the sūrah supplies one in the form of an oath on a visible object, before describing what no one else saw.

That method is characteristic of the Qur'an's treatment of the unseen: it anchors the invisible in something the hearer can see, and then asks him to accept the report on the strength of the anchor.

**The Star That Fades and the Light That Remains**

The image has a final resonance. A setting star withdraws its light, and what replaces it is the light of the sun. The sūrah is about a man accused of speaking from his own mind, and the answer is that what he speaks was given to him.

The oath on a fading star therefore prepares the distinction the sūrah draws between a person's own light and a light that comes to him. One withdraws; the other is sent down.

The next verse states the claim the oath supports, and it states it in the shortest possible form: he has not strayed, and he has not erred.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:2
> Your fellow man is neither misguided nor astray.

**Expanded Commentary**

**“Your Fellow Man” — The Word of Familiarity**

The verse refers to the Prophet ﷺ as *ṣāḥibukum*, your companion, your fellow. The word implies acquaintance: someone you have dealt with, travelled with, sat beside.

The choice is rhetorical and precise. The people being addressed had known him for forty years before the revelation. They had traded with him, married into his clan, and called him trustworthy. The verse appeals to that knowledge rather than to argument.

The same word returns later in the sūrah in a related context: and your fellow man is not insane (81:22). Both uses address people who could verify the claim from their own experience, and both deny a charge about his mind.

**The Charge in Its Meccan Setting**

The accusation of madness was not an insult thrown in anger; it was a considered classification with consequences. A *majnūn* in that society was a person whose speech could be dismissed without examination, since it was attributed to a jinn rather than to a mind.

The classification was therefore useful to those who made it. It removed the need to answer the Qur'an's arguments, and it explained the extraordinary quality of the language without conceding anything. The same function was served by the charge of soothsaying and by the charge of poetry.

The Qur'an records the charge as a general pattern rather than a local event: no messenger came to those before them without being told, a magician or a madman (51:52). The pairing is standard, and the verse in an-Najm answers it with the plainest possible denial.

**What the Community Could Testify To**

The strength of this verse lies in the audience it addresses. The people told that their companion has not strayed were people who had known him since childhood.

Their own testimony was available to them and contradicted the charge. He had been trusted with deposits, known for honesty in trade, and consulted in disputes — the reputation that earned him the title al-Amīn before the revelation. A community does not give that title to a person it believes to be deluded.

The verse appeals to that record without reciting it. The word *ṣāḥib* does the work: your companion, the one you know. The denial is placed where the evidence already exists, in the memory of the people hearing it.

**Straying and Guidance in the Qur'an's Own Usage**

The word *ḍalāl* is used elsewhere of the Prophet ﷺ himself, in a verse that needs careful reading. Did He not find you unguided and then guide you? (93:7). The verse describes a state before guidance, and a guidance given.

That statement is not in tension with the denial in this verse; it defines it. The denial is about the present condition — he is not astray now — and 93:7 explains how he came to be otherwise. The guidance was received, not self-produced, which is exactly what the following verses of this sūrah describe.

The pairing of the two verses closes the charge completely. A person who was guided by another is not deluded, and a person who is not astray cannot be the source of his own guidance. Both statements point to the same conclusion: the direction came from outside him.

**What the Denial Is About**

The verse is sometimes read as a claim about the Prophet's general character, and it is more precise than that. It is a claim about direction — that the path he is on is a path, and that he is on it correctly.

The two halves of the denial answer the two ways a religious claim can fail. A person can be sincere and lost, or guided in direction and deluded in execution. The verse denies both, and the denial is what allows the sūrah to move on to the question of source.

Nothing in the verse claims perfection in matters outside the message, and the Qur'an elsewhere corrects the Prophet ﷺ in such matters openly (80:1–10). The scope is the religion he brought, and within that scope the denial is absolute.

**Two Negations, Not One**

The verse denies two things: he has not strayed, and he has not erred. A single negation would have been sufficient if the two words meant the same, and the pairing indicates that they do not.

The doubling is characteristic of Qur'anic denial, which tends to close a claim from two directions. The same method appears in the sūrah's next verse and in the denials recorded in aṭ-Ṭūr, where the Prophet ﷺ is declared neither a soothsayer nor a madman.

Each pair covers a different accusation, and the pairs accumulate across chapters until the available charges are exhausted.

**Ḍalāl and Ghawā**

The two words describe different failures. *Ḍalāl* is the absence of guidance — not knowing the way, or losing it. *Ghawā* is closer to error of judgment or delusion, and the commentators connect it to ignorance that comes from holding a corrupt creed.

The distinction maps onto two different accusations. A person who has strayed is one who wandered off a path; a person who is deluded is one whose judgment has been captured by something false. The first is a failure of direction, the second a failure of understanding.

Denying both closes the ground completely. He is neither lost nor mistaken about what he is following.

**What the Verse Does Not Claim**

The denial is specific, and its limits matter. The verse does not say the Prophet ﷺ knows everything, or that he is beyond error in ordinary human matters, or that he has independent access to the unseen.

The Qur'an states those limits explicitly elsewhere. Say: I do not say to you that I possess Allah's treasuries or know the unseen, nor do I claim to be an angel — I only follow what is revealed to me (6:50). The same disclaimer appears in the sūrah that follows this one, where he is told to say that he has no power to benefit or harm himself except as God wills (7:188).

The verse therefore claims guidance in the matter at issue — the message — and nothing beyond it. That restraint is what makes the claim credible to the people it addresses.

**Why an Oath Was Needed**

The verse is the statement guaranteed by the oath in the first verse, and the oath indicates how seriously the charge was being pressed.

A plain assertion can be met with a counter-assertion. An oath stakes something on the claim, and the Qur'an uses the form when the denial is not merely asserted but sworn to.

The charge being answered was public and persistent. The accusations of madness and possession are recorded in at least six chapters, and the sūrah of al-Qalam opens by denying the charge under oath: by the grace of your Lord, you are not insane (68:2).

**The Charge Answered in the Addressees' Own Terms**

The verse does not defend the content of the message here. It defends the condition of the messenger, and it does so in terms the addressees can check.

That method is stronger than argument for this particular charge. A person accused of madness cannot refute the accusation by reasoning, since the reasoning itself would be cited as evidence. What refutes it is the testimony of people who have observed him over time, and the verse appeals to exactly that testimony by using the word *ṣāḥib*.

The people addressed were the witnesses. The verse asks them to consult their own memory.

**The Verse Sets Up the Following Claim**

Read with the next two verses, the three form a single statement: he is not lost, he is not deluded, and he does not speak from whim. The first two describe his state; the third describes his source.

The sequence is deliberate. A charge about the messenger's mind is answered first, because until it is answered nothing he says can be evaluated. Once the state is settled, the source can be addressed, and the sūrah turns to it immediately.

That is the structure of the whole passage: clear the man, then establish the message.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:3
> Nor does he speak of his own whims.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Hawā: The Qur'an's Word for Whim**

The word *hawā* means an inclination of the self — a desire that pulls a person toward what he wants rather than toward what is true. The Qur'an treats it as the opposite of guidance, and the opposition is stated as an either/or.

Have you seen those who have taken their own desires as their god? Allah left them to stray knowingly, sealed their hearing and their hearts, and placed a cover on their sight (45:23). The description is of a person whose desire has taken the place that God occupies, and the result is a closed perception.

The same word appears later in this sūrah, describing those who follow nothing but inherited assumptions and whatever their souls desire, although guidance has come to them (53:23). Both verses place *hawā* on the side opposite revelation.

**The Denial and Its Scope**

The verse denies that the Prophet ﷺ speaks from *hawā*, and the commentators differ on how wide the denial runs. The narrower reading limits it to the Qur'an: what he delivers as revelation is not his own composition. The wider reading covers his speech generally: his own words are also guided.

Both readings have support. The narrower one fits the immediate context, which is about the source of the revelation. The wider one fits the description of him elsewhere as one who does not speak from whim in any matter touching the religion.

The narrower reading is the safer and the more precise, since the Qur'an elsewhere acknowledges that the Prophet ﷺ made judgments in worldly matters that were his own, and corrected some of them. The verse is about the message, and in that domain the denial is absolute.

**Why the Verse Comes Third**

The placement of this denial is worth noting. The first verse swears, the second establishes the messenger's soundness, and the third establishes the source of his speech. Only after that does the sūrah describe the transmission.

The order is argumentative. A listener will not accept an account of revelation from someone he believes to be confused, and he will not accept a divine source from someone he believes to be self-serving. Both obstacles are removed before the account begins.

That is why three verses are spent on denial. The sūrah is not padding; it is clearing the ground for the narrative that follows, which would be dismissed outright if the ground were not cleared.

**What the Denial Establishes**

If what he speaks is not from his own inclination, then it has another source, and the next verse names it: it is only a revelation sent down. The denial in this verse and the affirmation in the next are two halves of one statement.

That structure is important. The Qur'an does not merely claim a divine source; it first removes the human one. The claim is established by elimination, and the elimination is what the oath of the first verse guarantees.

The method matters for the audience. A person who believes the message came from the man cannot be argued into believing it came from God; he has to be shown first that the man is not its author.

**The Answer to the Charge of Invention**

The previous sūrah recorded the accusation directly: or do they say he made this Qur'an up? — and answered it with the diagnosis that they do not believe (52:33) and with the challenge to produce something like it (52:34).

This verse answers the same accusation from the other side. Not by challenging the accusers to imitate it, but by stating what the thing is: not a product of his desire.

The two answers are complementary. The challenge addresses the possibility of imitation; this verse addresses the possibility of origin. Together they close the accusation.

**Why Desire Is the Specific Thing Denied**

Of all the human motives that could be named — ambition, fear, calculation, habit — the verse names *hawā*, and the choice is exact.

The accusation against the Prophet ﷺ was not that he was careless but that he was self-serving: that the Qur'an advanced his position, answered his critics, and served his interests. That is an accusation about desire, and the verse denies it in the same vocabulary.

The denial is also verifiable in a limited way. A message composed to serve a man's desires would be expected to flatter him, and the Qur'an contains passages that correct and rebuke the Prophet ﷺ (80:1–10; 33:37). A self-serving author does not include those.

**The Verse's Place in the Argument**

Three verses have now been given to a single claim: the man is sound, and the message is not his. The sūrah will spend the next several verses describing how the message reached him — the angel, the power, the approach, the nearness.

That sequence is not decoration. The accusation was about origin, and the answer is a description of the transmission, given in detail precise enough to be examined and vague enough to preserve reverence.

The verse therefore functions as a hinge. It closes the denial and opens the account.

**Desire and the Two Directions**

The Qur'an presents the religious life as a choice between two sources of direction, and *hawā* names one of them. A person either follows what has been revealed or follows what he wants, and there is no third source.

The verse in al-Jāthiyah describes the second option in its extreme form: a person who takes his own desire as his god (45:23). The formulation is severe because the substitution is complete — the desire occupies the place where the command should be.

The denial in this verse places the Prophet ﷺ on the other side of that division. Whatever he delivers as revelation is not his desire speaking, and the claim is verifiable in part by the content: a message that corrects its own deliverer is not the product of his inclination.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:4
> It is only a revelation sent down ˹to him˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Restriction**

The verse is built on *illā*, except, used restrictively: it is nothing but a revelation revealed. The particle excludes every alternative rather than asserting one among many.

That form of statement is stronger than a plain affirmation. To say the Qur'an is a revelation leaves other descriptions available; to say it is nothing but a revelation closes the list.

The exclusion is aimed at the specific alternatives in circulation: poetry, soothsaying, madness, invention. Each is a description of the same speech, and the verse denies all of them in four words.

**Waḥyun Yūḥā: The Doubled Form**

The phrase repeats the root: *waḥy* and *yūḥā*, a revelation that is revealed. The repetition is a recognised form of emphasis in Arabic, and the commentators draw two senses from it.

The first is intensification: this is revelation in the full sense, not something resembling revelation. The second is continuity — the passive participle suggests an ongoing act, a revelation being revealed, renewed at each moment of delivery.

Both senses serve the verse's purpose. The first answers the charge that the Qur'an is a lesser kind of inspiration; the second describes the actual mode of its arrival, which was episodic over twenty-three years rather than delivered in one piece.

**Revelation Is Not Inspiration**

The distinction the verse draws is between revelation and the inner movements of a gifted mind. *Waḥy* in the Qur'an is a communication from outside the person receiving it, with a sender, a content, and a moment of arrival.

The Qur'an classifies its own modes of communication: it is not possible for a human being to have Allah communicate with them except through inspiration, or from behind a veil, or by sending a messenger-angel to reveal whatever He wills by His permission (42:51). Three modes are named, and all three originate outside the recipient.

That taxonomy matters for this verse. The claim being made is not that the Prophet ﷺ had unusual insight but that he received something, and the following verses describe the receiving.

**The Passive Voice and the Unnamed Sender**

The verb is passive — *yūḥā*, it is revealed — and the sender is not named in this verse. The naming comes in the verses that follow, where the one of mighty power who taught him is introduced.

The delay is deliberate. The verse first establishes the nature of the speech before describing its route, and the route is described with a precision that would be premature before the nature was settled.

The passive also preserves a point of doctrine. The revelation is given, not achieved. Nothing in the description suggests that the Prophet ﷺ attained it by effort, discipline, or genius, and the following verses make the giving explicit.

**The Same Claim in Ash-Shuʿarāʾ**

The statement is not unique to this sūrah, and the parallel passage gives it in fuller form. This is certainly a revelation from the Lord of all worlds, which the trustworthy spirit brought down into your heart, so that you may be one of the warners, in a clear Arabic tongue (26:192–195).

The four elements in that passage correspond to the four verses here: the source, the agent, the recipient, and the language. Both accounts are given as statements of fact rather than as arguments, and both use the same vocabulary of descent.

The correspondence across two Meccan chapters shows that this was the standard answer to the charge of invention. It is not an improvisation for one occasion but a settled account, repeated in the same terms.

**Why the Restriction Matters Practically**

The claim that the Qur'an is nothing but revelation has an immediate consequence, and it is the one the Meccan audience cared about.

If the speech is a man's, its commands are advice and its warnings are opinions. If it is revelation, its commands bind and its warnings describe what will happen. The whole of the dispute about the Prophet's authority reduces to this question.

The Qur'an states the consequence plainly elsewhere. Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed Allah (4:80) — a statement that only holds if the messenger's message is not his own. The restriction in this verse is therefore not a metaphysical claim but the foundation of an obligation, and the sūrah's insistence on it is insistence on that foundation.

**The Verse Answers a Specific Alternative**

The restriction *nothing but* has an identifiable target, and the target was not vague. The alternatives in circulation were that the Qur'an was poetry, that it was the speech of a soothsayer receiving from jinn, that it was the product of a disturbed mind, and that it was composed by the Prophet ﷺ with help from others (25:4).

Each of those is a description of origin, and the verse excludes all four in a single phrase. It does not rank them or answer them individually; it removes them.

That method is efficient and deliberate. A verse that argued against each charge in turn would be long and would keep the charges in circulation. A verse that states what the thing is, exclusively, leaves nothing to argue with.

**Why the Source Matters So Much**

The whole of the Meccan dispute turned on the source. If the Qur'an came from God, then its commands bind and its warnings are true. If it came from a man, it is one opinion among others.

The Qur'an knows this and addresses it constantly. The challenge to produce a sūrah like it (2:23), the statement that it is a reminder to the worlds (81:27), the denials of invention (10:38; 11:13) — all are statements about origin.

This verse is the shortest of them and the most absolute. It does not argue about the source; it excludes the alternatives.

**The Verse Opens the Description**

What follows is an account of how the revelation came: a teacher of mighty power, a rising to true form, an approach, a nearness, a giving. The account is the support for the claim made here.

That arrangement is unusual in the Qur'an. Most statements about revelation's source are brief; this passage gives a narrative with spatial detail and a measurement.

The detail exists because the claim was being disputed by people who demanded evidence about the unseen. The sūrah answers with a description specific enough to be reported and restrained enough to remain a report.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:5
> He has been taught by one ˹angel˺ of mighty power

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dhū Quwwa: Possessor of Power**

The verse describes the teacher with a single attribute: *dhū quwwa*, one of mighty power. The construction — possessor of a quality — is used in the Qur'an for beings defined by an attribute rather than named.

Power in an angel is not metaphorical. The Qur'an describes the angels as harsh and mighty, not disobeying what they are commanded (66:6), and it describes a single angel's act in terms of catastrophic force: the cities of Lot were overturned (11:82).

The attribute is chosen for this context because the account that follows involves a form, an appearance, and an approach — all acts requiring capacity.

**Gabriel Identified**

The majority of commentators identify the one of mighty power as the angel Gabriel, the messenger through whom the Qur'an was delivered. The identification is supported by other passages.

Sūrah at-Takwīr gives the same description in almost identical terms: this is the word of a noble messenger angel, full of power, held in honour by the Lord of the Throne, obeyed there in heaven, and trustworthy (81:19–21). The correspondence of attributes — power, honour, trustworthiness — makes the identification straightforward.

Sūrah al-Baqarah names the agent directly: the trustworthy spirit brought it down upon your heart (2:97 in the sense of the revelation's descent), and Gabriel is named elsewhere as the one who brought the Qur'an down.

**The Minority Reading**

Some commentators take the one of mighty power to be God Himself, reading the following verses as a description of divine disclosure rather than angelic mediation.

The reading is not baseless, since the verses that follow describe an approach and a nearness that some have understood as between the Prophet ﷺ and God. But the plain sequence of the passage — a teacher, a form, a horizon, an approach — fits an intermediary better, and the following verses speak of a servant being given a revelation, which implies a giver distinct from God in the act of transmission.

Weighing them, the angelic reading is stronger. It fits the Qur'an's consistent description of revelation's route through 42:51, and it accounts for the spatial details that the divine reading has to treat as metaphor.

**Taught, Not Inspired**

The verb is *ʿallamahu* — he taught him. The choice is significant, since teaching implies a content conveyed and a learner receiving it.

Inspiration would have been described with a different verb. The Qur'an uses *awḥā* for the act of revelation and *ʿallama* for the act of teaching, and this verse uses the second for the angel's role.

The distinction preserves a point of doctrine. The Prophet ﷺ was not left to arrive at the message; he was instructed in it. The content came with the instruction, which is why the following verses describe an approach and a delivery rather than a state of mind.

**Why the Chain Is Named**

The verse inserts an intermediary into the account of revelation, and the insertion has a purpose. It answers the accusation of invention by describing a route.

A person who claims to have received something can be asked how. The verse answers: through a being of power, who appeared in a form, at a place, and approached to a measurable distance. The answer is a report rather than a proof, but it is the kind of report that can be examined for consistency.

The Qur'an uses the same method elsewhere. The angel is named, described, and located, so that the account has structure rather than mystery.

**The Trustworthy Spirit and the Heart**

The Qur'an describes the mode of delivery in ash-Shuʿarāʾ: the trustworthy spirit brought it down into your heart, so that you may be one of the warners (26:193–194). The heart is named as the place of reception.

That detail matters for the account in this sūrah. Teaching implies a recipient, and the recipient here is not the memory alone but the heart, which in Qur'anic usage is the organ of understanding and conviction.

The phrase *ar-rūḥ al-amīn*, the trustworthy spirit, adds the quality that matters for transmission. Trustworthiness in a messenger means the message arrives unaltered, and the Qur'an pairs it with power in at-Takwīr — full of power, held in honour, obeyed, and trustworthy (81:20–21).

Both attributes are needed. A trustworthy messenger who is weak can be prevented; a powerful messenger who is not trustworthy can distort. The description here supplies both.

**The Angel in Human Form**

The verses that follow describe the angel in his own form, and the contrast with his usual manner is worth stating. Gabriel normally came in a form people could perceive.

The Qur'an records the same method elsewhere: We sent to her Our angel, appearing before her as a man, perfectly formed (19:17). The well-known report of Gabriel coming to the Prophet ﷺ in the presence of the Companions describes a man in white garments with very black hair, whom nobody present recognised, and whom the Prophet ﷺ afterwards identified as Gabriel.

The appearance in true form was therefore exceptional, and the sūrah marks it as such by describing the rising to that form. The exception is what makes the event worth swearing by.

**Power as the Guarantee of Fidelity**

The attribute of power is not incidental to the account. A messenger who is weak can be interfered with; a messenger who is powerful cannot.

The Qur'an makes the connection explicit in at-Takwīr, where the messenger angel is described as obeyed in heaven and trustworthy (81:20–21), and where the Qur'an is distinguished from the word of an outcast devil (81:25). The contrast is between a transmission that can be corrupted and one that cannot.

That is the function of the attribute here. The one who taught him is powerful, and the teaching is therefore intact — which is the claim the sūrah opened by swearing to.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:6
> and great perfection, who once rose to ˹his˺ true form

**Expanded Commentary**

**Dhū Mirā: Soundness, Vigour, Completeness**

The second attribute is *dhū mirā*, and the word carries a cluster of related senses: physical soundness, vigour, and — in one reading — a beautiful stature. The root is connected to completeness of form.

The pairing with mighty power in the previous verse produces a description of capacity in two dimensions: strength and integrity. A being can be strong and unstable; this one is described as both powerful and sound.

The choice of a second attribute rather than a repetition of the first suggests the description is being built up deliberately, since the verses that follow describe an appearance that requires both.

**Istawā: He Rose and Stood Firm**

The verb *istawā* means to rise to a level, to stand upright, to become established. It is used in the Qur'an for the Ark coming to rest on the mountain (11:44), for a person mounting (43:13), and — in a different register — for God's establishment on the Throne (20:5).

Applied here it describes a change of posture or state: the one who taught him rose, straightened, presented himself. The commentators read it either as the manner of his appearance on the horizon or as the manner of his teaching.

The verb's sense of settling into a position is important. It is not a movement toward something but an arrival at a stance, which prepares the spatial description in the next verse.

**The Two Appearances in True Form**

The tradition records that the Prophet ﷺ saw the angel in his created form on two occasions. The first was at the beginning of the revelation, when the angel appeared filling the horizon. The second was during the ascension.

This passage is generally read as describing the first, and the later verses of the same sūrah (53:13–14) describe the second, near the Lote-Tree of the Utmost Boundary. The two accounts differ in setting and in what is seen, which is why the commentators treat them as distinct events.

The distinction matters for interpretation. The vision described here is located on the horizon — a place in the world — while the second is located at the boundary of the created order.

**What “True Form” Implies**

The phrase translated as true form indicates that the appearance was in the angel's own created nature rather than in the human shape in which he usually came.

The reports describe that form as vast, and some mention a great number of wings. Those details come from narrations of varying strength, and it is safer to note that the tradition describes a form of immense scale than to fix a number as established.

What the verse itself says is simpler and sufficient: he rose to his form. The appearance was not a vision of something else, nor a symbolic image, but the being himself.

**Why the Form Is Mentioned**

The mention of the form serves the sūrah's argument. The accusation was that the Qur'an came from the Prophet's own mind; the answer describes an external being, seen, in a form recognisable as not human.

A vision of a form is a different kind of report from an interior experience. The verse specifies that the appearance had magnitude and location, which are the features that make a report testable.

That is consistent with the sūrah's method throughout: it answers claims about the unseen with descriptions that have coordinates.

**The Rising and the Teaching**

The verb *istawā* has been read in two ways, and both bear on the account. One reading takes it as describing the manner of the appearance: he rose and stood firm on the horizon. The other takes it as describing the manner of the teaching: he established himself to deliver the revelation.

The two readings are not far apart. In both, the angel is presented as settled rather than passing, which is what the following verses require — an approach and a nearness presuppose a presence that lasts.

The preference for the appearance reading is supported by the next verse, which gives a location. A location fits an appearance more naturally than a method of instruction, and the sequence — form, position, approach — reads as a single spatial account.

**A Form Recognised Rather Than Invented**

The verse's claim is that the appearance was in the angel's own nature, and that claim has a consequence for the report. A vision of an invented image tells us about the one who sees it; a vision of a being in its own form tells us about the being.

The Qur'an is careful on this point elsewhere. It distinguishes the Qur'an from the word of an outcast devil (81:25) and describes the angel as obeyed in heaven and trustworthy (81:21). The distinction depends on the reality of the one appearing: an image projected into a mind has no such standing.

That is why the sūrah bothers with the form at all. The accusation it answers is that the experience was interior, and the answer is that what appeared was not interior to anyone.

**The Vision and the Night Journey**

The tradition connects the second appearance in true form with the night journey and ascension, which the Qur'an records separately: glory be to the One Who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, so that We may show him some of Our signs (17:1).

The two passages belong together without being the same account. The sūrah of the Star describes an appearance and an approach; the sūrah of the Night Journey describes a journey with a stated purpose — to show him signs.

This sūrah's later verses locate a second vision at the Lote-Tree of the utmost limit, which is the ascension setting. The verse here is generally read as the earlier of the two, at the beginning of the revelation, which is why the commentators treat the chapter as containing two accounts rather than one repeated.

**What the Description Leaves Out**

The verse says he rose to his form and stops there. No dimensions are given, no features are described, no words are quoted from the encounter.

The restraint is characteristic of the Qur'an's treatment of the unseen. Where detail would invite speculation, the text withholds it; where detail serves the argument, it supplies it. Here the argument needs the fact of an appearance in true form, not its description.

That economy also protects the account. A description of an angelic form would be argued about as imagery; a statement that he appeared in his form is a report, and a report is either accepted or rejected on its source.

**The Sequence of the Passage**

Three verses have now described the teacher: powerful, sound, and rising to his form. The next verses give the location and the approach.

The order is spatial in a way that suggests an eyewitness account. Attribute, posture, location, distance, delivery. Each element is added in the sequence a witness would give them.

The structure is the sūrah's strongest rhetorical feature. It does not argue that the revelation is divine; it describes how it arrived, and leaves the reader with an account rather than a conclusion.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:7
> while on the highest point above the horizon,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Ufuq al-Aʿlā**

The verse gives a location, and the phrase is *al-ufuq al-aʿlā*, the highest horizon — the upper edge of the visible field, where the sky meets the line of sight.

The word *ufuq* is ordinary Arabic for the horizon, used for the eastern and western edges of the sky. Adding *aʿlā*, highest, specifies which part: not the horizon at eye level but the upper reach of it.

The specificity is unusual in an account of the unseen, and it is deliberate. The sūrah is describing something seen, and a seen thing has a place.

**The Horizon and the First Revelation**

The tradition places the appearance at the beginning of the revelation, in the setting the Qur'an itself alludes to elsewhere. The reports describe the angel appearing in a form that filled the horizon, and the Prophet ﷺ seeing him wherever he turned.

The detail is consistent with the verse's language. A horizon-filling appearance is what the phrase *al-ufuq al-aʿlā* describes from the observer's side, and it explains why the location is given in terms of the visual field rather than in terms of a place on a map.

The reading is supported by the parallel verse in at-Takwīr, which uses the same construction — he saw him on the clear horizon — and by the fact that the sūrah later records a second occasion in a different setting. Two occasions, two locations, one account of each.

**Why Location Matters for a Claim**

A claim about an unseen event gains or loses force on its specificity. A report that something happened somewhere can be checked against the reporter's circumstances; a report that something happened nowhere in particular cannot.

The verse supplies the specificity, and it does so in terms the audience used daily. Everyone in Mecca knew where the horizon was and what the upper part of it looked like at dawn.

That is the function of the coordinate. It does not prove the event; it makes the event a claim of a kind that can be assessed. The sūrah then asks the reader to assess it, in the verse that follows the account: how can you dispute with him regarding what he saw?

**The Same Phrase in At-Takwīr**

Sūrah at-Takwīr uses the same description in a passage that parallels this one closely: and he certainly saw that angel on the clear horizon (81:23). The two verses describe the same event with different adjectives — the highest horizon here, the clear horizon there.

The correspondence supports the reading that this passage is about the angel rather than about God. Both chapters use the language of seeing an object at a location, and neither uses the language of interior experience.

The pairing also fixes the event. The commentators read both passages as describing the appearance at the beginning of the revelation, when the angel appeared filling the horizon.

**Why a Horizon**

The choice of location has a logic. The horizon is the furthest visible distance, and an appearance there is at once unmistakable and beyond reach.

That combination suits the account. The appearance was visible — it was not a private vision in the sense of something invisible to the physical eye — and it was not something the observer could approach or examine at will.

The horizon also carries a directional sense in Arabic usage. The eastern horizon is where light first appears, and the commentators who locate the event there connect it with the moment of the first revelation, which came at a moment of transition.

**The Disagreement About the Location**

The commentators differ on where exactly the appearance occurred. Some read it as the eastern horizon, where the angel appeared to the Prophet ﷺ after he asked to see him in his true form. Others place it in the highest heaven. Others connect it with the horizon reached during the night journey.

The differences follow from the two readings of the whole passage. If the account concerns the first revelation, the earthly horizon fits. If it concerns the ascension, the heavenly location fits.

The first reading is better supported by the parallel in at-Takwīr and by the sūrah's own later reference to a second occasion at the Lote-Tree, which implies that the occasion described here is the first.

**The Coordinate as Part of the Report**

The verse supplies what a report of an extraordinary event needs: a place. Without it the account would be a claim about an interior state, and interior states cannot be examined by anyone except the person having them.

The Qur'an's method here is consistent with its treatment of other unseen matters. It gives coordinates — a horizon, a nearness measured in bow-lengths, a Lote-Tree at a boundary — while withholding description of what was seen at each of those places.

The withholding and the supplying work together. Description would invite endless interpretation; coordinates allow a plain yes or no. The effect is to make the account a report rather than a metaphor, and a report is the kind of thing that can be believed or disbelieved on evidence about the reporter.

That is exactly what the sūrah asks for. It closes the account by turning to the audience and asking how they can dispute what he saw — a question that presupposes a report, a witness, and a place.

**A Location Everyone Can Check**

The horizon is the one part of the unseen event that every listener can verify for himself. Nobody has to accept a claim about it; anyone can look up and see where the sky meets the field of vision.

That anchoring is deliberate. The sūrah is asking its audience to accept an account of something only one person witnessed, and it attaches the account to something everyone sees daily. The method is not proof; it is a way of making the account concrete enough to be taken seriously.

The Qur'an uses the same method with its oaths. It swears by the dawn, the night, the fig, the mountain — visible things — and then states what is unseen. The oath supplies the anchor and the statement supplies the claim.

Here the anchor is inside the narrative rather than in front of it, but the function is identical. A reader who has seen a horizon can form a picture of what is being described, and a picture is more than an abstraction when it comes to deciding whether a report is credible.

**What the Verse Adds to the Sequence**

Three verses have described the one who taught: powerful, sound, and rising to his form. This verse places him. The next two describe the approach and the distance, and the verse after that records the giving.

The sequence is that of an eyewitness account: who, in what condition, where, then how close, then what happened. Each element is added in the order a witness would give it, and no element is explained, justified, or repeated.

That restraint is the passage's strength. It does not argue that the event happened; it describes it, and leaves the reader to decide what to do with the description. An argument can be met with a counter-argument, but a plain sequence of details has to be either accepted as testimony or dismissed as invention, and the sūrah has already dealt with the charge of invention in its first three verses.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:8
> then he approached ˹the Prophet˺, coming so close

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs of Nearness**

The verse uses two words for approaching, and the doubling is emphatic. *Danā* means he drew near; *tadallā* is an intensive or reflexive form from the same root, meaning he lowered or inclined himself, coming down toward something.

The combination describes an approach that is both movement and condescension. The one approaching does not merely close distance; he comes down.

That sense matters for the reading of the passage. The subject of the two verbs, on the majority reading, is the angel, and the object is the Prophet ﷺ. A being of the description given in the previous verses inclined toward a human being, and the verse records the act.

**Descent as the Qur'an's Verb for Revelation**

The verb of approach fits a larger pattern in the Qur'an's language about revelation, which is consistently a language of descent. The trustworthy spirit brought it down into your heart (26:193–194). We sent this Qur'an down on the Night of Glory (97:1). Blessed is the One Who sent down the Standard to His servant (25:1).

The direction is always downward and outward: from God, through the angel, to the recipient. Nothing in that vocabulary describes an ascent achieved by the recipient's own effort.

The verse here is part of that pattern. The one who approaches is the one sent, and the approach is the delivery. That is why the majority reading takes the angel as the subject — it keeps the direction of the whole account consistent.

**Approach Without Overwhelming**

The nearness described is close but survivable, and the distinction matters. The Qur'an records what happens when a creature asks for direct disclosure: Mūsā asked to see God, the mountain was levelled to dust, and Mūsā collapsed unconscious (7:143).

Nothing of that kind is reported here. The approach is measured, the distance is given, and the next verse describes the recipient's heart as steady rather than shaken.

The contrast shows that the event described belongs to a different category from a theophany. It is a delivery, and deliveries happen at a range where the recipient can function. The verse records the range, and the following verse records the steadiness that the range made possible.

**Who Approached Whom**

The commentators offer three readings. The majority take the subject as the angel and the object as the Prophet ﷺ: he drew near and came close. Others take the subject as God: God drew near to His servant. A third reading divides the verbs between the two — he drew near, and He came close.

The majority reading fits the sequence better. The preceding verses describe the angel's form and location; an approach by that subject continues the narrative without a shift. The divine reading requires a change of subject that the text does not mark.

The third reading has a devotional appeal and is supported by the general Qur'anic theme of nearness, but it is less likely here on grammatical grounds.

**The Approach as Condescension**

The second verb, *tadallā*, carries a sense the first does not: lowering or inclining oneself toward something below. Its presence in the pair adds a direction to the approach.

The one who approaches is not being drawn up; he is coming down. That is consistent with the whole Qur'anic vocabulary of revelation, in which descent is the standard direction and ascent is never the means by which the message arrives.

The theological point is small but real. Revelation in this Book is an act of accommodation: the infinite addresses the finite in the finite's own terms, in its language, at its range. The verb of lowering describes that accommodation in a single word, and the verse that follows measures the range at which it occurred.

**Nearness as a Category**

Nearness between God and a servant is a theme the Qur'an develops elsewhere. The famous ḥadīth qudsī in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī describes it: a servant draws near to God through the obligatory acts, and continues drawing near through supererogatory acts until God loves him.

The nearness in this verse is of a different order. It is not the nearness of a servant approaching through worship but the nearness of a messenger arriving with a message, and it is measured in the next verse.

That distinction keeps the passage within its subject. The sūrah is describing the delivery of a revelation, not the ascent of a soul, and the nearness described is the nearness of delivery.

**What the Approach Excludes**

The description removes two possible characterisations of the event. It was not a distant communication — an inspiration received without contact. And it was not an overwhelming manifestation that destroyed the recipient.

The Qur'an records the second possibility elsewhere. When Mūsā asked to see God directly, the mountain was levelled to dust and Mūsā collapsed unconscious (7:143). The event described here has none of that: an approach, a measured distance, and a revelation received with the heart unshaken.

That contrast is important for the verse that follows. The recipient's composure is reported as a fact, and the composure is possible because the approach was ordered rather than overwhelming.

**The Verse in the Sequence**

The narrative has moved steadily: attribute, form, location, approach. Each verse adds one element and does not return to the previous one.

The economy is remarkable for a passage describing the unseen. Nothing is repeated, nothing is explained, and no theological comment is inserted into the account.

That economy is the reason the passage has been debated for centuries. A description this spare allows several readings, and the sūrah does not resolve them, which suggests that the resolution was not the point.

**The Measurement That Follows**

The approach is not left unquantified. The next verse gives a distance, and the giving of a distance is the most striking feature of the whole passage.

A report that says someone drew near is vague. A report that gives a measurement is a claim, and a claim invites examination. The sūrah makes that claim, and it makes it in the units its audience used.

The verse therefore prepares what follows by narrowing the description from movement to position, and the position is the last detail the account will give before turning to the revelation itself.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:9
> that he was only two armslengths away or even less.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qāba Qawsayn: The Measure**

The phrase *qāba qawsayn* means the length of two bows. A *qaws* is an Arab bow, and *qāb* is the measure of its length or of the curve it describes. The unit was familiar to the audience: bows were everyday objects, and their length was a standard rough measure.

Using a customary measure for an event of this kind is a striking choice. The verse does not say the distance was beyond description or beyond measure; it gives a figure.

The figure is small. Two bow-lengths is the distance at which two people can speak without raising their voices, and the verse describes the delivery of a revelation at that range.

**The Particle Aw**

The word rendered as or is *aw*, and in this position the commentators read it in three ways: as expressing approximation (about two bow-lengths), as expressing a choice between alternatives (two bow-lengths, or less), and as intensification (nay, closer).

The readings converge on the same point: the distance was not more than two bow-lengths, and may have been less. The particle functions to reduce rather than to expand.

That direction is significant. A description that approximates upward would suggest majesty through distance; this one approximates downward, toward intimacy.

**Precision as Testimony**

The giving of a measurement turns the account into testimony. A witness who says he saw something at a certain distance is making a claim that can be assessed, and the claim carries the risk of being wrong.

The verse accepts that risk. It does not hedge the distance with language of mystery, and it does not present the nearness as ineffable.

That is consistent with the sūrah's purpose. The accusation being answered was that the revelation came from the Prophet's own mind. An answer to that accusation needs an account with specifics, and the specifics are what this verse supplies.

**Nearness Measured Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The Qur'an uses two other measures of nearness, and comparing them clarifies this one. God is described as closer to a person than his jugular vein (50:16), and as truly near to those who call on Him (2:186).

Both of those are statements about God's relation to creatures in general, and neither is spatial in the way this verse is. They describe knowledge and responsiveness rather than position.

The measurement here belongs to a narrative. A distance is given between two described parties at a described moment, and the units are physical. The difference in kind between the two uses of nearness is part of why the commentators disagree about the subject — a physical distance fits an angel more readily than it fits God.

**Bow-Lengths in Arabic Measure**

The choice of the bow as a unit is not arbitrary. The bow was an object every Arab household understood, and its length was a common rough measure for short distances.

Using it here places the event in the ordinary vocabulary of the audience. The verse does not invent a unit for an extraordinary occurrence; it borrows one from daily life.

That borrowing is the same method used throughout this passage. The horizon, the approach, the distance, the servant, the heart — all are ordinary words applied to an event that is not ordinary. The ordinariness of the language is what makes the account reportable, and a reportable account is what an accusation of invention requires as an answer.

**The Two Readings of the Distance**

If the subject of the approach is the angel, the distance is between the angel and the Prophet ﷺ. If the subject is God, the distance is between God and His servant, and the measurement becomes a much larger theological claim.

The majority reading takes the first, and the following verse supports it: then Allah revealed to His servant what He revealed, which describes a transmission rather than an immediate presence.

The second reading has been favoured by those who read the whole passage as describing a vision of God. The disagreement about this verse is therefore a disagreement about the whole passage, and it is examined more fully in the verses that follow.

**The Two Arcs**

Some commentators read *qawsayn* as two arcs rather than two bows, and see in them the two halves of a circle — the arc of descent by which creation comes from God, and the arc of ascent by which it returns.

The reading is elegant and has a long history in the mystical commentaries. It is not required by the Arabic, which most naturally denotes bows, and it turns a measurement into a symbol.

Weighing them, the plain reading is stronger: the verse is describing a distance in a narrative, and the narrative's other details are spatial rather than symbolic. The symbolic reading can be held as an additional layer without displacing the plain sense.

**What the Distance Does Not Say**

The measurement describes proximity and nothing else. It does not describe contact, union, or identity, and the sūrah does not draw any of those inferences from it.

That restraint matters, because the verse has been read in traditions that go considerably further than the text does. A distance given in a narrative is a fact about a narrative; it is not a statement about the nature of God or the possibility of union with Him.

The Qur'an's own statements on those matters are separate and clear: no vision encompasses Him (6:103), and He does not speak to a human being except by revelation, from behind a veil, or through a messenger (42:51). The verse here does not override either, and reading it as though it did imports a claim the text does not make.

**Why the Verse Does Not Explain Itself**

The verse states a distance and stops. It does not say what the nearness meant, what was felt, or what it implies about the relationship between God and His messenger.

The silence is deliberate. The sūrah is establishing that the revelation was received from outside, and the measurement serves that purpose exactly. Anything added would invite speculation about matters the text has chosen not to address.

The next verse turns to what happened at that distance: a servant received a revelation, and what he received is left unnamed.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:10
> Then Allah revealed to His servant what He revealed ˹through Gabriel˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Deliberate Vagueness**

The verse describes a revelation and does not name it. *Mā awḥā* — what He revealed — leaves the content in a relative clause, and the withholding is the verse's most striking feature.

A text concerned with establishing that a revelation occurred would be expected to specify what was revealed. This one does not, and the omission has been explained by the commentators as reverence: the thing revealed is too exalted to be named in the sentence that reports its giving.

The effect is to keep the emphasis on the act rather than the object. The verse is about transmission, and the transmission is complete in the telling even without the content.

**Three Readings of the Chain**

The commentators identify three possible arrangements of the transmission. First: Gabriel revealed to the Prophet ﷺ what God had revealed to Gabriel, in which case the first pronoun refers to the angel. Second: God revealed directly to the Prophet ﷺ. Third: God revealed to Gabriel, who then transmitted.

The readings differ in whether the angel is the recipient, the transmitter, or both, and the Arabic allows each because the pronouns are not fully specified.

The first and third readings agree on the substance: God is the source, the angel is the intermediary, and the Prophet ﷺ is the recipient. The second reading removes the intermediary, and it is less consistent with the surrounding verses, which describe the angel's approach.

**Why the Ambiguity Is Tolerated**

The Qur'an does not resolve the ambiguity here, and the unresolved state has a function. What matters for the sūrah's argument is that the revelation came from God and reached the Prophet ﷺ. The precise mechanics of the transmission are secondary.

That priority is consistent with the rest of the Book. The Qur'an describes revelation's modes in general terms (42:51) without providing a theory of how each mode operates. The emphasis is always on the source and the recipient rather than on the process.

The verse therefore states what needs to be stated and leaves what does not. The reader is not given a metaphysics of revelation; he is told that a servant received something from his Lord.

**“His Servant”**

The recipient is called *ʿabd*, servant, and the word is chosen with care. It is the same word used in the opening of Sūrah al-Isrāʾ: glory be to the One Who took His servant by night (17:1), and in the description of the revelation's descent in Sūrah al-Baqarah and al-Furqān.

The title establishes the relationship on which the whole account depends. A servant does not originate; he receives and delivers. The accusation of invention assumed a man speaking for himself, and the title removes that assumption in one word.

It also answers the charge from the other direction. A person who claims to be God's partner would not be described by God as His servant, and the description is given by God rather than claimed by the man.

**The Servant Title Across the Qur'an**

The description of the Prophet ﷺ as God's servant is not local to this verse; it is a standing title used at the most important moments of the account.

Blessed is the One Who sent down the Standard to His servant, so that he may be a warner to the whole world (25:1). Glory be to the One Who took His servant by night (17:1). The revelation is described as sent down to your heart (26:194), addressed to the same servant.

The consistency of the title across chapters and occasions suggests it is doing doctrinal work. It fixes the Prophet's position as recipient rather than originator, and it does so in the very verses that make the largest claims about what he received.

That is why the verse uses it here. The claim being established is the highest in the passage, and the title attached to the recipient keeps the claim from being misread.

**Revelation Given at a Point in Time**

The verse describes an act with a beginning: then He revealed. The particle *fa* marks sequence, and the sequence is temporal — after the approach, the giving.

The Qur'an insists on this temporality elsewhere. The revelation came down on a named night (97:1; 44:3), in a named language, to a named person, over a period of years. It is not an eternal truth grasped by a mind but an event that occurred in history.

The temporality is part of the answer to the charge of invention. A man's thoughts have no arrival time; a revelation does, and the sūrah gives one. The verse in question is the moment of arrival, described in a single clause.

**What Was Revealed**

Although the verse does not name the content, the sūrah identifies it later: he certainly saw some of his Lord's greatest signs (53:18). The revelation included both the message and the signs shown to the one who received it.

That distinction matters for understanding the event. The Prophet ﷺ received a text to deliver and a vision to testify to, and the sūrah treats both as parts of the same giving.

The vision is what the later verses of the chapter dispute, and the text is what the earlier sūrah defended. The verse here covers both without specifying either.

**The Verse's Function**

The narrative sequence is now complete: the teacher, his power, his form, his location, his approach, the distance, and the giving. Eight verses have produced an account of a revelation's arrival.

The verse stands at the end of that sequence as its conclusion. Everything before it described the arrangement; this verse records the result.

The next verse turns to the recipient's response, and the response is stated in a single negation: his heart did not falter at what he saw.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:11
> The ˹Prophet’s˺ heart did not doubt what he saw.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Heart as the Organ of Vision**

The verse attributes the seeing to the heart. *Al-fuʾād* in Qur'anic usage is the seat of understanding and conviction, not merely of feeling, and the Book distinguishes it from the eye while connecting the two.

The distinction matters here. A vision of the eye can be doubted by the mind; a vision of the heart is conviction itself. The verse states that there was no gap between what was seen and what was accepted.

That is the claim being made. The recipient of the revelation did not hesitate about it, and the absence of hesitation is reported as a fact about the event rather than as a virtue of the recipient.

**Mā Kadhaba: He Did Not Lie, and He Did Not Falter**

The verb allows two readings, both supported. The first takes it as *kadhaba*, he did not lie — the heart did not falsify what it perceived. The second takes it as *kadhdhaba*, it did not falter or waver — the perception was steady.

The two readings describe the same reliability from different angles. A heart that does not lie reports accurately; a heart that does not falter perceives without wavering.

Both readings answer the same objection, which was that the experience was confused or imagined. The verse denies confusion and denies deception in a single phrase.

**The Disagreement About What Was Seen**

The commentators differ on the object of the vision. Some read the passage as describing the Prophet's seeing of the angel, which fits the preceding verses. Others read it as a vision of God, and support the reading with reports from Ibn ʿAbbās that the Prophet ﷺ saw his Lord.

The disagreement is old and it is genuine. Both positions are attributed to Companions, and both have been defended by later scholars.

The verses that follow describe a second occasion at the Lote-Tree, and some read that occasion as the one in which the vision occurred, which allows both readings to stand: the angel on the horizon, and something greater at the boundary.

**The Heart in the Qur'an's Account of Perception**

The verse's attribution of seeing to the heart follows the Qur'an's general account of how people perceive truth. It is not the eyes that are blind, but the hearts in the chests that grow blind (22:46). Elsewhere the failure is described in the same terms: they have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with (7:179).

In that account the organs of sense supply data and the heart supplies recognition. A person can see and not recognise, which is the condition the Qur'an attributes to those who reject the signs.

This verse describes the opposite condition. The perception arrived and the recognition followed without a gap. That is what the negation states, and it is the highest claim the Qur'an makes about the reliability of its recipient.

**Why the Recipient's State Is Reported**

The verse is not only about the event; it is about the witness. An account of an unseen occurrence depends on the reliability of the one reporting it, and the sūrah addresses that dependence directly.

The charge of invention implied a defect in the witness — that he was confused, deluded, or self-serving. The verse answers by describing the witness's condition at the moment of the event: steady, accurate, undoubting.

That is why the verse reports the heart rather than the content. The content is disputed; the condition of the one who received it is a fact about a person the audience had known for decades. The claim is placed where it can be tested.

**ʿĀʾishah's Position and Its Grounds**

ʿĀʾishah denied the vision of God in explicit terms. When Masrūq asked her whether the Prophet ﷺ had seen his Lord, she replied that his hair stood on end at the question, and she named three claims that constitute a great lie — among them that Muhammad ﷺ saw his Lord (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 177).

Her grounds were scriptural, and she cited them: no vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision (6:103). She also cited the verse stating that God does not speak to a human being except by revelation, from behind a veil, or through a messenger-angel (42:51).

The argument is not a denial of the event described in this sūrah. It is a denial that the event was a vision of the divine essence with the physical eye, and she located the vision in the angel instead.

**Abū Dharr's Question**

The other side of the record comes from Abū Dharr, who asked the Prophet ﷺ directly: did you see your Lord? The answer given was: He is light — how could I see Him? In another wording: I saw light (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 178).

The report is significant because it comes from the Prophet ﷺ himself and is not a later inference. The answer does not affirm a vision of the essence; it describes an obstacle of light.

The commentators who hold that the Prophet ﷺ saw his Lord read the report as describing a vision veiled by light rather than its absence, and they cite the saying that His veil is light. The report itself is consistent with either reading, which is why the disagreement persists.

**What Both Sides Agree On**

The disagreement is narrower than it appears. Both sides agree that the event described in this passage was real, that it was a perception rather than an inference, and that the heart of the one who perceived it did not falter.

Both sides also agree that no creature comprehends God, and that whatever was seen was seen within limits. The difference is about the object of the vision, not about its reality or its effect.

That common ground is what the verse itself states, and the verse states it without resolving the question. The heart did not doubt what he saw — and the sūrah leaves what he saw to be examined in the verses that follow.

---
## Sūrah an-Najm 53:12
> How can you ˹O pagans˺ then dispute with him regarding what he saw?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question Turns on the Disputants**

The verse changes direction. Eleven verses have described an event; this one addresses the people arguing about it. *Afa-tumārūnahu ʿalā mā yarā* — will you dispute with him about what he sees?

The verb *tumārūn* carries more than disagreement. It denotes resisting, contesting, pushing back against something presented. The word choice describes an active opposition rather than a puzzled enquiry.

The shift from narrative to address is abrupt and deliberate. The sūrah has given its account, and now it asks what the account is being met with — not evidence, but contest.

The structure of the question exposes its own problem. The disputants were not present at the event. They had no observation of their own to set against the report, only a refusal to accept it.

That asymmetry is the point. A dispute between two witnesses can be resolved by comparing testimony; a dispute between a witness and someone who was absent can only be resolved by assessing the witness. The verse reduces the matter to that.

The question therefore is not asking them to produce a better account of the event. It is asking what their dispute consists of, given that they were nowhere near it.

**What Their Objection Actually Was**

The charge in circulation was that the experience was interior — a dream, a fabrication, or the product of a disturbed mind. The sūrah has already answered the third possibility in its opening verses and the second in the verses that followed.

Against the first, the account supplied coordinates: a horizon, a form, an approach, a measured distance, a location at a boundary. Those are not the features of a dream, and the sūrah presented them as features of an event.

The verse's reproach is that the dispute continued after the account was given. The objection had been answered in detail, and the answering changed nothing.

By asking how they can dispute what he saw, the verse classifies the preceding verses as testimony rather than as doctrine. Testimony is accepted or rejected on the basis of the witness, and the witness here had been known to the audience for decades.

That is the method the sūrah has used from the beginning. It called him your companion in the second verse, appealing to acquaintance rather than argument. This verse repeats the appeal in the form of a question.

The classification matters for how the passage should be read. It is not offering a metaphysics of vision; it is reporting an event and asking whether the reporter is credible.

**The Dispute Over the Night Journey**

The commentators connect this verse either to the vision of the angel or to the night journey and ascension, and the second connection has a documented history.

The Qur'an records the journey in a single verse: glory be to the One Who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, so that We may show him some of Our signs (17:1). The reports describe the Meccan response as immediate and hostile — a journey described as taking a month was said to have taken a night, and some who had accepted the message withdrew.

The dispute was therefore not private or theoretical. It was public, it cost the community members, and this verse answers it in the same register: you are contesting what he saw.

The verse implies a standard for legitimate contest. A person who disputes a report should have either a contrary observation or a defect in the report — an inconsistency, an impossibility, a motive to lie.

The sūrah has already removed the third: it established that the man is neither astray nor deluded, and that he does not speak from whim. The second is not available, since the account is internally consistent and matches the parallel description in at-Takwīr. The first was never offered.

What remains is bare refusal, and the verse names it as such by asking the question rather than answering it.

**The Witness and the Absent Party**

A dispute has two parties, and the verse draws attention to the difference between them. One of the two was present at the event and is reporting it. The other was not present, has no report of his own, and is contesting the first party's account on the basis of unwillingness rather than observation.

Courts handle this situation by examining the witness. They ask whether he was in a position to see, whether he has a motive to lie, and whether his account is consistent with itself and with other evidence. The sūrah has already answered all three questions in the affirmative: he was in the position described, he has been shown to speak from revelation rather than whim, and his account matches the description given elsewhere in the Qur'an of an angel seen on the horizon.

The verse's question is therefore not a rhetorical flourish. It is a request that the audience apply to this report the same standards they would apply to any other, and a suggestion that they have not done so.

**What the Verse Does Not Do**

The verse does not threaten the disputants, and it does not describe their fate. It asks a question and leaves the answer with them.

That restraint is worth noticing in a passage that has just described an extraordinary event. A text intending to overwhelm would have followed the vision with a warning. This one follows it with an enquiry about the reasoning of the people contesting it, which treats them as capable of answering.

The same restraint governs the whole of the sūrah's opening movement. The vision is described without embellishment, the witness is characterised without praise, and the disputants are addressed without insult. The account is offered in the plainest available form, on the assumption that plainness is sufficient.

**A Question That Closes a Section**

Structurally this verse ends the first movement of the sūrah. The account is complete and the contest is identified. What follows turns away from the vision entirely and takes up the idols, the angels, and intercession.

The turn is not a change of subject so much as a change of ground. The dispute about the vision cannot be settled by argument, so the sūrah moves to the things the disputants actually believe and examines those instead.

That is the method of the whole chapter: where a claim about the unseen cannot be verified by the audience, the sūrah examines the audience's own claims, which can be.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:13
> And he certainly saw that ˹angel descend˺ a second time

**Expanded Commentary**

**Nazla Ukhrā: A Second Descent**

The verse states that the seeing happened twice. *Nazlah ukhrā* — another descent, a second time. The noun is from the root of descending, and it describes an occasion of coming down rather than a movement in progress.

The emphasis particle *laqad*, certainly, marks the statement as confirmed. The sūrah is not adding a detail casually; it is asserting a second occasion against anyone who might treat the first as a single unusual event.

Two occasions of the same kind establish a pattern. A single vision can be explained away as an aberration; a repeated one requires a different account.

The tradition records two appearances of the angel in his created form. The first was at the beginning of the revelation, at Mount Ḥirāʾ, when the angel appeared filling the horizon. The second was during the ascension.

The earlier verses of this sūrah describe the first — the horizon, the approach, the measured distance. This verse introduces the second, and the following verses give its location at the Lote-Tree of the utmost limit.

The division is what makes the chapter's structure intelligible. It contains two accounts, and readers who treat it as one narrative find the sequence confusing.

**The Alternative Reading**

Some commentators read the two occasions as two visions of God rather than of the angel, locating them at the rending of the highest veil above the Throne and at the Lote-Tree of the boundary.

The reading depends on taking the whole passage as describing divine disclosure, which was discussed in the earlier verses. Its difficulty is the same: the surrounding language is spatial and observational in a way that fits an angelic appearance more naturally.

The angelic reading also has the support of the parallel passage in at-Takwīr, which describes seeing the angel on the clear horizon. Weighing the two, the majority position is better grounded in the text.

The repetition serves the argument the chapter opened with. The charge was that the revelation came from the Prophet's own mind; the answer is an account of external contact, and two instances of it are harder to dismiss than one.

There is also a chronological function. The first occasion belongs to the beginning of the mission, the second to a later event. Together they bracket a period, which suggests that the mode of contact was not a single interruption but a feature of the prophetic experience.

The verse does not elaborate on either point. It states the second occasion and moves to its location.

**The Certainty Particle**

*Laqad raʾāhu* — he certainly saw him. The combination of the emphatic particle with the past tense is the Qur'an's strongest form of assertion about a past event.

The strength is proportionate to the dispute. The verse follows immediately after the question about contesting what he saw, and it answers the contest with the plainest possible affirmation.

That pairing is worth noting. The sūrah does not meet the dispute with argument or with evidence the disputants could examine; it meets it with an emphatic statement, on the ground that the report is testimony and testimony is either accepted or rejected.

The earlier verses described an appearance with a location on the horizon and a measured approach. This verse adds a second appearance with a different location, and the difference is the information.

Two locations at two times, both described with the same observational language. The account acquires the shape of a record rather than a vision-story, and that shape is what the sūrah needs for its purpose.

The verse also prepares the reader for what comes next. The first account ended with the revelation given; the second will end with the signs seen.

**The Interval Between the Two Sightings**

The two occasions named in this passage are separated by years. The first belongs to the beginning of the prophetic mission at Mount Ḥirāʾ, when the revelation started; the second belongs to the journey recorded in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ, which the tradition places in the later Meccan period.

The interval matters for the argument the sūrah is making. Two appearances separated by a long stretch of ordinary life are not a single sustained trance or a continuous state of exaltation. Between them the Prophet lived in Mecca, was opposed by his own tribe, lost his wife Khadījah and his uncle Abū Ṭālib, and continued delivering a message that brought him nothing but hardship.

That context is what makes the second sighting credible as a report rather than as a psychological episode. It came to a man whose circumstances had given him every reason to abandon the claim, and it did not change his conduct or his message.

**Seeing in the Created Form**

The commentators are agreed on one point about both occasions: what was seen was the angel in the form in which he was created, not in the form in which he usually came with revelation.

The distinction is important. The Qur'an describes revelation as coming through a spirit sent down upon the heart (26:193–194), and it describes the angel appearing to Maryam in the likeness of a well-proportioned man (19:17). The ordinary mode of contact did not involve a visible appearance at all.

What this sūrah reports is the exception to that mode — two occasions on which the angel appeared as he is, and the appearance is described in observational language: a horizon, a form, a distance, a location. The verse's assertion of a second occasion therefore asserts a second exception, and exceptions of that kind are what the audience found hardest to accept.

**A Report That Invites Examination**

The verse's brevity is part of its method. It asserts a second sighting and gives no detail beyond it, leaving the detail to the next three verses.

That economy is characteristic of the passage. Nothing is elaborated for effect; each verse adds one fact and stops. The cumulative effect is a spare account with several checkable elements — times, places, and a description of the observer's composure.

The reader is given enough to decide, and not enough to be overwhelmed. The next verse supplies the location of the second descent.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:14
> at the Lote Tree of the most extreme limit ˹in the seventh heaven˺—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sidrat al-Muntahā**

The location of the second vision is named, and the name is one of the most discussed in the Qur'an. *Sidrat al-muntahā*, the Lote-Tree of the utmost boundary.

The name has two parts. The first identifies a tree; the second identifies a limit. Together they describe a specific object at a specific edge, and the combination is unusual enough that the commentators have debated it for centuries.

The naming follows the method of the preceding verses. A location is given rather than a description, and the location is stated in terms that imply a geography of the unseen.

The *sidr* is a real tree, the lote or buckthorn, well known in Arabia. It grows in dry ground, gives dense shade, and is valued in a landscape where shade is scarce. The Qur'an mentions it elsewhere: the people of the garden in Sūrah Sabaʾ were given a land with two gardens and the lote-tree was among what they had (34:16), and the people of the right are described as being among lote-trees without thorns (56:28).

The choice of a familiar tree for a location at the edge of creation is deliberate. The Qur'an consistently describes the unseen using objects its audience knew, and the lote-tree is one of the most ordinary trees in the Arabian landscape.

An ordinary tree at the boundary of everything is an image that resists allegory. It is not a symbol of growth or life; it is a marker.

**Muntahā: The Utmost Limit**

The second part of the name is the significant one. *Muntahā* is the place where something ends — the terminus, the point beyond which there is no going.

The commentators explain it in two ways. Some say the tree is at the limit because it marks the end of Paradise or the end of the created order, so that nothing passes beyond it and nothing beyond it is known. Others locate it at the boundary between the sixth and the seventh heaven.

Both readings agree on the function: the tree marks an edge. That is why the vision is located there, and why the sūrah names it rather than describing it.

The majority position places the tree in the seventh heaven, to the right of the Throne. The alternative places it at the boundary between the sixth and seventh, with the Garden of Refuge understood as the seventh heaven itself.

The difference matters for the geography of the account but not for its substance. Either way the location is at the farthest reachable point of the created order, and the vision occurred there.

The Qur'an does not resolve the question, and the unresolved state is consistent with its treatment of the unseen throughout: names and functions are given, maps are not.

**The Connection to the Night Journey**

The verse belongs to the account of the ascension, which the Qur'an records separately: glory be to the One Who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, so that We may show him some of Our signs (17:1).

The stated purpose in that verse — to show him signs — corresponds to what this sūrah reports at the end of the passage: he certainly saw some of his Lord's greatest signs. The two chapters describe the same event from different angles.

That correspondence supports the reading that this verse locates the second of the two occasions mentioned in the verse before it.

A boundary could have been marked by a wall, a gate, or a line. The Qur'an marks it with a tree, and the choice is worth considering.

A tree is a living thing that grows where it is planted and stays there. It is the opposite of a barrier: it does not exclude, it occupies. A boundary marked by a tree is a boundary reached rather than imposed.

That fits the account. The vision occurred at the limit of the created order, and the limit is described as a place where something grows — the last living thing before what lies beyond.

**The Tree in the Reports of the Ascension**

The reports of the night journey mention the Lote-Tree, and the descriptions given in them are worth recording with their status noted. The accounts describe a tree of enormous size, with leaves like the ears of elephants and fruits like large jars, and they describe it as being covered by angels.

These details come from the exegetical tradition rather than from the Qur'an, and they vary between reports. What the verse itself states is only the name, the limit, and the nearness of the Garden. Everything beyond that is commentary, and the honest way to treat it is as the tradition's attempt to picture what the text declined to picture.

The variety of the descriptions is itself informative. Where a text gives a name without a description, later generations supply descriptions, and the supplies differ. The verse's silence produced a literature; it did not produce a settled image.

**Naming What Cannot Be Mapped**

The Qur'an's treatment of the unseen follows a consistent rule. It names places and beings, and it describes their functions, but it does not provide the dimensions that would allow a map to be drawn.

The rule is visible here. A tree is named, a limit is stated, and a garden is placed near the tree. No distances are given, no directions, no account of what lies beyond the limit or how the regions relate to one another.

That restraint protects the account from a particular failure. A detailed map of the unseen could be checked against nothing and would invite endless speculation; a set of names and functions can be believed or disbelieved without the believer being committed to a cosmology. The sūrah asks for the second kind of response, and the verse is constructed to allow it.

**What the Verse Establishes**

The verse gives the second sighting a location at the edge of everything. The first sighting was on the horizon, which is a boundary of the visible world; this one is at a boundary of the created order.

The progression is deliberate. The sūrah moves from a location anyone can see to a location nobody can reach, and it describes both in the same spare style.

The next verse adds what is near that location, and the addition places the boundary of creation beside the Garden.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:15
> near which is the Garden of ˹Eternal˺ Residence—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Jannat al-Maʾwā**

The verse names a second location and gives its relation to the first: near the Lote-Tree is the Garden of Refuge. *Jannat al-maʾwā* — the garden of shelter, of dwelling, of the place one returns to for safety.

The word *maʾwā* is formed from a root meaning to take shelter or to give shelter. It is used in the Qur'an for a place of lodging and for refuge in danger.

Applying it to the Garden defines the Garden by its function rather than by its contents. Paradise here is not described as a reward but as a shelter.

The choice of name is significant given how the Qur'an describes paradise elsewhere. It is a place of rivers, fruit, thrones, and companions, and those descriptions emphasise abundance.

This name emphasises safety. A refuge is what a person needs when something is pursuing him, and the Qur'an's descriptions of the other destination are consistently about exposure — no shelter, no shade, no escape.

The verse in Sūrah al-Mursalāt makes the contrast explicit: proceed into a shade of smoke rising in three columns, providing neither coolness nor shelter from the flames (77:30–31). The word for shelter there is the same family as *maʾwā*. What is refused there is named here.

**The Spatial Relation**

The verse places the Garden near the Lote-Tree, and the nearness has been read in two ways. On the majority reading, the tree is in the seventh heaven and the Garden is at that limit, so the two are adjacent features of the same region.

On the alternative reading, the tree marks the boundary between two heavens and the Garden of Refuge is the seventh heaven itself, which would make the nearness one of containment rather than adjacency.

Neither reading changes the point. The boundary of the created order is located beside the place of shelter, and the verse states the proximity as a fact about the geography.

The Qur'an uses the same phrase for a specific company. Whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be with those Allah has blessed — the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous — and what excellent companions they are (4:69). The description in that verse is of a company rather than a place, and the word for the righteous there, *ṣiddīqūn* and *ṣāliḥūn*, matches the categories the Qur'an uses for the people of the Garden.

The commentators connect the Garden of Refuge with the place where the spirits of the prophets, the righteous, and the witnesses take refuge, which is consistent with the name.

That connection gives the location a population. The boundary of creation is not empty; beside it is a garden, and the garden is occupied.

**The Verse's Function in the Sequence**

Three verses have now described the setting of the second vision: the Lote-Tree, the limit, and the Garden beside it. The account has acquired a landscape.

The economy continues. Each verse adds one feature and does not describe it. No dimensions are given for the tree, no description of the Garden, no account of what lies beyond the limit.

That restraint keeps the account within the limits of a report. A description would invite speculation; a set of named locations invites belief or disbelief, which is the choice the sūrah is asking for.

The naming belongs to a wider pattern. The Qur'an describes God as a refuge and describes the believers as being brought into shelter, and it describes the Fire as the absence of both.

The pattern gives the verse its weight in this passage. The vision is located at the farthest point of creation, and at that point is the Garden — the destination the sūrah's audience was disputing.

The location is therefore not incidental. It places the disputed reward at the edge of everything, beside the tree that marks the limit, and it does so in the same spare style as the rest of the account.

**Refuge as the Qur'an's Word for Salvation**

The naming of the Garden as a refuge belongs to a wider usage in which shelter is the image of salvation and exposure is the image of ruin.

The Qur'an describes God as the one who gives shelter and describes the believers as being brought under it, and it describes the day of judgment as a day with no shelter except His. The word family runs through those descriptions, and the verse here applies it to the destination itself.

The usage explains why this particular name is chosen at this point in the passage. The account has reached the farthest point of creation, and the thing named there is not a throne or a treasury but a shelter — the need that every human being has and that the Qur'an says is finally met in one place only.

**The Garden Named in the Nearest Terms**

The Qur'an's descriptions of paradise usually work through abundance: gardens with rivers running beneath them, fruit in season, thrones raised, drink that does not intoxicate. Those descriptions appeal to appetite and to rest.

This verse appeals to something more basic. A refuge is what a person needs before he needs anything else, and the naming puts that need first.

The effect on the account is to keep it grounded. The vision is located at the edge of creation, in a region the audience could not picture, and the one feature named there is a shelter — an ordinary word for an ordinary need, placed at the limit of everything. The pairing is the verse's whole method: the unfamiliar location is described in the most familiar terms available.

**What the Juxtaposition Does**

The Lote-Tree and the Garden are named together, and the pairing has an effect. A boundary suggests exclusion; a garden suggests welcome. The verse puts them side by side.

The reader is left with an image rather than a doctrine: the edge of creation, a tree marking it, and shelter beside it. The image is available to anyone who has stood at the edge of cultivated ground in a dry country and seen a tree marking where the settled land ends.

The next verse describes what was covering the tree at the moment of the vision, and the description is the most reticent in the passage.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:16
> while the Lote Tree was overwhelmed with ˹heavenly˺ splendours!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Yaghshā Mā Yaghshā**

The verse is built from a verb and its own undefined object: *idh yaghshā as-sidrata mā yaghshā* — when there covered the Lote-Tree that which covered it.

The construction refuses to name the covering. The verb is repeated, the object is left as a relative pronoun, and the sentence ends without saying what was there.

That is not a gap in the text. It is a deliberate rhetorical form, and the commentators have recognised it as such: the structure indicates something that cannot be adequately described, and the withholding is the description.

The Qur'an is not generally reticent. It describes the Fire in detail, the Garden in detail, and the day of judgment in detail. Its descriptions of the unseen are usually concrete enough to picture.

Here the description stops. The reason given by the commentators is reverence: what covered the tree was of a kind that language would diminish by naming.

The effect on the reader is specific. A named covering could be imagined and then judged inadequate; an unnamed one leaves the imagination without material. The verse produces awe by subtraction rather than by addition.

**The Readings of What Covered It**

The commentators supply what the text does not. Some describe the covering as light and brilliance, beauty and purity beyond description. Others report that it was covered with angels, and some add that they were like butterflies of gold circling it.

These are reports of varying strength, and the honest way to cite them is as interpretations rather than as facts established by the verse. The verse itself names nothing.

The variety of the readings is itself evidence for the reticence. Where a text withholds, the tradition fills, and the fillings differ. The verse's refusal to name is what kept the matter open, and the openness is arguably its point.

The construction has a parallel in the Qur'an's treatment of other overwhelming events. When Mūsā's people asked to see God, the mountain was levelled to dust and Mūsā collapsed unconscious (7:143) — the description there is of the effect on the observer rather than of the thing observed.

The same method appears here in the next verse, which describes the Prophet's sight as neither wandering nor overreaching. The focus is on the observer's steadiness, and the object is left unnamed in both cases.

That is the Qur'an's standard procedure for theophany and near-theophany: describe the recipient, not the source.

**What the Withholding Accomplishes**

The sūrah is answering a charge that the revelation came from the Prophet's own mind. Its method has been to describe an external event with coordinates. The coordinates have been given; the content of the experience is withheld.

The combination is instructive. The account is specific about where and when, and silent about what. That is the shape of a report from someone who saw something he cannot fully convey, and it is the shape the sūrah wants.

A fabrication would be expected to supply the spectacular detail. The account withholds it, and the withholding is evidence about the account.

Four verses have described the second occasion: the descent, the tree, the limit, the Garden beside it, and now the covering. The narrative has reached the moment of the vision itself.

The next verse does not describe the vision. It describes the observer: his sight did not wander and did not exceed. The turn is characteristic — at the point of maximum intensity, the text turns from the object to the subject.

The reader is given the one thing he can assess: how the witness behaved while seeing it.

**The Covering and the Reports of the Ascension**

The exegetical tradition supplies descriptions of what covered the tree, and the supplies should be reported with their status clear. Some accounts describe a light so intense that nothing else could be seen; others describe angels descending upon the tree in numbers; others add detail about their appearance.

None of these descriptions is stated in the verse. The verse says that something covered the tree and declines to say what, and the tradition's various attempts to fill the gap are interpretations rather than text.

The point is worth making because the passage is often quoted as though it described a specific spectacle. It does not. It records that a covering occurred and withholds its nature, and every specific description in circulation comes from a later generation's reading of that withholding.

**Restraint as Evidence**

The withholding has an evidential value that is easy to miss. A person constructing an impressive account would be expected to supply the impressive detail, because the detail is what persuades an audience.

This account does the opposite. It gives coordinates — a tree, a limit, a garden, a horizon, a measured distance — and refuses the spectacle. The refusal is consistent with the behaviour described in the next verse, where the observer is said not to have looked beyond what he was shown.

A report shaped by the observer's composure would look like this: precise about the setting, silent about the overwhelming content, and unembellished throughout. The sūrah's account has exactly that shape, and the shape is part of the case it makes for the reporter's reliability.

**The Verse and the Charge of Exaggeration**

Passages describing visionary experience invite exaggeration, and the accounts that circulate later usually supply it. This one does the opposite, and the contrast is worth setting out.

A later generation describing the same event would be expected to add scale, splendour, and detail — the size of the tree, the appearance of the covering, the words spoken. The verse adds none of it, and it removes the one element that would have supplied scale: the nature of what covered the tree.

The result is an account that gives the reader less than he wants and more than he can dismiss. There is enough to establish that something occurred, in a named place, observed by a named person whose composure is described; there is nothing that could be turned into a spectacle.

That balance is difficult to achieve by accident. It is the balance a truthful reporter strikes when he is describing something he cannot fully convey and is unwilling to embellish.

**The Steadiness That Follows**

The reticence of this verse prepares the claim of the next. If the covering was beyond description, then the observer's composure in its presence is the more remarkable, and the verse that follows states it in two negations.

The sequence is deliberate. The text establishes that something overwhelming was present, and then establishes that the one in its presence remained steady and accurate.

That is the whole of the sūrah's claim about the event: something real was there, and the witness did not falter. Everything else is left unnamed.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:17
> The ˹Prophet’s˺ sight never wandered, nor did it overreach.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mā Zāgha al-Baṣar**

The verse turns from the object to the observer. *Mā zāgha al-baṣar* — the sight did not swerve, did not wander, did not turn aside.

*Zāgha* describes a deviation of the eye, a looking away or a glancing off to the side. The negation states that the gaze stayed where it was directed for the whole of the event.

The claim is about attention rather than about ability. It says that nothing distracted him and nothing made him look away — neither the intensity of what was present nor the strangeness of the setting.

The second negation is different in kind. *Wa mā ṭaghā* — and it did not exceed, did not transgress, did not overreach.

*Ṭaghā* is a word the Qur'an uses for going past a limit, and it is used elsewhere for Pharaoh and for those who overstep. Applied to sight, it describes a look that tries to take in more than is given, or that seeks something it has not been permitted to see.

The negation therefore denies ambition as well as distraction. He did not look away from what was shown, and he did not try to see beyond it.

**Two Negations, One Claim**

The pair covers both directions in which a vision could be faulty. A gaze can fail by losing its object or by grasping at more than its object. Both failures are excluded.

The result is a statement about the reliability of the report. The witness was neither distracted nor presumptuous; he saw what was shown and reported what he saw.

That is the claim the sūrah needs. The dispute recorded earlier in the chapter was about the accuracy of the account, and this verse addresses accuracy directly rather than arguing for it.

The verse follows immediately after the statement that the Lote-Tree was covered by something unnamed. The juxtaposition is the point: whatever was there, the observer did not flinch and did not strain.

The Qur'an elsewhere describes the opposite reaction to overwhelming disclosure. When the mountain was levelled to dust, Mūsā fell down unconscious, and when he came to he said glory be to You, I turn to You in repentance (7:143). That is the human response to direct divine presence.

What is described here is different. The observer remains composed and attentive, which suggests a vision of something other than the divine essence — consistent with the reading that the object was the angel in his created form.

**The Steady Gaze in the Qur'an's Language**

Steadiness of sight is used elsewhere in the Qur'an as a marker of inner state. The eyes of those who disbelieve will not return to them on the day of judgment, and their hearts will be empty (14:43); the sight returns twice, humbled and wearied, when a person tries to find a flaw in the creation and fails (67:3–4).

The pattern associates a wandering gaze with disturbance and a held gaze with certainty. This verse assigns the held gaze to the witness at the moment of the greatest disclosure reported in the Qur'an.

The assignment is a claim about his state, not merely about his eyes.

The verse's function in the argument is to characterise the reporter. A report is only as good as the reporter, and the reporter is here described as attentive, undistracted, and unambitious.

Those are the qualities a court would look for in a witness. The sūrah is making a case, and this verse supplies the character evidence.

It is also the strongest thing the sūrah says about the Prophet in this passage. He is not praised for his wisdom or his virtue here; he is described as having kept his eyes where they were told to be, which is a narrower and more useful claim.

**The Gaze as a Measure of the Heart**

The Qur'an treats the direction of the eye as an index of the inner state, and this verse uses that convention deliberately.

Where the heart is disturbed, the gaze wanders. The description of the day of judgment includes eyes that do not return to their owners and hearts that are empty (14:43), and the description of the hypocrites includes a turning away that begins in the look. Where the heart is settled, the gaze holds, and the Qur'an asks the reader to look again and again at the creation until the eye returns wearied and defeated (67:4).

Applying that convention here, the verse is making a statement about the Prophet's inner condition at the moment of the vision. He was neither agitated nor grasping. The composure attributed to him is not a physical detail but a claim about his state, and it is the claim that supports the reliability of what he reported.

**Against the Charge of Fabrication**

The verse answers a specific accusation without naming it. The charge was that the account was invented, and an invented account of an overwhelming experience would be expected to show certain features: elaboration, escalation, and a claim of extraordinary attainment.

This account shows none of them. It records that the observer's sight stayed where it was directed and did not exceed its limit, which is the language of a man describing discipline rather than achievement. The emphasis falls on what he did not do.

That is the answer the sūrah gives to the charge of invention. Not a denial, but a report whose texture is inconsistent with invention — spare where invention would be lavish, and modest where invention would claim.

**The Turn of the Passage**

Structurally the verse closes the account. The event has been described, located, and witnessed, and the witness has been characterised. The next verse states what he saw, and the passage after that changes subject entirely.

The turn is marked by a shift from narrative to polemic — from the Lote-Tree to the idols of Mecca. The connection between the two is the dispute recorded at the start of the chapter: the people contesting the vision are the same people worshipping the stones.

That is where the sūrah goes next, and it goes there because the first line of argument has been exhausted.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:18
> He certainly saw some of his Lord’s greatest signs.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Āyah al-Kubrā**

The account closes with a summary of what was seen: he certainly saw one of the greatest signs of his Lord. *Min āyāti rabbih al-kubrā* — from the signs of his Lord, the greatest.

The superlative *al-kubrā* is feminine, agreeing with *āyah*, a sign. The verse states that what he saw belonged to the highest category of signs, without listing what else is in that category.

The emphasis particle *laqad*, certainly, appears again, as it did in the verse recording the second sighting. The sūrah marks its central claims with it.

There is a question in the grammar. *Al-kubrā* is singular, but the phrase *min āyāt*, from among the signs, implies plurality, and some read the superlative as a plural, making the meaning that he saw the greatest signs.

On the singular reading, what he saw was one sign of supreme rank. On the plural reading, he saw a number of signs of the highest rank.

The difference is small but real. The singular reading focuses the account on a single object; the plural reading makes it a survey. The context, which describes one appearance and one covering, favours the singular.

**What the Sign Was**

The commentators offer two main identifications. The first is the angel in his created form — the figure with the six hundred wings that filled the horizon, seen twice. The second is a specific object of the vision, described in some reports as a curtain or veil of light.

Both readings agree that the sign was a created thing. Neither identifies the sign as God Himself, which is consistent with the account's spatial language and with the Prophet's own reported statement that his Lord is light and that he did not see Him.

The verse's silence on the identification is consistent with the previous one's silence about the covering. The category is given; the content is not.

The word *āyah* has a defined range in the Qur'an. It covers natural phenomena presented as evidence — the alternation of night and day, the ships on the sea, the rain — and it covers miraculous events given to prophets as credentials.

The superlative form is used for the greatest of these. The Qur'an tells Mūsā to go to Pharaoh with Our greatest sign (79:20–21), and it describes the splitting of the moon as a sign (54:1).

Placing the vision in that category classifies it. It was not a private experience but a sign of the kind the Qur'an presents as evidence for its claims.

**The Parallel in al-Isrāʾ**

The verse corresponds closely to the other chapter's account of the same event: glory be to the One Who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, so that We may show him some of Our signs (17:1).

The two verses share the vocabulary of showing and of signs. al-Isrāʾ states the purpose of the journey; an-Najm reports that the purpose was fulfilled.

The correspondence is one of the strongest indications that the two passages describe one event, and it supports the reading that this sūrah's account of the second descent is the ascension.

Eleven verses have described the vision, and this one summarises it. The summary is deliberately modest: a sign, of the greatest kind, belonging to his Lord.

There is no claim of union, no description of divine presence, no statement of what was learned. The account ends where it began, with an event witnessed and reported.

That modesty is the account's defence. The charge was that the Prophet invented the revelation, and the response is a spare report of an external event, closed with a claim that stops short of anything the audience could call extravagant.

**The Category of the Greatest Signs**

Placing this event among the greatest signs assigns it a rank in a recognised classification, and the classification is worth setting out.

The Qur'an uses the word for sign in three main ways: for natural phenomena offered as evidence of a creator, for miraculous events given to prophets as credentials before their people, and for the verses of the revelation itself. The superlative form is reserved for the highest instances, and it is used sparingly.

Assigning the vision to that category does two things at once. It connects the event to the rest of the Qur'an's evidence, so that the vision is not an isolated wonder but part of a continuous case; and it commits the sūrah to the claim that the event was real, since a sign in that category is by definition something that happened.

**Why the Sign Is Not Described**

The verse names the category and withholds the content, and the withholding has been the method of the whole passage. The covering was not named; the sign is not described.

The reason is not embarrassment or vagueness. It is that the audience had no framework for the description, and a description they could not interpret would have added nothing to the case. What they could assess was the reporter, the setting, and the coherence of the account, and those are what the sūrah supplied.

There is also a discipline in it. A sign that is fully described becomes a story, and stories are argued about. A sign that is named and ranked remains a claim, and claims are accepted or rejected — which is the decision the sūrah has been asking for since its opening oath.

**The Turn to the Idols**

The verse that follows opens with a question about al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā, and the abruptness of the turn has puzzled readers.

The connection is the dispute. The people contesting the vision are the same people who worship three named deities, and the sūrah now examines those deities on their own terms — what they are, what they can do, and how they came to be assigned a gender and a rank.

The argument moves from what cannot be verified to what can. The vision is beyond the audience's reach; their own idols are not.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:19
> Now, have you considered ˹the idols of˺ Lât and ’Uzza,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Abrupt Turn**

The verse changes subject without transition. The Lote-Tree and the Garden give way to two stones in the Arabian desert, and the address turns from narrative to question.

*Afaraʾaytum* — have you considered, have you given thought? The formula is used in the Qur'an to introduce an examination of something the audience takes for granted. It invites reflection rather than belief.

The turn is the sūrah's central move. Having described a vision the audience cannot check, it now turns to a belief system the audience practices every day, and asks whether that system survives reflection.

Al-Lāt was a white stone venerated at Ṭāʾif, the town of the Thaqīf tribe in the mountains east of Mecca. It was kept in a house with a custodian, and the Thaqīf prized it as their own sanctuary.

The name is formed on the pattern of the divine name Allāh with the feminine ending, and the commentators note the connection. A name derived from the name of God, applied to a stone, is a fact about how the veneration was constructed.

The reports of its destruction are specific: after the conquest of Mecca the Prophet sent al-Mughīrah ibn Shuʿbah, with Abū Sufyān ibn Ḥarb, to demolish it, and a mosque was built on the site afterwards. The custodians were the Banū Muʿaṭṭib, a clan of Thaqīf, who had held the shrine and its offerings. The account is recorded in the early histories and is cited by Ibn Kathīr in his commentary on these verses.

**Al-ʿUzzā**

Al-ʿUzzā was venerated at Nakhlah, a valley on the road between Mecca and Ṭāʾif, and it was associated particularly with the Quraysh, who considered it the most important of their deities.

Its name is built from the root of might and honour — *al-ʿizzah* — with the feminine form, so that it means the mighty one or the most honoured. The Quraysh gave personal names derived from it, including ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā, the name of the Prophet's uncle Abū Lahab.

The reports describe it as three trees or a house containing three trees, and its destruction is attributed to Khālid ibn al-Walīd, who was sent to demolish it after the conquest of Mecca.

Both destructions are recorded in the early histories, and the accounts share a feature: the idols did nothing when they were struck.

That detail matters for the argument the sūrah is making. A deity that cannot defend its own shrine cannot protect a worshipper, and the reports were well known to the audience within a few years of this revelation.

The sūrah does not need to argue the point from theory. It asks the audience to consider two objects that everyone knew, and the question does the work.

**What the Question Asks**

The verse does not assert that the idols are false. It asks whether the audience has thought about them.

The form is significant. An assertion can be rejected; a question obliges the hearer to run the reasoning himself. The sūrah has already spent eighteen verses on a claim the audience could not verify; here it offers something they can check against their own knowledge.

The answer to the question is supplied by the following verses, and the answer is that the deities have names without referents and ranks without grounds.

Three deities are named in this verse and the next, and they were venerated together in Arabia as a group, described by their worshippers as daughters of Allāh.

The construction is the object of the sūrah's criticism. It takes the one God, gives him offspring, assigns the offspring the gender the worshippers themselves considered inferior, and derives the arrangement from no evidence at all.

The next two verses take the arrangement apart piece by piece, beginning with the gender and the inheritance rule that reveals it.

**The Question as a Method**

The formula *afaraʾaytum* is used repeatedly in the Qur'an, and its function is consistent: it introduces an examination of something the audience accepts without having examined it.

The method assumes that the belief will not survive the examination, and it is used where that assumption is safe — where the audience has all the facts needed to reach the conclusion himself. Nothing outside their experience is required, no revelation has to be accepted first, and no authority has to be granted.

That is why the sūrah turns to the idols at this point. The vision could not be verified by the audience, and the argument about it had reached its limit. Their own worship could be verified by them, and the question form obliges them to do the verifying rather than being told the result.

**What the Stones Could Not Do**

The audience knew what their deities could and could not do, and the knowledge was practical rather than theoretical. The shrines had custodians, treasuries, and offerings; they had festivals and pilgrimages; they had names that people gave to their children.

What they did not have was any recorded instance of intervention. No battle had been decided by al-Lāt, no illness cured by al-ʿUzzā, no outcome allocated by Manāt. The offerings went in and nothing came back, and the arrangement continued because it was inherited rather than because it worked.

The verse does not make this point explicitly. It asks whether the audience has considered the three deities, and the consideration produces the point on its own — which is why the question is more effective than the assertion would have been.

**The Report Attached to These Verses**

The early histories transmit a report that during the recitation of this passage, words were added praising these three deities, and that the addition was later withdrawn.

The report has been much discussed. Its chains are widely judged defective — al-Qāḍī ʿIyāḍ states that no trustworthy narrator related it with a sound uninterrupted chain, and al-Albānī concluded that it was fabricated — though Ibn Ḥajar held that the number of chains indicates the story has some basis. A chronological difficulty also arises, since the sūrah is dated to the tenth year of the mission while the event described belongs to the fifth.

The text of the Qur'an contains no such words, and the verse as it stands asks a question about three idols.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:20
> and the third one, Manât, as well?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Manāt: The Name of Fate**

The third deity is named, and the name is the most revealing of the three. *Manāt* is derived from the root m-n-y, which carries the meanings of allotting, apportioning, and measuring out — the same root as *manīyah*, fate or death.

The deity's name therefore means something like the one who allots or the goddess of destiny. The commentators connect it with the idea that the worshippers attributed their fortunes to her allocation.

The name is the clearest evidence in the triad of what the worship was for. Al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā are named for divinity and for might; Manāt is named for the distribution of outcomes, which is the thing people actually want from a god.

Manāt was venerated at al-Mushallal, a coastal place near Qudayd between Mecca and Madīnah, and it was particularly associated with the tribes of Khuzāʿah and with the Aws and Khazraj of Yathrib.

That location is significant for the audience of this sūrah. The Aws and Khazraj were the two tribes of Madīnah who would later receive the Prophet, and the idol was part of their pre-Islamic practice.

The verse names a deity whose worship extended beyond Mecca into the region that would become the centre of Islam. The three idols named in these two verses were the most widely venerated in western Arabia.

**The Third of the Other Two**

The phrase *ath-thālithat al-ukhrā* — the third of the other two — has been read in different ways. Some take it as marking Manāt's rank as the least of the three, the one added to complete the set.

Others connect it with the practice of the Arabs, who would speak of a person as the third of two when describing someone who joins a pair and is thereby defined by inferiority to it.

Either way the phrase marks a hierarchy within the triad. The deities are not equals; they are ranked, and the ranking was assigned by the worshippers without any basis in the deities themselves.

Manāt's destruction is recorded in the early histories, though the reports differ over who was sent: some name Saʿd ibn Zayd al-Ashhalī, others Abū Sufyān ibn Ḥarb or ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib. All agree that the shrine was demolished after the conquest of Mecca.

The variation in the reports is worth noting rather than smoothing over. What is consistent across them is the fact of the destruction and the fact that the idol offered no resistance, which is the point the sūrah's question depends on.

The shrine had a custodian and accumulated offerings, which means it had an economy — staff, income, and a stake in its own continuation. That is what the sūrah is examining when it asks what these deities actually are.

They were institutions with property and function, and the question posed in the preceding verse is about the reality behind the institution.

**Why the Sūrah Names Three**

Naming the deities individually serves the argument. A general statement that idols are false invites the response that one's own deity is an exception; naming three specific ones removes the exception.

Each name is tied to a place and a tribe — Ṭāʾif, Nakhlah, al-Mushallal. The audience knew where the stones were and who kept them. The sūrah is not attacking an abstraction but a known set of objects with known custodians.

The specificity also dates the passage. These three were destroyed within a decade of the revelation, and their disappearance is a checkable fact about the sūrah's claims.

The verse ends with a question mark in the sense rather than the punctuation. Three names are given, and no verdict is announced.

The verdict comes in the verses that follow, and it is built from the worshippers' own conduct: they assign the female offspring to God and keep the male for themselves, they give the deities names with no reality behind them, and they follow conjecture and desire.

The method is to let the audience supply the conclusion. The names are stated, the ranking is stated, and the audience is left to notice that nothing supports either.

**The Economies of the Three Shrines**

Each of the three deities named in these two verses was supported by an institution, and the institutions are documented in the early histories.

Al-Lāt had a house at Ṭāʾif with a custodian who collected the offerings of pilgrims. Al-ʿUzzā had a structure at Nakhlah with attendants and a treasury. Manāt had a shrine at al-Mushallal with its own custodian, and the Aws and Khazraj did not consider their pilgrimage complete until they had visited it and shaved their heads there — a practice ʿĀʾishah described and al-Bukhārī recorded.

The detail matters for the argument. These were not private superstitions but public establishments with income, staff, and a stake in their own continuation. The sūrah's question about what the deities are is therefore also a question about who benefits from the arrangement, and the audience could answer it without reference to theology.

**The Triad and the Qur'an's Response**

The three deities were venerated together across western Arabia, and their worshippers assigned them a place in relation to God: they were his daughters, and they interceded with him on behalf of their devotees.

The Qur'an's response to that arrangement occupies the next eleven verses, and it proceeds by dismantling each element separately. The gender assignment is exposed as a biased distribution; the names are shown to have no authority behind them; the intercession is shown to require a permission nobody has; and the whole structure is traced to conjecture and desire.

The dismantling is methodical, and it does not rely on the audience accepting the Qur'an. Every step appeals to something the audience already believed or could observe, which is what makes the passage an argument rather than a declaration.

**The Verse's Function in the Argument**

This verse completes the list and sets up the examination. Nineteen verses have described a vision; this one and the two before it name three stones.

The juxtaposition is the sūrah's argument in miniature. On one side, a report of the unseen that is specific about its coordinates and reticent about its content; on the other, three named objects with temples, custodians, and treasuries.

The sūrah asks the audience which of the two deserves belief, and it does so by describing both accurately.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:21
> Do you ˹prefer to˺ have sons while ˹you attribute˺ to Him daughters?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reversal Stated**

The question is short and cutting. *A-lakum adh-dhakaru wa lahu al-unthā* — is the male for you and the female for Him?

It restates the worshippers' own arrangement and holds it up. They had assigned daughters to God and kept sons for themselves, and the verse asks whether that distribution makes sense.

The question does not argue. It restates, and the restatement is the argument, because the arrangement is indefensible once it is put plainly.

The force of the question depends on a fact about its audience: they valued sons and considered daughters a diminishment. The Qur'an records the attitude directly elsewhere: when news of a daughter is brought to one of them, his face darkens and he is filled with inward grief (16:58).

Given that attitude, assigning the female sex to God was not an honour. It was assigning to the deity what the assigner would not accept for himself, and the verse points at the inconsistency without comment.

That is the whole of the argument, and it requires no premise the audience could dispute. Their own conduct supplies it.

**The Parallel in an-Naḥl**

The same argument appears at greater length in Sūrah an-Naḥl: do you prefer males for yourselves while He takes females? That is truly an unfair division. They attribute to Allah what they themselves dislike, and their tongues utter the lie that they will have the best (16:57–62).

The phrase *qismatun ḍīzā*, an unfair division, is the term used there for exactly the arrangement this verse describes.

The repetition across sūrahs shows that this was a standing argument rather than a one-off rhetorical device. The charge of an unfair distribution appears in a Meccan sūrah here and in another Meccan sūrah there, addressed to the same practice.

The method is worth isolating because it recurs throughout the chapter. The sūrah does not argue from revelation, which the audience rejected, or from metaphysics, which they had no framework for.

It argues from behaviour the audience could observe in themselves. They prefer sons; they assign daughters to God; therefore their theology contradicts their preferences, and a belief that contradicts its holder's own preferences has no evidential basis.

That is a form of argument available to anyone, believer or not, and it is why the sūrah uses it at the point where its other arguments have run out.

**What the Charge Was**

The underlying claim being examined is that God has offspring, and specifically female offspring. The Qur'an addresses this claim repeatedly and in several sūrahs.

The relevant point here is the internal incoherence rather than the theological error. Even on the worshippers' own premises — that God exists, that offspring are possible, that the deities are his daughters — the distribution makes no sense, because it gives God what they themselves refuse.

The verse therefore does not need to establish that God has no offspring. It only needs to show that the claim, as its proponents held it, is not one they actually believe.

Three verses form a unit: the naming of the deities, the naming of the third, and the question about the genders. The unit moves from the concrete to the examination.

The structure is a common one in the Qur'an: state the audience's position accurately, then ask a question that exposes it. No refutation is offered, because the position refutes itself once it is stated.

The economy is deliberate. A long refutation would imply that the position deserves one; a single question implies that it does not.

**The Preference Behind the Theology**

The verse works because it identifies a preference that the audience would not have denied. Sons were valued: they carried the name, contributed to the household's strength, and were the reason a man's line continued. Daughters were a burden in a society where marriage required a dowry and where tribal honour was thought to be at risk through them.

The preference was not hidden. It shaped inheritance, it shaped the language of congratulation and condolence, and it shaped the practice the Qur'an condemns elsewhere of burying infant daughters alive and then asking them for what sin they were killed (81:8–9).

Given a preference that strong, the theological arrangement is transparent. The audience assigned to God what they did not want, and the verse holds the assignment up without commentary, because no commentary is needed once the preference is acknowledged.

**Why the Argument Works Without Revelation**

The verse is a useful test case for the kind of reasoning the Qur'an expects from its readers. It offers no proof text, no appeal to earlier scripture, and no claim that God has said otherwise.

It offers a comparison between two facts the audience already knew: what they preferred for themselves, and what they assigned to God. The conclusion follows from the comparison, and any reader in any period can make it.

That is the standard the sūrah sets for its own arguments. Where a claim can be established by observation and ordinary reasoning, it is established that way, and the reader is not asked to suspend judgement or to trust an authority. The verse's brevity is a sign of confidence in the reasoning rather than a lack of material.

**The Inheritance Rule Beneath the Charge**

The question about males and females is not only about theology. It touches the rule that governed the audience's own households, and the connection sharpens the charge.

Inheritance in that society passed through the male line, and the female share was reduced or absent. The arrangement the audience made in heaven reproduced the arrangement they maintained on earth: the male portion for themselves, the female portion assigned elsewhere, and the assignment presented as proper order rather than as preference.

The verse exposes the reproduction by stating it in a single line. Once the parallel is visible, the theological claim loses its independent standing, because it is no longer a belief about God but a restatement of a social practice that its holders benefited from.

That is why the verse needs no argument. It needs only to be heard, and the audience heard it in the context of their own inheritance disputes, where the same distribution was applied to property.

**The Verse's Place in the Argument**

This verse begins the dismantling of the idolatrous system, and the dismantling continues through the next several verses: the division is unfair, the names are empty, the intercession is unauthorised, and the whole arrangement rests on conjecture.

Each step examines a different feature of the same belief. The verse's question establishes the method — examine the belief against the believer's own conduct — and the following verses apply it.

The sūrah has moved from the unseen, which cannot be checked, to the audience's own practice, which can.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:22
> Then this is ˹truly˺ a biased distribution!

**Expanded Commentary**

**Qismatun Ḍīzā**

The verse is four words in Arabic and delivers a verdict. *Tilka idhan qismatun ḍīzā* — that, then, is a division that is unjust, skewed, biased.

*Ḍīzā* is a rare word. It denotes a distribution in which one party is shorted, a division that departs from equity. Its rarity is part of its effect: the sūrah reaches for a word it does not use often, and uses it for this arrangement.

The demonstrative *tilka*, that, points back at the arrangement described in the previous verse. The verdict is delivered on something already established rather than argued for here.

A division is unfair when the party making it takes the better share and assigns the worse to another, without grounds.

That is precisely the arrangement. The worshippers kept the male children for themselves and assigned the female to God, and they did so while considering the female the lesser portion. There is no principle behind the assignment other than their own preference.

The verse names the arrangement for what it is: a distribution made by an interested party in his own favour, presented as theology.

**The Judgment Announced**

The structure of the two verses is worth noting. The first asks a question; the second announces a judgment. No premises are stated between them, because none are needed.

That economy is a feature of the sūrah's method. Where the audience's position is self-evidently indefensible, the sūrah states it and judges it, and does not argue.

The particle *idhan*, then or in that case, marks the judgment as following from what was just said. Given the arrangement you have described, this is what it is.

The identical term appears in another Meccan sūrah: do you prefer males for yourselves while He takes females? That is truly an unfair division (16:57–58).

The recurrence shows that the charge was a standing one in the Meccan revelation. The same word, *ḍīzā*, applied to the same practice, in two sūrahs separated in revelation.

It also shows that the argument did not depend on the audience accepting the Qur'an. The charge is that their own practice is internally inconsistent, which is a claim they could assess on their own terms.

**Why the Sūrah Returns to It**

The passage has moved a long way from the opening oath. It began with the truthfulness of the Prophet, described a vision, and has now arrived at a critique of Arabian idolatry.

The return to the idols is not a digression. The dispute that prompted the chapter was about the vision, and the vision was denied by the same people who maintained the idolatrous system. Examining the system is a way of examining the denial.

The verse's judgment on the division is therefore part of the answer to the question asked in verse twelve.

Four words, and the sūrah moves on. The brevity is a judgment about the position rather than about the reader.

A position that requires extensive refutation is one worth taking seriously. A position that can be disposed of in four words is one that never had support, and the sūrah treats it accordingly.

The reader is not given the impression that a great intellectual achievement has occurred. A bias has been named, and the naming is sufficient.

**The Verdict as the End of an Argument**

The verse is unusual in that it ends an argument rather than continuing one. No further reason is given for the verdict, and no response to a possible objection is anticipated.

That is a judgement about the position being examined. Where a belief can be defended, a text argues with it; where it cannot, the text names it and moves on. The four words here perform the naming, and the sūrah proceeds immediately to the next element of the system.

Readers sometimes look for a missing premise in verses like this one, assuming that something has been left out. Nothing has. The premise was supplied by the audience's own conduct in the preceding verse, and the verdict is the only thing that remained to be said.

**Bias in the Distribution of the Unseen**

The charge has a wider application than the specific arrangement it addresses. Any account of the unseen that gives its holders a privileged position deserves the same examination.

The audience's arrangement did exactly that. It placed them in a special relation to God through deities they controlled, staffed, and benefited from, and it made the relation hereditary rather than earned. The distribution of favour in the unseen mirrored the distribution of status in Mecca.

That mirroring is what the word *ḍīzā* identifies. The division is unfair not only because God receives the lesser portion but because the people making the division arranged the unseen to match their own advantages, and called the result theology.

**The Distribution and Its Beneficiaries**

Every distribution has beneficiaries, and identifying them is usually the fastest way to assess it.

The arrangement condemned in this verse benefited the custodians of the three shrines, who collected the offerings; the tribes whose prestige rested on their local deity; and the households that derived status from the association. The cost fell on those who supplied the offerings and received nothing in return.

The theological form of the arrangement concealed this. It was presented as a statement about God's family rather than as a system of payments, and the presentation is what the word *ḍīzā* strips away. A biased division is not merely an error; it is an error that advantages the person holding it.

That observation does not by itself disprove the belief. It explains why the belief was held so firmly and why argument alone did not dislodge it, which is a point the sūrah makes implicitly by moving on to instruction rather than continuing the debate.

**From Division to Naming**

The verse closes the first stage of the critique — the arrangement of genders — and the next verse opens the second, which concerns the names themselves.

The progression is from the arrangement to its foundation. Having shown that the distribution is biased, the sūrah examines whether the deities who receive the shares have any standing at all, and finds that their names were assigned without authority.

That is the next step, and it goes deeper than the question of gender, because it questions whether the objects of the arrangement exist in the sense their worshippers suppose.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:23
> These ˹idols˺ are mere names that you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized. They follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions and whatever ˹their˺ souls desire, although ˹true˺ guidance has already come to them from their Lord.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Names Without Referents**

The verse moves from the arrangement to the objects of the arrangement. *In hiya illā asmāʾ sammaytumūhā* — they are nothing but names you have named.

The formulation is precise. The verse does not say the stones do not exist; it says the names have no referent. Al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā exist as words, as stones, and as institutions, but not as the deities the names are supposed to denote.

That distinction is the core of the Qur'an's critique of idolatry. The error is not the existence of the object but the projection of divinity onto it, and the projection has no source.

The verse names the agents of the naming: you and your fathers. The addition of the ancestors is significant.

It identifies the mechanism by which the names persist. No living person chose them; they were inherited, and their authority consists in the fact that they are old.

The Qur'an treats this mechanism as an argument against the practice rather than for it. Inheritance explains how a belief survives; it does not supply evidence for it. Several passages make the point directly, asking whether the believers would follow their forefathers even if their forefathers understood nothing and were not guided (2:170).

**No Authority Sent Down**

The verse states what is missing: *mā anzala allāhu bihā min sulṭān* — God has sent down no authority for them.

*Ānzala*, sent down, is the Qur'an's word for revelation, and *sulṭān* is proof, warrant, or authorising evidence. The phrase sets a standard: a claim about the unseen requires a warrant that comes from outside the claimant.

The standard is the same one the sūrah applied to the Prophet in its fourth verse — he does not speak from whim, it is nothing but revelation revealed. The two verses are mirror images: one establishes that the Prophet's claims have a source, the other that the idols' do not.

The verse identifies the actual sources of the belief: *in yattabiʿūna illa aẓ-ẓanna wa mā tahwā al-anfus* — they follow nothing but conjecture and what the souls desire.

Two sources are named, and they correspond to two different failures. Conjecture is an epistemic failure — the belief rests on guesswork rather than evidence. Desire is a motivational failure — the belief is shaped by what the believer wants.

The pairing is important. A belief can rest on weak evidence without being self-serving, and it can be self-serving without being unevidenced. This belief is both, and the verse says so in a single clause.

**Guidance Has Already Come**

The verse ends with the fact that removes any excuse: *wa laqad jāʾahum min rabbihim al-hudā* — and guidance has certainly come to them from their Lord.

The emphasis particle *laqad* appears again, marking the statement as established. The audience is not in a position of ignorance; they have received a message and are following conjecture anyway.

That changes the character of the critique. It is not addressed to people who have never heard an argument but to people who have heard one and preferred their inherited practice. The verse's charge is not ignorance but preference.

Both halves of the verse have close parallels elsewhere. On the names: you worship instead of Allah only idols, and you invent a lie (29:17); the names you have named have no basis in anything sent down.

On conjecture and desire: have you seen the one who takes his own desire as his god (45:23)? And: those who do wrong follow their own desires without knowledge (30:29).

The recurrence across Meccan sūrahs shows that this was a developed critique rather than an improvised one. The same two diagnoses — conjecture and desire — are applied to the same practice in several places.

**The Standard of Authority**

The verse sets out a requirement that runs through the whole Qur'an: a claim about the unseen needs a warrant that comes from outside the person making it.

The requirement is stated negatively here — God has sent down no authority for these names — and positively in the sūrah's fourth verse, where the Prophet's speech is described as nothing but revelation revealed. The two statements are the same standard applied to two different claimants.

The standard is not arbitrary. Claims about beings nobody can see cannot be tested by observation, so the only available test is the source. A claim with a source can be examined; a claim without one can only be asserted, and assertion is what the verse says the audience's practice amounts to.

**How Inherited Beliefs Persist**

The verse names the mechanism by which the names survived: you and your fathers. The addition of the ancestors is the most informative part of the statement.

An inherited belief does not require evidence to persist. It requires repetition, and repetition is supplied by the household, the festival, the shrine, and the name given to a child at birth. The audience's children were called ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā and ʿAbd Manāt, and every use of the name reinforced the deity's standing without anyone having to argue for it.

The verse identifies this as the actual foundation of the practice, and the identification is a criticism. Age explains survival; it does not supply warrant. The Qur'an makes the point directly elsewhere, asking whether people would follow their forefathers even when their forefathers had no understanding and were not guided (2:170).

**The Verse's Argument in Full**

The verse contains a complete case in four moves. The deities are names without referents; the names were assigned by the worshippers and their ancestors; no authority supports them; and the belief rests on conjecture and desire despite the availability of guidance.

Each move is short, and each depends on facts the audience could check. The stones existed; the names were inherited; no revelation supported them; the message had arrived.

The case does not require the audience to accept the Qur'an's claims about God. It requires them to notice that their own belief has no warrant, and that is the whole of the argument.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:24
> Or should every person ˹simply˺ have whatever ˹intercessors˺ they desire?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Rhetorical Question**

The verse is a single question, and it expects the answer no. *Am li-l-insāni mā tamannā* — or does man have whatever he wishes?

*Tamannā* is from a root connected with wishing, hoping, and constructing scenarios in the mind. It is not the neutral word for wanting; it carries the sense of imagining a situation one would like to be true.

The question therefore is not about ordinary desire. It is about the desire that shapes what a person believes, and it follows directly from the previous verse's statement that the worshippers follow what their souls desire.

The verse makes a claim about how religions are formed. Where a belief corresponds to what the believer wants, its origin is suspect.

The application to the audience is specific. They wanted intercessors who would secure their position with God, and they had three named deities who supplied exactly that function. The correspondence between the want and the belief is the evidence.

That is not an argument that the belief is false. It is an argument that the belief is unexamined, and the sūrah has already established that the examination was never carried out.

**The Parallel Claim in al-Baqarah**

The same challenge appears in the Madinan sūrah: they say none will enter Paradise except the Jews or the Christians. That is their wishful thinking. Say, produce your proof if you are truthful (2:111).

The phrase there, *dhālika amānīyuhum*, that is their wishful thinking, uses the same root as *tamannā* here. The charge is identical: a belief about the afterlife that matches the believer's hopes, offered without evidence.

The recurrence across a Meccan and a Madinan sūrah, addressed to different groups, shows that the Qur'an treats this as a general pattern rather than a local accusation.

The question denies a principle rather than a particular belief. It is not saying that this specific desire will go unfulfilled; it is saying that desire does not determine reality.

The principle has consequences for the audience's whole system. Their deities were assigned functions — intercession, protection, allocation of fortune — that corresponded to their needs. If desire does not determine what exists, then none of those functions is established.

That is why the question is placed here, immediately after the verse about conjecture and desire. It generalises the diagnosis into a principle.

**The Link to the Preceding Verse**

The previous verse ended with the statement that guidance had come from their Lord. This verse asks whether humanity gets what it wishes, and the connection between the two is the answer.

Guidance exists and is available; what people wish for is not thereby true. The verse separates the two things that the audience had conflated — what they wanted and what was so.

The separation is the sūrah's central intellectual move. It appears in the verse about the Prophet's speech, in the verse about conjecture, and here.

Two verses now stand between the critique of the idols and the statement about ownership that follows. The question generalises the critique; the next verse states the actual position.

The movement is from what is not the case to what is. The audience's wishes do not determine the arrangement of things, and the arrangement is stated in the verse that follows.

That structure — a negation followed by an assertion — is the sūrah's standard form, and it appears here in its most compressed version.

**The Wish and the World**

The verse connects two things the audience had separated: what they wanted and what was so. Their deities existed because they were wanted — intercessors who would secure a favourable outcome, patrons who would protect the tribe, allotments of fortune that could be influenced by offerings.

Each of those functions answered a need, and the need was the reason the function was assigned. The verse names the mechanism without accusing anyone of dishonesty. People do not usually notice that they are constructing a belief out of a wish; the construction happens quietly, over generations, and arrives as tradition.

That is why the question is put as a question. It invites the audience to notice the mechanism in themselves rather than being told that they are deceiving themselves, which is a claim most people reject on hearing it.

**Why the Question Is Asked**

The verse could have been a statement: humanity does not have whatever it wishes. The interrogative form does something a statement cannot.

A statement is met with agreement or disagreement, and a person who dislikes the statement simply disagrees. A question has to be answered, and answering it requires the hearer to consider cases from his own experience — wishes that were not granted, prayers that were not answered, outcomes that went against every hope.

The audience had that experience in abundance. Their deities had not delivered what was asked of them, and the verse's question directs attention to the fact. The answer comes from their own history rather than from the text, which is the most persuasive place for it to come from.

**Desire and the Shape of Belief**

The verse identifies a mechanism that operates quietly, and its operation can be traced in the audience's own system.

They needed protection in a world where a man's safety depended on alliances, so their deities were patrons. They needed their status to be recognised, so their deities were connected to God by kinship. They needed the future to be negotiable, so one of the deities was named after the allotment of outcomes.

Each belief answered a need, and the needs were real. The verse does not criticise the needs; it asks whether the beliefs that answered them were therefore true. The question is the one the audience had never put to themselves, and putting it is the whole of the examination the verse demands.

The mechanism is not confined to that society. Belief systems everywhere tend to take the shape of the fears and hopes of the people holding them, and the verse's question is the standard test: what does this belief do for the one who holds it?

**The Answer That Follows**

The verse leaves the answer unstated because the next verse supplies it. To Allah belongs whatever is in the Hereafter and this world.

The juxtaposition is the argument. If the arrangement of both worlds belongs to God, then no human wish can alter it, and no intercessor can be appointed to it by human preference.

The reader is led from the question to the answer across a verse break, and the answer is a statement of ownership rather than a permission.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:25
> In fact, to Allah ˹alone˺ belongs this world and the next.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ownership of Both Worlds**

The verse answers the question of the previous one with a statement of ownership. *Wa li-llāhi mā fī al-ākhirati wa al-ūlā* — and to Allah belongs whatever is in the Hereafter and the first.

The word *al-ūlā*, the first, denotes this world in relation to the one that follows. The pairing covers the whole of existence, present and to come, and leaves nothing outside the statement.

The form of the sentence is significant. The prepositional phrase to Allah comes first, which in Arabic places the emphasis on the owner rather than on the owned. The sentence asserts not that God has possessions but that possession, as such, belongs to Him.

The Hereafter is named before this world, which reverses the natural order of experience.

The reversal is deliberate and appears elsewhere in the Qur'an. Where the point at issue concerns the afterlife, the afterlife is named first, and the point at issue here is precisely the afterlife — the audience's belief that their deities would intercede for them in it.

The ordering also carries a valuation. What is named first is what matters in the argument, and the argument concerns what happens after death.

**What Ownership Excludes**

The verse's function is to exclude the audience's arrangement. If both worlds belong to God, then no part of either can be allocated by human preference, and no being can hold a share of authority in it independently.

That excludes the deities as intercessors in the sense the worshippers supposed. An intercessor who acts on his own authority presupposes a domain in which he has standing, and the verse denies that any such domain exists.

It also excludes the allocation the worshippers had made — assigning daughters to God and sons to themselves presupposes that they have a say in the arrangement, and the verse denies that too.

Three verses have now examined the idolatrous system: the division is unfair, the names have no authority, and desire does not determine reality. This verse supplies the positive statement that the negations point toward.

Its brevity is proportionate to its function. It is a premise rather than a conclusion, and the sūrah states it without elaboration because the audience did not dispute it.

That is a feature of the argument worth noting. The Meccan audience accepted that God was the creator and the owner; their error was in the additions they made to that belief. The verse appeals to what they already held.

**Ownership Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The claim is made repeatedly and in similar terms. To Allah belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth and whatever is in them (5:120); and to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth (2:284); blessed is He to Whom belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth and everything between them (43:85).

The formula is one of the most frequent in the Qur'an, and it functions as a premise for many arguments — about judgment, about provision, about the authority of revelation.

Its use here is continuous with those uses. Ownership entails authority, and authority entails that no arrangement in either world can be made without the owner's permission.

Eleven words in Arabic, and the sūrah moves on to intercession. The brevity reflects the audience's position: they did not deny God's ownership, so the point needed stating rather than proving.

That economy is characteristic of Meccan argument. Where a premise is shared, it is stated in a clause and used; where it is disputed, it is argued for at length.

The reader should not mistake the brevity for a minor point. The whole of the sūrah's treatment of intercession in the next several verses depends on this statement.

**The Shared Premise**

The verse makes a claim the audience accepted, and its persuasive force depends on that acceptance. The Meccan opponents of the Prophet did not deny God's existence or His role as creator; the Qur'an records them affirming both when asked who created the heavens and the earth (31:25).

Their error lay in the additions: partners, offspring, intercessors who held standing independently of God's permission. The verse appeals past those additions to the belief underneath them.

That is the shape of much Meccan argument. It does not begin by establishing what the audience denies; it begins from what the audience affirms and draws out the consequences. Here the consequence is immediate: if God owns both worlds, no arrangement in either can be made by anyone else, and no share of authority can be held apart from Him.

**Ownership and the Afterlife**

The verse names the Hereafter before this world, and the ordering is significant for the argument at hand.

The audience's belief concerned the afterlife specifically — that their deities would intercede for them in it and secure their position there. By naming the afterlife first, the verse meets the claim where it was made and states that this domain, like the other, belongs wholly to God.

The statement removes the ground of the entire system. An intercessor presupposes a jurisdiction in which he can act, and the verse denies that any jurisdiction exists outside God's ownership. What remains of intercession is addressed in the verse that follows, where it appears not as a right held by beings but as a permission granted by the owner.

**From Ownership to Intercession**

The verse sets up the passage that follows. If God owns both worlds, then intercession in either requires His permission, and the audience's deities have no permission to give.

The next verse states exactly that, in the form of a rhetorical question about how many angels there are whose intercession will not avail except by His leave.

The movement from ownership to permission is the logical core of the sūrah's treatment of the unseen, and this verse is its foundation.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:26
> ˹Imagine˺ how many ˹noble˺ angels are in the heavens! ˹Even˺ their intercession would be of no benefit whatsoever, until Allah gives permission to whoever He wills and ˹only for the people He˺ approves. Are Angels Allah’s Daughters?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kam Min Malak**

The verse opens with a quantitative challenge. *Wa kam min malakin fī as-samāwāt* — and how many angels are there in the heavens?

*Kam* in this construction is exclamatory rather than interrogative: how many there are. The verse points at a multitude, and the multitude is the point.

The angels are the highest beings the audience acknowledged as intermediaries. If intercession were a function that beings could hold in their own right, the angels would hold it first.

The verse states that their intercession will not benefit at all — *lā tughnī shafāʿatuhum shayʾan*. The negation is emphatic: not any benefit, not in any degree.

The subject of the intercession is the angels, who are real, who inhabit the heavens, and who stand before God. The verse is not about imaginary beings; it is about the highest beings in the created order.

That is what makes the argument effective against the audience's position. If the angels' intercession is subject to permission, then the intercession of stones named by human beings is not merely subject to permission but without standing at all.

**The Condition of Permission**

The exception is stated in two parts. The first: except after Allah gives permission to whoever He wills — *illā min baʿdi an yaʾdhana allāhu liman yashāʾ*.

Permission is prior to intercession. The intercessor does not approach and then seek approval; the approval comes first and makes the approach possible.

The order reverses the audience's model. In their model, the worshipper approached the deity and the deity approached God. In the verse's model, God authorises and the intercessor then acts, and the authorisation is not owed to anyone.

The second part restricts the beneficiaries: and only for those He is pleased with — *wa yarḍā*. The permission is not general; it is directed at specific people.

That closes the arrangement the audience had constructed. Their deities were supposed to intercede for their worshippers on the basis of the worship, and the verse replaces the basis: the intercession, where it occurs, rests on God's pleasure with the person interceded for.

The two conditions together mean that intercession is not a resource a worshipper can accumulate. It is not purchased by offerings or secured by allegiance to a particular deity.

**The Parallel Verses**

The doctrine is stated in several places, and the formulation is consistent. Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission (2:255)? Intercession will not avail on that Day except from one to whom the Most Merciful has given permission and whose word He approves (20:109). They do not intercede except for whom He approves, and they are in awe of Him (21:28).

The verse in Sūrah Sabaʾ adds the scene: intercession with Him will not avail except for those He permits, until when terror is banished from their hearts they will say, what did your Lord say? They will say, the truth (34:23).

The consistency across sūrahs shows that this was a settled doctrine rather than a polemical device, and the verse here applies it to the angels specifically.

The verse's argument is a fortiori — from the greater to the lesser. The angels are real, they dwell in the heavens, and they stand in God's presence; their intercession nevertheless requires permission and applies only to those God is pleased with.

The audience's deities are stones with names assigned by human beings, located in three places in western Arabia, destroyed within a decade of this revelation. Their claim to intercede does not reach the level at which the angels' claim is examined.

The verse does not need to say this. It states the position of the angels, and the comparison is left to the reader.

**The Angels' Own Position**

The verse chooses the angels as its example, and the choice is deliberate. They are the highest created beings the audience acknowledged, they dwell in the heavens, and they stand in God's presence in a way no other creature does.

If intercession were a function that beings could hold by virtue of their rank, the angels would hold it. The verse states that they do not hold it: their intercession avails nothing until permission is given, and then only for those God is pleased with.

The statement is a stronger denial than one addressed to the idols directly. It establishes that intercession is not a property of rank at all, so that no being, however exalted, can claim it as an entitlement. Once that is granted, the claim of three stones in western Arabia collapses without further argument.

**Intercession as a Gift, Not a Right**

The two conditions in the verse convert intercession from a right into a gift, and the conversion has consequences for how worship is understood.

If intercession depended on the intercessor, a worshipper could acquire it by attaching himself to a powerful being — by offerings, by allegiance, by pilgrimage to a shrine. That was the audience's model, and it made religion a matter of choosing the right patron.

The verse removes the possibility. The permission is God's to give, it is given to whom He wills, and it applies only to those He is pleased with. Nothing the worshipper does to a third party affects it. What remains is the worshipper's own standing with God, which is the point the sūrah returns to in the verses that follow.

**The Verse's Place in the Sequence**

Four verses have now examined the idolatrous system from different angles: the unfairness of the division, the emptiness of the names, the futility of desire, the ownership of both worlds, and now the conditions of intercession.

The sequence is cumulative. Each verse removes a different support from the system, and the last removes the one that mattered most to its practitioners — the belief that their deities could secure their position in the afterlife.

The next verse turns to the gender assigned to the angels, which was the other half of the audience's arrangement.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:27
> Indeed, those who do not believe in the Hereafter label angels as female,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Female Names**

The verse turns to the second half of the audience's arrangement. *Inna alladhīna lā yuʾminūna bi-al-ākhirati la-yusammūna al-malāʾikata tasmīyat al-unthā* — those who do not believe in the Hereafter name the angels with the naming of the female. They had called the angels daughters of God, and the verse states the practice without offering a refutation of it. The naming is presented as a fact about them, and the fact is left for the hearer to assess.

The phrase *tasmīyat al-unthā*, the naming of the female, describes a category of naming rather than a single label. It is a way of classifying the angels, not merely a word applied to them, and that is why the verse uses the noun of naming rather than simply saying that they called the angels female. The error lies in the classification, in the decision that these beings belong to a sexed category at all.

The emphatic particle *inna* combined with the *la* of the verb gives the sentence a double emphasis, marking it as a statement of fact that the audience would not have disputed. Everyone in Mecca knew what the angels had been called.

**Who Does It**

The verse identifies the people by their disbelief in the Hereafter rather than by their tribe, their city, or their idols, and the identification is not incidental to the charge. Those who deny the afterlife have no stake in getting its inhabitants right. Their claims about the angels are claims they will never have to answer for, and the verse connects the error directly to the absence of that stake.

The connection is a psychological one, and it recurs in the Qur'an. Belief in the hereafter is what makes a person careful about assertions concerning it, because it introduces a consequence for being wrong. Its absence removes the care, and what remains is speculation offered with confidence.

That is why the verse names the disbelief first and the naming second. The order is causal in effect: the denial of the afterlife produces the carelessness about its contents, and the carelessness produces the claim about the angels' sex.

**The Charge and Its Grounds**

The charge is that they named the angels female without knowledge, and the grounds of the charge are worth setting out, because they are the whole of it. No revelation supports the naming, since no scripture they possessed described the angels. No observation could supply it, since nobody has seen an angel in a form that would settle the question.

The audience had no access to the angels in any form whatsoever. Their claim about the angels' sex is therefore a claim about something entirely outside their experience, and it was asserted publicly and confidently, as the basis of a religious system with temples and offerings.

That is the pattern the sūrah has been tracking since the verse about conjecture and desire: confident claims about the unseen with no source for them. The verse does not need to demonstrate that the claim is false. It needs only to establish that nothing supports it, and that is a point the audience could not contest.

**The Parallel in az-Zukhruf**

The same charge appears at greater length in another Meccan sūrah: they have made the angels, who are servants of the Most Merciful, females. Were they witnesses to their creation? Their testimony will be recorded and they will be questioned (43:19).

The question in that verse is the sharpest available form of the charge: were they present? The answer is no, and the absence of presence removes any possible basis for the claim. The verse then adds that the testimony will be written down and the witnesses questioned, which converts the matter from an intellectual error into an accountable statement.

The recurrence of the charge in two Meccan sūrahs shows that the practice was well known and widely held, and that the Qur'an treated it as a specific, checkable error rather than a general failing of character. It is presented as something a person could be asked about and could not answer.

**What the Naming Reveals**

The naming reveals something about the namers rather than about the angels. They assigned the angels a sex, and they assigned the sex they themselves considered inferior, and they did so while worshipping three deities they had also made female.

The arrangement is internally coherent when seen from outside. A pantheon of female deities, female angels as their counterparts, and the male sex reserved for the worshippers and their households — this is a projection of the social order onto the unseen, with the ranking of that society reproduced in the heavens.

The verse does not make this observation. It states the practice, and the pattern becomes visible to anyone who sets the three named deities beside the named angels and notices that the same distinction runs through both. The sūrah's method throughout has been to state facts the audience already knows and let the arrangement speak.

**The Verse's Brevity**

One sentence, and the sūrah moves on. The brevity reflects the character of the charge: a claim with no basis does not require an extended refutation, only a statement of its groundlessness, and the following verse supplies that statement in general terms.

There is also a rhetorical economy at work. A long refutation would imply that the position deserved one and that reasonable people might hold it. A single declarative sentence implies the opposite — that the practice is not a view but an unexamined habit.

The pair of verses works as charge and diagnosis. This one says what they did; the next says what the doing rests on, which is conjecture, and adds the principle that conjecture does not avail against a truth that has been stated.

**The Connection to the Preceding Verses**

The verse belongs to the sequence that began with the naming of the idols. The audience assigned genders to three deities, assigned genders to the angels, and derived from those assignments an inheritance arrangement in which the male share went to them and the female to God.

Each element of that construction has now been examined. The division is unfair; the names lack authority; desire does not determine reality; God owns both worlds; intercession requires permission and applies only to those He is pleased with; and the naming of the angels is a naming without knowledge.

The examination is complete after the next verse, and the sūrah then turns from the audience to the Prophet, instructing him on how to conduct himself toward people who have heard the argument and turned away from it.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:28
> although they have no knowledge ˹in support˺ of this. They follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions. And surely assumptions can in no way replace the truth.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mā Lahum Bihi Min ʿIlm**

The verse states the ground of the charge plainly. *Wa mā lahum bihi min ʿilm* — and they have no knowledge of it whatsoever.

The construction is emphatic in three ways: the negation *mā*, the prepositional phrase placing the object before the verb, and the particle *min* that intensifies the negation. Together they leave no degree of knowledge in the statement.

The object, *bihi*, refers back to the naming described in the previous verse. The claim is not that they know little about the angels but that they know nothing about the matter they are asserting.

The verse returns to the word the sūrah used several verses earlier: *in yattabiʿūna illā aẓ-ẓann* — they follow nothing but conjecture.

The repetition is deliberate. The first occurrence diagnosed the worship of the idols; this one diagnoses the naming of the angels. The same failure underlies both, and the sūrah applies the same word to both.

That consistency is worth noting. The sūrah does not treat idolatry and the angelic naming as separate errors requiring separate refutations. It treats them as one error appearing in two places.

**Conjecture Does Not Avail Against Truth**

The final clause generalises the diagnosis into a principle. *Wa inna aẓ-ẓanna lā yughnī min al-ḥaqqi shayʾan* — and conjecture does not avail against the truth in anything.

The principle is not that conjecture is worthless. In ordinary matters, estimation and probability are the best available guides, and the Qur'an does not require certainty for everyday decisions.

The principle is narrower and stronger: where the truth is known, conjecture cannot displace it. The audience had received a message stating the facts of the unseen, and they opposed it with guesswork. The verse says that guesswork does not defeat a known fact.

The Qur'an makes the same distinction repeatedly. Say, do you have any knowledge that you can produce before us? You follow nothing but conjecture, and you are only guessing (6:148).

The verse in Sūrah Yūnus is a direct challenge: bring forward your proof, if you are truthful (2:111 makes the same demand about Paradise). The demand for proof is the standard the Qur'an sets for claims about the unseen.

The verses in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ add the prohibition: do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge (17:36). The claim about the angels' sex falls under that prohibition directly.

**The Report on Conjecture**

The commentaries cite a saying of the Prophet warning against conjecture, describing it as the most lying form of speech, and the report is transmitted in the collections of al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

The saying belongs to the same conceptual field as the verse: conjecture is unreliable, and relying on it in matters that can be known is a form of falsehood.

The verse itself makes the point without the report, and the report's function in the commentary tradition is to extend it from theology to ordinary speech, where the same caution applies.

This verse closes the examination of the audience's beliefs. Seven verses have taken the system apart: the unfair division, the empty names, the futility of desire, the ownership of both worlds, the conditions of intercession, the naming of the angels, and now the absence of knowledge underlying all of it.

The diagnosis is complete and it is a single one. Every element of the system rests on conjecture, and conjecture does not avail against a truth that has been stated.

What follows is not further argument but instruction, addressed to the Prophet rather than to the audience.

**The Limits of Conjecture**

The principle stated at the end of the verse is often read as a blanket condemnation of conjecture, and the reading is too broad.

The Qur'an does not require certainty in ordinary matters. Estimates, probabilities, and judgements based on evidence are the normal tools of daily life, and Islamic law itself distinguishes between matters requiring certainty and matters settled by the preponderance of evidence.

What the verse condemns is narrower and sharper: conjecture set against a known truth. The audience had been given a message stating the facts of the unseen, and they opposed it with guesswork about the angels. The principle is that guesswork does not defeat knowledge, not that guesswork is always wrong.

**Knowledge and the Unseen**

The verse marks a boundary in the Qur'an's treatment of knowledge. Some matters are open to investigation; the unseen is not, because no observation reaches it.

That has a consequence for how claims about it should be handled. Where observation is impossible, the only legitimate sources are revelation and silence. A person who has no revelation on a matter and no observation of it has nothing to say, and the confidence with which the audience spoke about the angels' sex is what the verse objects to.

The objection is not to their conclusion but to their method. They spoke as though they had seen, and the verse records that they had no knowledge at all. The distinction between the two is the whole of the charge.

**The Charge Applied Beyond Theology**

The diagnosis in this verse has a range wider than the angels, and the commentaries have always applied it that way.

The warning against conjecture covers ordinary speech: reporting what one has not verified, attributing motives one has not observed, and repeating an account because it is plausible rather than because it is known. The saying cited in the tradition, describing conjecture as the most mendacious form of speech, extends the verse into that territory.

It also covers religious claims. Every assertion about the unseen that has no revealed source falls under the same charge, whichever community makes it, and the standard applies to the believer as strictly as to the idolater.

That is the verse's lasting force. It is not a polemical point scored against a particular group in seventh-century Arabia but a rule about evidence, and the rule is stated once, briefly, and without qualification: conjecture does not avail against the truth in anything.

**From Diagnosis to Instruction**

The verse ends the polemical section and the next verse begins the pastoral one. The sūrah moves from what the audience believes to what should be done about it.

The transition is characteristic of Meccan revelation. Argument is directed at the audience while there is hope of persuading them; when the argument has been completed and the response is refusal, the address shifts to the believer and tells him how to conduct himself.

The shift is not a surrender. It is a recognition that the matter has been argued as far as argument can take it.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:29
> So turn away ˹O Prophet˺ from whoever has shunned Our Reminder, only seeking the ˹fleeting˺ life of this world.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Instruction to Turn Away**

The address changes. The sūrah has been speaking about the audience; now it speaks to the Prophet. *Fa-aʿriḍ ʿan man tawallā ʿan dhikrinā* — so turn away from the one who turns away from Our reminder.

*ʿAʿriḍ* is an imperative, and the word denotes a turning of the face, a deliberate withdrawal of attention. It is not hostility; it is the cessation of an effort that has ceased to be useful.

The symmetry in the verse is deliberate: turn away from the one who has turned away. The instruction matches the conduct it responds to.

The phrase *tawallā ʿan dhikrinā* describes someone who has turned away from God's reminder, and the word *dhikr* is used in the Qur'an for the revelation itself.

The turning is described as prior to the instruction. The person has already turned; the instruction responds to a completed act rather than anticipating one.

That sequence matters for how the verse should be read. It is not a licence to abandon anyone who hesitates or asks difficult questions. It concerns a person who has turned away after the message was delivered and argued.

**Desiring Nothing But This Life**

The verse adds a description of the person's aim: *wa lam yurid illā al-ḥayāt ad-dunyā* — and he desires nothing but the life of this world.

The word *illā*, nothing but, restricts the desire to this world alone. The person is not described as loving this world, which everyone does to some degree, but as having no object of desire beyond it.

That restriction connects to the previous verses. The denial of the Hereafter, the naming of the angels, and the worship of stones are all consistent with a life whose horizon ends at death, and the verse identifies that horizon as the underlying condition.

The verse does not authorise withdrawal from the mission. The Qur'an continues elsewhere to command the delivery of the message and to describe the Prophet's persistence with those who rejected him.

The instruction is best read as governing the Prophet's emotional investment rather than his conduct. He is told not to exhaust himself over people who have made their decision, and the same theme appears in the verse telling him not to grieve over those who turn away (16:127) and in the verse asking whether he will destroy himself with grief because they do not believe (18:6).

That reading is supported by the surrounding verses, which shift to statements about God's knowledge and His ownership of both worlds — consolations rather than commands to withdraw.

**The Parallel Verses**

The theme recurs. So remind, for you are only a reminder; you are not a controller over them (88:21–22). And: if Allah had willed, they would not have associated others with Him, and We have not appointed you a guardian over them (6:107).

The verses in Sūrah an-Nāziʿāt add the scope of the task: you are only a warner for those who fear it (79:45).

The consistent element across them is the limitation of the messenger's role. He delivers and warns; the outcome is not in his hands, and the verse here releases him from responsibility for it.

The verse marks the sūrah's turn from argument to consolation. Eighteen verses of polemic have preceded it, and the polemic has reached its conclusion: the system rests on conjecture and will not be argued out of it.

What follows is addressed to the Prophet and to those with him. The remaining verses describe God's knowledge, His ownership, His justice in rewarding, and the fate of those who turn away, and they function as reassurance rather than as further proof.

The turn is a structural feature of Meccan sūrahs, and it usually comes at the point where the argument has been completed.

**What Turning Away Means**

The word *aʿriḍ* describes a turning of the face, and its use here has been read in two ways that differ in scope.

On the narrower reading, the instruction concerns the Prophet's inner investment: he is not to wear himself out over people who have made their decision. The Qur'an addresses that exhaustion directly elsewhere, asking whether he will destroy himself with grief because they do not believe (18:6) and telling him not to grieve over those who turn away (16:127).

On the broader reading, it concerns the direction of his effort: the message continues to be delivered, but the argument is not to be pressed on those who have already refused it. Both readings are consistent with the surrounding verses, which turn to God's knowledge and His justice rather than to any change in the mission.

**The Prophet's Persistence**

The instruction must be read against the record of the Prophet's conduct, which shows no withdrawal from the people it concerns.

He continued to address the Meccan notables who rejected him, continued to attend their gatherings, and continued to invite individuals who had insulted him. After the conquest of Mecca he pardoned the people who had driven him out, and at Ṭāʾif, where he was stoned, he prayed for the town's guidance rather than its destruction.

The verse is therefore not a description of his behaviour toward the rejecters. It is a release from responsibility for their outcome — a statement that the effort can be given without the result being required of him, which is what made the effort sustainable across twenty-three years of opposition.

**The Judgment That Follows**

The instruction is followed by a statement about God's knowledge of who has strayed and who is guided, and the connection between the two is the reassurance.

The Prophet is told to turn away because the matter is not his to settle. God knows who has strayed, and the knowledge is complete, and the verse that follows says so.

That is the consolation the verse offers: the effort can be released because the outcome is known and accounted for elsewhere.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:30
> This is the extent of their knowledge. Surely your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Extent of Their Knowledge**

The verse returns to the subject of knowledge with a verdict. *Dhālika mablaghuhum min al-ʿilm* — that is their reach of knowledge, the limit of what they attain.

*Mablagh* is from a root meaning to reach or to attain, and the word denotes the furthest point something extends to. Applied to knowledge, it describes a ceiling.

The verdict is that the conjecture described in the previous verses is not a step toward knowledge but the whole of it. They have not gone partway and stopped; the guesswork is the extent of what they have.

The same formulation appears in Sūrah al-Qalam: your Lord knows best who has strayed from His Way, and He knows best who is guided (68:7).

The repetition of the second half of the verse, in nearly identical words, in a sūrah revealed at a different point in the Meccan period, indicates a set piece — a statement the revelation returns to when the subject of human claims about guidance arises.

The first half of the verse, about the extent of their knowledge, is unique to this sūrah, and it is what gives the repetition its particular force here.

**What the Verse Concedes**

The verse makes a concession that is easy to miss. It does not say the audience is stupid or uninformed in general. It says their knowledge has a limit, and that the matters at issue lie beyond it.

The concession is significant because it is accurate. The audience's conjectures about the angels and the deities were not the product of ignorance about the world they lived in; they were the product of applying ordinary reasoning to a domain where reasoning without evidence produces nothing.

The verse identifies the boundary rather than insulting the people on the wrong side of it.

The second half of the verse states the counterpart: your Lord knows best who has strayed from His way, and He knows best who is guided.

*ʿAʿlam* is the elative form — more knowing, best knowing. The statement is comparative in form but absolute in effect: no one else's knowledge of the matter competes with His.

The two objects are named in the order of the argument. Straying comes first because it is what the audience has been accused of, and guidance second because it is what the Prophet has been charged with possessing.

**The Verse as Consolation**

The verse follows the instruction to turn away, and its function is to make the instruction bearable.

The Prophet has been told to withdraw his attention from people who have turned away, and the verse supplies the reason: the assessment of those people is not his task. God knows who has strayed, and the knowledge does not depend on the Prophet's continuing efforts.

That is the consolation the Meccan revelation offers repeatedly. The messenger is responsible for delivery and not for outcome, and the verses that state God's knowledge are the ground of that division.

The same statement appears in another form: your Lord knows best who strays from His way, and He knows best those who are guided (6:117).

The recurrence in three sūrahs — al-Anʿām, al-Qalam, and here — marks it as one of the Qur'an's standing responses to dispute about guidance. When the argument turns on who is right, the response is that the matter is known and will be settled by the One who knows it.

The response does not end the argument. It relocates it, from a contest between human claims to a matter held in divine knowledge.

**The Boundary of Human Reasoning**

The verse identifies a limit that is not a matter of intelligence or effort. The audience's failure was not that they thought too little but that they applied ordinary reasoning to a domain where reasoning without evidence produces nothing.

Human reasoning works where there is material to work on: observations, reports, and consequences that can be checked. Applied to the inhabitants of the unseen, it produces plausible constructions — deities with functions, angels with sexes, intercessors with jurisdictions — and the constructions feel like knowledge because they are coherent.

Coherence is not evidence, and that is the distinction the verse draws. The audience's system was internally consistent and entirely unsupported, and the verse describes it as the furthest reach of their knowledge rather than as a step toward the truth.

**Why the Statement Is Repeated**

The second half of the verse appears in Sūrah al-Qalam in almost identical words, and a related form appears in Sūrah al-Anʿām. The repetition marks it as a set piece of the Meccan revelation, returned to whenever the argument turns on who is guided and who is not.

The reason for the repetition is that the question is unavoidable. Every dispute about revelation eventually becomes a dispute about who has understood correctly, and the parties to such a dispute are not neutral judges of it.

The statement relocates the question. It does not settle the argument in the sense of persuading the other side; it declares that the settlement belongs to a knowledge that neither party possesses, and it frees the believer from the need to win. That is why the verse follows the instruction to turn away, and why it functions as consolation.

**The Verse's Function in the Passage**

The verse closes the section on the audience's claims and opens the section on God's dealing with human beings.

The movement is from what people know to what God knows, and from there to what God will do with the knowledge. The next verse states the ownership of both worlds and the rewarding that follows from it.

The sequence is the sūrah's argument in its final form: human knowledge has a limit, divine knowledge does not, and the outcome rests with the One whose knowledge is complete.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:31
> To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth so that He may reward the evildoers according to what they did, and reward the good-doers with the finest reward—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ownership Stated Again**

The verse returns to the statement made six verses earlier: to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.

The repetition is not redundant. The first statement was a premise for the argument about intercession; this one is a premise for the statement about rewarding. Ownership of the worlds entails authority over what happens in them, including the settlement of accounts.

The phrasing differs slightly. The earlier verse said whatever is in the Hereafter and the first; this one says whatever is in the heavens and on the earth. Both cover everything, and the variation keeps the statement from becoming a formula recited without attention.

The verse connects the ownership to a purpose: *li-yajziya alladhīna asāʾū bimā ʿamilū* — so that He may reward those who did evil with what they did.

The particle *li*, so that, makes the rewarding the consequence of the ownership. The worlds are His, and the arrangement within them includes a settlement.

The clause is the answer to the verse about human desire. Humanity does not get what it wishes; it gets what it did, and the correspondence is exact.

**Reward According to the Deed**

The verse states the correspondence twice, and the two statements differ in a way that has been much discussed.

The evil is rewarded *bimā ʿamilū*, with what they did — the reward matches the deed. The good is rewarded *bi-al-ḥusnā*, with what is best — the reward exceeds the deed.

The asymmetry is the Qur'an's consistent position. Elsewhere it states that whoever brings a good deed will have ten times its like, and whoever brings an evil deed will be recompensed only with its like (6:160). The verse here states the same principle in compressed form.

The word *al-ḥusnā* is the feminine superlative of the root ḥ-s-n, goodness and beauty. It is used in the Qur'an for the finest outcome, and the same root appears in the description of those who do good.

The use of the superlative rather than a matching term is the point. The good deed is met not with its equivalent but with the best, and the word chosen makes the excess explicit.

The pairing of *asāʾū*, they did evil, with *aḥsanū*, they did good, in the same verse, sets the two categories against each other in the plainest possible terms.

**The Parallel Verses on Purpose**

The Qur'an states the purpose of creation in similar terms elsewhere: We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything between them except in truth, and for a specified term (46:3); and: We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything between them in play (21:16).

The verses in Sūrah an-Naḥl add the denial of purposelessness: He created the heavens and the earth in truth, exalted is He above what they associate (16:3).

The verse here belongs to that family. Ownership, purpose, and settlement are stated together, and the connection between them is that a world owned by God and created with purpose cannot end without accounting.

The verse consolidates the sūrah's argument. Ownership has been established, human knowledge has been shown to be limited, and the settlement has been announced with its terms — exact for evil, exceeding for good.

That consolidation matters for the sūrah's overall shape. It opened with an oath and a denial, described a vision, dismantled an idolatrous system, and has now arrived at the moral order that replaces it.

The remaining verses apply the moral order to specific cases, beginning with the people who turn away and the one who gives little and withholds.

**The Asymmetry of Reward**

The two halves of the verse are not symmetrical, and the asymmetry is the point.

The evil is met with its like: those who did evil are rewarded for what they did, and the correspondence is exact. The good is met with something better than its like: those who did good are rewarded with the best.

The Qur'an states the same asymmetry with a number elsewhere: whoever brings a good deed will have ten times its like, and whoever brings an evil deed will be recompensed only with its like, and they will not be wronged (6:160). The verse here gives the principle without the figure, and the wording makes the direction of the excess unmistakable.

The asymmetry answers a question the sūrah has been circling. If the arrangement of both worlds belongs to God, then the arrangement is not arbitrary and not indifferent; it is weighted, and the weighting runs in favour of the one who does good.

**Ownership, Purpose, and Settlement**

Three claims are compressed into the verse, and they depend on one another. God owns whatever is in the heavens and the earth; the ownership has a purpose, expressed in the clause so that He may reward; and the purpose is realised in a settlement that matches each person's conduct.

Remove any one of the three and the others weaken. Ownership without purpose makes the world a possession with no direction; purpose without ownership makes it an intention nobody is able to carry out; settlement without both makes it an accounting with no authority behind it.

The verse states all three in a single sentence, and the sentence is the sūrah's answer to the system it has just dismantled. The audience's arrangement promised favour through patronage; this one promises a settlement through conduct, administered by the One who owns the estate in which the conduct occurred.

**The Movement Toward the Conclusion**

The verse sets up the final movement of the sūrah. Having stated the principle of reward, the text turns to the individuals it applies to, and the descriptions become concrete.

The movement from principle to case is the sūrah's standard procedure. It states a rule and then shows it operating, and the showing is what makes the rule memorable.

The next verse begins that showing, with a description of the person who turns away and gives a little, then stops.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:32
> those who avoid major sins and shameful deeds, despite ˹stumbling on˺ minor sins. Surely your Lord is infinite in forgiveness. He knew well what would become of you as He created you from the earth and while you were ˹still˺ fetuses in the wombs of your mothers. So do not ˹falsely˺ elevate yourselves. He knows best who is ˹truly˺ righteous.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Lamam: The Word for the Minor Sin**

The verse describes a category of fault with a single word: *al-lamam*, the small slip, the thing a person brushes against without committing himself to it. The commentators gloss it as the minor sins from which a person repents, and the word itself suggests a touching rather than a dwelling.

There is a report that fixes the meaning precisely. Ibn ʿAbbās was asked about *al-lamam* in this verse, and he answered by citing what Abū Hurayrah had heard from the Prophet: God has written for the son of Adam his share of fornication, which he will inevitably reach; the fornication of the eye is the look, the fornication of the tongue is the speech, the heart wishes and desires, and the private part confirms all of it or denies it. Ibn ʿAbbās said he had seen nothing more closely resembling *al-lamam* than that. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6243, and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2657 gives the longer version that adds the ears, the hands, and the feet.

The point of the report is not to license the small sins but to define them: what stops short of the act is *lamam*, and the act itself is not.

**The Major Sins and the Absence of a List**

The verse pairs the major sins with shameful deeds, and the pairing has been discussed because the Qur'an nowhere settles on a single definition of the major category.

The commentators offer functional definitions rather than lists. One common formulation is that a major sin is one whose retribution is not removed except by repentance, which distinguishes it from a fault that the ordinary round of prayer and good deeds effaces. The word translated as shameful deeds, *fawāḥish*, is often used for the most atrocious offences and is frequently associated with fornication specifically.

The Qur'an elsewhere makes the same distinction the basis of a promise: If you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will absolve you of your ˹lesser˺ misdeeds and admit you into a place of honour (4:31). The structure of that verse matches this one — avoidance of the great, forgiveness of the small — and the two together show that the distinction was a working category of the revelation rather than a later scholarly invention.

**Forgiveness Described as Vast**

The middle of the verse makes a statement about God rather than about the believer: your Lord is of vast forgiveness. The word for vast denotes breadth and capacity, and the commentators explain the breadth in two directions.

He forgives the small sins by keeping a person away from the great ones, and He forgives the great ones through repentance. Both operations are acts of forgiveness, and neither depends on the person having been sinless beforehand.

The placement of the statement matters. It comes immediately after the description of those who avoid the major sins, so that a reader who has not avoided them does not conclude that the description excludes him. The Qur'an makes that intention explicit elsewhere: O My servants who have exceeded the limits against their souls! Do not lose hope in Allah's mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins (39:53). The verse here performs the same function in a single phrase.

**Knowledge That Precedes Birth**

The verse grounds the statement about forgiveness in a statement about knowledge: He knew well what would become of you as He created you from the earth and while you were fetuses in your mothers' wombs.

Two moments are named. The first is the creation of humanity from the earth, understood as the creation of Adam from dust; the second is the formation of each person in the womb. Between them lies the whole of a person's history, and the verse says that God knew it from the beginning of both.

The commentators connect the first moment to the covenant taken from the descendants of Adam, when they testified to His lordship (7:172). The connection gives the verse its weight: the knowledge is not observation from a distance but knowledge of a creature He made and formed, stage by stage, from dust to birth.

**Do Not Elevate Yourselves**

The instruction follows from the knowledge: do not falsely elevate yourselves. The prohibition is directed at a specific act — declaring oneself pure, or declaring another pure as a settled verdict.

The reason given is that only God knows who is righteous. A person can describe what he has seen of another's conduct; he cannot certify the state of a heart or the outcome of a life. The Qur'an makes the same point in a verse that this one closely resembles: Have you not seen those who falsely elevate themselves? It is Allah who elevates whoever He wills (4:49).

There is also a subtler target. The person who avoids the major sins may conclude that he has achieved purity by his own effort, and the verse closes that door: the deeds themselves come about only through God's support, so there is nothing to claim.

**The Verse's Function in the Passage**

This verse completes the description of the people who will be rewarded and adds a qualification to it. The reward belongs to those who do good, but the standard is not sinlessness — it is avoidance of the great offences, repentance from the small, and no claim to purity.

That qualification protects the passage from a misuse. Without it, the promise of reward could be read as a promise to the self-satisfied, and the sūrah has spent its earlier verses dismantling exactly that posture: conjecture, desire, and a distribution arranged to flatter those who made it.

The verse ends where the sūrah has ended before, with God's knowledge as the ground of the whole arrangement.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:33
> Have you seen the one who turned away ˹from Islam,˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question Addressed to One Person**

The sūrah turns from the general description of reward to a single individual. *Afaraʾayta alladhī tawallā* — have you seen the one who turned away?

The formula is singular and direct. It does not describe a type of person or a class of behaviour; it points at one man, and the pointing is the whole of the verse. The description of what he did is left to the verse that follows.

*Afaraʾayta* is used in the Qur'an to introduce an examination of something the hearer is expected to find extraordinary. It carries an element of disbelief in the questioner's tone: have you seen this, can you credit it?

**The Meaning of Tawallā**

The verb denotes a turning away, and in this context it describes someone who had been facing one direction and then faced another.

The word is used elsewhere in the Qur'an for turning away from a message after receiving it, and for turning away from an obligation after acknowledging it. It is not the word for someone who never accepted anything; it is the word for someone who did and then withdrew.

That distinction is what gives the verse its force. The man described is not an outsider who never came near. He is someone who approached, and the verse asks whether the observer has considered him.

**The Report of Its Occasion**

The commentators connect these verses with a specific incident, and the incident is recorded in the exegetical literature with a named chain of transmission.

Al-Ṭabarī reports it on the authority of Mujāhid and Ibn Zayd, both of them Successors, and it names al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah. He had inclined toward the Prophet's religion; an idolater rebuked him for abandoning the faith of his forefathers; and when al-Walīd said that he feared God's punishment, the man offered to carry that punishment in his place in exchange for part of his wealth, on condition that he returned to idolatry. Al-Walīd agreed, paid part of the sum, and then refused the rest.

Because the report comes from Successors rather than from a Companion, it is not a graded ḥadīth, and it is cited here as an exegetical report. The verses do not depend on it. They describe a pattern the report illustrates: a bargain in which a person's standing in the hereafter was bought with money, and the money was not paid in full. The pattern was familiar enough in Mecca for the question to be understood at once.
**What the Verse Assumes**

The question assumes that the turning away was visible. It is addressed to someone who saw it happen, and the community did see it happen: a man who had attended the gatherings, heard the recitation, and then withdrew.

Public withdrawal of that kind had an effect on those who remained. It was evidence, to the wavering, that the message could be left without loss, and the sūrah answers it by examining the man rather than by reassurance.

That is the method the sūrah has used throughout. Where a claim is made about the world, the sūrah examines the claim rather than denying its effect.

**The Question as Diagnosis**

The verse does not condemn the man. It asks whether he has been observed, and the observation is left to produce the judgement.

The diagnosis comes in the verses that follow, and it is a diagnosis of motive rather than of action. The man gave a little and then stopped, and the stopping reveals that the giving was never motivated by conviction. A person who gives because he believes continues to give when it costs him; a person who gives as part of a transaction stops when the transaction ends.

The verse's question is therefore an invitation to look at the evidence and draw the conclusion, and the conclusion is available to anyone who watched the man's conduct.

**Tawallā Compared With Other Words for Leaving**

Arabic has several verbs for departing from a message, and the Qur'an uses them with some discrimination.

*Aʿraḍa* describes turning the face away, a refusal to listen; it is used for those who will not engage at all. *Kadhdhaba* is the flat denial of a claim's truth. *Istakbara* names the pride that produces the refusal, and it is applied to those who consider themselves above the message and its bearer.

*Tawallā* is none of these. It describes a person who was oriented toward something and then reoriented himself, and the Qur'an uses it for people who had some contact with the message before the turning. That is why the word fits this verse: the man in question had approached, and the verse's question depends on the fact that he had.

The distinction matters for the reader. The verse is not about an opponent who never came near; it is about someone whose departure is more informative than his arrival, and the word chosen carries that meaning in itself.

**The Man's Standing in the Community**

The question is addressed to someone who knew the man, and the knowledge was shared. Mecca was a small city in which a person's movements were visible: who attended a gathering, who withdrew from it, who changed his company, who altered his conduct.

That visibility is what the verse relies on. It does not need to describe the man's conduct in detail because the audience had watched it. The giving, the stopping, the return to the religion of his tribe — each step was public, and the verse's question is a request to recall what everyone had already seen.

The shared knowledge also explains the verse's brevity. A description would have been redundant, and a redundancy in an argument weakens it. The sūrah states the turning, and the audience supplies the rest from memory.

**What the Question Does Not Ask**

The verse does not name the man, does not condemn him, and does not describe his fate. Those omissions are as deliberate as the question itself.

Naming him would have made the passage a personal attack, and the audience would have been able to treat it as such — a quarrel between individuals rather than an examination of conduct. Leaving him unnamed keeps the focus on the pattern, which is what the following verses analyse.

The absence of condemnation is equally purposeful. A condemnation announced before the evidence is examined invites the response that the conclusion was reached in advance. The sūrah puts the question first, and the diagnosis follows from the conduct described in the verses after it.

The result is a passage that reads as an enquiry rather than an attack, and the enquiry is harder to dismiss than the attack would have been.

**The Sūrah's Turn to Conduct**

The earlier part of the sūrah dealt with belief: what the audience asserted about the unseen, and on what grounds. This verse opens a section about behaviour: what people did with their wealth, and what their doing revealed.

The turn is significant. Belief can be argued about; conduct can be observed. The sūrah moves to the ground where its audience has least room to manoeuvre, and it begins with a case they had all seen.

The next verse supplies the detail of what the man did, and the detail is what makes the diagnosis possible.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:34
> and ˹initially˺ paid a little ˹for his salvation˺, and then stopped?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Akdā: The Digger Who Hits Rock**

The verb that ends this verse is one of the most economical in the Qur'an. *Akdā* comes from *al-kudyah*, the layer of hard rock that a well-digger meets beneath the soil. When the digger reaches it and cannot go further, the Arabs said of him that he dug and hit hard rock.

From that image the verb came to mean any endeavour that fails before reaching its goal. The digger's work is not worthless because he was lazy; it is worthless because he stopped short of water.

Applied here, the word describes a man whose giving began and did not finish. The image carries the judgement inside it: the effort was real, the direction was right, and the stopping undid both.

**Giving a Little and Then Stopping**

The verse describes two movements: a small amount given, and then a cessation. The order matters, because a person who gives nothing at all is described differently in the Qur'an from a person who begins and stops.

The beginning indicates that something was recognised. The man saw that giving was required of him, or that it would serve him, and he acted on it. The stopping indicates that the recognition did not last, and the reason for the stopping is what the following verse examines.

The combination is a familiar human pattern and the verse names it without elaboration. Effort that is abandoned partway leaves the person with neither the reward of completion nor the excuse of never having started.

**The Report Behind the Verse**

The occasion recorded in the commentaries fits the description exactly, and it identifies which side of the transaction the man in this verse occupies.

Al-Ṭabarī reports from Mujāhid and Ibn Zayd that the verses concern al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, who agreed to pay part of his wealth to an idolater who undertook to carry his punishment in the hereafter, on condition that he abandoned the Prophet's message. He paid a portion of the agreed sum and then refused the remainder. The report reaches no Companion, so it stands as an exegetical account rather than a graded tradition, and it is cited here on that footing.

What the report contributes is the identity of the two movements in the verse. The giving was the payment agreed for the guarantee; the stopping was the withheld remainder. On that reading the verse describes a bargain that was never completed, and the failure lay with the man who was buying, which is why the question in the following verse asks whether he has knowledge of the unseen.
**What the Stopping Reveals**

The verse's diagnosis is that the giving was never rooted in conviction. A person who gives because he believes that giving is right continues to give when the circumstances change; a person who gives as part of an exchange stops when the exchange ends.

The distinction is not visible in the act itself. Both men hand over money. It becomes visible in the stopping, and the stopping is what the verse isolates.

That is why the verse is followed by a question about knowledge rather than by a condemnation. The man's conduct is treated as evidence of what he believed, and what he believed is examined in the verse that follows.

**The Parallel in al-Qiyāmah**

The same figure appears in another Meccan sūrah describing a man who neither believed nor prayed, but persisted in denial and turned away (75:31–32).

The parallel is close in structure. Both passages describe a turning away, and both treat the turning as the revelation of a prior state rather than as a new decision. The man in al-Qiyāmah did not begin and then stop; he turned away after having approached, and the turning disclosed what had been there.

The recurrence shows that the Qur'an regarded this as a distinct category of person: not the outright opponent, but the one who came near and left, and whose leaving is more informative than his coming.

**Akdā Compared With Other Words for Withholding**

The Qur'an has a range of vocabulary for refusing to give, and the choice of *akdā* here is not the obvious one.

*Bakhila* names stinginess as a settled character trait; *amsaka* describes the simple act of holding back; *qatara* denotes meanness in spending. Each of these would have conveyed the refusal, and none of them is used.

*Akdā* is different because it describes an interrupted process rather than a disposition. The man is not called a miser; he is described as someone who began and did not finish, and the image is of a digger whose work stops at the rock.

The difference changes the diagnosis. A miser never starts, and his fault lies in the refusal. This man started, which means something in him responded, and his fault lies in the stopping. The verse's choice of word makes that distinction without stating it, which is why the commentators dwell on the verb's origin in the language of well-digging.

**Why the Verse Ends With a Question**

Both this verse and the one before it are interrogative, and the form deserves notice.

A statement about the man would have been met with agreement or contradiction, and the audience — many of whom liked him or owed him money — would have had reason to contradict. A question cannot be contradicted in the same way; it has to be considered.

The two questions also work together. The first asks whether the turning away has been observed; the second asks about the giving and the stopping. Between them they assemble the evidence, and the evidence assembles itself into a conclusion the audience reaches on its own.

That method recurs in the sūrah. Where a charge could be resisted, the sūrah asks a question; where the answer is obvious once asked, the charge is unnecessary.

**The Verse's Warning to the Giver**

The verse has an application beyond the man it describes, and the commentators have drawn it out. A person who is satisfied that his past deeds will suffice, or who considers himself pure, may stop performing good deeds — and the stopping may undo the deeds that preceded it.

The image of the well makes the point without needing elaboration. The digging was genuine, the direction was correct, and the work produced nothing because it did not reach water.

The warning is addressed to anyone who has begun. Beginning is not the achievement; reaching is. The verse's single verb carries the whole of that distinction.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:35
> Does he have the knowledge of the unseen so that he sees ˹the Hereafter˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Knowledge the Qur'an Reserves**

The verse asks whether the man possesses knowledge of the unseen, and the question is pointed because the Qur'an reserves that knowledge as a divine attribute.

The unseen, *al-ghayb*, covers everything beyond human observation: the future, the outcome of a life, the state of hearts, and the arrangement of the world to come. The Qur'an states repeatedly that its keys are with God alone, and it presents the claim to possess them as the mark of a false prophet or an arrogant man.

Applied here, the question asks whether this man has been given what God has not given him. The answer is obvious, and the obviousness is the point: the man's conduct presupposes a knowledge he cannot have.

**What His Conduct Presupposed**

The commentators read the verse as addressed to a specific calculation. The man had stopped giving, and the stopping implies that he knew his past deeds would suffice, or that his wealth would be preserved if he kept it.

Neither claim is available to him. Whether his past deeds will suffice is a matter for the judgment he has not faced; whether his wealth will be preserved is a matter for a future he cannot see. Both are questions about the unseen, and he acted as though he had answers to them.

That is the arrogance the verse identifies. It is not a claim made in words but a claim made in conduct, and conduct of that kind is harder for a person to notice in himself than a spoken boast would be.

**The Parallel Verses**

The same question is put in two other chapters, in nearly identical words. Or do they have access to the Record in the unseen, so they copy it for all to see? (52:41, and the same sentence at 68:47).

The image in those verses is of a register that can be consulted and transcribed. The question asks whether the deniers have been admitted to it, and the absurdity of the suggestion is what carries the argument.

The recurrence of the question across three sūrahs marks it as one of the Qur'an's standing responses to confident claims about the future. Where a person acts on a certainty he cannot have, the response is to ask where the certainty came from.

**Seeing as Knowing**

The verb at the end of the verse is the verb of sight: *fa-huwa yarā* — so that he sees. The commentators take it as knowledge presented in the form of vision, which is the Qur'an's common idiom for certainty.

The choice of the visual verb is deliberate. The man does not merely believe that his calculation is correct; he sees it, in the sense that he treats it as established fact. The verse questions the seeing rather than the conclusion, because a conclusion drawn from nothing visible cannot be seen at all.

The same idiom runs through the earlier part of this sūrah, where the Prophet's vision is described in detail and his sight is said not to have wandered. There the seeing was real and its limits were respected; here the seeing is claimed and has no object.

**The Question as the End of an Argument**

The verse does not offer a counter-argument to the man's calculation. It asks a question that removes the ground of the calculation entirely.

If he has no knowledge of the unseen, then his decision to stop giving rests on nothing. The decision may still be comfortable, and it may still be profitable in the short term, but it has no warrant, and the verse reduces it to that.

That is the sūrah's method with the audience's other claims. Where a belief rests on conjecture, the sūrah says so; where a conduct rests on an assumed knowledge, the sūrah asks where the knowledge came from. Both moves end the matter without needing to establish the opposite.

**The Keys of the Unseen**

The Qur'an states what the unseen contains and who holds it, and the statement leaves nothing with human beings.

With Him are the keys of the unseen—no one knows them except Him (6:59). The verse in Sūrah Luqmān adds the specifics: the knowledge of the Hour, the sending down of rain, what is in the wombs, what any soul will earn tomorrow, and the land in which it will die (31:34).

The list is instructive because every item on it is something people want to know and none of them is available. The man who stopped giving was acting on exactly this kind of knowledge — whether his wealth would be preserved, whether his past deeds would suffice — and the verses deny that anyone outside God possesses it.

The Prophet himself is told to say that he does not possess the treasuries of God, nor does he know the unseen (6:50). If the messenger does not hold it, the man who withheld his giving certainly does not.

**Knowledge Claimed and Knowledge Given**

The verse distinguishes two conditions that look similar from outside. A person may act with confidence because he has been told something, or because he has assumed it, and the conduct is identical in both cases.

The difference is the source. Knowledge given comes through revelation and is held with the awareness that it was given; knowledge claimed comes from the claimant and is held with the same confidence but no ground.

The sūrah has been tracking this distinction since its opening verses, where the Prophet's speech is described as nothing but revelation revealed. The same standard is applied here to a man making no such claim: his conduct asserts a certainty he never received.

The verse's question is the test. Where a person acts on knowledge of the unseen, the question is always where it came from, and the only acceptable answer is that it was given.

**The Turn to the Scriptures**

The verse is followed by a reference to the scriptures of Moses and Abraham, and the connection between the two is the alternative to private knowledge.

The man had no access to the unseen. What he did have access to was revelation already given — the scrolls of Moses and the record of Abraham, both of which state the principles that govern the outcome of a life.

The sūrah therefore moves from what the man cannot know to what has already been told, and the next verses set out what those scriptures contain.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:36
> Or has he not been informed of what is in the Scripture of Moses,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Scrolls of Moses**

The verse names a written revelation, and the name is specific. *Ṣuḥuf Mūsā*, the scrolls of Moses — not the Torah as a whole but the written sheets given to him, which the Qur'an mentions elsewhere as a distinct thing.

The reference is to a text the audience recognised. The Meccans did not possess the scrolls, but they knew of Moses and of the revelation given to him, and the Qur'an's frequent appeals to earlier prophets assume that familiarity.

The verse's appeal is therefore to something outside the Qur'an. It does not ask the man to accept the Prophet's word; it asks whether he has been informed of what an earlier revelation already stated.

**Why Moses Comes Before Abraham**

The order of the two names has been noted by the commentators, since Abraham precedes Moses by generations and the natural sequence would put him first.

The explanation given is that the scrolls of Moses were more widely known. The audience had heard of the Torah and of the law given to Moses in a way they had not heard of the scrolls of Abraham, and the sūrah begins with the more familiar of the two.

The ordering is a small instance of a general feature of the Qur'an's argument. It arranges its material for the person being addressed rather than for chronological tidiness, and it begins where the hearer's knowledge is strongest.

**The Argument From Earlier Revelation**

The verse makes a claim about continuity, and the claim is doing real work in the argument.

The principles set out in the verses that follow — that no soul bears another's burden, that a person has only what he strove for, that the outcome will be seen and fully repaid — are presented not as new information but as the content of scriptures given long before. The man who stopped giving is not being told something unprecedented; he is being reminded of what was already recorded.

That framing matters for the audience's response. A new teaching can be dismissed as an innovation; a teaching already present in the scriptures of Moses and Abraham cannot be dismissed by anyone who accepts those scriptures.

**Nabba'a: The Verb of Being Informed**

The verb used is not the ordinary word for knowing. *Lam yunabbaʾ* — has he not been informed? — comes from a root carrying the sense of a significant report, news of consequence delivered to someone who did not have it.

The word implies that the information was available and that the man had access to it. The question is not whether the principles exist but whether he has been told them, and the expectation is that he has.

That is the force of the interrogative form. It presumes the informing has happened and asks why the conduct does not reflect it, which is a sharper question than a bare statement of the principles would have been.

**The Audience and the Scriptures**

The verse is addressed to a man who did not accept the Qur'an, and the appeal to earlier scriptures needs to be understood in that light.

The Meccan audience was not a community of scripture readers. Their knowledge of Moses and Abraham came through contact with Jews and Christians in Arabia and through the general currency of the prophets' stories. The verse appeals to that knowledge rather than to textual study.

What it asks is not that the man verify a text but that he acknowledge a tradition he already knows: that the prophets before Muhammad taught that each person is answerable for his own conduct. The acknowledgement requires no conversion and no scholarship.

**What the Scrolls of Moses Were**

The Qur'an distinguishes between the scrolls given to Moses and the Torah as a whole, and the distinction is worth setting out.

The word *ṣuḥuf* denotes sheets or written pages, and the Qur'an uses it for revelations given in written form. Sūrah al-Aʿlā refers to the scrolls of Abraham and Moses as containing a shared teaching (87:19), which is the same pairing this verse makes.

The scrolls are therefore presented as an early written record, distinct in form from the law given later at Sinai. What the verse claims about them is not their extent but their content: they stated the principles that the following verses set out.

That claim is modest and therefore hard to dispute. It does not require the audience to accept the Qur'an's account of the scrolls in full, only to accept that the prophets before Muhammad taught individual responsibility — a teaching that was common ground with the scripture-bearing communities of Arabia.

**The Appeal and Its Limits**

The verse's appeal to earlier revelation has a limit, and the limit is worth stating plainly.

The audience did not possess the scrolls and could not consult them. The appeal therefore rests on what they knew of the prophets' teaching through the Jewish and Christian communities around them, not on a text they could read.

That is a weaker form of evidence than a quotation, and the sūrah knows it. What the verse asks is not verification but acknowledgement: that the man has heard this teaching before, from a source he regards as older and more authoritative than the Qur'an, and that his conduct contradicts it.

The appeal is thus rhetorical rather than evidential in the strict sense. It does not prove the principles; it removes the excuse of novelty, which was the audience's most convenient objection to the message.

**The Continuity Claimed**

The verse establishes a line running from Abraham through Moses to the present message, and the line is the ground of the sūrah's authority.

The claim is that the moral order described in the following verses is not a Meccan innovation but the consistent teaching of the prophets, recorded in writings older than the audience's own traditions. The three named stones, by contrast, have no scripture, no prophet, and no record.

That contrast is implicit rather than stated, and it connects this passage back to the examination of the idols earlier in the sūrah. One side has a written record; the other has names without authority.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:37
> and ˹that of˺ Abraham, who ˹perfectly˺ fulfilled ˹his covenant˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ibrāhīm Alladhī Waffā**

The verse attaches an epithet to Abraham and leaves it unexplained: Abraham, who fulfilled. The verb *waffā* means to complete, to discharge in full, to leave nothing outstanding.

The Qur'an uses the same root elsewhere for Abraham, describing him as a model of excellence, devoted to God, perfectly upright, not a polytheist, utterly grateful for God's favours (16:120–121). The epithet here is shorter and more open, and the openness has produced several readings of what exactly he fulfilled.

What all the readings share is the idea of completion. Abraham is named not for what he believed but for what he carried through to the end.

**The Readings of What He Fulfilled**

The commentators offer three main answers, and each has its ground in the text.

The first is that he fulfilled the trials he was given, enduring what no one else was made to endure. The second is that he fulfilled the command to deliver God's message, holding nothing back. The third is that he fulfilled the covenant God made with him.

The readings differ in object but agree in substance. Each describes a man who was given something difficult and completed it, and the verse's brevity allows all three to stand. The commentators did not feel obliged to choose, because the choice would not change what the epithet establishes: Abraham finished what he was given.

**The Fire and the Sacrifice**

Two events are usually cited as the content of the fulfilment, and both are recorded elsewhere in the Qur'an.

The first is the fire. His people concluded that he should be burned to avenge their gods, if they had to act at all (21:68), and the answer was given in a single command: O fire! Be cool and safe for Abraham! (21:69). The second is the sacrifice. Abraham said, O my dear son! I have seen in a dream that I ˹must˺ sacrifice you (37:102), and the son answered, O my dear father! Do as you are commanded (37:102), and the outcome was the call: O Abraham! You have already fulfilled the vision (37:104–105).

The verb used there is the same verb as in this verse. The Qur'an itself describes Abraham as having fulfilled, in the account of the sacrifice, which supports the reading that the fulfilment concerns the trials he endured.

**Fulfilment as a Standard**

The epithet sets a standard that the sūrah then applies to its audience, and the application is implicit but direct.

Abraham completed what was asked of him. The man described in the preceding verses began and stopped. The contrast between the two is the argument of the passage, and it is made without stating the comparison.

That method is characteristic of the Qur'an's use of the prophets. They are named not for admiration but as measures, and the reader is left to measure himself against them. The verse does not say that the man who withheld his giving fell short; it puts Abraham's completion in front of him and lets the shortfall appear.

**Why Abraham Is Named Here**

Abraham held a particular position in the audience's self-understanding. The Quraysh traced their descent to him through Ishmael, regarded the Kaʿbah as his foundation, and performed a pilgrimage they believed he had instituted.

Naming him here is therefore an appeal to their own claimed ancestor. The sūrah asks whether they have been informed of what is in the scrolls of Moses and of Abraham, and the second name is the one they claimed as theirs.

The appeal has an edge. A people who claim descent from a man who fulfilled are being asked to consider what they themselves completed, and the answer, in the case of the man who stopped giving, is that he completed nothing.

**The Trials Abraham Was Given**

The Qur'an states that Abraham was tested by his Lord with commands, and that he fulfilled them, and the statement is close enough to this verse to be read as its commentary.

The trials are not listed in a single place, and the commentators gather them from the accounts: the command to leave his father's house and his people's religion, the confrontation with the idols and the fire, the migration to a land he did not know, the leaving of his wife and infant son in a waterless valley, and the command to sacrifice his son.

Each of these was completed. That is what the epithet in this verse asserts, and the assertion is supported by the record rather than by a claim. The Qur'an's accounts of Abraham show him carrying each command through to the point where its completion was beyond doubt.

The gathering of the trials from separate chapters is itself informative. No single account claims to be the fulfilment mentioned here, and the epithet covers the whole of his obedience rather than one episode of it.

**Fulfilment in the Qur'an's Moral Vocabulary**

The root of *waffā* carries a specific sense in the Qur'an: giving in full, holding to a commitment without shortfall.

It is used for God as well as for human beings. The Qur'an states that every soul will be repaid in full for what it earned, using the same root, and it describes the repayment as complete and undiminished. It is also used for the keeping of covenants and the giving of full measure in trade, where the shortfall is the specific wrong condemned.

Applied to a person, the word describes someone who does not underdeliver. That is the quality the verse attributes to Abraham, and it is the quality the man described in the preceding verses lacked: he gave a little and stopped.

The contrast between the two figures is therefore built into the vocabulary. One fulfilled; the other fell short of his own beginning, and the sūrah sets the two within a few verses of each other.

**What the Two Scriptures Establish**

The verse closes the introduction to the list that follows. Two revelations have been named, and both are presented as containing the same principles.

The naming establishes that the moral order about to be stated is not the Prophet's invention. It was recorded in the scrolls of Moses and in the record of Abraham, and it is being restated here in the same terms.

That is the ground on which the following verses stand. They are not new claims requiring new evidence; they are the content of a teaching that the audience already accepted in its sources, and the verses set them out one by one.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:38
> ˹They state˺ that no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Statement of the Axiom**

This verse is the earliest statement in the order of revelation of a principle the Qur'an repeats many times: no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another.

The formulation is compact. *Wizr*, a burden, is the word used for a load carried, and by extension for a sin carried by the person who committed it. The verse denies the transfer of that load: what one person has taken on himself cannot be shifted to someone else.

The repetition of the word in the sentence — no burdened soul bears the burden of another — is not ornament. It fixes the subject and the object in the same term, so that the denial of transfer is unmistakable.

**The Parallel Verses**

The same sentence appears in several chapters, and the recurrence marks it as a fixed formula of the revelation.

No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another appears in Sūrah al-Anʿām within a statement about lordship and return (6:164), and in Sūrah al-Isrāʾ immediately before the statement that God does not punish until a messenger has been sent (17:15). Sūrah Fāṭir adds the case of a person who calls for help with his load and receives none, even from a close relative (35:18).

The consistency of the wording across sūrahs revealed at different times indicates a settled doctrine rather than a passing remark. The Qur'an returns to it whenever the question of who is answerable for whom arises.

**What the Axiom Excludes**

The principle rules out vicarious atonement. No person can take on another's guilt, and no person's punishment can be discharged by someone else's suffering.

The exclusion is directed at a belief with real currency in the audience's world. Systems of sacrifice in which one party bears the guilt of another were familiar in Arabia and in the surrounding religions, and the verse removes the possibility from the account of judgment the Qur'an gives.

The exclusion has a protective side as well. It means that no one is punished for another's crime — not for a father's sin, not for a tribe's offence, not for the errors of an ancestor. The individual stands alone before the judgment, and the aloneness is a guarantee as much as a burden.

**What the Axiom Does Not Exclude**

The commentators are careful to note the limit of the principle. A person is not punished for another's sin, but he is answerable for the influence he exerted over it.

The Prophet's saying on the point is recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1017: whoever sets an evil precedent in Islam bears its burden and the burden of those who acted on it afterwards, without anything being deducted from their burden. The same report gives the positive case, for the one who sets a good precedent.

The distinction is precise. The burden is not transferred — the followers carry their own — but the one who established the practice carries the weight of having established it. Influence is not vicarious guilt, and the verse does not exempt anyone from the consequences of what he made easy for others.

**The Double Punishment of Those Who Lead Others Astray**

The Qur'an states the same point in another form, describing those who misled others as receiving a double share of the punishment, and recording the followers asking that the torment of their leaders be multiplied, and the answer that it has already been multiplied for all (7:38).

The request is refused in the sense that both receive their share; neither is exempted by the other's greater guilt. That is the axiom operating in a scene: the burden stays with its bearer, and the one who caused the bearing carries his own additional weight.

Sūrah al-Aḥzāb records the plea of those in the Fire: Our Lord! Give them double ˹our˺ punishment, and condemn them tremendously (33:68). The consistency across chapters shows that the Qur'an treats influence as a distinct category of responsibility, not as an exception to the axiom.

**Burden as a Legal Image**

The word *wizr* carries a legal and physical weight at once, and both senses operate in the verse.

In its original sense it denotes a load carried on the back, the kind of burden a person takes up and transports. Applied to conduct, it denotes the sin as something the person has taken on himself and now carries, with the implication that the carrying is his and cannot be handed over.

The image is what makes the denial vivid. A load can in principle be transferred — one person can take up what another has set down — and the verse says that in the matter of sin this transfer does not happen. The load stays with the one who took it up.

The same image underlies the verse in Sūrah Fāṭir describing a person weighed down by his load, calling for help with it, and receiving none, even from a close relative (35:18). The calling is the natural human response; the refusal is the axiom operating.

**The Axiom and Tribal Responsibility**

The principle stated here cut against a settled feature of the audience's society, and the cutting is part of its significance.

Arabian tribal law worked on collective responsibility. A blood debt was owed by the tribe, not only by the individual; a man's offences could bring consequences on his kin, and his kin's offences could bring consequences on him. Protection and liability were shared, and the sharing was the basis of the tribal order.

The verse transfers the whole of the liability to the individual. On the day it describes, no tribe stands behind a person, no lineage supplies cover, and no ancestor's standing is available to draw on.

That is a radical statement in its setting, and it explains why the Qur'an repeats the axiom so often. It was not merely a theological proposition but a reordering of where responsibility lies, and the reordering required repetition to be absorbed.

**The Verse's Place in the List**

This verse is the first of a series stating what the earlier scriptures contain, and its position is deliberate.

The principle of individual responsibility is the foundation of everything that follows. If burdens could be transferred, then striving would be pointless, since another's effort could supply the deficiency; and if striving were pointless, the seeing and the rewarding that the next verses describe would have no object.

The verse therefore establishes the condition that makes the rest of the list coherent, and it is stated first for that reason.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:39
> and that each person will only have what they endeavoured towards,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Endeavour as the Measure**

The verse states the positive side of the principle given before it. A person has nothing except what he strove for, and the word used for striving, *saʿā*, denotes effort exerted with movement toward a goal.

The measure is therefore not intention alone, nor result alone, but the exertion itself. A person who strove and failed has his striving; a person who succeeded without striving has nothing to claim on that account.

The formulation is exclusive: nothing except what he strove for. The exclusion closes off the alternatives — inherited merit, the standing of one's tribe, the reputation of one's ancestors — all of which the audience's system had relied on.

**The Two Sides of the Rule**

The verse is the counterpart of the one before it, and the two together cover both directions of the account.

No one carries another's sin, and no one receives credit for another's good. The first protects a person from the offences of his kin; the second removes any claim on the virtues of his kin. Together they make the individual the sole unit of the reckoning.

That pairing is what the audience's system could not accommodate. It depended on intermediaries whose standing could be borrowed, and on a kinship with the divine that could be inherited. Both mechanisms are closed by these two verses.

**The Continuing Effect of Deeds**

The commentators add a qualification that keeps the verse from being read too narrowly. A person has what he strove for, and the effects of his striving can outlive him.

The Prophet's saying on the point is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1631: when a man dies his acts come to an end, except three — a recurring charity, knowledge from which people benefit, and a righteous child who prays for him. The same report is transmitted by al-Tirmidhī, Abū Dāwūd, and al-Nasāʾī.

The three exceptions are not contradictions of the verse. Each of them is something the deceased strove for while alive: the charity he established, the knowledge he taught, the child he raised. The reward continues because the striving occurred, not because another's effort is credited to him.

**The Apparent Tension and Its Resolution**

Readers have sometimes seen a conflict between this verse and the practice of doing good on behalf of the dead, and the resolution lies in the wording.

The verse concerns what a person has by right of his own endeavour. It does not concern what God chooses to grant, and the Qur'an describes God's generosity as exceeding the measure of the deed: those who did good are rewarded with what is best, as this sūrah itself states a few verses earlier.

The distinction is between the earned and the given. The earned is bounded by the striving; the given is bounded by nothing, and the verse does not limit it. That is why the commentators could hold both the strictness of the rule and the reality of continuing benefit without contradiction.

**Striving and Its Direction**

The verse has a further implication about the quality of effort. Striving is measured, and effort spent on what does not last produces nothing that lasts.

The man described in the preceding verses strove to preserve his wealth, and the striving produced the wealth's preservation and nothing else. The Qur'an's consistent position is that effort directed at this world receives its reward in this world and is exhausted by it.

That is the practical edge of the verse. It does not say that striving is rewarded regardless of its object; it says that a person has what he strove for, which leaves the choice of object with him and the consequence of the choice with the object.

**Saʿā: The Word for Striving**

The verb in this verse denotes effort exerted with movement toward an end, and the choice of word is precise.

*Saʿā* is not the general word for doing. It describes a person setting out and working toward something, and it is used in the Qur'an for walking with purpose to the Friday prayer and for the running between the two hills in the pilgrimage rites. In each case the word carries the sense of directed exertion rather than mere activity.

That sense matters for the measure the verse establishes. A person is credited with what he strove for, and striving implies direction: the effort was aimed at something. Effort expended without direction, or aimed at what does not last, is still striving, and the verse's terms apply to it accordingly.

The word also excludes accident. What comes to a person without his exertion is not something he strove for, and it does not enter the account on his side. The measure is the exertion, and nothing else.

**The Verse and the Audience's Claim to Merit**

The verse removes a ground the audience relied on, and the removal is as significant as the promise it contains.

The Quraysh regarded themselves as the custodians of the sanctuary and the descendants of Abraham, and the standing that came with those positions was treated as a form of credit. A person could be secure because of his lineage, his office, or his tribe's relation to the sacred house.

The verse reduces the account to the individual's striving. Lineage supplies nothing; office supplies nothing; the standing of one's people supplies nothing. What a person has is what he exerted himself for, and the exertion is his alone.

That is the same blow the earlier verses struck at the idolatrous system, applied now to social position. The system promised security through affiliation; the verse states that the reckoning recognises no affiliation, and the statement would have been heard as a direct challenge to the audience's own sense of standing.

**The Verse's Place in the Sequence**

Three statements have now been given: the burden stays with its bearer, the reward belongs to the one who strove, and the striving will be seen. The sequence moves from the principle to its application.

The next statement concerns the visibility of the deeds, and after that their repayment in full. The order is the order of a judgment: the rule, the evidence, the verdict.

The verse supplies the rule, and its brevity is proportionate to its certainty. Nothing is argued here, because nothing needs to be.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:40
> and that ˹the outcome of˺ their endeavours will be seen ˹in their record˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Deeds Will Be Seen**

The verse states that the striving will be seen, and the passive form of the verb leaves the seeing open: it will be seen, without saying by whom.

The commentators supply the answer from elsewhere in the Qur'an. The deeds will be seen by God, by the person who performed them, and by others on the day of judgment. The seeing is not a private review but a disclosure.

That openness is the point of the verse. The striving described in the previous verse was performed in this world, often in private and often without witness; the verse says that its visibility is deferred rather than absent.

**The Verse in Sūrah al-Tawbah**

The Qur'an states the same disclosure in an explicit form. Tell them, O Prophet: do as you will. Your deeds will be observed by Allah, His Messenger, and the believers. And you will be returned to the Knower of the seen and unseen, then He will inform you of what you used to do (9:105).

The verse names three witnesses and adds the return, and its structure matches the one here: the deeds, the seeing, the return, the informing.

The difference is one of context. The verse in al-Tawbah is addressed to people whose conduct was under suspicion, and it functions as a warning. The verse here is part of a statement of the moral order recorded in the earlier scriptures, and it functions as a description. The same fact serves both purposes.

**The Record**

The translation's insertion points to a feature of the Qur'an's description of judgment: the deeds are recorded, and the record is presented.

The Qur'an describes a book that leaves out nothing small or great, and it describes the person reading it and recognising his own conduct in it. The record is not an external accusation but the person's own history made visible to him.

That feature answers an objection to the idea of judgment. The person is not judged on another's account of him; he is shown his own, and the showing is what the word for seeing in this verse denotes.

**Seeing as Public Disclosure**

The disclosure has a social dimension that the commentators draw out. The deeds will be seen by others, and the seeing is part of the reckoning rather than an incidental feature of it.

In this world a person's conduct is partially hidden. He is known by what he chose to reveal, and his motives are known to no one. The verse states that the concealment is temporary: the striving will be seen, in full, by those who did not see it before.

For the one who strove well, the disclosure is a vindication; for the one who performed and concealed his performance for effect, it is the exposure of the effect he was seeking. The same event serves both, and the difference lies in what is disclosed.

**The Verse's Function in the List**

Three statements have now been made about the reckoning: the burden stays with its bearer, a person has what he strove for, and the striving will be seen. The fourth will be the repayment.

The order is the order of a court. The rule is stated, the evidence is produced, and the verdict follows. The verse supplies the evidence, and it does so in a single clause because the evidence is not in dispute — it is the person's own conduct.

The economy of the statement is worth noting. No procedure is described, no tribunal is pictured, and no account is given of how the seeing occurs. The fact is stated and the description is left out, as it was in the earlier part of this sūrah when the covering of the Lote-Tree was left unnamed.

**The Passive Form and Why It Matters**

The verb in this verse is passive: the striving will be seen. The form leaves the agent unnamed, and the leaving-out is informative.

An active form would have named the one who sees, and the naming would have narrowed the statement. The passive allows all three witnesses named in the parallel verse — God, the Messenger, and the believers — to be included, and it allows the person's own seeing of his deeds to be included as well.

There is also a rhetorical effect. The passive states the event as certain and impersonal, like a fact about the world rather than an action performed by a particular party. The deeds will be seen in the way that a verdict will be delivered: the process does not depend on anyone's decision to carry it out.

The Qur'an uses the same construction elsewhere for the disclosure of what is hidden, and the consistency shows that the form was chosen for its implication of inevitability rather than for stylistic variety.

**The Deeds Made Visible to Their Doer**

The disclosure described in the Qur'an includes the person's own confrontation with his record, and that confrontation is the sharpest part of it.

The Qur'an describes the book being placed and the wicked crying, Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted? (18:49). The horror in that verse is not the punishment but the completeness of the record: nothing omitted, nothing forgotten, nothing mitigated by the passage of time.

The verse here points at the same event with a single verb. The striving will be seen, and the first person to see it fully will be the one who performed it, in a form he cannot dispute because it is his own.

That is why the seeing precedes the repayment in the sequence. The verdict follows the evidence, and the evidence is not supplied by an accuser but by the person's own history, presented to him.

**From Seeing to Repayment**

The verse ends at the seeing, and the next statement concerns what follows it. The connection is causal: the deeds are seen, and on the basis of the seeing they are repaid.

The sequence gives the reckoning its shape. Nothing is repaid that has not been seen, and nothing seen is left unrepaid. The two halves of the process are stated in consecutive verses, and the second completes the first.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:41
> then they will be fully rewarded,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Jazāʾ al-Awfā**

The verse is three words in Arabic: *thumma yujzāhu al-jazāʾ al-awfā* — then it will be repaid with the fullest repayment.

*Awfā* is the superlative of a root meaning to complete and to give in full. It is the same root as the verb used of Abraham in the verse a few lines earlier, where he is described as the one who fulfilled. The word denotes a payment from which nothing has been held back.

The superlative form makes the statement stronger than a promise of fair treatment. The repayment is not merely adequate; it is the fullest there is.

The word also carries a temporal sense. A full payment is one that is not deferred in part or paid in instalments, and the Qur'an's descriptions of that day exclude both. The account is settled once, in full, and nothing is carried over into a further period in which the person waits for the remainder.

**The Reading That Identifies the Fullest Reward**

The commentators ask what the fullest repayment consists of, and one answer given is that it is the vision of God.

The reading rests on the principle that no reward could exceed it, so that if the repayment is the fullest, it must include the greatest thing granted. The reading is not stated in the verse, and it belongs to the exegetical tradition rather than to the text.

The verse itself describes the manner of the repayment rather than its content. It says that the repayment will be complete, and it leaves the identification of the greatest element to the reader's understanding of what God gives.

The reading should therefore be treated as an inference rather than as an exposition of the words. Nothing in the verse names the vision or excludes it, and the commentators who advanced the reading did so because it satisfied the superlative rather than because the text stated it.

**Repayment Without Deduction**

The word carries a specific implication about the reckoning. A full payment is one from which nothing has been subtracted, and the Qur'an's descriptions of judgment consistently emphasise the absence of deduction.

No person is wronged by the reduction of his reward, and no good deed is lost through the passage of time or the obscurity of its performance. The verse in Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ describes the setting up of the scales of justice, and states that no soul will be wronged in the least, and that even a deed the weight of a mustard seed will be brought forth (21:47).

That assurance is what the superlative form delivers here. The person who strove in secret, without witness and without reward in this world, receives the complete measure, and the completeness is the answer to the anonymity of his striving.

**The Asymmetry With the Earlier Statement**

This sūrah has already stated the terms of the reckoning, and the two statements differ.

Earlier the verse said that those who did evil are rewarded for what they did, while those who did good are rewarded with what is best. The evil is met with its equivalent; the good is met with more than its equivalent.

The verse here continues that pattern. The fullest repayment belongs to the one who strove, and the fullness is on the generous side of the account. The reckoning is exact in what it takes and exceeding in what it gives, and the asymmetry is stated twice in the same sūrah.

The doubling of the statement within one chapter is not careless repetition. The first occurrence sets out the terms of the reckoning as part of the moral order; the second states them as the conclusion of the sequence about striving and seeing. The same fact is doing two different jobs, and the reader meets it twice for that reason.

**The Verse's Brevity**

Three words, and the list moves on. The brevity is proportionate to the statement's function: it completes the sequence begun two verses earlier and requires no elaboration.

The sequence has moved from the rule of individual responsibility, through the ownership of the striving, to its visibility, and now to its repayment. Each step has been stated in a single clause, and the whole forms a complete account of the reckoning in five verses.

That economy is a feature of the passage. The principles are given as a list, not as an argument, because they are presented as the content of earlier revelations rather than as new claims requiring demonstration.

**Jazāʾ: The Word for Repayment**

The word used for the repayment is *jazāʾ*, and it carries the sense of a return given in correspondence to an act.

The root denotes requital, and the Qur'an uses it for both the reward of good and the recompense of evil. The word itself is neutral; what determines its sense is what is being repaid and how.

That neutrality is why the verse needs the superlative. A repayment could be partial, could be reduced, or could fall short of the deed; the addition of *al-awfā* excludes all three. The repayment will be the fullest form of itself, and the fullness is the assurance the verse gives.

The choice of a requital word rather than a gift word is deliberate. The verse is not describing a favour granted irrespective of conduct; it is describing a payment owed on the basis of striving, and the payment is described as complete. The earned character of the reward is preserved alongside its generosity.

**The Fullest Reward and the Sūrah's Earlier Promise**

This sūrah has already described the reward of the good-doers, and the two descriptions agree.

The earlier verse stated that those who did evil are rewarded for what they did, while those who did good are rewarded with what is best. The word for what is best there is the feminine superlative of the same root as goodness, and the word here is the superlative of fullness.

The two superlatives describe the same reward from different angles. One says that the reward exceeds the deed in quality; the other says that it lacks nothing in completeness. Together they close off the two ways a payment could disappoint: by being less than deserved, or by being delivered in part.

The repetition of the idea within a single sūrah is a feature of the passage rather than an accident. The reckoning is stated in the list of what the earlier scriptures contained, and it is stated again in the sūrah's own voice, and the two statements match.

**Why the Repayment Is Described Rather Than Itemised**

The verse says nothing about what the repayment consists of, and the silence is consistent with the passage.

A description would have invited comparison with the rewards the audience already imagined — the pleasures of the Garden, the company of the righteous, the vision of God — and the comparison would have become the subject rather than the assurance. The verse gives the assurance and leaves the content.

The method matches the earlier part of the sūrah, where the covering of the Lote-Tree was left unnamed and the sign seen by the Prophet was categorised but not described. In each case the Qur'an states the fact and withholds the detail, and the withholding keeps the reader's attention on the fact.

The result is a promise that cannot be argued with on the grounds of its content, because no content has been specified. What is asserted is the completeness, and the completeness is what the striving person needs to know.

**The Return That Completes the Account**

The verse ends the description of the repayment, and the next statement names the destination to which everything returns.

The connection is necessary. A repayment requires a place and a time in which it occurs, and the verse that follows supplies the first: the ultimate end is to the Lord. The reckoning described here has a location, and the location is the same for everyone.

The list continues from that point with statements about what God does, and the moral order gives way to the description of His power.

The order of the two is significant. The reader is first told what he will receive and on what basis, and only then told what the One who gives it is able to do. The assurance comes before the demonstration, which is the arrangement a person needs when the assurance concerns his own case.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:42
> and that to your Lord ˹alone˺ is the ultimate return ˹of all things˺.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Muntahā: The Ultimate End**

The verse names the destination with a single word: *al-muntahā*, the furthest point, the place where movement stops. To your Lord is the ultimate end.

The word is formed from a root meaning to reach an end or to refrain, and as a noun of place it denotes the terminus of a journey. Everything that moves has a point at which it can go no further, and the verse identifies that point.

The identification is exclusive. The end is not to a tribe, a lineage, an intercessor, or a deity of one's own choosing. It is to the Lord, and the exclusivity is what the verse is asserting.

**The Same Word Used Twice in One Sūrah**

The word *al-muntahā* appears earlier in this sūrah, in the name of the Lote-Tree of the utmost limit beside which the Prophet's second vision occurred. The same term, the same form, applied first to a place in the created order and then to the destination of all things.

The repetition is unlikely to be accidental. The tree marks the limit of the created universe, the point beyond which nothing passes; the verse here marks the limit of every life, the point beyond which no one goes. The two uses of the word frame the sūrah's account of the unseen with the same idea of a boundary.

The first boundary was reached by one man on one occasion. The second will be reached by everyone, and the verse states it as the conclusion of the moral order given in the preceding verses.

**Two Readings of the End**

The commentators read the verse in two ways, and the readings are compatible rather than opposed.

The first takes the end as the day of judgment, when everyone stands before God and the reckoning described in the previous verses takes place. On this reading the verse supplies the occasion of the repayment.

The second takes it as a statement about God Himself — that He is the necessary being from whom all beings derive and to whom they return, whether or not a day of judgment is in view. On this reading the verse is metaphysical rather than eschatological.

The first reading fits the immediate context better, since the preceding verses describe a reckoning that requires an occasion. The second is not excluded by it, and the commentators who hold it take the verse as stating the ground on which the reckoning rests.

**Ends That Differ**

The verse says that the end is to the Lord, and the commentators add that the ends of different groups are not the same.

The Qur'an states the difference plainly: The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that under it rivers flow; eternal is its fruit as well as its shade. That is the ˹ultimate˺ outcome for the righteous. But the outcome for the disbelievers is the Fire! (13:35).

The verse in Sūrah ʿAbasa describes the faces of that day: On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, laughing and rejoicing (80:38–39). The return is common to all; what follows the return is not.

That distinction preserves both halves of the statement. Everyone arrives at the same destination, and the destination is where the difference between them becomes final.

**The Verse as the Hinge of the Passage**

Structurally this verse closes one section and opens another. It completes the account of the reckoning — the rule, the striving, the seeing, the repayment, the return — and the verses that follow begin a catalogue of what God does.

The hinge is logical as well as structural. Once the return is established, the description of God's acts has a purpose: it explains who it is that everything returns to, and what His power extends over.

The catalogue that follows runs from laughter and weeping through life and death, the creation of the pairs, enrichment and impoverishment, the lordship of a star, and the destruction of two tribes. Each item extends the domain of the One to whom the return is made.

**The Return in the Qur'an's Vocabulary**

The Qur'an uses a small family of words for the return, and each carries a slightly different sense.

*Rujʿā* and *marjiʿ* denote the place or fact of returning; *maṣīr* denotes the destination arrived at; *munqalab* describes the turning back from one state to another. The verse here uses *al-muntahā*, the furthest point, which is the strongest of the set because it excludes any further movement.

The choice is significant. A return can be temporary — a person returns and then sets out again — and a destination can be a stopping place on the way to somewhere else. The furthest point is neither. It is where the journey ends, and the verse says that the ending is to the Lord.

That is the assurance and the warning in one word. Nothing lies beyond the destination, so nothing can be appealed to after it, and no further refuge exists for the one who arrives there unprepared.

**Why the Return Is Stated at the End of the Sequence**

The placement of this verse is the conclusion of an argument rather than the start of a new topic, and the sequence that precedes it explains why.

The list has moved from the rule of individual responsibility, to the ownership of the striving, to its visibility, and to its repayment. Each step required a setting in which it could occur, and none of them can occur in this world, where the striving is often unrewarded and the deeds are often unseen.

The return supplies the setting. It is the place and time in which the seeing happens and the repayment is made, and without it the preceding verses would describe a process with no occasion.

That is why the verse comes last in the moral sequence and first in the catalogue of divine acts. It closes one argument and grounds the next, and its single clause does both.

**What the Return Makes Possible**

The statement about the end is what gives the preceding verses their force. Without a return, the rule that a person has only what he strove for would be a description of futility: effort expended and nothing received.

The return is what converts striving into an account. The deeds are seen because there is a place where they are seen; the repayment occurs because there is a time when it occurs; and both are guaranteed by the fact that everything arrives at the same destination.

That is why the verse is placed at the end of the moral sequence rather than the beginning. The sequence needed to be completed before the destination could be stated as its conclusion.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:43
> Moreover, He is the One Who brings about joy and sadness.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Laughter and Weeping as Created Things**

The catalogue opens with the two states that seem most private and most one's own: laughing and weeping. The verse assigns both to God.

The assignment is not that God forces a person to laugh or to cry in a given moment. It is that the capacity for both, the occasions that produce them, and the outcomes that settle into one or the other are all within His decree.

The choice of these two as the catalogue's opening item is deliberate. They are the most immediate expressions of a human being's condition, and they are the ones a person would least think to attribute to anyone but himself.

**Three Readings of the Verse**

The commentators give three readings, and each locates the laughter and the weeping differently.

The first is that God creates the causes of both in this world: He brings joy and grief, and both come from Him. The second is that He makes the believers laugh in the Garden and the disbelievers weep in the Fire, which places the two states in the hereafter.

The third combines them: He causes the believers to weep in this world and to laugh in the next, while the disbelievers laugh here and weep there. The third reading is the one that gives the verse its edge, because it reverses the appearance of things — the weeping of this life is not a verdict, and the laughter of this life is not a certificate.

**The Faces of That Day**

The Qur'an describes the outcome of the reversal in concrete terms. On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, laughing and rejoicing (80:38–39), while ˹other˺ faces will be dusty, cast in gloom (80:40–41).

The description uses the same two states as this verse. The laughing belongs to one group and the weeping to the other, and the assignment is final.

The verse in Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn adds the detail that the believers will laugh at what the deniers used to laugh at, and that the reversal will be visible to both sides. The laughter of this world is thus not merely transient but reversible, and the verse here states the reversal's ground: both states are God's to give.

**Why the Verse Follows the Return**

The placement is significant. The preceding verse established that everything returns to the Lord, and this verse begins to describe what He does.

The first thing described is not creation or judgment but the inner states of human beings. The order suggests that the return leads to a settlement of those states — that the grief and the joy of this life are not the last word about a person, and that the last word belongs to the One to whom the return is made.

That is the consolation the verse offers and the warning it contains. The one who weeps now is not thereby condemned, and the one who laughs now is not thereby secure.

**Human Feeling and Divine Decree**

The verse raises a question about responsibility that the Qur'an answers elsewhere. If joy and grief are from God, what remains of human choice?

The answer the Qur'an gives is that the decree concerns the occurrence of things, not the justification of the responses to them. A person is not blamed for feeling grief; he is answerable for what he does with it — whether it drives him to patience or to complaint, to reliance or to despair.

The verse in Sūrah al-Ḥadīd states the principle: No calamity ˹or blessing˺ occurs on earth or in yourselves without being ˹written˺ in a Record before We bring it into being (57:22), and the purpose given is so that you neither grieve over what you have missed nor boast over what He has granted you (57:23). The decree is stated as the ground of composure rather than as its removal.

**The Two States as the Shortest Account of a Life**

Every human life can be described in terms of these two states, and the verse's choice of them is a choice of the most basic available description.

A person's condition is either one that produces joy or one that produces grief, and the alternation between the two is the ordinary experience of living. Wealth and loss, health and sickness, company and bereavement — each produces one of the two, and no life escapes both.

By assigning both to God, the verse removes the ground for two errors. The person in joy cannot regard his state as a permanent possession he secured, and the person in grief cannot regard his state as a verdict on his worth. Both states are given, and both are temporary in this world.

The economy of the statement is what makes it effective. The verse does not moralise about patience or gratitude; it states the origin of the two states, and the moral consequence follows for anyone who has experienced the alternation.

**Laughter and Weeping in the Qur'an's Scenes**

The Qur'an uses these two states in its descriptions of the hereafter, and the usage gives the verse its eschatological edge.

The believers are described as laughing and rejoicing on that day, with bright faces; others are described as covered with dust and overcome by darkness. In Sūrah al-Muṭaffifīn the reversal is made explicit: those who believed will laugh at what the deniers used to laugh at, and the laughter changes sides.

The scenes show that the two states are not merely psychological events but outcomes. The laughter of this world can be the laughter of contempt, and it can end; the weeping of this world can be the weeping of the oppressed, and it can end as well.

The verse here states the ground of all of it. Both states are God's to bring about, and the distribution between the two worlds is His arrangement rather than a continuation of this one.

**The Catalogue Begins**

This verse is the first in a series describing what God does, and the series moves from the inner states of human beings outward to life and death, creation, provision, and the destruction of nations.

The opening item is the smallest in scale and the closest to the reader. Before the verse speaks of nations destroyed and stars governed, it speaks of laughter and tears, and the ordering makes the catalogue personal before it becomes cosmic.

The next verse takes the pair that every human being experiences in common: life and death.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:44
> And He is the One Who gives life and causes death.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Pair Stated in Full**

The verse names the two acts that bound every human life, and it assigns both to the same agent. He gives life, and He causes death.

The pairing is complete in a way that leaves no room for a third party. Birth is not attributed to nature or to the parents as originators, and death is not attributed to accident or to an enemy. Both are acts, and both belong to one actor.

The statement is short because it needs no support. Every member of the audience had seen both acts performed, and neither had ever been performed by anyone he could name.

**The Order of the Two Words**

The Arabic places death before life: *amāta wa aḥyā*, He caused death and He gave life. The translation reverses the order to match the sequence of a life, and the reversal is worth noting because the original order carries a point.

Death first, then life. The order matches the Qur'an's argument elsewhere, where the fact of death is used to establish the possibility of a life that follows it. A God who brings the living from the dead can bring the dead to life again, and the sequence in the Arabic states the two acts in the order the argument requires.

The same pairing appears throughout the Qur'an — in the challenge of Abraham to the king who claimed the power of life and death (2:258), in the statement that it is Allah who gives life and causes death (3:156), and in the statement that to Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth and He gives life and causes death (57:2).

**The Argument From Life Against Denial**

The verse functions as evidence for the resurrection, which is the claim the sūrah's audience denied.

The argument is straightforward. A person who has been brought into existence from nonexistence has no ground for denying that he can be brought back. The first bringing was not something he contributed to or witnessed; the second requires no greater power than the first.

The Qur'an makes the argument explicitly elsewhere, asking whether the One who created the heavens and the earth cannot easily resurrect the deniers, and answering that He is the Master Creator, All-Knowing (36:81). The verse here states the premise without drawing the conclusion, because the conclusion was already drawn in the verses that preceded it.

**The Spiritual Reading**

Some commentators read the verse at a second level, taking the death and the life as states of the heart rather than of the body.

On this reading, God causes the hearts of His enemies to die through disbelief and darkness, and gives life to the hearts of His friends through faith and knowledge. The reading is not a rejection of the literal sense but an extension of it, and it has support in the Qur'an's own usage, where the one who was dead and was given life and light is contrasted with the one in darkness (6:122).

The extension is legitimate because the Qur'an uses the vocabulary of life and death for both. What matters is that the literal sense stands first: the verse is about the body, and the spiritual reading is an application rather than a substitute.

**The Verse in the Catalogue**

The catalogue has moved from laughter and weeping to life and death, and the movement is from the states within a life to its boundaries.

The ordering is deliberate. The reader is led from what he experiences daily to what happens to him twice, and the second of those happenings is the one he cannot observe in himself.

That placement prepares the verses that follow, which describe the origin of the human being in a drop and the re-creation that lies beyond death. The boundaries are stated before the process between them is described.

**The Formula Across the Qur'an**

The pairing of life and death is one of the most frequently repeated statements in the Qur'an, and the repetition marks it as a foundational claim.

It appears in Abraham's answer to the king who boasted of the power to give life and to kill (2:258), where the challenge is to bring the sun from the west. It appears in the statement that it is Allah who gives life and causes death (3:156). It appears in the statement that to Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, that He gives life and causes death, and that He is Most Capable of everything (57:2).

The consistency of the formula across Meccan and Madinan chapters shows that it was not an argument devised for a particular dispute but a settled description of God's action. Wherever the Qur'an needs to establish power over existence, this is the pair it names.

The verse here uses the formula in its shortest form, within a catalogue of such acts, and the brevity indicates that the audience did not dispute it. The disagreement was about the consequences of the pairing, not the pairing itself.

**Death as an Act, Not an Accident**

The verse's verb makes death an act performed by an agent, and that framing excludes several alternative descriptions.

Death is not presented as a natural process reaching its end, nor as the outcome of an enemy's success, nor as a random event. It is caused, and the causing belongs to one party.

The framing has practical consequences that the Qur'an draws out. The verse in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān addresses those who said of their fallen kin that they would not have been killed had they stayed at home, and the Qur'an states elsewhere that no soul can ever die without Allah's Will at the destined time (3:145).

That answer removes death from the account of human calculation. It is not a consequence of a decision, and no decision can avoid it. The verse here states the principle in two words, and the principle is what the later verses rely on when they argue that the one who causes death can reverse it.

**What the Pair Establishes**

The verse establishes a domain of exclusive divine action, and the establishment has consequences for the sūrah's argument.

If life and death belong to God alone, then no deity can promise protection from death and no intercessor can secure a life. The audience's system offered both, and the verse removes the ground of both in a single clause.

The claim is made without reference to the idols. It is stated as a fact about the world, and its application to the three stones named earlier in the sūrah is left to the reader.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:45
> And He created the pairs—males and females—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Zawjayn: The Two of a Pair**

The verse states the creation of the pair, using the dual form *az-zawjayn*, the two mates, and then specifies them: the male and the female.

The dual is significant. It does not describe a multiplicity of kinds but a single pair, and the pairing is what the verse is asserting — that the two were created as counterparts rather than separately and independently.

The word *zawj* is used in the Qur'an for a spouse, for a mate among animals, and for one of any two corresponding things. Its application here to the sexes presents them as a matched set, each defined in relation to the other.

**Created From a Single Soul**

The Qur'an states the origin of the pair in terms that this verse assumes. He created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate (4:1; 7:189). The two sexes are not two separate creations but one creation extended.

That origin has a consequence for the argument of the sūrah. If the pair comes from a single source, then neither member of it is derived from the other in rank, and the arrangement the audience had made — assigning the female to God and keeping the male for themselves — has no basis in the order of creation.

The verse therefore does real work in the sūrah's case against the idolatrous system, and it does it without argument. The fact of a common origin is stated, and the ranking built on the difference collapses.

**The Pair as a Sign**

The Qur'an treats the pairing of the sexes as one of its signs, and the treatment is consistent across chapters.

He created spouses from among yourselves so that you may find comfort in them, and He placed between you compassion and mercy, and in that are signs for people who reflect (30:21). The pairing is presented not as a biological fact but as evidence of design, since a pairing that produces rest and affection is not the product of chance.

The verse here states the fact in its shortest form. No purpose is given and no description added, because the fact is already familiar to the audience and its status as a sign has been established elsewhere.

**The Connection to the Earlier Passage**

The sūrah spent several verses examining the audience's assignment of genders to the angels and the deities, and concluded that the assignment rested on conjecture without knowledge.

This verse returns to the subject from the opposite direction. The genders that exist were created, and their creation belongs to God; the genders that the audience invented for beings they had never seen have no such origin.

The contrast is between a distinction that is real and one that is projected. The sūrah does not need to argue that the audience's assignment was false; it needs only to state where the real distinction comes from, and the invented one is left without a source.

**Creation as Prerogative**

The verse's verb is the verb of creation, and the attribution is exclusive. He created the pair, and no one else is named as contributing.

The exclusivity matters for the sūrah's argument about the deities. A being that creates has a claim to worship; a being that does not create has none. The Qur'an makes the point directly, asking whether the one who creates is like the one who does not create (16:17).

The verse supplies the premise of that question in a single clause. The creation of the pair — the most basic fact of human existence, repeated in every generation — belongs to God alone, and the three stones named earlier in the sūrah created nothing.

**Pairing Beyond the Sexes**

The Qur'an extends the idea of the pair well beyond the human sexes, and the extension strengthens the claim made in this verse.

The word is used for the pairing of night and day, of the sweet and the bitter in what the earth produces, and of the kinds of plants whose pairs are known and unknown. Sūrah Yā Sīn states that Glory be to the One Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs —˹be it˺ what the earth produces, their genders, or what they do not know! (36:36).

The extension makes the pairing a feature of creation rather than a peculiarity of human beings. Wherever there is a pair, there is a maker of the pair, and the maker is one.

Applied here, the point is that the sexes are not an independent arrangement that arose and persisted on its own. They belong to a pattern that runs through the created order, and the pattern is the evidence for the single source the verse names.

**The Verse's Answer to the Inherited Ranking**

The sūrah examined a ranking the audience had built on the difference between the sexes, and this verse answers it without reference to the ranking.

The answer lies in the common origin. If both members of the pair come from one source, then the difference between them is a difference of function within a single creation, not a difference of standing before the Creator. The one who made both is not partial to either.

That is the point the audience's theology could not accommodate. It required one sex to be preferred and the other assigned elsewhere, and the verse removes the basis by stating the creation of both in one breath.

The method is characteristic of the sūrah's argument. It does not argue against the ranking; it states a fact about the origin of the two, and the ranking is left without a foundation.

**The Next Verse's Detail**

The verse states the creation of the pair and the verse that follows describes its origin, and the two together form a single statement divided for emphasis.

The division allows the sūrah to make two points in sequence: that the sexes were created as a pair, and that the pair comes from a single small beginning. The second point is the one the sūrah uses for its argument about resurrection.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:46
> from a sperm-drop when it is emitted.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Nutfah: The Word and Its Meaning**

The verse names the origin of the pair with a single word: *nutfah*, a drop, a small quantity of fluid. The word is used in Arabic for a mere amount, and its application here is deliberately unimpressive.

The addition of the participle — when it is emitted — fixes the moment. The verse is not describing a substance in the abstract but an event, and the event is the beginning of a human being.

The choice of so small a thing as the origin of the two sexes is the verse's method. It does not describe the complexity of what follows; it names the least of it, and leaves the distance between the beginning and the result to be measured by the reader.

**The Stages Described Elsewhere**

The Qur'an gives the sequence in several places, and the descriptions agree.

Sūrah al-Ḥajj sets it out at length: We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh —fully formed or unformed— (22:5). Sūrah al-Qiyāmah asks whether people were not once a sperm-drop emitted, then a clinging clot, then developed and perfected in form, producing from it both sexes (75:37–39).

The verse in Sūrah al-Ṭāriq adds the detail of the fluid's origin: ˹They were˺ created from a spurting fluid, stemming from between the backbone and the ribcage (86:6–7).

The consistency across chapters shows that the description was a settled part of the revelation's argument, repeated because it was the argument's strongest evidence.

**The Emission as the Starting Point**

The participle in the verse marks a transition: the drop is described at the moment it is emitted, before it has become anything.

That moment is the earliest point at which a human being can be said to begin, and the verse chooses it as the starting point of the description. What precedes it belongs to the parents; what follows it belongs to a process the person does not direct.

The choice has an argumentative purpose. A person who begins as an emitted drop has no claim to have made himself, and no ground for supposing that the power which formed him from that beginning is unable to form him again.

**The Argument From the Origin**

The verse belongs to an argument the Qur'an presses repeatedly against the denial of resurrection, and the argument runs from the origin to the return.

Sūrah al-Qiyāmah draws the conclusion explicitly, Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life? (75:40). The reasoning is that the one who produced a formed human being from an emitted drop has demonstrated the capacity, and the second production requires nothing that the first did not already require.

The verse here supplies the premise without the conclusion, as the neighbouring verses do. The catalogue states what God does, and the inference is left to the reader — though the verse that follows makes it explicit by naming the re-creation.

**The Verse That Breaks the Pattern**

The commentators note a feature of this passage: every verse in the series begins with the particle meaning and that, continuing the list of what was in the scriptures of Moses and Abraham, except this one.

The exception has been explained in two ways. Some hold that the verse is a continuation of the one before it, describing the manner of the creation of the pair, and therefore does not need the particle. Others read the particle in the surrounding verses as carrying the sense of truly or surely rather than that, which would make each verse an independent statement and remove the anomaly.

Either way the verse's attachment to the one before it is close. The pair was created, and it was created from an emitted drop; the two statements are one claim stated in two parts.

**The Qur'an's Use of Embryonic Language**

The vocabulary of the stages appears repeatedly, and the repetition gives it a technical character within the revelation.

*Nuṭfah* is the drop; *ʿalaqah* is the clinging thing, described in the translations as a clot; *muḍghah* is the lump of flesh. Sūrah al-Muʾminūn sets out the sequence in order and adds the forming of bones and their clothing with flesh (23:14). Sūrah al-Ḥajj adds the qualification that the lump is sometimes formed and sometimes not (22:5).

The consistency of the terms across chapters indicates a settled description rather than an improvised one. The Qur'an returns to the sequence because it is the evidence it relies on most often for the claim that the human being is created rather than self-made.

The verse here names only the first stage, and the naming is enough. The audience knew the rest of the sequence from the other chapters in which it had already been recited to them.

**Why the Beginning Is Named and Not the Process**

The verse stops at the emission and does not describe what follows, and the stopping is deliberate.

A description of the process would have moved the reader's attention to the mechanism, and the mechanism was not in dispute. What was in dispute was whether a person could be brought back after death, and the answer to that question lies in the beginning rather than in the middle.

By naming the least of the process — an emitted drop — the verse sets the distance between the origin and the result as large as it can be. The reader measures that distance in his own existence, and the measurement is the argument.

The method is the same one the Qur'an uses elsewhere, asking the person to consider what he was created from and then asking whether the Creator who did that can be unable to do it again.

**The Smallness That Carries the Argument**

The verse's force lies in the disproportion it presents. The smallest of beginnings produces the whole of a human being — the two sexes, the faculties, the life, the deeds, the reckoning described in the verses before this catalogue.

The Qur'an returns to that disproportion because it is the hardest thing in its argument to refuse. A person can dispute a report about the unseen; he cannot dispute the fact of his own origin, which he can verify in his own existence.

The verse therefore occupies a strategic place in the sūrah. It moves the argument from what was reported to what is observable, and it does so in four words.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:47
> And it is upon Him to bring about re-creation.

**Expanded Commentary**

**An-Nashʾah al-Ukhrā: The Second Genesis**

The verse names a second bringing-into-being. *An-nashʾah al-ukhrā*, the other genesis, the second origination, and it assigns it to God with the preposition of obligation: it is upon Him.

The word *nashʾah* comes from a root meaning to bring something into existence and to raise it up. The Qur'an uses it for the first origination as well, and the addition of the other marks this one as distinct from the creation already described in the preceding verses.

The verse therefore states a second act of creation, and it identifies the actor and the certainty of the act in a single clause.

**It Is Upon Him**

The preposition deserves attention. The phrase *wa anna ʿalayhi* — and that upon Him — uses the construction that denotes what someone has taken upon himself, not what is imposed on him by another.

The same construction appears elsewhere in the Qur'an: It is certainly upon Us ˹alone˺ to show ˹the way to˺ guidance (92:12), and It is upon Allah ˹alone˺ to ˹clearly˺ show the Straight Way (16:9). In each case the obligation is self-assumed, a commitment God has made rather than a duty owed to Him.

Read that way, the verse states that the re-creation is something God has committed Himself to. The promise is not contingent on a human claim or a bargain; it belongs to what He has undertaken, and the undertaking is the ground of its certainty.

**The Majority Reading: Resurrection**

Most commentators take the second genesis as the resurrection — the bringing back of the dead at the end of the world.

The reading follows directly from the context. The preceding verses describe the origin of a human being in an emitted drop, and the Qur'an's standard argument runs from that origin to the possibility of a return. The verse in Sūrah al-Qiyāmah draws the inference in the form of a question: is such a Creator unable to bring the dead back to life? (75:40).

On this reading the verse is the conclusion of the argument the catalogue has been building. The God who creates from a drop, who gives life and causes death, and who enriches and impoverishes is the same God who will create again.

**Al-Rāzī's Reading**

One commentator reads the second genesis differently, taking it as the breathing of the spirit into the human being rather than the resurrection.

The reasoning is that what distinguishes a human being from other animals is not the body but the spirit, so that the second origination — the one that makes him what he is — is the moment the spirit is breathed in. The Qur'an describes that moment in the account of Adam's creation, where God describes fashioning Adam and having a spirit of His own creation breathed into him (15:29).

The reading is coherent and it has textual support, but it fits the immediate context less well than the resurrection reading. The surrounding verses are about origins and ends, and the argument the passage is making concerns the return rather than the formation. The majority position is the stronger of the two on contextual grounds.

**The Argument the Verse Completes**

The catalogue has moved through a sequence of divine acts, and this verse is its logical destination.

Laughter and weeping, life and death, the creation of the pair from a drop — each item extends the domain of God's action, and the last of them supplies the evidence for the claim this verse makes. A power demonstrated once in the origination of a human being is a power that can be exercised again.

The Qur'an presses the point in several chapters, arguing that the one who originates creation and then repeats it is not unable to do the second because He did the first. The verse here states the conclusion without the argument, because the argument has just been given in the items that precede it.

**The First Creation as the Evidence**

The Qur'an's argument for the re-creation rests consistently on the first creation, and this verse is the conclusion of that argument.

The reasoning is set out in several chapters. Sūrah Yā Sīn records the man who asked who could give life to bones that have crumbled, and the answer that they will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has perfect knowledge of every created being (36:78–79). Sūrah al-Rūm states that He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him (30:27).

The argument does not depend on the audience accepting the Qur'an's authority. It depends on a fact they could not dispute — that they exist, and that they did not make themselves — and it draws the only conclusion the fact supports.

The verse here states that conclusion in the catalogue's compressed form. The re-creation is upon Him, and the ground is the origination already named in the verses before it.

**Why the Re-Creation Belongs in a Catalogue**

Placing the re-creation among acts such as giving life and death, enriching and impoverishing, and governing a star is a deliberate choice of framing.

A catalogue presents its items as facts of the same order. The re-creation is therefore not set apart as a mystery or a doctrine but listed alongside the ordinary operations of the world, as one more thing the same Lord does.

The framing answers the audience's objection. Their difficulty with the resurrection was that it seemed remote and unprecedented; the catalogue places it beside the acts they witnessed daily, so that its strangeness is reduced to the strangeness of the other items — which is to say, none.

That is the persuasive work the catalogue performs. It does not argue for the re-creation; it locates it, and the location makes it credible.

**The Catalogue Continues**

The verse is not the end of the list. It states the re-creation and then moves on to enrichment and impoverishment, to the lordship of a star, and to the destruction of two nations.

The placement is deliberate. The re-creation is the greatest of the acts named, and it is stated in the middle of the catalogue rather than at its end, so that the remaining items appear as further evidence rather than as additions to a completed case.

The next verse returns to the matter this sūrah began with: the wealth that a man gave and then withheld, and the One who gives wealth and takes it away.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:48
> And He is the One Who enriches and impoverishes.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Aghnā wa Aqnā: The Two Verbs**

The verse pairs two verbs, and the second of them has been read in two ways that differ in meaning.

The first verb is clear: *aghnā*, He enriched, He made someone free of want. The second, *aqnā*, is read either as He granted possessions — giving a person property and means — or as He made content, giving a person sufficiency of heart.

The two readings produce different pairings. On the first, the verse describes two kinds of giving: wealth and property. On the second, it describes a contrast: He enriches some and leaves others with little, and the pairing is between the enriched and the impoverished.

The second reading fits the context better, since the verses before and after describe pairs of opposites — life and death, laughter and weeping. The translation follows it.

**Wealth as Given**

The verse's central claim is that both conditions come from the same source. Richness is not the achievement of the rich man alone, and poverty is not the failure of the poor man alone.

The Qur'an makes the same point repeatedly, stating that Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills (13:26). The restriction is not presented as a punishment or the expansion as a reward; both are described as distributions made by the One who owns what is distributed.

That is the ground of the verse's placement in this catalogue. The sūrah has established that everything belongs to God, and the distribution of wealth is one instance of that ownership in operation.

**The Verse After the Story of the Man Who Withheld**

The catalogue is not abstract, and this verse is the item that connects it back to the sūrah's earlier material.

Several verses before, the sūrah described a man who gave a little and then stopped, and asked whether he had knowledge of the unseen that would justify his calculation. The calculation was about wealth: whether to keep it or to give it.

This verse answers the calculation. The wealth is not his to hoard in the sense he supposed, because it is not his in origin. The one who enriched him can impoverish him, and the withholding does not preserve what was never secured by the keeping.

The connection is made without comment. The catalogue states the fact, and its application to the man who stopped giving is left where the sūrah left it.

**The Test in Both Directions**

Both conditions are tests, and the Qur'an treats them as such rather than as verdicts.

The enriched are tested by the wealth: whether they give from it, whether they attribute it to themselves, whether they suppose it will last. The impoverished are tested by the want: whether they remain patient, whether they complain against the distribution, whether they keep the obligations that do not require money.

The verse in Sūrah al-Fajr describes the two errors directly: as for man, when his Lord tests him and honours him and favours him, he says, my Lord has honoured me; and when He tests him and restricts his provision, he says, my Lord has humiliated me (89:15–16). Both statements are corrected by the verses that follow, and the correction is that neither condition is the verdict the person takes it for.

**The Verse's Function in the Catalogue**

The catalogue has moved from the inner states of human beings, through life and death and the origin of the pair, to the re-creation, and now to provision.

The movement is from what a person experiences inwardly, to what happens to him at his boundaries, to what will happen to him at the end, and now to what he has in between. The sequence covers the whole of a life, and the provision is the item that occupies most of a person's attention within it.

Placing provision after the re-creation is a deliberate ordering. The thing people strive hardest to keep is named after the thing they cannot avoid, and the juxtaposition puts the two in their proper proportion.

**Provision and the Sūrah's Earlier Question**

The verse answers a question the sūrah put several verses before, and the answer is given without reference to the question.

The earlier question asked whether the man who stopped giving had knowledge of the unseen. The unstated content of the question was about provision: whether keeping his wealth would preserve it, and whether giving it away would diminish him.

This verse settles the matter. Provision is not a possession to be defended but a condition to be given and withdrawn, and both movements belong to the same party. The man's calculation assumed that the wealth was his and that its continuation depended on his decisions; the verse states that neither assumption holds.

The separation between the question and the answer is a feature of the sūrah's structure. The moral account is stated in one passage, the catalogue of divine acts in another, and the reader connects them. The connection is the argument's payoff, and it is left to be made rather than announced.

**Enrichment as Responsibility**

The verse describes enrichment as an act of God, and the description carries an obligation that the Qur'an states elsewhere.

Wealth that is given is given for a purpose, and the purpose is specified: spend from what He has provided, and the spending is not a favour to the recipient but a discharge of a trust. The Qur'an describes the wealth of a community as a trust that its members do not own outright, and it warns those who hoard it of a reckoning in which the hoarded wealth is brought against them.

The verse here supplies the premise of that warning. If enrichment is God's act, then the enriched person holds his wealth on terms set by the one who gave it, and the terms include the spending.

That reading connects the verse to the man who stopped giving more closely than the surface suggests. His fault was not merely an interruption of generosity but a refusal of the terms on which the enrichment was held.

**The Lordship That Follows**

The verse is followed by a statement about a single star, and the transition moves the catalogue from human affairs to the heavens.

The connection is the same throughout the list: everything named belongs to God, whether it is a person's wealth or a star in the sky. The catalogue does not distinguish between the great and the small, because the ownership does not distinguish between them.

The next verse names the star, and the naming returns the sūrah to the worship it examined at the beginning.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:49
> And He alone is the Lord of Sirius.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Ash-Shiʿrā: The Star Named**

The verse names one star, and the naming is specific: *ash-Shiʿrā*, the star known in English as Sirius.

The identification is not in doubt. Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky, the chief star of the constellation Canis Major, and it is visible from Arabia for much of the year. Its brightness made it a marker for the seasons and a point of orientation for travellers.

The Qur'an names very few individual stars, and the naming of this one is not incidental. A star is named because something was being done with it.

**The Star and the Worship It Received**

The pre-Islamic Arabs venerated Sirius, and the veneration is recorded in the exegetical tradition as the reason this verse names it.

Some of the tribes regarded the star as a power in its own right, associated with the rising seasons and with the fortunes of those born under it. Al-Qurṭubī states in his commentary on this verse that the first person to worship Sirius was a man called Abū Kabshah, described in the reports as a noble of Khuzāʿah whose tribe followed him in the veneration, and that the Quraysh therefore used the name as a term of abuse for the Prophet, calling him the son of Abū Kabshah because he had opposed their idols as Abū Kabshah had opposed theirs.

The explanation of the nickname is recorded in the classical works on names and genealogy, including Ibn al-Athīr's al-Nihāyah and al-Dāraquṭnī's al-Muʾtalif wa-l-Mukhtalif. The identification of Abū Kabshah as a maternal ancestor is a report rather than settled genealogy, and it is cited here as such. What is not in doubt is that the verse names a star that was venerated, and that the naming denies the veneration.
**The Return to the Idol Passage**

The verse closes a circle that the sūrah opened many verses earlier. The examination of al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt established that the deities had names without authority and functions without power.

This verse extends the examination to the heavens. The stones were on the ground and could be seen; the star is above and cannot be reached, and the temptation to ascribe power to it is correspondingly greater. The verse meets the temptation by stating the star's Lord.

The commentators note this return explicitly: the verse comes back to the theme of the earlier passage, which was that God is the Lord of everything to which the pagans ascribe independent power. The list of such things included stones, angels, and stars, and the catalogue names all three.

**Why One Star Rather Than the Stars**

The verse could have said the Lord of the heavens or the Lord of the stars. It names one, and the particularity has a purpose.

A general statement about the heavens can be accepted while a particular veneration continues. A person who agrees that God rules the sky may still regard one star as having influence over his affairs, and the general statement does not touch the particular belief.

Naming the star removes the refuge. The verse does not say that the heavens belong to God and leave Sirius as an open question; it names Sirius and states its Lord, so that the specific object of the veneration is covered by the specific claim.

**Lordship Over the Visible**

The verse makes a claim about the visible world that the audience could check.

Sirius rises and sets on a fixed schedule, and the schedule is not altered by offerings, prayers, or veneration. The star's behaviour is the same for the one who worships it and the one who does not, and the uniformity is the evidence that the verse relies on.

That is the same method the sūrah used with the stones. The shrines had custodians and treasuries, and they produced nothing; the star is bright and regular, and it produces nothing either. In both cases the verse asks the audience to consider the object of the veneration and notice what it does not do.

**Sirius in the Arabian Calendar**

The star's importance in Arabia was practical before it was religious, and the practical importance explains the religious development.

Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky, and its heliacal rising — the first appearance of the star before sunrise after a period of invisibility — was used across the ancient world as a marker of the season. In Egypt the same event marked the flooding of the Nile; in Arabia it was associated with the height of summer and with the turning of the year.

A celestial body that announces the seasons acquires a reputation for governing them, and the reputation is the beginning of veneration. The star that tells you when to plant comes to be regarded as the power that decides the harvest, and the transition from observation to worship requires no argument.

The verse answers the transition at its root. The star is a marker governed by a Lord, not a governor, and the naming of its Lord is the correction of the error at the point where it began.

**Lordship as Against Influence**

The verse's claim is specifically about lordship, and the word choice distinguishes it from a mere statement of creation.

God could be described as the creator of the star, which would leave open the question of whether the star exercises any influence of its own. The verse says He is its Lord, and lordship implies ongoing authority: the star is governed, directed, and subject.

That distinction was the crux of the audience's error. They did not deny that God made the heavens; their error lay in assigning to particular heavenly bodies an independent power over human affairs. The verse removes the independence by asserting the lordship.

The same correction applies to the stones named earlier in the sūrah. Their worshippers did not claim they created anything; they claimed they had influence. The answer in both cases is that lordship belongs to God, and nothing governed can govern in turn.

**The Destructions That Follow**

The catalogue turns after this verse from what God governs to what He has done, and the turning is toward two nations destroyed.

The connection is the argument's final movement. The star is beyond human reach and cannot be influenced; the nations were within the world and were removed from it. Both are stated as facts about the same Lord, and both answer the same question about where power lies.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:50
> And He destroyed the first ˹people of˺ ’Ȃd,

**Expanded Commentary**

**ʿĀd al-Ūlā: The Former**

The verse names a destroyed people and adds a qualification: ʿĀd, the first. The qualification implies that there was a second, and the commentators supply the distinction.

ʿĀd the former is identified with Iram, the tribe descended from a grandson of Noah of the same name, while the later ʿĀd was referred to simply as ʿĀd. The distinction matters for reading the Qur'an's references, since the tribe appears in several chapters and the descriptions vary in detail.

Sūrah al-Fajr names the first of them directly: Did you not see how your Lord dealt with 'Ȃd—the people of Iram—with their great stature, unmatched in any other land? (89:6–8). The description of stature and of columns is what distinguishes the earlier tribe in the accounts.

**The Prophet Sent to Them**

The Qur'an's account of ʿĀd is attached to a prophet named Hūd, and the account is given at length in several chapters.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf records the message and the refusal (7:65–72); Sūrah Hūd gives the exchange in fuller form, including the people's challenge and Hūd's answer (11:50–60); Sūrah al-Qamar describes the destruction in detail (54:18–21). Sūrah Fuṣṣilat adds the people's own boast, boasting, Who is superior to us in might? (41:15).

The consistency of the account across chapters indicates a single tradition with a fixed shape: a prophet sent, a boast of strength, a refusal, and a destruction that came through the very element the people trusted.

**The Destruction and Its Means**

The Qur'an describes the destruction of ʿĀd as a wind, and the description is unusually specific for an account of judgment.

Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah describes a furious, bitter wind (69:6) unleashed on them non-stop for seven nights and eight days, so that its people were seen lying dead like trunks of uprooted palm trees (69:7). Sūrah al-Aḥqāf records them saying, This is a cloud bringing us rain, and the reply that it is what they sought to hasten: a ˹fierce˺ wind carrying a painful punishment! (46:24).

The detail serves the argument. The destruction is not described as a vague catastrophe but as an event with a duration, a means, and a mistaken interpretation by the victims, and the specificity is what makes it usable as a warning.

**Why ʿĀd Is Named First**

The order of the two tribes in these verses has been noted, since Thamūd came after ʿĀd chronologically and is named second here.

The explanation lies in the audience's knowledge. ʿĀd was the more famous of the two in Arabian tradition, and the reference to the first of them would have been recognised immediately. The Qur'an's habit of beginning with the more familiar appears here as it did in the naming of Moses before Abraham a few verses earlier.

The ordering also serves the argument's scale. ʿĀd was described as unmatched in the land, and the destruction of the greatest comes first, so that the second destruction appears as a continuation rather than as a lesser case.

**The Warning to an Arabian Audience**

The two tribes were Arabian, and their ruins were known to the audience of this sūrah. The Qur'an appeals to that knowledge directly, telling the Quraysh that they pass by their ruins day and night (37:137–138).

The appeal is what gives the naming its force. These were not distant peoples in foreign lands but neighbours in time and place, whose remains the listeners had seen. The destruction is therefore not a story but a fact about the region they lived in.

That is the point the verse makes in a single clause. The same Lord who governs the star named before it removed a people from this land, and the removal is visible.

**The Boast of Strength and Its Answer**

The Qur'an records the boast of ʿĀd, and the boast is the key to their destruction.

Sūrah Fuṣṣilat reports their question: who is greater than us in strength? (41:15). The people had built greatly, possessed physical power, and regarded themselves as beyond the reach of consequence. Their prophet answered by reminding them that the One who created them was greater than they, and the answer was rejected.

The destruction then came through the element they could not resist. Sūrah al-Ḥāqqah describes the wind that levelled them, and the description of their bodies as hollow palm trunks is a deliberate reversal: the people who boasted of strength are described as empty husks.

The verse here names the destruction without the boast, but the naming recalls it. The audience of this sūrah knew the account, and the knowledge of the boast is what makes the destruction usable as a warning to people who made a similar claim about their own position.

**The Ruins as Evidence**

The Qur'an appeals to the remains of the destroyed peoples as evidence available to its audience, and the appeal is made in the language of travel.

The Quraysh were traders whose caravans passed through the country where these peoples had lived, and the Qur'an tells them that they pass by their ruins day and night (37:137–138). Sūrah al-Ḥijr names them as the residents of the Stone Valley, who were given God's signs and turned away from them (15:80–81).

The appeal converts the accounts from stories into observations. A story can be dismissed as an invention; a ruin passed on a familiar road cannot. The verse's naming of ʿĀd therefore points at something the audience had seen or could see.

That is the strongest form the warning takes. It does not ask for belief in a report but attention to a landscape, and the landscape carries the report in it.

**The Verse's Function in the Catalogue**

The catalogue has moved from what God creates and governs to what He has destroyed, and the movement is the argument's last stage.

Creation establishes power; destruction establishes that the power is exercised. The audience's system assumed a world in which nothing ultimately changes, and in which a person's position could be secured by patronage and offerings. The naming of ʿĀd answers that assumption with a historical fact.

The next verse names the second tribe, and the two together complete the warning.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:51
> and ˹then˺ Thamûd, sparing no one.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Thamūd: The Second Tribe**

The verse names the second of the two destroyed peoples, and the naming follows immediately on the first without a verb of its own. The destruction stated for ʿĀd is carried over to Thamūd, and the addition is the phrase that closes the verse.

Thamūd was an Arabian tribe dwelling in the region of al-Ḥijr, in the rocky country between the Ḥijāz and Syria, and their dwellings were cut into the rock. The Qur'an refers to their carving houses from the mountains as one of their achievements (26:149), and the ruins were known to the traders who passed through.

The tribe is mentioned in many chapters, which indicates that its story was among the most frequently used in the Meccan preaching.

**Mā Aqbā: Sparing No One**

The phrase that ends the verse is a negation of remaining: He did not leave alive, He spared none. The verb is from a root meaning to keep or to leave over, and the negation states that nothing was left.

The completeness is the point of the addition. The verse does not merely say that Thamūd was destroyed; it says that the destruction left no remnant, and the specification answers the assumption that a people might survive in part.

The Qur'an uses similar language elsewhere for total destruction, describing the generation as cut off so that no trace remained. The phrase here performs the same function in two words, and its position at the end of the verse gives it emphasis.

**Ṣāliḥ and the Accounts**

The Qur'an's account of Thamūd is attached to a prophet named Ṣāliḥ, and the account has a fixed shape across the chapters in which it appears.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf records the message and the demand for a sign, and the sign given was a she-camel with a specified share of the water (7:73). Sūrah ash-Shuʿarāʾ gives the exchange at length, including the people's accusation that Ṣāliḥ was bewitched (26:141–158). Sūrah al-Qamar describes the hamstrung camel and the destruction that followed (54:23–31). Sūrah ash-Shams condenses the whole account into a few verses, ending with the statement that their Lord crushed them for their crime, levelling all to the ground, and that He has no fear of consequences (91:14–15).

The consistency across chapters shows that the account was a settled part of the preaching, told repeatedly because its elements were known to the audience.

**The Total Destruction and What It Shows**

The completeness of the destruction is what the verse is asserting, and the assertion has an argumentative purpose.

A partial destruction leaves room for the interpretation that the people were unlucky, or that their precautions failed, or that a different choice would have saved them. A total destruction removes those readings: the whole people went, and the going was not an accident.

The Qur'an makes the same point about the fate of previous generations, stating that not one of them remained and that the destruction was not a random event but a response to a refusal. The verse in Sūrah ash-Shams states the lack of fear about the consequence, which is another way of saying that the act was deliberate and unregretted.

**The Two Tribes as One Argument**

ʿĀd and Thamūd are named together throughout the Qur'an, and the pairing has become a fixed expression for the destroyed generations.

The pairing serves the argument by establishing a pattern. Two peoples, in different times and places, with different prophets and different boasts, met the same end. A pattern of that kind cannot be explained as bad luck, and the Qur'an presents it as evidence of a consistent dealing rather than as two unrelated catastrophes.

The verse's economy is proportionate to the pattern's familiarity. The audience had heard the accounts before, and the naming of the second tribe completes the pattern without needing to restate it.

**The She-Camel and the Sign**

The account of Thamūd turns on a sign, and the sign is what makes their destruction a case of refusal rather than of ignorance.

The people demanded a proof from Ṣāliḥ, and the proof was given in the form of a she-camel, with a specified arrangement for the water: one day for her and one day for them. The Qur'an records the command not to harm her, and it records the harm — they roused a companion of theirs, so he dared to kill her (54:29).

The sign matters for the argument of this verse. A people destroyed after receiving a visible sign cannot be described as having been uninformed, and the destruction that followed the killing was a response to a deliberate act rather than to a misunderstanding.

The verse states the outcome without the story, because the story was known. What it adds is the completeness: no one was spared. The sign was given to all of them, and the consequence reached all of them.

**The Houses Cut in the Rock**

The Qur'an mentions the dwellings of Thamūd as one of their achievements, and the mention is part of the argument.

Sūrah ash-Shuʿarāʾ records their boast that they carved houses from the mountains, and the boast is answered by the destruction that left the houses standing and the people gone (26:149). The dwellings outlasted their builders, and the outlasting is the point.

The detail also makes the ruins identifiable. A people who cut their houses into rock leave remains that endure, and the remains were on the trade route between the Ḥijāz and Syria, where the caravans of the audience's own people passed.

That is why the verse can state the destruction in a clause and move on. The evidence was not in the recitation but in the stone, and the audience had access to it. The completeness of the destruction — sparing no one — is confirmed by the empty houses the listeners could visit.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah**

This verse closes the catalogue that began with laughter and weeping, and the catalogue has covered the whole range of divine action: the inner states of human beings, life and death, the origin of the pair, the re-creation, provision, the governance of a star, and the destruction of two nations.

The range is the argument. Everything named belongs to one Lord, from the smallest drop to the greatest nation, and the sūrah has been establishing that ownership since its opening verses.

What follows turns from the catalogue to the warning that the arrival of the Hour is near, and the catalogue supplies the grounds on which the warning rests.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:52
> And before ˹that He destroyed˺ the people of Noah, who were truly far worse in wrongdoing and transgression.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Case in the List**

The sūrah's list of destroyed peoples now reaches back to its beginning. Two Arabian tribes have been named; the verse goes past them to the people of Noah, who are the first community the Qur'an describes as destroyed for rejecting a messenger.

The ordering is chronological in reverse. The catalogue moved from the nearer to the farther, and this verse reaches the farthest point, which is also the first case on record.

The placement matters. A reader who has just been told about ʿĀd and Thamūd might take those two as local events, particular to Arabia and its history. The verse removes that reading by naming a destruction that preceded both and belonged to neither.

**Aẓlam and Aṭghā: Two Superlatives**

The verse describes Noah's people with two superlative adjectives, and the two are not the same word.

*Aẓlam*, more wrongdoing, comes from the root of ẓulm, which denotes placing a thing where it does not belong, and by extension injustice and wrongdoing. *Aṭghā*, more rebellious, comes from the root of ṭughyān, which denotes exceeding a limit, and it is the word used for Pharaoh and for those who overstep.

The pairing distinguishes two kinds of failure. Wrongdoing concerns what a person does to others and to what is owed; rebellion concerns the refusal of a limit. Noah's people are described as extreme in both, and the combination is what makes them the standard case in the Qur'an's account of destroyed nations.

**Why They Were the Worst**

The commentators give two reasons for the superlatives, and the two correspond to the two words.

They were the most wrongdoing because they established practices that others then imitated, particularly the worship of idols, so that their offence continued in the conduct of those who came after them. They were the most rebellious because the great majority of them refused the message, and refused it over a period the Qur'an describes as nine hundred and fifty years.

Both reasons concern scale rather than intensity. A single generation's sin ends with that generation; a practice established and inherited does not. The verse's superlatives measure the reach of the offence as well as its gravity, and on that measure the people of Noah stand first.

**The Accounts Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The story is told at length in several chapters, and the accounts agree on its essentials.

Sūrah Hūd gives the fullest version, beginning with Noah's statement that he has been sent with a clear warning, that they should worship none but Allah, and that he fears for them the torment of a painful day (11:25–26). Sūrah al-Muʾminūn records the people's rejection on the grounds that he was only a man like them (23:24). Sūrah ash-Shuʿarāʾ opens with the statement that the people of Noah rejected the messengers (26:105). Sūrah Nūḥ is given entirely to his preaching and their refusal.

The repetition across chapters indicates that the account served a fixed function: it was the first and clearest instance of the pattern the Qur'an sets out, in which a messenger is sent, refused, and answered by a destruction that vindicates him.

**The Argument From Precedent**

The verse's function in the sūrah is to establish that the pattern is not new, and the establishment is an argument.

An audience facing a warning can respond that the warning has no precedent, that things have always continued as they are, and that no generation has ever been cut off for what it believed. The verse removes that response by naming the oldest case, and the case is not presented as a rumour but as a matter the audience's own traditions acknowledged.

The Qur'an's use of destroyed peoples follows this logic throughout. It is not telling stories for their own sake; it is supplying precedents, and the precedents are cumulative. Three destroyed peoples in a single passage are harder to dismiss as coincidence than one.

**What the Verse Adds to the Two Tribes**

The two tribes named before this one were Arabian, and their ruins were on the audience's trade routes. The people of Noah were neither, and the difference strengthens the argument rather than weakening it.

A local destruction can be explained locally: a drought, a war, a change of climate. A destruction that reaches back beyond the tribes, to the earliest generation the audience knew of, cannot be explained that way. The verse extends the pattern across time as well as across place.

It also raises the stakes. If the earliest community to reject a messenger was destroyed, then the audience's own rejection is not an exception to a rule but an instance of it, and the instance is the one they are living.

**The Duration and Its Significance**

The Qur'an states the length of Noah's preaching among his people, and the figure is unusual in the revelation: he remained among them a thousand years less fifty (29:14).

The figure serves the argument of this verse. A refusal sustained over that period is not a misunderstanding that could have been cleared up with better explanation, and a messenger who persisted over that period is not one whose message could be dismissed as unclear.

The duration also answers an objection the audience might make — that they had not been given time. The first community was given the longest period recorded anywhere in the Qur'an, and the outcome was the destruction this verse names.

**The Verse's Place in the Sequence**

Three peoples have now been named, and the naming closes the historical section of the sūrah. What follows turns from what God did to what the audience should make of it.

The turn is prepared by the ordering. The nearest case comes first, the farthest last, and the reader is left with the whole range: from the tribes whose houses he could visit to the generation whose story began the record.

The next verse adds the second destruction in the sequence, and the two together complete the list before the sūrah puts its question.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:53
> And ˹it was˺ He ˹Who˺ turned the cities ˹of Sodom and Gomorrah˺ upside down.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Muʾtafikāt: The Overturned Cities**

The verse names its subject with a participle used as a name: *al-muʾtafikāt*, the overturned ones, the cities that were turned over. The word is formed from a root meaning to invert or to overturn, and in the Qur'an it functions as a designation rather than a description.

The designation is unusual. The cities are not named, and they are not described by location or by their people; they are named by what happened to them. The event became their identity, and the Qur'an uses it as their title.

That naming has an effect on the reader. A city is normally identified by its inhabitants, its position, or its history; this one is identified by its destruction, which tells the reader what the Qur'an considers the significant fact about it.

**The Cities of the People of Lot**

Most commentators identify the cities with those of the people of Lot, and the identification is supported by the Qur'an's own account.

Sūrah Hūd describes the event: when Our command came, We turned the cities upside down and rained down on them clustered stones of baked clay, marked by your Lord (11:82–83). Sūrah al-Ḥijr gives the same account in shorter form (15:74). The cities are associated with Lot's people throughout, and the destruction follows their refusal and their conduct.

The verse here does not name Lot or his people. It states the overturning, and leaves the identification to the accounts the audience already knew — which is consistent with the sūrah's compressed treatment of the whole list.

**The Act Attributed to God**

The verse's grammar puts God as the subject of the overturning. He turned the cities over; the act is His, and the verse does not describe it through intermediate causes.

That attribution is a feature of the Qur'an's descriptions of the destroyed peoples throughout. The wind that destroyed ʿĀd, the blast that took Thamūd, the stones that fell on the cities of Lot — each is presented as an act rather than a catastrophe, and the difference matters for what the events mean.

A catastrophe is an event in the world; an act is a statement. The verse's attribution makes the destruction a statement about the conduct that preceded it, and that is how the sūrah uses it in the argument that follows.

**The Inversion as a Sign**

The specific form of the destruction — the turning over of cities — is not incidental, and the Qur'an draws attention to it elsewhere.

The inversion reverses the most basic fact about a settlement: that it stands on the ground and its roofs are above. A city turned upside down is a settlement unmade, and the unmaking is total in a way that burning or flooding would not be.

Sūrah al-Ḥijr adds the detail of the stones of baked clay rained down afterwards, so that the account combines inversion from below and bombardment from above. The two together describe a destruction with no surviving structure and no surviving population, which is why the verse that follows can speak of what covered them without qualification.

**A Second Reading of the Cities**

Some commentators read the verse more widely, taking the overturned cities as a reference to all settlements destroyed by upheavals rather than to the cities of Lot alone.

The reading is possible grammatically, since the participle is plural and general, and it has the merit of extending the warning beyond a single historical case.

The narrower reading is better supported by the context. The list in the surrounding verses names specific peoples — ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Noah — and the cities are most naturally read as the case that belongs with them, namely the people of Lot. The general reading is not excluded by the wording, but it is not required by it.

**What the Verse Omits**

The verse does not describe the people, their conduct, or the reason for their destruction, and the omission is deliberate.

The Qur'an gives the account of Lot's people in detail elsewhere, including their refusal of his message and the conduct for which they are condemned. Here none of that appears, because the sūrah is not retelling the story; it is citing the outcome.

The economy has a purpose. The audience knew the account, and a repetition would have slowed the list. What the list needs is the fact of the destruction, and the verse gives it in a single clause with the agent named.

**The Stones Rained Down Afterwards**

The overturning was not the whole of the event, and the parallel accounts add a second element that this verse leaves out.

Sūrah Hūd describes clustered stones of baked clay rained down after the cities were turned over, and adds that they were marked by your Lord (11:82–83). Sūrah al-Ḥijr gives the same two elements in a single verse (15:74).

The addition of the marking is significant. Stones described as marked are not debris from a natural upheaval; they are presented as designated, and the designation is attributed to God. The detail answers the reading that would take the destruction as a geological event that happened to occur at the right moment.

The verse here does not repeat the detail, because the list it belongs to is compressed and the detail is available in the chapters that tell the story. What the verse supplies is the agent and the act, and the act is the overturning.

**Overturning in the Qur'an's Language of Judgment**

The verb of overturning appears in the Qur'an's descriptions of judgment as well as in its historical accounts, and the connection is worth noting.

The Qur'an describes the day of judgment as the day when what is hidden is brought out and the contents of the hearts are made known, and it uses the vocabulary of overturning and turning over for the reversal of states: those who were low become high, those who laughed are laughed at, those who were certain are shown to have been wrong.

The cities are the physical instance of that reversal. A settlement is the most stable thing human beings make, and its inversion is the clearest possible picture of a state reversed.

That is why the verse's single verb carries so much weight in the argument. It names an event that is at once historical and symbolic, and the audience is meant to see both in it.

**The Verse in the Sequence**

Three destructions have now been named in the space of three verses: two tribes and a set of cities, with the people of Noah preceding them. The list is complete, and the completeness is the point.

The sūrah has assembled its precedents, and the precedents cover the range of cases an audience might raise. Local peoples and distant ones, ancient and recent, destroyed by wind, by upheaval, and by flood.

The next verse describes what covered the cities, and the description is the most compressed in the passage — and deliberately so.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:54
> How overwhelming was what covered ˹them˺!

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Construction Used Twice in One Sūrah**

The verse uses a construction that appeared much earlier in this sūrah, and the repetition is one of the most striking features of the chapter.

In the account of the vision, the Lote-Tree was described as covered by that which covered it, with the covering left unnamed. Here the punishment of the cities is described in the same form: there covered them that which covered.

The identical construction applied to two opposite things — the splendour at the boundary of creation and the destruction of a condemned people — is not accidental. It marks both events as beyond description, and the inability to describe them is the same inability in both cases, though the reason differs.

**What Covered Them**

The Qur'an supplies elsewhere what this verse withholds, and the supply is specific.

Sūrah Hūd describes the stones of baked clay, clustered, rained down on the cities after they were overturned, and adds that they were marked by your Lord (11:82–83). Sūrah al-Ḥijr gives the same two elements: the inversion and the stones of baked clay (15:74).

So the covering is not a vague catastrophe. It is described in the parallel accounts with a material, a form, and a source. The verse here declines to repeat the description, not because it is unknown but because the description is available elsewhere and its repetition would serve no purpose in a list.

**The Exclamatory Form**

The verse is an exclamation rather than a statement, and the form changes what it does.

A statement reports; an exclamation registers an effect on the speaker. The verse does not say that a great thing covered them; it says how overwhelming the thing was, and the emphasis falls on the magnitude rather than on the event.

The Qur'an uses exclamations of this kind sparingly, and they mark points where the subject exceeds ordinary description. The form tells the reader that the event is not being recounted for information but presented for effect, and the effect intended is awe at the scale of what happened. A reader who registers the magnitude has taken the first step the warning requires, and the exclamation exists to produce that registration.

**Wonder and Terror in the Same Words**

The two uses of the construction produce opposite reactions, and the contrast is worth setting out.

The covering of the Lote-Tree produced an account that withheld its content out of reverence: something was there that language would diminish. The covering of the cities produced an account that withheld its content out of horror: something happened whose scale the language of the verse could not contain.

The commentators note the parallel explicitly, observing that the phrase conveys indescribable wonder in the earlier verses and indescribable terror here. The same words carry both, and the carrying is possible because in both cases the speaker is describing something he did not witness and cannot fully convey.

**Why the Description Is Withheld**

The withholding has an argumentative function that is easy to miss.

A detailed description of the punishment would invite the audience to compare it with punishments they knew and to judge whether it was proportionate. The verse removes that possibility by refusing the comparison: what covered them is presented as beyond measurement, and the only available response is the one the verse models.

That response is not despair but attention. The exclamation asks the reader to register the magnitude, and the magnitude is what makes the question in the following verse possible.

**The Verse as the End of the List**

Three destructions have been named, and this verse closes them with the most compressed statement of the three. The compression is the conclusion.

A list of precedents needs an ending that gathers them, and the verse gathers them by describing their common feature: in each case something overwhelming came, and in each case it came from God. The specific means differ; the overwhelming does not.

The verse therefore completes the historical section, and the sūrah turns from the past to the audience standing in front of it.

**The Exclamation and Its Audience**

The exclamation is addressed to someone who did not witness the event, and the address is part of its function.

No survivor of the cities could have made this statement, and no eyewitness is invoked to support it. The verse speaks in the voice of one who has been told, and the telling carries the weight that eyewitness would otherwise carry.

That is the position of the audience as well. They had not seen the destruction; they had heard of it, and the hearsay was old and widespread enough to be treated as fact. The verse adopts the same stance and asks them to join it: not to verify the event, but to register its magnitude.

The move is subtle and effective. A demand for verification would have produced argument; an exclamation invites agreement, and the agreement is the first step in accepting the warning that follows.

**Covering as the Qur'an's Word for Punishment**

The verb of covering is used elsewhere in the Qur'an for what overtakes a people, and the usage gives the word here its force.

Punishment in the Qur'an is frequently described as something that comes over a people from above or around them — a cloud, a blast, a wind, a covering — and the descriptions emphasise the inability to escape it. Sūrah al-ʿAnkabūt lists the forms the seizure took: against some a storm of stones, against some a mighty blast, some swallowed by the earth, and some drowned, with the closing statement that Allah would not have wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves (29:40). Sūrah al-Aḥqāf records the people of ʿĀd taking the approaching punishment for a rain cloud (46:24).

The covering is therefore not merely a description of scale but of inescapability. What covers a person cannot be stepped aside from, and the verse's choice of word makes that the point.

The audience's own case is the application. The warning has been delivered, and a warning that is not answered becomes, in the Qur'an's language, something that covers.

**The Turn to the Question**

The verse is followed by a question addressed to the audience, and the question depends on what has just been said.

The list of destructions is not an end in itself. It establishes that favours were given, warnings were sent, and refusals were answered, and the question that follows asks which of those favours the audience intends to dispute.

The turn is the sūrah's standard move: assemble the facts, then ask the question the facts make unavoidable.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:55
> Now, which of your Lord’s favours will you dispute?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Question and the Refrain**

The verse is a question, and the question has an echo that its first hearers would have recognised.

Sūrah ar-Raḥmān is built on a refrain that asks which of the favours of the Lord will be denied, and the refrain occurs thirty-one times in that chapter. The form is the same: a question about favours, expecting no answer, because no answer is possible.

The verse here is shorter and uses a different verb, but the shape is identical. A favour is named or implied, and the hearer is asked to dispute it. The impossibility of the answer is what makes the question an argument rather than a request.

**The Verb and Its Echo Earlier in the Sūrah**

The verb used here comes from the same root as the one in the sūrah's twelfth verse, where the audience was asked how they could dispute with the Prophet regarding what he saw.

The echo is significant. The earlier dispute concerned a report they could not verify; this one concerns favours they cannot deny. The sūrah has moved the ground from the contestable to the undeniable, and it uses the same word for both to make the movement visible.

A person who disputes a vision may have some ground, however weak. A person who disputes a favour has none, because the favour is in his possession while he disputes it.

**The Favours in Question**

The verse does not list the favours, and the omission is filled by the sūrah itself.

The chapter has described the creation of the human being from a drop, the giving of life and death, the provision of wealth and its withdrawal, the governance of the heavens, and the sending of messengers with warnings. Each of these is a favour in the Qur'an's usage, and each has been named in the preceding verses.

The question therefore refers to what the audience has just heard. It is not a general enquiry about blessings but a specific one about the blessings the sūrah has enumerated, and the audience cannot claim that the list was hidden from them.

**The Warning Inside the Question**

The verse follows a list of three destroyed peoples, and the placement gives the question an edge.

The peoples named were given the same favours: creation, provision, messengers, warnings. They disputed them, and the disputes ended in the destructions just described. The question asks the audience which of the favours they intend to dispute, and the context supplies the consequence of disputing.

That is why the verse is not a soft appeal. It is a question with a known answer attached, and the audience is being asked to identify itself with one of the two groups — those who received and accepted, or those who received and disputed.

**The Refrain Elsewhere in the Qur'an**

The form of the question belongs to a wider pattern in which the Qur'an lists what God has given and then asks what remains to be denied.

Sūrah ar-Raḥmān runs through the creation of the heavens, the balance, the fruits, the ships, and the two gardens, and after each item asks the refrain. The method is accumulation: the favours are piled up until denial becomes impossible, and the question is repeated until the hearer stops trying to answer it.

The verse here uses the same method in a single line. The favours have been accumulated in the preceding verses, and the question is asked once, at the point where the accumulation is complete.

**The Verse's Function in the Sūrah**

This verse closes the section that began with the destruction of ʿĀd and opens the section that ends the chapter.

The closing function is clear: the historical precedents have been assembled and the question they support has been asked. The opening function is also clear, because what follows concerns the warning itself — the warner, the nearness of the Hour, and the audience's reaction to the recitation.

The verse therefore stands at a hinge, and it is short because a hinge does not need to be long. The precedents behind it and the warning ahead of it do the work.

**Favours the Audience Could Not Disown**

The question asks which favours will be disputed, and the audience's difficulty is that the favours are in their possession while they dispute them.

They existed, which the sūrah has traced to an emitted drop. They were fed and provisioned, which the sūrah has assigned to the One who enriches and impoverishes. They were spared the destructions that overtook three peoples around them, and they lived in a city that had not been overturned.

They had also been sent a messenger from among themselves, speaking their language, whose history they knew. That is the favour the question presses hardest, because it is the one they were disputing at the time.

The list is not abstract. Every item on it could be checked by the audience in the course of an ordinary day, and the checking is what the question asks for.

**The Question and the Two Groups**

The verse's context divides people into two groups, and the question asks the audience to place itself.

The destroyed peoples received favours and disputed them; the outcome is described in the three verses before this one. The alternative is not described here, because the sūrah has described it earlier — the reward of those who strove, the fullest repayment, the Garden of refuge at the boundary of creation.

The question therefore functions as a choice presented in the form of an enquiry. Disputing the favours places a person in the first group; acknowledging them places him in the second. No third position is offered, and the absence of a third is the verse's pressure.

That is why the question is not softened. It is the last point at which the audience can identify its own position before the sūrah turns to the warning itself.

**Why No Answer Is Expected**

The question is rhetorical in a specific sense: any answer would concede the point.

To name a favour and dispute it, the audience would have to claim either that the favour was not given or that it came from another source. The first claim is contradicted by the audience's own existence and provision; the second is contradicted by everything the sūrah has just said about who gives life, death, and wealth.

The silence the question produces is the sūrah's aim. Where an argument ends in the opponent's silence, it has achieved more than a refutation would, because the silence cannot be answered either.

---
## Sūrah an-Najm 53:56
> This ˹Prophet˺ is a warner like earlier ones.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Readings of a Short Verse**

The verse is short enough that its grammar has been read in two ways, and the two readings differ in what the word refers to.

On the first reading, the subject is the Qur'an itself: this is a warning of the same kind as the earlier warnings, a message belonging to the continuous stream of revelation. On the second, the subject is the Prophet: this is a warner from among the warners of old, a man like the men sent to previous communities.

The readings differ in reference but agree in substance. Both place what the audience is hearing inside a series, and both deny that it is unprecedented. The commentators hold both without choosing, because the verse's function is the same either way.

**The Series of Warners**

The word used for the earlier ones is the plural of warner, and the series is one the Qur'an describes repeatedly.

Every community is said to have had a warner, and the warners are named across the chapters: Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Lot, Shuʿayb, Moses. The verse in Sūrah Fāṭir states the pattern directly — there is no community to which a warner has not come (35:24) — and the verses in this sūrah name three of the communities whose warners were refused.

Placing the present message in that series does two things. It removes the claim of novelty, and it supplies the audience with the record of what happened to previous communities that made the same response.

**What the Continuity Implies**

The continuity has a consequence that the audience could not avoid. If this is one of the warnings, then it carries the authority of the warnings before it, and the audience's treatment of it will be judged by the same standard.

The communities named in the preceding verses did not reject their warners because the warnings were unclear. They rejected them because they did not want the message, and the reasons they gave were reasons about the messenger — that he was a man like them, that he sought advantage, that his followers were the lowly.

The audience of this sūrah was making the same objections. The verse places them in the series at the point where the objection is made, and the series tells them what came next.

**The Verse After the Destructions**

The placement is deliberate. Three peoples have just been named as destroyed, and the verse that follows identifies the present message with the messages they received.

The connection is the argument. The destructions are not presented as arbitrary events but as the outcomes of refused warnings, and the warning now being delivered is of the same kind. The audience is being told where it stands in a sequence whose earlier entries have known endings.

That is a stronger claim than a direct threat would have been. A threat can be dismissed as rhetoric; a pattern with three completed instances cannot.

**The Function of the Warning**

The Qur'an describes the function of a warner with some precision, and the precision matters here.

A warner is not a predictor of specific events and not a guarantor of outcomes. He delivers a message about what follows from conduct, and the delivery is his whole task. The verse in Sūrah al-Kahf makes the limit explicit: I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God (18:110).

The verse here places the Prophet in that role and no other. He is one of the warners, and the warners are messengers rather than miracle-workers, kings, or judges. The role is what the audience objected to, and the verse affirms it rather than answering the objection.

**Why the Verse Does Not Argue**

No evidence is offered for the claim that this is one of the earlier warnings, and none is needed.

The evidence is the message itself. Its content — one God, a reckoning, a resurrection, a moral order — is the content of the earlier warnings, and the audience knew that content from its contact with the scripture-bearing communities around it. The sūrah has already appealed to the scrolls of Moses and Abraham for the same reason.

The verse states the identity and moves on, on the assumption that the identity is evident to anyone who has heard both.

**The Warners and Their Fates**

The verse places the present messenger in a series, and the series has a record that the audience knew.

Noah warned his people and was refused, and the refusal ended in the flood. Hūd warned ʿĀd and was refused, and the refusal ended in the wind. Ṣāliḥ warned Thamūd and was refused, and the refusal ended in the blast. Lot warned the people of the cities and was refused, and the refusal ended in the overturning.

Four warners, four refusals, four outcomes, and the outcomes are the ones the sūrah has just listed. The verse's identification of the present messenger with the warners of old is therefore not a claim about his character but a statement about his position, and the position has known consequences.

The audience's response to the identification was to reject it, and the rejection is what the following verses describe.

**Why the Verse Calls Him a Warner**

The title is specific, and the sūrah has already established what the Prophet is not.

He is not described here as a king, a soothsayer, a poet, or a magician, each of which the audience had suggested. He is described as a warner, which is a role with a defined content: he delivers a message about the consequences of conduct and does not compel belief.

The choice of title answers the audience's objections by refusing their terms. They wanted a messenger with power, or none at all; the verse offers a messenger with a message, and places him in a line of men who had nothing else either.

That is the sūrah's consistent presentation. It does not claim more for the Prophet than the role requires, and the modesty of the claim is what makes it defensible.

**The Turn to the Hour**

The verse closes the section on the message and its place in the series, and the sūrah now turns to the thing the message is about: the approaching event that the warning announces.

The turn is natural, because a warning without an object is empty. The warners warned of something, and the verse that follows names how near that something has come.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:57
> The approaching ˹Hour˺ has drawn near.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Al-Āzifah: The Approaching**

The verse names the Hour with a participle: *al-āzifah*, the approaching one, that which draws close. The word is used as a title, and the title is the verse's first claim.

The naming is significant. The Hour is not described as appointed, fixed, or coming at a distant time; it is named for its nearness. The quality chosen to identify it is the one most likely to be doubted, and the verse puts it in the name itself.

The same naming appears in Sūrah Ghāfir, where the warning of the approaching Day is announced and the Day is described as one when hearts will be at the throats (40:18). The participle functions there as it does here: the event is defined by its approach.

**The Hour Has Drawn Near**

The Qur'an states the nearness of the Hour in several places, and the statements are consistent.

Sūrah al-Qamar opens with the statement that the Hour has drawn near and the moon was split (54:1). Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ states that ˹The time of˺ people's judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away (21:1).

The consistency across chapters revealed at different times indicates that nearness was a standing claim of the Meccan preaching rather than a response to a particular situation. It was said to an audience that had heard it before, and its repetition was part of its force.

**Nearness in Divine Time**

The claim of nearness raises a question that the Qur'an's usage answers: near relative to what?

The revelation measures time differently from a human life. A thousand years in the sight of the Lord is like a day, and the commentators note that what is near in divine reckoning may be far in human terms. The nearness is therefore not a prediction about years but a statement about proportion.

That reading does not weaken the claim; it sharpens it. A person's life is short in any reckoning, and the Hour is near relative to the span in which a person must decide. The verse is addressed to individuals whose time is limited, and for them the nearness is exact.

**What the Nearness Does**

The effect of the claim is to remove the option of postponement.

A person who believes that a reckoning lies far ahead can defer his response indefinitely, and the deferral becomes a habit. The verse removes the ground of the habit by stating that the event is near, and the nearness converts the response from a future possibility into a present obligation.

The Qur'an states the difference in perception plainly: They truly see this ˹Day˺ as impossible, but We see it as inevitable (70:6–7). The difference is not information but attention, and the verse here is an appeal to attention.

**The Link to the Destructions**

The verse follows a list of three destroyed peoples, and the connection between them supplies the argument.

The peoples named did not expect their destruction. The people of ʿĀd took the approaching wind for a rain cloud; the cities were overturned while their inhabitants were in them. In each case the event arrived at a moment when it was not expected, and its arrival was not postponed by the expectation.

That is what the nearness means in practice. The verse is not telling the audience when the Hour will come; it is telling them that its coming does not wait for their readiness, and the precedents show what happens to people who count on the delay.

**The Statement Without a Date**

The verse asserts nearness and gives no date, and the absence is consistent throughout the revelation.

The Qur'an repeatedly refuses to specify the time of the Hour, and the refusal is presented as a feature of the arrangement rather than a gap in the information. The audience asked about the time, and the answer given was that the knowledge belongs to God alone.

The combination — near, and undated — is what makes the claim useful. A dated event allows preparation to be scheduled; an undated near event requires constant readiness, and constant readiness is what the warning is meant to produce.

**The Hour in Meccan Preaching**

The nearness of the Hour was a central claim of the Meccan preaching, and its prominence explains both the audience's objection and the revelation's insistence.

The Meccan chapters return to the Hour constantly: its nearness, its suddenness, the scenes of its arrival, the division of people on that day. The claim was not incidental to the message but one of its two main contents, the other being the oneness of God.

The audience's denial was directed at both. They rejected the oneness because it abolished their deities, and they rejected the Hour because it abolished the assumption that this life is the whole of the matter. The verse here addresses the second rejection in the shortest possible form.

The insistence across so many chapters indicates that the claim was contested throughout the Meccan period, and that the revelation did not soften it in response.

**Suddenness and the Precedents**

The Qur'an describes the Hour as coming suddenly, and the description is supported by the precedents this sūrah has just named.

The people of ʿĀd saw the wind approaching and took it for rain; the cities were overturned without warning reaching their inhabitants in time; the flood came after a period in which nothing indicated it. In each case the event arrived at a moment when it was not expected, and the expectation of delay was what destroyed them.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf states the pattern: it will only come upon you suddenly (7:187). The suddenness is not an additional feature of the Hour but the way it arrives, and the precedents show that the way it arrives is not altered by human expectation.

That is the practical content of the nearness. A person cannot prepare for a sudden event at the last moment; he can only be prepared in advance, and the advance is the interval the verse tells him is short.

**The Verse's Function in the Sequence**

This verse and the one after it form a pair: the nearness is stated, and the exclusive knowledge of it is stated.

The pairing answers two objections at once. The audience cannot say the event is distant, and it cannot claim to know when it will come. Both escape routes are closed in two verses, and the closing is what prepares the turn to the audience's reaction.

The next verse states who alone can disclose the event, and the statement is as short as this one.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:58
> None but Allah can disclose it.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Lā Yujallīhā: The Unveiling**

The verse uses a verb of disclosure: *yujallīhā*, he unveils it, he brings it into the open. The word comes from a root meaning to reveal or to make manifest, and it is used for the removal of a covering.

Applied to the Hour, the verb describes both the knowledge of it and its occurrence. To unveil an event can mean to disclose when it will happen, and it can mean to bring it about, since an event that has not occurred is covered and an event that has occurred is revealed.

The commentators hold both senses, and the verse's wording supports both. The exclusive claim — none but God — covers the knowledge and the act together, and the covering of both in a single verb is what makes the statement so compact.

**The Two Senses Together**

The first sense is epistemic: no one other than God knows when the Hour will come. The second is causal: no one other than God will bring it about.

The two are connected. An event whose timing is known only to its agent cannot be anticipated by anyone else, and the ignorance is not a temporary lack of information but a permanent feature of the arrangement.

That is why the verse follows the statement of nearness. The Hour is near, and no creature can calculate the nearness, so the only rational response is readiness rather than estimation. The two verses together close the question completely.

**The Parallel Verse in al-Aʿrāf**

The Qur'an states the same position at length in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, in answer to a question the audience was asking.

They ask you, O Prophet, regarding the Hour, when will it be? Say, that knowledge is only with my Lord. He alone will reveal it when the time comes. It is too tremendous for the heavens and the earth and will only take you by surprise (7:187).

The verse there supplies what this one leaves out: the audience's question, the instruction to answer it, and the description of the event as sudden. The verse here gives the conclusion in six words, on the assumption that the fuller statement was already known.

**What the Concealment Achieves**

The concealment of the Hour's time is presented in the Qur'an as purposeful, and the purpose is worth setting out.

If the time were known, the response to the warning could be scheduled. A person who knows the date of an examination can defer preparation until the week before; a person who does not know must prepare continuously. The concealment produces the continuous readiness that the warning is meant to produce.

The concealment also protects the seriousness of the interval. A life lived in the certain expectation of a distant date becomes a countdown, and the counting replaces the living. The arrangement leaves a person with an undated nearness, which is the condition under which conduct rather than calculation determines the outcome.

**The Verse and the Audience's Claims**

The exclusive disclosure answers a claim the audience was making in conduct if not in words: that there was time, and that the time was theirs to use.

The sūrah has already examined claims of this kind. The man who stopped giving acted as though he knew that his past would suffice; the people who assigned genders to the angels spoke as though they had seen them. In each case the claim was to a knowledge nobody had been given.

This verse closes the last of those claims. The one thing that would allow the audience to postpone — knowledge of when — belongs to God alone, and the verse says so without elaboration.

**The Verse's Brevity**

Six words, and the subject is closed. The brevity matches the certainty: there is nothing to argue about, because the claim is not disputed by anyone who accepts that God is God.

The audience did not deny that God knows the unseen; their error lay elsewhere. The verse therefore states a premise they accepted and draws the consequence the premise entails, which is that no calculation of the Hour is available to them.

That is the sūrah's method with shared premises throughout. It states them briefly and uses them, reserving its length for the points that are actually contested.

**The Audience's Question About the Hour**

The Qur'an records that the audience asked about the Hour, and the asking is informative about the state of the discussion.

They ask you regarding the Hour, when will it be? (7:187). The question was not asked in order to prepare; it was asked in order to test. A person who wants a date is usually looking for a way to defer, and the deferral is what the question was meant to produce.

The answer given refused the date and stated the reason: the knowledge belongs to God alone. The refusal is not evasive but exact, because a date would have changed the nature of the warning from a call to readiness into a countdown.

The verse here closes the same question in six words. Nothing is added to the earlier answer, because nothing needed to be.

**Disclosure and Bringing About**

The two senses of the verb in this verse are worth separating, because each excludes a different claim.

The epistemic sense excludes the soothsayer. Arabian society had practitioners who claimed knowledge of hidden matters, and their claims were part of the religious economy the sūrah examines. The verse states that the Hour is not among the things any of them can disclose.

The causal sense excludes the intercessor. A being who could bring the Hour about, or delay it, would hold a power over the arrangement of the worlds, and the sūrah has already established that both worlds belong to God and that nothing happens in them without His permission.

The two exclusions cover the two ways the audience might have expected the Hour to be managed — by divination or by intercession — and both are closed in a single clause.

**From the Hour to the Recitation**

The pair of verses about the Hour is followed by a question about the audience's reaction to this discourse, and the turn is from the event to the response.

The connection is that the warning concerns the event, and the audience's laughter is a response to the warning. The sūrah moves from what is coming to how it is being received, and the receiving is what the closing verses examine.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:59
> Do you find this revelation astonishing,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Marvel at the Discourse**

The verse asks a question about a reaction, and the reaction is astonishment. *Afa-min hādhā al-ḥadīthi taʿjabūn* — do you marvel at this discourse?

The word for discourse, *ḥadīth*, denotes speech, a report, a thing spoken. Applied here it means the revelation being recited, and the word choice is significant: the audience's astonishment is directed at the fact that a speech exists at all.

The question does not deny that the discourse is astonishing. A message containing what this one contains — an account of a vision at the boundary of creation, a reckoning, a resurrection, an undated nearness — is astonishing. The question asks whether astonishment is the right response, and the following verses answer that it is not.

**The Parallel Questions in the Qur'an**

The same question is put in several chapters, and the parallels show what the astonishment consisted of.

Is it astonishing to people that We have sent revelation to a man from among themselves? (10:2). The deniers are astonished that a warner has come to them from among themselves, and the disbelievers say, this is an astonishing thing! (50:2). The pagans are astonished that a warner has come to them from among themselves (38:4).

The object of the astonishment is consistent in all of them: not the content of the message but the humanity of the messenger. A revelation was not astonishing; a revelation delivered by a man they knew was.

**Why Marvel Is a Refusal**

The verse treats the astonishment as an answer rather than a question, and the treatment is precise.

A person who is astonished has not yet judged; he has stopped. The stopping is the refusal, because it substitutes surprise for examination. The audience did not argue against the message; it expressed wonder at it, and the wonder functioned as a way of not engaging.

That is why the question is followed by a description of laughter. Astonishment that ends in laughter is not wonder at the truth of something but a way of dismissing it, and the verse's sequence — marvel, laugh, do not weep — traces the movement from surprise to mockery.

**The Messenger From Among Themselves**

The objection behind the astonishment is stated in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf, where the people are asked whether they find it astonishing that a reminder should come to them from their Lord through one of their own (7:63).

The question implies the answer: a messenger from among the audience is what makes the message credible rather than what makes it doubtful. He speaks their language, they know his history, and they can assess his character from decades of acquaintance. The sūrah made the same appeal at its beginning, when it called him your companion.

The audience inverted the argument. Familiarity, which should have been evidence, became the ground of objection — a man we know cannot be a messenger. The verse's question exposes the inversion by asking whether the familiarity is really what astonishes them.

**The Link to the Sūrah's Opening**

The charge that opens this chapter was that the Prophet had gone astray or spoken from his own whim, and the sūrah answered it in its first verses.

The verse here returns to the same charge in a different form. The audience's astonishment at the discourse is the emotional version of the claim that it was invented: a speech so unusual must have a human source. The question asks whether that inference is sound, and the sūrah has already answered it.

The return at the end of the chapter is deliberate. The argument began with the claim and ends with the reaction to it, and the two are the same problem seen from opposite sides.

**What the Verse Assumes About the Discourse**

The question assumes that the discourse is what it claims to be, and the assumption is not argued here because it has been argued throughout.

The sūrah has established that the Prophet does not speak from whim, that his account of the vision is testimony from a witness who did not falter, that the moral order it describes is recorded in earlier scriptures, and that the destructions it cites are matters of record.

By the time the closing question is asked, the case has been assembled. The verse can therefore treat the discourse as established and examine the reaction to it, which is the only part of the matter still open.

**The Word Ḥadīth and What It Claims**

The word used for the discourse carries a claim inside it, and the claim is part of what the audience found astonishing.

*Ḥadīth* denotes a thing spoken, a report, a communication. It is not the word for a book or a text but for speech, and speech implies a speaker. The audience's astonishment at this discourse was therefore astonishment at the claim that someone had spoken it.

The Qur'an uses the same word for itself repeatedly, and the usage distinguishes it from other kinds of speech: this is not a human report but a communication with a divine source. Sūrah aṭ-Ṭūr describes the same refusal in another form: even if they were to see a deadly piece of the sky fall down upon them, they would still say it was a pile of clouds (52:44). The discourse has been delivered and heard, and the hearing has not produced acknowledgement.

The astonishment the verse asks about is thus an astonishment at the source, and the source is what the sūrah spent its opening verses establishing.

**Astonishment in the Qur'an's Other Uses**

The Qur'an uses astonishment in two directions, and the distinction matters for reading this verse.

There is the astonishment of wonder at a sign, which the revelation approves: the descriptions of the heavens, the alternation of night and day, and the origin of the human being are all presented as matters for wonder. That wonder leads to reflection and ends in acknowledgement.

There is also the astonishment of refusal, which the revelation rejects, and it is directed not at the evidence but at the fact that the evidence exists. The deniers are astonished that a warner has come to them from among themselves (50:2), which is astonishment at the arrangement rather than at the content.

The verse here asks which kind the audience's astonishment is, and the following verses answer it by describing the laughter that follows. Wonder does not turn into mockery; refusal does.

**The Reaction That Follows**

The verse is incomplete on its own, and the incompleteness is deliberate. It asks whether the audience marvels, and the next two verses describe what the marvel turns into.

The structure makes the reaction visible in stages: astonishment, then laughter, then persistence in heedlessness. Each stage is a further removal from the message, and the sūrah describes them in order rather than summarising them.

The final verse answers all three with a command, and the command is the sūrah's last word.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:60
> laughing ˹at it˺ and not weeping ˹in awe˺,

**Expanded Commentary**

**Laughter as Mockery**

The verse names the reaction that follows the astonishment, and the laughter it describes is not amusement.

In the Qur'an's usage, laughter directed at a message is mockery. Sūrah al-Muʾminūn records the address to those in the Fire: you were ˹so busy˺ making fun of them that it made you forget My remembrance. And you used to laugh at them (23:110). The laughter there is a settled practice, not a passing reaction, and it produced forgetfulness.

The verse here describes the same practice. The audience laughed at the recitation, and the laughter was how they dealt with it — a way of ending the encounter without answering the content.

**Weeping as the Fitting Response**

The verse contrasts the laughter with the weeping that is absent, and the contrast defines what the recitation calls for.

Weeping in the Qur'an is the response of those who understand what they are hearing. Sūrah as-Sajdah describes the true believers in the revelation as those who—when it is recited to them—fall into prostration and glorify the praises of their Lord and are not too proud (32:15). Sūrah Maryam states that whenever the revelations of the Most Compassionate were recited to them, they fell down, prostrating and weeping (19:58).

The response combines two movements: the body goes down and the eyes fill. Neither is performed for effect, and both are described as the natural outcome of understanding rather than as acts of devotion added to it.

**The Verified Saying on Laughter and Tears**

The Prophet's own statement on this contrast is recorded in several chains, and the wording is consistent.

If you knew that which I know, you would laugh little and weep much. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6485 from Abū Hurayrah and 6486 from Anas, and Bukhārī records a version addressed to his followers from ʿĀʾishah at 6631: by Allah, if you knew what I know, you would weep much and laugh little.

The saying is conditional, and the condition is knowledge. It does not condemn laughter as such; it states that the proportion between laughter and tears follows from what a person knows. The audience's laughter was therefore a statement about its knowledge rather than its temperament, and the verse's contrast makes the same point.

**The One Who Weeps Alone**

The value the tradition places on tears appears in one of the most widely transmitted sayings about the day of judgment.

Seven people will be shaded by God on the day when there is no shade but His, and among the seven is a person who remembers God in seclusion and whose eyes overflow with tears. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 660 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1031.

The detail that matters here is the seclusion. The tears counted are not those shed in company or in performance but those shed alone, where no audience exists to reward them. The verse's contrast between public laughter and absent weeping turns on the same distinction: one is performed for others, and the other is not performed at all.

**The Two Reactions Compared**

The verse sets the two responses against each other, and the comparison is not between two temperaments but between two assessments.

A person who laughs at a warning has judged it either untrue or unimportant. A person who weeps at it has judged it true and important, and the weeping is the judgement expressed rather than described.

The Qur'an treats the second as the sign of understanding throughout. Those given knowledge before the revelation are described as falling on their faces in prostration when it is recited to them, saying that the promise of their Lord was fulfilled (17:107–108). The physical response is presented as evidence of the intellectual one.

**What the Verse Leaves Unstated**

The verse does not command weeping, and the omission is significant.

A command to weep would produce performance, and performance is what the verse is criticizing. What the verse does is describe an absence — the audience is not weeping — and leave the absence to be felt by the hearer.

The method is the same as the sūrah's earlier questions. It does not instruct; it holds up a fact about the audience and lets the fact do the work. A person who hears that he is laughing where others wept has been given something to consider, and the consideration is the verse's aim.

**The Laughter Recorded in the Accounts**

The accounts of the Meccan period record the mockery this verse describes, and the records give it a specific form.

Sūrah al-Anfāl states that the prayer of the Quraysh at the Sacred Mosque was nothing but whistling and clapping (8:35), which the commentators connect with their conduct during the recitation. The reports describe people raising their voices to drown out the Qur'an, and the sūrah al-Fuṣṣilat records the instruction attributed to them: do not listen to this Qur'an and make noise during it, so that perhaps you will overcome (41:26).

The picture is of organised disruption rather than spontaneous amusement. The laughter this verse describes was a tactic, and the tactic was to prevent the recitation from being heard — by others as much as by the listeners themselves.

That context sharpens the verse's contrast. The weeping it asks about requires hearing, and the audience's conduct was arranged to make hearing impossible.

**Weeping in the Reports of the Companions**

The tradition preserves accounts of the response the verse calls for, and the accounts come from those who heard the recitation directly.

The reports describe Companions weeping at the recitation, and the sūrah of Maryam states the pattern for the prophets before them: when the verses of the Most Merciful were recited to them, they fell down, prostrating and weeping (19:58). The physical description is the same in both cases — the body descending and the eyes filling — and it is presented as a response rather than a performance.

The contrast with the audience's conduct is exact. One group went down; the other laughed and stayed upright. The verse does not moralise about the difference; it states it, and the statement is left with the hearer.

The command at the end of the sūrah asks for the first of the two responses, in the plainest possible terms.

**The Verse's Function in the Closing**

Three verses now describe the audience's reaction in sequence: astonishment, laughter without weeping, and persistence in heedlessness.

The sequence traces a descent. Astonishment is at least a form of attention; laughter withdraws the attention; heedlessness removes the capacity to recover it. Each stage makes the next easier, and the sūrah describes them in the order in which they occur.

The final verse answers the sequence with a single command, and the command is directed at the whole of it.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:61
> while persisting in heedlessness?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sāmidūn: The Word and Its Readings**

The verse ends the description of the audience's reaction with a single word: *sāmidūn*, and the word has been read in three ways.

The commonest reading takes it as heedless, inattentive, holding the head high in indifference. A second reading takes it as diverted, turned away from the matter at hand. A third, reported from the Yemeni dialect, takes it to mean that they sang when they heard the recitation, which would make it a description of mockery through performance.

The three readings are not opposed. Heedlessness, diversion, and mockery through song describe one condition from three angles, and the commentators hold them together rather than choosing between them.

**Heedlessness as a Condition**

The word describes a state rather than an act, and the distinction matters for the charge the verse makes.

An act can be repented of and corrected; a condition persists and shapes the acts that follow. Heedlessness is not a decision made at a moment but a settled way of encountering things, in which the significance of what is heard is not registered.

The Qur'an treats heedlessness as the underlying condition of disbelief more often than it treats disbelief as a series of decisions. Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ opens with the statement that the time of people's judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away (21:1), and the pairing of nearness with heedlessness is the same pairing this sūrah makes.

**The Reading That Describes Singing**

The Yemeni reading deserves attention because it identifies a specific practice.

The reports describe the audience's response to the recitation as a kind of performance — whistling, clapping, or singing over the words so that they could not be heard. Sūrah al-Anfāl records the practice directly: Their prayer at the Sacred House was nothing but whistling and clapping (8:35).

On that reading the verse is not describing an inner state but a deliberate tactic. The recitation was drowned out, and the drowning out was organised. The charge would then be stronger than heedlessness: not inattention but the active prevention of attention, in themselves and in others. The tactic is recorded because it worked, and the verse's question asks whether it is still working.

**What Heedlessness Does**

The verse's charge is the most serious of the three reactions it describes, and the reason is what heedlessness prevents.

Astonishment can turn into enquiry, and mockery can turn into anger, and both remain engagements with the message. Heedlessness does not engage at all. It allows a person to hear the recitation repeatedly and remain unchanged, because nothing heard is allowed to reach the point where it must be answered.

That is why the verse ends the sequence with it. The descent described in the closing verses reaches its end not in hostility but in indifference, and indifference is the condition from which recovery is hardest.

**The Three-Part Charge Complete**

Three verses have described the audience's reaction, and the three parts form a single account.

They marvel at the discourse; they laugh at it and do not weep; they persist in heedlessness. The first is a failure of judgement, the second a failure of feeling, and the third a failure of attention. Together they describe a response that never reaches the point of answering the message on its content.

The sūrah's argument throughout has been that the message can be answered — that its claims can be examined, its precedents checked, its premises tested. The closing verses describe an audience that has avoided answering, and the description is the sūrah's final diagnosis.

**The Question Mark**

The verse is interrogative, and the form completes the sequence begun two verses earlier.

The sūrah asks whether the audience marvels, describes the laughter, and ends with a question about the heedlessness. The question form leaves the diagnosis with the hearer rather than announcing it, which is consistent with the method used since the verse about the three idols.

A statement about the audience's condition would have been met with denial. A question about it obliges the hearer to check, and the checking is the only thing that can break the condition the verse describes.

**Heedlessness and the Sūrah's Earlier Charges**

The charge of heedlessness connects back to the diagnosis the sūrah made in its middle section, and the connection is precise.

The audience was described there as following conjecture and the desire of the soul, and as having no knowledge of what it asserted. Both descriptions are descriptions of a mind not attending: conjecture is what a person asserts without checking, and desire shapes belief without being examined.

Heedlessness is the condition that makes both possible. A person who attended to what he believed would notice that it had no warrant; a person who attended to what he wanted would notice that his beliefs matched his wants. The sūrah's earlier diagnosis and its final charge are the same fault described at two points.

That is why the closing verses do not introduce a new accusation. They name the condition that produced the accusations already made.

**Sāmidūn in the Language**

The word deserves a closer look, because its range explains the variety of the readings.

The root carries the sense of raising the head, of standing upright in a manner that suggests indifference or pride. Applied to a person hearing something serious, it describes the posture of one who is not moved — the head stays up, the body does not descend, the face does not change.

The Yemeni extension to singing is not unrelated. To sing over a recitation is to fill the space where the words would land, and the filling is a physical form of the same refusal. Both readings describe a body that declines to respond.

The word therefore completes the verse's sequence in a physical register. The audience marvelled, laughed, and remained standing with their heads up, and the command at the end of the sūrah asks them to do the opposite with their bodies.

**The Command That Answers It**

The verse is followed by the sūrah's final command, and the command answers all three reactions at once.

Marvel is answered by an act that requires no explanation; laughter by an act that cannot be performed mockingly; heedlessness by an act that requires attention to perform at all. The command is short, and its shortness is part of the answer.

The sūrah ends where it began, with the Prophet and the message, and the last word is an instruction to the hearer rather than a statement about him.

## Sūrah an-Najm 53:62
> Instead, prostrate to Allah and worship ˹Him alone˺!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse of Prostration**

This verse is one of the verses at which the reciter prostrates, and its status in the tradition is specific.

The verses of prostration are counted at fifteen on the common reckoning, though the schools differ on the number and on which verses are included. Most jurists hold that the prostration after this verse is obligatory on the reciter and the listener; Imam Mālik held that in this instance it is recommended rather than required.

The reports indicate that the Prophet would prostrate on completing the recitation of this sūrah, and the commentators note that the practice may have been a habitual supererogatory act rather than the discharge of an obligation. The difference between the schools concerns the ruling, not the practice, and the practice itself is not in dispute.

**The Command and Its Two Parts**

The verse contains two imperatives, and the second is broader than the first.

Prostrate is a specific act with a specific form. Worship is the whole of what a person owes, of which prostration is one expression. The verse names the act first and the general obligation second, which is the order a recitation would suggest: the hearer is addressed in the moment of hearing.

The particle translated as instead carries a sense of turning away from what preceded. The audience was marvelling, laughing, and remaining heedless; the verse tells them what to do instead, and the instead is the whole of the answer.

**Prostration as the Answer to Mockery**

The command answers the three reactions described in the preceding verses, and it answers each of them by its nature.

Astonishment is a state of arrest, and prostration is a movement that resolves it. Laughter requires an upright posture and an audience, and prostration removes both. Heedlessness requires inattention, and prostration cannot be performed without attending to what is being done and to whom.

The act is therefore not an arbitrary addition to the message but the specific response to the specific failure. The sūrah ends by prescribing the physical opposite of the condition it has just diagnosed.

**The Object of the Worship**

The verse names the object explicitly, and the naming is the sūrah's final word on the matter it opened.

The chapter began by denying that the Prophet speaks from whim and by describing a vision at the boundary of creation. It examined three named idols and found their names unsupported. It stated that intercession belongs to God's permission alone, that both worlds are His, and that He is the Lord of the star the audience venerated.

The command to worship Him alone is the conclusion of all of it. Every element of the audience's system has been examined and found without warrant, and what remains is the single object the verse names.

**The Parallel Verses on Prostration**

The Qur'an describes prostration before revelation in several places, and the descriptions agree in treating it as a response rather than a ritual.

Sūrah al-Inshiqāq asks what is the matter with them that they do not believe, and when the Qur'an is recited to them, they do not bow down in submission (84:20–21). The question implies that bowing is what recitation calls for, and that its absence is a failure to respond.

Sūrah as-Sajdah describes the believers as those who fall into prostration when the revelation is recited to them and are not too proud (32:15). The phrase not too proud identifies the obstacle, and the obstacle is the same pride that produced the laughter in the verses before this one.

**The Sūrah's Ending**

The chapter ends with a command rather than a conclusion, and the ending is characteristic of Meccan revelation.

A conclusion summarises what has been established; a command tells the hearer what to do with it. The sūrah has assembled a vision, an examination of idolatry, a moral order drawn from earlier scriptures, three destroyed peoples, and an undated nearness, and it closes by asking for one act.

The proportion is deliberate. The argument occupies sixty-one verses and the demand occupies one, and the demand is physical, immediate, and available to anyone hearing the recitation. Nothing in the chapter is required of the hearer before that act.

**The Report of the Public Recitation**

The tradition reports that this sūrah was the first the Prophet recited publicly in Mecca, and the report has a bearing on the final verse.

The accounts describe a gathering at the Sacred Mosque in which the recitation was delivered aloud to an audience that included both believers and disbelievers, and they report that those present prostrated at its conclusion. Some of these reports are connected with the disputed account discussed earlier in the chapter's history, and they should be treated with the same caution: the fact of an early public recitation is well attested, while the details attached to it in certain chains are contested.

What matters for the verse is the practice it describes. The command at the end of the sūrah was answered physically by the people who heard it, and the answering became the practice of the recitation.

That is the sūrah's final claim about itself: it is not only read but responded to, and the response is built into its ending.

**Prostration in the Qur'an's Wider Usage**

Prostration is used throughout the Qur'an as the marker of acknowledgement, and the usage gives the command its weight.

Sūrah al-Ḥajj describes everything in the heavens and the earth bowing to God — the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, and the living creatures — along with many human beings (22:18). The prostration of a believer is therefore presented as joining a universal act rather than performing a private ritual.

The same chapter describes the angels, who do not disobey what they are commanded, and the sūrah's own account of the vision places the observer at the boundary of creation where those beings are. The command at the end of the chapter asks the hearer to do what the account has just described being done.

That is the sūrah's closing movement: from the vision at the boundary, through the examination of the stones and the stars, to the act that aligns the hearer with the worshippers described at the beginning.

**The Recitation and the Response**

The verse is addressed to whoever hears it, and the address does not distinguish between the first audience and every later one.

The sūrah is reported to have been the first the Prophet recited publicly in Mecca, and the reports describe both believers and disbelievers prostrating when it was recited. The command at the end of the chapter has been answered in that way ever since, in the prostration made at its conclusion.

That is the sūrah's final claim: the recitation produces a response in the body, and the response is the answer to the marvel, the laughter, and the heedlessness the closing verses described.
