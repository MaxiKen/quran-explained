# Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah (Chapter 56) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Inevitable Cataclysm and the Three Tiers of Destiny**

Al-Wāqiʿah, "The Inevitable Event," is the fifty-sixth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 96 verses of early Makkan revelation. It takes its name from its opening verse: *“When the Inevitable Event occurs (*Idhā waqaʿati al-wāqiʿah*), there is, at its occurrence, no denial!”* (56:1–2). In prophetic tradition, Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah holds a celebrated station as a divine protection against material and spiritual poverty: ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd reported that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: *“Whoever recites Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah every night will never be afflicted by poverty (*faqr*)”* (Shuʿab al-Īmān by al-Bayhaqī 2498), and Ibn Masʿūd famously commanded his daughters to recite it every evening. With pulsating, dramatic urgency, Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah describes the total dismantling of the terrestrial order—the earth violently shaken and the mountains crumbled into scattered dust—and establishes the ultimate tripartite division of humanity into three eternal categories: *Al-Sābiqūn* (The Foremost in Faith), *Aṣḥāb al-Yamīn* (The Companions of the Right Hand), and *Aṣḥāb al-Shimāl* (The Companions of the Left Hand).

**The Structural Anatomy and the Threefold Assembly**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Wāqiʿah is built around four symmetrical movements:

1. The Cataclysm and the Tripartite Division (1–10): The earth shaken, mountains pulverized, and humanity divided into three groups: the Companions of the Right, the Companions of the Left, and the Foremost (*al-Sābiqūn al-Sābiqūn*).

2. The Rewards of the Two Righteous Tiers (11–40): - *Al-Sābiqūn* (11–26): The elite souls brought nearest to God in the Gardens of Pleasure, a multitude from the early generations and a few from later times; reclining on thrones woven with gold and precious stones, served by immortal youths with chalices of flowing wine causing no headache, with flesh of fowl they desire, companions with large beautiful eyes like hidden pearls (*ka-amthāli al-lu'lu'i al-maknūn*), hearing no vain speech or sinful talk, only the serene greeting: *“Salām! Salām!”* - *Aṣḥāb al-Yamīn* (27–40): The Companions of the Right in thornless lote trees, banana trees layered with fruit, extended shade, constantly flowing water, and abundant fruit never withheld, a multitude from the early and later generations.

3. The Torment of the Left and the Four Cosmic Questions (41–74): - *Aṣḥāb al-Shimāl* (41–56): Scorched by scorching winds and boiling water, in shadows of black smoke neither cool nor noble, forced to eat from the bitter tree of Zaqqūm, filling their bellies and drinking on top of it scalding water like thirsty camels (*shurba al-hīm*). - The Four Existential Interrogations (57–74): Did you create the human sperm-drop or are We the Creators? Each question demands the response of verse 74: *“So exalt the name of your Lord, the Most Great (*Fa-sabbiḥ bi-ismi Rabbika al-ʿAẓīm*)!”*

4. The Oath of the Stars and the Dying Soul (75–96): A colossal oath sworn by the setting-places of the stars (*bi-mawāqiʿi al-nujūm*): that this is a honorable Qur'an in a well-guarded Book, touched only by the purified angels. It portrays the terrifying, intimate scene of the dying human soul when the breath reaches the collarbone (*al-ḥulqūm*), and family members stand by watching helplessly: *“And We are closer to him than you, but you do not see!”* (56:85). The sūrah concludes by confirming the three destinies, sealing the discourse with the final, sublime doxology: *“Indeed, this is the absolute truth of certainty! So exalt the name of your Lord, the Most Great!”*

The sūrah confronts the stubborn deniers who mocked resurrection—asking incredulously: *“When we have died and become dust and bones, will we indeed be resurrected? And our forefathers of old?”* (56:47–48)—demonstrating that the God who engineered human life from a drop of fluid can effortlessly reconstitute human consciousness.

## Verse 56:1
> When the Inevitable Event takes place

**A Sūrah Named by What It Refuses to Describe**

The first word after the time-conjunction is al-wāqiʿah — "the Event that falls" — and the sūrah will go almost its entire length without adding a definition. The root is waqaʿa, to fall or come down, and in Arabic the falling of a thing is the phrase of its certainty — decrees fall, judgments fall, the pen falls upon the page — so "the Inevitable" is not an addition but the word's own weight: when what must happen happens. A reader arriving from the sūrah of the Merciful, which ends "Blessed be the Name of your Lord," lands here on a sentence whose subject is the end of everything the previous chapter enumerated, its gifts, its two gardens, and all of them included. Arabic's idhā — "when" — opens a clause and waits. For six verses this sūrah withholds the verb that answers it, piling one descriptive scene atop another (the denial at v2, the debasing at 3, the shaking earth at 4, the pulverized mountains at 5, the dust at 6) before the sentence finally lands on its "you" — "you will all be divided into three groups" (7). Some reciters and exegetes pause after verse 1 as if the terror were too great to complete — "When the Event comes to pass." Stop.

**What This Sūrah Was Recited For**

The chapter's afterlife in Muslim practice grew out of a famous report: "Whoever recites Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah every night will never be afflicted by want." It is transmitted from Ibn Masʿūd by al-Bayhaqī in Shuʿab al-Īmān. Al-Albānī graded it weak in his chain (Silsilat al-Aḥādīth al-Ḍaʿīfah 289), which is worth stating plainly since the hadith is often quoted without its grading. Weakness in the chain did not stop the practice: Ibn Masʿūd is reported to have ordered the daughters of his own household to recite it every night.

**The Sūrahs That Turned His Hair Gray**

The weight of this chapter is registered in a report about the Prophet ﷺ himself. Abū Bakr said to him, "O Allah's Messenger, you are becoming gray," and the answer named five chapters: Hūd, al-Wāqiʿah, al-Mursalāt, ʿAmma yatasāʾalūn and Idhā al-shamsu kuwwirat — "these have made me gray." At-Tirmidhī collected the report and graded it ḥasan gharīb, sound in its text but singular in its chain. Al-Wāqiʿah stands second in that list.

A chapter can carry that weight because its title-word is no metaphor. Al-wāqiʿah is one of the Qur'an's own names for the Day of Resurrection, and the Book uses it that way again in the chapter of the Inevitable itself (69:15 — *“on that Day the Inevitable Event will have come to pass”*). The commentators call it a name of the Day for the plainest of reasons: that Day is real, and it will certainly come.

## Verse 56:2
> then no one can deny it has come

**The Day the Tongue Is Fired**

The next clause inverts everything the word "deny" had meant in the sūrah's hearers' lives. In this world, denial is an act — a thing people do repeatedly, at price, with effort, on schedule; the Quraysh had a standing industry of it, the "woe to the deniers" of verse 1 of the sūrah that opens this juz. Here the act is abolished not by compulsion but by evidence: when the Event falls, there is no one who can say it has not, because the Event's whole nature is to be the end of argument about it. The Arabic of the classical recensions is a bare existential — "no denial" — the total negation of the verbal noun, which denies even the category. Not "no one denies," which would describe a mood; "denial is not," which describes a world with one fewer option, like a country where the currency of the old reign has stopped being legal tender.

**What Denial Was Always About**

Ask what the sūrah's audience actually denied, and the answer in this chapter's own mouth is not a creed but a date. The mocking question arrives later in the same sūrah in the first person plural of the scoffers: "When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?" (56:47). Denial of the Event was never a denial that something enormous could happen; it was the claim that it would not happen to me, not now, not in this body, not on their schedule. Verse 2 answers the only form the denial ever took: kadhdhabat — the lie rendered inert, its verb itself made passive and past, a lie that has had its past tense. The moment it falls, the question "when?" is answered so completely that no tense remains for doubt to work in.

**Two Glosses on the Word for Denial**

The early glosses on the clause both keep the negation total, and they differ in what they make of it. Muḥammad bin Kaʿb read the denial as an active certainty: it will certainly occur. Qatādah read it as the closing of every exit: the Day shall not be discontinued, taken back or aborted. The Qur'an states the same impossibility in its own summons (42:47 — *“Respond to your Lord before the coming of a Day from Allah that cannot be averted”*) and in the challenge of the asker (70:1-2 — *“for the disbelievers—to be averted by none”*).

## Verse 56:3
> It will debase ˹some˺ and elevate ˹others˺

**Two Participles Without Objects**

The Arabic of this verse is three words long and names its victims to no one: khāfiḍatun, rāfiʿatun — a debasing, an elevating. The objects are simply absent, and that absence is the whole sermon. An event that debases and elevates with no stated targets is the final universalization of a process this world runs in partials: here the powerful lift their own and press down rivals, and the lifting is always local, always reversible. The two indefinite active participles at verse 3, with the verbs' subjects supplied only by "the Event" of verse 1, state the reversal as a law without a constituency. Translators have to smuggle "some" and "others" into brackets, as this one does — the honest mark of an ellipsis that is in the original, not in the reader's need.

**The Reversal as the Sūrah's Blueprint**

Take the two verbs forward through the chapter and they turn out to be its entire architecture. The "companions of the right" and the "foremost" are the elevated half of verse 3 given furniture — thrones, faces, cups, shade (15–34); the companions of the left are the debased half given its weather — the scorching wind and the black smoke (42–44). Even the cosmic scenery is organized by the same pair: the mountains are lowered into dust (5–6), the earth is raised flat (4), and the stars — those ancient signs of fixed rank — fall (81:2 — *“and when the stars fall down”*). A sūrah that opens with the Event falling describes nothing but falling and raising from here on.

**Which Reading Is Right: People or Cosmos?**

The commentators split early over the verse's object, and both wings of the debate have real text to hold. One party takes the debasing and elevating as cosmic: the falling is the stars put out, the sky torn, the mountains blown away — and they cite the parallel cascade of the Rattles (77:8–10 — *“So when the stars are put out”*), where exactly that sequence stands: "So when the stars are put out. The sky is torn apart, and the mountains are blown away." (77:8–10) The other party insists that verse 2's denial fixes the topic on the deniers: the Event debases those who denied it into the Fire and elevates those who were denied into the Garden.

**Those Who Heard It, Near and Far**

One old reading of the two participles is about sound rather than rank. Al-ʿAwfī reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that the debasing and the elevating made the near and the far hear it, and ʿIkrimah gave the mechanism: the Event lowered, so those who were close heard it, and it elevated, so those at a distance heard it. Ad-Ḍaḥḥāk and Qatādah said the same. The other reading is about destination, and al-Ḥasan and Qatādah put it plainly: the Event drops into the lowest parts of the Fire people who were mighty in this life, and lifts to the highest grades of eternal delight people who were weak in it. Verse 3 needs no object for either claim, which is why both readings are old.

## Verse 56:4
> When the earth will be violently shaken

**The Second "When"**

Verse 1 opened with "when" and verse 4 repeats the conjunction — idhā again — restarting the clock that never finished ticking. The grammar of the sūrah is a stack: when the Event falls (1), when the earth shakes (4). The mountains crumble (5), and only at verse 7 does the pile of whens land its answer, "you will be of three groups." The repetition is the rhetoric: a single event is being described from multiple surfaces at once, the way one earthquake is recorded by several instruments. The hearer is never allowed to feel that the first "when" was completed and a new topic begun; the Event and the quake are the same falling, measured on different dials. And this second idhā uses the verb of being shaken, rujjat, in the passive — the earth is shaken and does not shake: agency is already out of the picture, matter and its appointed hour are everything.

**Rujjā: the Word for Shaking Done Properly**

The Arabic verbal noun here, rujjā, is intensive — the shaking that shakes completely — and the sūrah of the Quaking (99) uses the very same verb with its own superlative attached: "When the earth is shaken ˹in˺ its ultimate quaking" (99:1), which reads as an explicit gloss of this verse: there can be an ultimate shaking, and verse 4 names the event where it happens. In ordinary speech rujj al-rajulu could mean a man was stirred to rage or delusion; the root carries violence done to a person's balance, not merely to his footing. Applied to the ground under a city, the word means the end of footing as a category. The translators' "violently" is honest but faint: the passive with this verb is the earth losing the pretense of solidity it has maintained since the first day it was spread out (79:30 — "and the earth — He extended it").

**The Earth That Will Speak: 99's Twin**

The sūrah of the Quaking (99) takes the same quake and gives it a mouth: after the earth is shaken in its final shaking, it "brings out its tales," and the day's verdict is that the earth will testify to what was done upon it (99:2–6 — *“and when the earth throws out ˹all˺ its contents and humanity cries, “What is wrong with it?”— on that Day the earth will recount everything having been inspired by your Lord ˹to do so˺ On that Day people will proceed in separate groups to be shown ˹the consequences of˺ their deeds”*). Verse 4's passive rujjat therefore does not merely announce demolition — the floor is being cleared of its pretense to mute neutrality. The same earth that swallowed the buried dead will hand back what it held, which is exactly the claim verse 60–62 will then make about people: the God who takes the ground's evidence is the God who "has ordained death among you" and "is not outdone." The shaking is an evidentiary proceeding staged in geology.

The most exact twin of this verse stands in the sūrah of the Enshrouded, where the opening summons the Prophet to a night of recitation against the coming quake: "on the Day the earth and mountains will shake ˹violently˺. Mountains will be ˹reduced to˺ dunes of shifting sand." (73:14) The pairing of earth-shake and mountain-sand, in the same order as verses 4–6 here, shows a fixed prophetic tableau — the earth and its mountains moving together, the one shaken, the other scattered — preserved in two different sūrahs that were recited for different occasions.

**A Sifter Shaken with Its Contents**

The glosses agree that nothing on the earth is exempt from the shaking. Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid and Qatādah read the verb as violent shaking, and ar-Rabīʿ bin Anas gave it a picture: the earth will be shaken with everything in it the way a sifter is shaken with what it holds. The Qur'an issues the same warning in its own opening address (22:1 — *“O humanity! Fear your Lord, for the ˹violent˺ quaking at the Hour is surely a dreadful thing”*), which is why the line from the sūrah of the Quaking (99:1, quoted above) reads less like a parallel than like an echo of one announcement.

## Verse 56:5
> and the mountains will be crushed to pieces

**Crushed to Pieces, or Set Travelling?**

The English "crushed to pieces" covers an Arabic that is stranger and more exact: suyyirat al-jibāl — the mountains were made into sayr, a going, a journeying, from the same root as the sīrah, the traveling life-story. The sūrah is not describing a demolition site; it is describing motion where motion is the one thing mountains are not. The Book elsewhere keeps that strangeness visible: "Now you see the mountains, thinking they are firmly fixed. They are travelling ˹just˺ like clouds." (27:88) The shaking earth and the moving peaks are one image of the world with its anchors lifted — "crushed" is what the travelling looks like from below, where the ground has stopped holding things still. The verb is passive, as in verse 4: no army, no cause, no second party; the mountains are simply put in motion by the One who never had to touch them to fix them. The Qur'an paints the mountain's end in three textures across its late sūrahs. The audience of al-Wāqiʿah would have felt each one with the hands: "and the mountains will be like carded wool" (101:5) — stone made fluff, the softest ruin imaginable, wool teased apart for spinning, the domestic act of every winter evening turned into apocalypse. The dunes of sand in the Enshrouded sūrah; and here, in verse 6, light itself will show what remains.

**The Three Offices of a Mountain, Cancelled at Once**

The Qur'an gives mountains three jobs, and verse 5 ends all three in a single passive. They are anchors — "firmly set mountains, lest the earth should shift with them" (21:31) — so suyyirat is the un-pinning of the world's floor, the same peg-image of 78:7 — *“and ˹made˺ the mountains as ˹its˺ pegs”* pulled in the moment the tent is struck. They are cover — the Book credits God with making "from the mountains, shelters" (16:81), hiding places for the frightened and the fugitive, and a mountain set travelling is a wall with no rooms. They are signs — "landmarks" by which travelers, and in this Book also the reasoning mind, navigate (16:16 — *“Also by landmarks and stars do people find their way”*); a mountain that is no longer there is not just a lost view but a lost coordinate. The sūrah does not argue from all three offices; it simply enacts their joint removal, and the verse's terseness is the argument — a people whose permanence, safety, and direction were literally the same stones are handed one clause and no consolation. When verse 7 sorts the audience into groups, it will be the first time since verse 3 that anything in the scene has a fixed address.

The sūrah of Ta-Ha puts the same dissolve into plain words when the Prophet is asked about them: "My Lord will wipe them out completely, leaving the earth level and bare, with neither depressions nor elevations to be seen." (20:105–107) Elevation itself, the verse says, is what is being tried — not this mountain or that.

**What the Commentators Made of the Crushing**

The verb was glossed as relentless pulverizing by Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah and Qatādah, and Ibn Zayd tied the verse to the Qur'an own description of the same moment in the sūrah of the Enshrouded, where the mountains are (73:14 — *“on the Day the earth and mountains will shake ˹violently˺, and mountains will be ˹reduced to˺ dunes of shifting sand”*). That is the tableau verses 4 to 6 of this chapter build in three steps, in the same order: the shaking, the crumbling, the sand.

## Verse 56:6
> becoming scattered ˹particles of˺ dust

**habāʾ: What Only Light Makes Visible**

The word for the dust of mountains, habāʾ, is not any dirt but a specific sight: the particles that swim in a shaft of sunlight coming through a crack, visible only because they are small enough to float and the light is strong enough to strike them. Nothing heavier became nothing lighter, and the image quietly corrects the mockery the same audience directed at resurrection: when they sneer "when we are dead and become dust and bones" (56:2? Better, their own later words at v47 — "will we really be resurrected?"), they imagine dust as the end of the story, and the verse hands the mountains the same dust to teach them it is only the middle. Habāʾ is dust in its most mobile state: the Day does not crush the mountains into powder; it renders them airborne.

**Scattered: the Participle of No Return**

Manthur — "scattered, cast about" — completes the word: not one heap, not even a ruin anyone could visit. The same two-word phrase carries the sūrah's other great dissolution, the one that undoes not geography but biography: "Then We will turn to whatever ˹good˺ deeds they did, reducing them to scattered dust." (25:23) Deeds of deniers and mountains of the world share the identical state in the Book's language — the pairing is a theology of the unrealized: what has no weight before God acquires no permanence. The verse about the mountains is legible as judgment precisely because 25 already made works scatter the same way.

**kāna at Full Power: Becoming, Not Resemblance**

Arabic's verb "to be" (kāna) does double duty, and this verse makes it do the harder one. "They became scattered dust" is not the sūrah saying the mountains were like dust; kānat here takes the predicate of real transformation — what they are, in the new state, is habāʾ. The same verb, in the same sūrah, describes what God will do with people at the end of the passage on death: He is able "from transforming and recreating you in forms unknown to you." (56:61) Mountain and human share the one verb of re-making. The sūrah's point is exact: the dust-moment is not the mountains' death but their change of function, and the human parallel is resurrection's own mechanism — transformation, not repair. The deniers of verse 47 imagine "dust and bones" as the terminus of the person; this verse has already shown that dust, for God, is a verb's object, never a sentence's end.

If the mountains have become floating specks, then the next question — where did they go, who kept track — is answered by the Book's standing claim about matter: "We certainly know what the earth consumes of them after death. With us is a well-preserved Record." (50:4) The verse does not invoke it here, but the sūrah's audience heard the pairing, because the same chapters tie the earth's appetite to the record that misses nothing: dust is not loss, it is inventory.

**Four Pictures of the Same Dust**

Four glosses survive on the scattered dust, and they do not agree, which is useful. Abū Isḥāq narrated from al-Ḥārith, from ʿAlī, that it will be like a rising dust storm that soon fades and leaves no trace of itself. Al-ʿAwfī reported from Ibn ʿAbbās the embers that rise when a fire is kindled and are extinguished as soon as they land. ʿIkrimah took it for the dust the wind scatters in every direction, and Qatādah for the dry parts of trees that the wind carries off. Every picture has the same property: the mountain ends as something briefly visible that cannot be gathered again.

## Verse 56:7
> you will ˹all˺ be ˹divided into˺ three groups

**The "You" the Whole Opening Was Holding**

Since verse 1, the sūrah has spoken about everything — the Event, denial, debasing, earth, mountains, dust — without once saying "you." Verse 7 lands the suspended clause: fa-takūnūna, "and you will be," the answer to both whens, the Event and the earthquake. The grammar has been staging a lesson in proportion the whole time: cosmic convulsions are the scenery, and the scene's subject, once it finally speaks, is the congregation being addressed right now. Everything that fell, fell for this sentence. The "you" also arrives with the brackets a translator must use to make the Arabic's middle voice legible: you will be classed, sorted, you will find yourselves in groups — nobody divides himself, and the division is described as what will simply have happened to the assembly when the mountains are done. The sūrah's people are the apodosis of its cosmos.

**Three Classes Where Two Would Be Expected**

Judgment in this Book usually runs in pairs — the people of Paradise and the people of the Fire, the left-hand people and the right-hand people — and other late sūrahs stop there (90:18–19 — *“These are the people of the right”*). The "foremost" is not a middle path but a lane of its own, the runners who won while the others were still debating the race, and its position first in the list means the list teaches, before anything is explained, that judgment has a gradation inside mercy, not only a division.

**Right and Left: The Hands That Decide the Scroll**

"Right" and "left" here are not metaphors of favor and ill luck; the classes are named for the hand in which the record arrives, the scene the chapter's neighbors play out in full: "As for those given their records in their right hand, they will cry ˹happily˺, “Here everyone! Read my record!" (69:19) and "As for those who are given their record in their left hand, they will cry ˹bitterly˺, “I wish I had not been given my record." (69:25) The custom that makes the gesture legible — the right hand for eating, covenant. Honor; the left for its opposites — is old Arabic's own, and the sūrah uses it without comment because its audience lived in it. The Day's first act, in this sūrah's logic, is bureaucratic: everyone receives a document, and the hand that hands it is the hand that reads you. The three groups are so assigned before any verdict is spoken, which is exactly how a chapter on the Inevitable wants its audience to feel the certainty: the scroll decides, the scenery only comments.

**Where the Two Hands Come From**

The commentators explained the three classes with an origin story and a proportion. The people of the right are those brought out from Ādam's right side, given their records in their right hands and taken to the right; the people of the left are those brought out from his left side, given their records in their left hands and taken to the left. As-Suddī added the proportion: the right will make up the majority of the residents of Paradise, while the foremost are fewer and higher — the chiefs of the right, and they include the messengers, the prophets, the truthful and the martyrs.

The Book draws the same threefold line inside a single community (35:32 — *“Then We granted the Book to those We have chosen from Our servants. Some of them wrong themselves, some follow a middle course, and some are foremost in good deeds by Allah’s Will”*). One further note belongs to this verse: the sorting into these three groups happens at death, as the end of this sūrah shows, and not only on the Day.

## Verse 56:8
> the people of the right, how ˹blessed˺ will they be

**A Question Mark on a Class of People**

The Arabic of the second half is a bare exclamation — mā aṣḥāb al-yamīn — literally, "what, the companions of the right!" — and the grammar has troubled no reader because the phrase is still alive in every Arabic dialect: the word mā with no verb and a definite noun is the construction for overwhelming wonder, "what a thing they are!" The translation has to supply an adjective the Arabic never uses ("how blessed"). The bracket ˹blessed˺ is the honest mark of that debt. The sūrah will describe the right-hand people for twenty-five verses before saying a single word of their fate, and here at verse 8 it refuses the word completely — as if to say that the class is knowable in its condition only, not in any predicate. The reader who wants the adjective must go work for it in the coming verses, and every one of those verses is an answer to the exclamation: thornless trees, unfolded shade, water that asks nothing. The wonder is the only verdict the verse will give.

**The Hand That Receives the File**

"Right" here is not a honorific floating free of context. The companions are named by the hand that takes their record, the gesture the late sūrahs spell out, where the one given his record in his right hand is reckoned with a reckoning easy and light (84:7–8 — *“they will have an easy reckoning”*) The two halves of the custom are joined in one image — the file in the right hand, the audit made light — and the sūrah of the Event relies on its audience knowing which hand in that courtroom does the congratulating. The old manners of the Hijaz reinforce it: the right hand eats, seals covenants, and counts; the left does its negatives, and the phrase for the unlucky was to be "left-handed" in one's affair.

**The Phrase as Structural Hinge**

The phrase of the right as the place of the saved is not an Arab invention. The sūrah uses the hand its audience had already heard in the older congregations' the teaching about the Last Day, where at the final judgment the sheep are set at the right hand and the goats at the left (Matthew 25:31–33 — *“He will set the sheep on his right hand, but the goats on the left.”*).

## Verse 56:9
> the people of the left, how ˹miserable˺ will they be

**The Mirror Verse, and What Its Symmetry Does**

Verse 9 is verse 8 with one consonant changed. The structure — noun of direction, exclamation of wonder, semicolon — repeats so exactly that the sūrah makes the listener feel the reversal before any content arrives: what was marvel at is now marvel at. The grammar refuses to tell you which direction the marvel runs until the Arabic supplies its own nothing. Here too the translator brackets "how miserable," because the original says only "mā aṣḥāb al-shimāl" — "what, the companions of the left!" The symmetry is the first thing the verse teaches: the Day's classes are not a spectrum with a bright end and a dim one; they are two hands. The sūrah will spend verses 11–44 describing the right in full before turning to the left at 41–56, which means the left-hand people live in the text exactly as the rejected do — out of the picture until the picture is taken down.

**Where the Left-Hand People Came From in the First Audience**

The verse's sharpest historical edge is that the left-hand people are not, in this chapter, "the pagans of Quraysh" under another name. The categories in 56 are moral offices, and the early commentaries list the left's inhabitants by quality — those given the book in the left hand by the same 69:25 — *“And as for those given their record in their left hand, they will cry ˹bitterly˺, “I wish I had not been given my record”*'s register — rather than by tribe. The audience at verse 9 could not point at a neighbor and say, "he is the left," and that indeterminacy is the sūrah's design of the same piece as the bracketed adjective: nothing in the verse identifies a single living person, and everything in it identifies a possibility the reciter carries.

---

## Verse 56:10
> and the foremost ˹in faith˺ will be the foremost ˹in Paradise˺

**A Sentence That Says Its Word Twice**

The Arabic is the bluntest possible: al-sābiqūna al-sābiqūn — "the foremost, the foremost." The same phrase serves as subject and predicate, an phrase Arabic keeps for the thing too well known to define: the lion is the lion, and the foremost are the foremost. Where verses 8 and 9 startled with wonder at a class, verse 10 refuses even that concession: the class's name is its own description.

**sabaqa: What a Race Teaches the Day**

The root s-b-q gives sabīq, the runner who pulls ahead in a race — the verb sabaqa ilā, to outstrip toward. The image is not combat, commerce, or labor; it is running toward, which means a straight road, effort in one direction, and a finish line that decides everything before the crowd arrives. The sūrah of Iron commands this race to its own readers — "So compete with one another for forgiveness from your Lord" (57:21) — and here, in the chapter of the Event, the command's object is shown at the moment of victory: the race is over. The word for the runner is now the entire vocabulary of rank. The choice of a racing noun over a royal or military one keeps the foremost democratic in one respect: whatever their line, they ran — no one outranks the fore by possession, and the sūrah's audience of merchants heard that acoustics exactly.

**Who the Foremost Were Said to Be**

Two answers were given about the class itself. Muḥammad bin Kaʿb and Abū Ḥazrah Yaʿqūb bin Mujāhid took "the foremost, the foremost" to mean the prophets, and as-Suddī took it for the people of the highest heights, ahl al-ʿilliyyīn. The reading this section has followed keeps the racing sense: the foremost were first in doing what God commanded, and the Book commands exactly that hurry (3:133 — *“And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth”*). The rule drawn from the pairing is worth keeping, because it is the rule the whole chapter runs on: the reward matches the kind of deed, and as a person does, so he is judged.

## Verse 56:11
> They are the ones nearest ˹to Allah˺

**A Passive Name for the Highest Rank**

The word is al-muqarrabūn — "the ones brought near" — and the morphology decides the theology before any sentence is spoken. Arabic's passive participle says the nearness happened to them; nobody climbs to God in this sūrah's vocabulary, and no verse from here to the end of the chapter suggests otherwise. The Book's whole language of proximity runs the same way: nearness is conferred (the angelic (4:172 — *“The Messiah would never be too proud to be a servant of Allah, nor would the angels nearest to Allah. Those who are too proud and arrogant to worship Him will be brought before Him all together”*) and the prophets alike are "those near to Him"), pride is what removes it, and the verse's own class-name is the passive of grace. The bracketed "˹to Allah˺" is the translator's completion of what the participle leaves open. The sūrah itself will fill the space in verses 12 and 26 with gardens and the greeting "peace, peace," which is to say that nearness is described by where you are placed and what you hear, not by miles.

**The Ring the Chapter Completes at the Deathbed**

This is the verse whose word the sūrah's ending reclaims, and hearing it early makes the later return land. At verses 88–89, the dying soul is sorted, and the first outcome reads: "So, if the deceased is one of those brought near to Us, then such a person will have serenity, fragrance. A Garden of Bliss." (56:88–89) — the same muqarrabūn, now met at a throat rather than in an estate. The bracketed "to Us" appears there in the text itself, supplying exactly the dative the translators of verse 11 had to insert. The sūrah so frames its whole middle section with one word used twice: the near ones in the vision of the Day, the near ones at the moment of death, and everything between the two brackets is the commentary. The Day of division and the hour of dying are, on this ring, the same single sorting, and verse 11 is the class's permanent title.

**Nearness, a Rank Shared with the Angels**

The plain sense of the verse's position — the claim of nearness sits before the claim of gardens. The sūrah's own later verses use the same phrase for the angels (81:19, "honored, obeyed there") — makes "nearness as rank in God's regard" the safest single answer, with the garden then named as its address rather than its substance.

---

## Verse 56:12
> in the Gardens of Bliss

**Naʿīm: The Word That Chooses the Soft**

The gardens named here are jannāt al-naʿīm — the gardens of bliss, or of naʿma, ease — and the qualifier is worth weighing against the sūrah's other garden-words. Paradise in the Qur'an is most often just "the garden," jannah, whose root means coverage: a place so densely treed that the ground is hidden, the heat is hidden, the exposure of the desert is hidden. The addition here, naʿīm, selects the texture: not garden-as-shelter but garden-as-comfort, the noun of the soft thing done gently. The same pairing is the Qur'an's standing shorthand for paradise as the reward of the patient — a pairing readers have found as far apart as the Heifer's provision without measure and the Repentance's gardens of delight (2:212, 9:21) — and what carries the weight here is the lexical sense, not the cross-reference. What matters is what the sūrah needs in this spot: the foremost, named in 10–11 for speed and proximity, get as their address the word that means ease, because the race has finished and the chapter's furniture is about to begin — and a couch (15) only makes sense in a country called naʿīm.

**A Cross-Reference the Neighboring Sūrah Writes Itself**

The same jannāt-with-bliss phrase stands, a chapter earlier in the Qur'an's recitation, in the light-verse of the Iron sūrah: "Today you have good news of Gardens, under which rivers flow, ˹for you˺ to stay in forever." (57:12) The near-neighborhood of the two sūrahs (55, 56, 57 in the codex's ordering, revealed and recited in the same orbit) means this verse's four words have a gloss close at hand: gardens are rivers and permanence first, naʿīm second — the ease is not a mood but a hydrology, water below you and no exit date on your stay. Verse 12 takes the same estate and calls it, explicitly, bliss, and the class above it (11) is near to the Owner of it. Between the neighboring chapters, "in the Gardens of Bliss" reads like the whole promised economy of the Qur'an's end in one prepositional phrase: rivers, forever, ease, nearness, named in that order.

## Verse 56:13
> ˹They will be˺ a multitude from earlier generations

**Ṣabwa: A Word That Means Too Many**

The noun for "multitude" here is ṣabwa, an old Arabic word for a swarm — a mass large enough to be disordered, the word that describes ants, and locusts, and crowds at wells. That it should be chosen for the foremost is a quiet joke the sūrah plays on its audience's idea of an elect: the elite class of the Day contains, in its own counting noun, a swarm of the old generations. The bracket at the verse's start (˹They will be˺) is the translator bridging the ellipsis from verse 12 — "in the gardens of bliss — a multitude" — and the seam is honest: the sūrah never says "they will be". The verse is the fragment of a sentence, and the fragment is the fact. The earliest peoples fill the first half of the roster; the present audience arrives, at verse 14, in the second.

**"The Earlier Ones" — a Qur'anic Category**

Al-awwalūn, "the former ones," is the sūrah's term for everyone before this community: the generations of the prophets, the nations of the warning-tales, the long ledger of humankind that this Book keeps citing as evidence (and in the mocking question at verse 48, as provocation: "And our forefathers as well?"). The same pairing — the former and the latter — appears in the sūrah's own claim about the resurrection gathering: "Most certainly, earlier and later generations will surely be gathered ˹together˺ for the appointed Day." (56:49–50) The category is so not nostalgic. "The earlier ones" are the sūrah's word for the whole dead half of the human count, and verse 13 announces that the foremost are many in it.

**The First Generation of This Community, Read into the Verse**

Where the sūrah of the Repentance speaks of "the first forerunners (al-sābiqūn al-awwalūn) among the Emigrants and the Helpers" (9:100), later readers heard verse 13's "multitude of the earlier ones" point, in the sūrah's own audience, at that founding generation of Islam — the Muslims of Makkah and the first Madinah, whose precedence the Qur'an itself had registered. The reading is not forced: the foremost are defined by running, and the first converts ran the race with the least support, which is exactly why 9:100 — *“As for the foremost—the first of the Emigrants and the Helpers—and those who follow them in goodness, Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him.”* attached the same word to them.

**A Multitude Named Before a Single Blessing**

The Qur'an's standing epitaph for those peoples is final — that community has passed. What it earned went with it (2:134 — *“That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done”*) — and the whole preaching apparatus of the late Makkān sūrahs runs on the corpses of that argument: the water of Noah, the wind of Ād, the cry of Thamūd, the cities turned upside down.

**A Debate Over Which Generations Are Meant**

The commentators disagreed about who counts as the earlier ones, and the disagreement decides how verse 14 lands. Mujāhid and al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, in the collection of Ibn Abī Ḥātim, took the former to mean the believing nations before this one and the later to mean this community — the view Ibn Jarīr preferred, and supported with the saying of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ that "we are the later nation, but the foremost on the Day of Resurrection." The objection to that reading is short and strong: this community is the best of the nations by the Qur'an own text, so its foremost cannot be outnumbered by the foremost of the nations that came before it.

The better reading is the one this section has taken. Al-Ḥasan said so when the verse was read to him — a multitude from the earlier generation of this community — and Ibn Sīrīn reported that the Companions said, or hoped, that they would all be from this community. Two reports were read beside the verse for the same reason: that the best people are the Prophet's generation, then the next, then the next; and that a group of this community will remain upon the truth, uppermost and unharmed by those who let them down or oppose them, until the Hour begins.

## Verse 56:14
> and a few from later generations

**The Few Who Are Being Counted Right Now**

The audience of a Makkān sūrah is "the later ones," and verse 14 does not let them miss the fact: the foremost of their own time will be a minority — qalīl, a few. The pairing with verse 13 has the shape of a rebuke delivered as statistics: the race was crowded when the road was new; you will run it alone, and few of your contemporaries will finish. What makes the bluntness bearable is the very next scene — couches, cups, shade — the long comfort of verses 15–38: the few are so carefully provided for, in the chapter's furniture, that the sūrah turns the smallness of the present group into an intimacy rather than an insult.

**Fewness in the Book's Arithmetic**

The Qur'an is not shy about the minority of the saved. This sūrah repeats the motif elsewhere: the companions of the left are many in the mocking majority of the world (41–56's audience). The Book elsewhere warns that most people will not believe (12:103 — *“And most people will not believe—no matter how keen you are”*), and it counts the grateful as few in the same breath as it commands them (34:13 — *“Work gratefully, O family of David!”*) — the command followed at once by the observation that only a few of God's servants are truly grateful (34:13 — *“˹Only˺ a few of My servants are ˹truly˺ grateful”*). Verse 14 belongs to that arithmetic: fewness as data, not despair. The verse is doing the same thing the prophet's own reports did when he told his companions that at the end a person holding on to a tenth of what was commanded will be saved and another performing a tenth will be safe — traditions the commentaries quote here, of varying reach and grading (the "one-tenth" report is transmitted in the devotional collections; here without a numbered citation). The sūrah's own number — a few — is the only count it treats as settled.

**"Later" — al-Ākhirūn as a Theological Noun**

The adjective ākhir, "later," in the sūrah's mouth does not merely mark a date. The paired terms (awwalūn/ākhirūn) are the Qur'an's standing way of naming the whole of humanity as one assembly in two shifts — every person who ever lived is either of the first or of the second, and both are summoned for the "appointed Day" at verse 50's "earlier and later generations" (56:49). Verse 14's "few" is therefore a slice taken out of the audience's own identity: not "people who come after you," but you, counted into the late half of all mankind. The sūrah's the teaching about the Last Day has been staging exactly this: the Event (1), the two hands (8–9), the runners (10), the address (11–12), and now the generation. When the later chapters of the sūrah come to the deathbed scene, its verdict will apply to the dying person without ever asking which half he is in. The few have already been named into the roster of the brought-near, and "later" is no longer a disadvantage, it is the roster's last line.

**The Verse That Frightened the Companions**

An occasion is attached to this verse, and it ends in reassurance. Abū Hurayrah reported that when these two verses came down — a multitude from the first, a few from the later — the news weighed on the Prophet's Companions. Then verses 39 and 40 were revealed with the adjective changed (56:39-40 — *“˹who will be˺ a multitude from earlier generations and a multitude from later generations”*), and the Prophet ﷺ said: "I hope that you will be a quarter of the residents of Paradise, a third of the residents of Paradise — rather, you are half of the residents of Paradise, and you will have a share in the other half." Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded the report, and Aḥmad recorded it too.

A second number was read here for the same comfort. In reports carried by many routes the Prophet ﷺ named seventy thousand of this community who will enter Paradise without reckoning, and some versions add that with each thousand come another seventy thousand. The few of verse 14 was not the last word this audience heard about itself.

## Verse 56:15
> ˹All will be˺ on jewelled thrones

**A Preposition, a Throne, and the Beginning of Furniture**

Verse 12 put the class "in the gardens"; verse 15 puts them "on thrones" — ʿalā, the preposition of elevation and of being seated upon — and between the two the chapter has stopped describing the estate and begun describing a room. The furniture arrives as a claim about posture: they will be upon them, the way a person is upon what is under him, and nothing in the verse asks them to get there. Arabic's sarīr, the word translated "throne," is not a seat of judgment in this usage but a raised couch with a canopy — the piece of royal display around which an eastern household organized honor — and when the sūrah later borrows it for the beds of the Garden it is elevating, with a single noun, the ordinary piece of a well-born home into the furniture of nearness to God (the same word that describes Bilqīs's magnificent seat, 27:38–41 — *“Solomon asked, “O chiefs! Which of you can bring me her throne before they come to me in ˹full˺ submission?”*, and Solomon's, 38:34–35 — *“And indeed, We tested Solomon, placing a ˹deformed˺ body on his throne, then he turned ˹to Allah in repentance˺ He prayed, “My Lord! Forgive me, and grant me an authority that will never be matched by anyone after me. You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺.”*). Paradise keeps the vocabulary of courts and gives it to everyone.

**Jewelled: Mardūwiyyāt and the Luxury Vocabulary of Revelation**

The adjective here, mardūwiyyāt, is classically glossed in two directions the sūrah's commentators recorded — woven through, or encrusted, with pearls and gold, "inlaid couches." Either way it belongs to the Qur'an's small family of imported luxury words for the Garden's surfaces: silk brocades (the istabraq of 76:21 — *“The virtuous will be ˹dressed˺ in garments of fine green silk and rich brocade, and adorned with bracelets of silver, and their Lord will give them a purifying drink”*), thick tapestries and carpets (the rafraf and abqariyyāt that clothe the blessed of the all-Merciful sūrah, 55:76 — *“All ˹believers˺ will be reclining on green cushions and splendid carpets”*), and here the pearl-woven couch. The linguistic fact is unglamorous and important: the Qur'an's vocabulary for the afterlife's surfaces borrows the trade-lexicon of the very commerce that raised Makkah, so that the audience's finest goods become, in their transfigured form, the markers of divine reward. What the caravan imported, Paradise inlays; and the verse's bracketed ˹All will be˺ keeps the census from forgetting that the class of 13–14 — swarm and remnant — is what wears the jewels.

**The Beds of the Garden and the Bed of the World**

One word-family runs through all of this. The Book asks, in the sūrah of the Cliff, "Have We not smoothed out the earth ˹like a bed˺. ˹made˺ the mountains as ˹its˺ pegs" (78:6–7) — the world itself is a mattress pinned at its corners for the use of its sleepers — and this chapter's right-hand people will be given "elevated furnishings" (furush, 34), while the foremost get jewelled thrones (15). The sūrah is working the same noun up the ladder of its classes: earth-as-bed for everyone, spread cushions for the right, inlaid couches for the nearest. That is why verse 15 reads less like furniture than like promotion: the elevated are not moved to a different planet, they are given a better mattress on the logic of the same creation, the world's own softness perfected. And when the mountains are unpegged in verses 5–6, the image completes: the bed of the world, whose pegs are pulled for the Day, is replaced by beds whose cushions are jewelled for the people of nearness. The chapter's geology and its hospitality are one argument, conducted in bedding.

**Woven with Gold: the Glosses**

Ibn ʿAbbās glossed the thrones as woven with gold, and the same gloss is reported from Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, Saʿīd bin Jubayr, Zayd bin Aslam, Qatādah and ad-Ḍaḥḥāk. The word keeps both of its directions — woven through, or inlaid — and the roster settles the material rather than the technique: whatever was woven into these couches, the early readers agreed that it was gold.

## Verse 56:16
> reclining face to face

**Faces: What the Sūrah Lets the Elevated See**

Face-to-face in a Book that loves faces means the verse also hints at what the audience of these people will look like and what they will see. The Garden's faces elsewhere in the Qur'an are bright, working, and — highest of all — beholding: "On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, looking at their Lord." (75:22–23). Al-Wāqiʿah does not quote that; but it places its reclining class in the chapter's first mention of nearness to God (11) so that the geometry is complete: the nearest face one another in a room whose door, we were told, opens on being brought near. A mutual gaze among the guests, with a prior dative of God at its back — that is verse 16's quiet maximum.

**Muttakiʾūn: The Grammar of Leaning Done Right**

The verb is ittakā, Form VIII of w-k-y — to lean back, to prop oneself, to be at rest against support — and the sūrah's use is the Qur'an's own settled phrase for the posture of the blessed: the people of the Garden recline (the word returns at 36:56 — *“They and their spouses will be in ˹cool˺ shade, reclining on ˹canopied˺ couches”*, 44:53 — *“dressed in fine silk and rich brocade, facing one another”*, 52:20 — *“They will be reclining on thrones, ˹neatly˺ lined up ˹facing each other˺. And We will pair them to maidens with gorgeous eyes”*), a posture the world reserved for sultans and the dead, since in pre-Islamic practice only kings reclined at table while others sat forward. Verse 16 gives the whole assembly the sultan's spine, and in the chapter's own terms this is the completion of verse 3: the elevating has finished, and what it elevated now leans. The root also carries, in Arabic's older usage, the net into which game falls (muttakā is a pit or snare) — a resonance the sūrah's next images will cash in quietly: the birds of verse 21 arrive without being hunted, and the only snare in Paradise is rest.

**Muqābilīn: Nobody's Back Is to You**

The face-to-face — muqābilīn, reciprocal participle, turning toward one another — is the sūrah's single clause of social engineering. Every room on earth has a geometry of backs: those seated behind the honored, the queue, the listener's shoulder, the angle a rival must lean into to be seen. The Garden abolishes backs; all faces are toward all, which means no one is audience to another's honor, and honor itself is no longer scarce enough to face sideways for. The chapter's parallel at 37:44 makes the same clause serve the companions of the right ("facing each other on thrones," 37:44). 15:47 supplies the psychological equipment it assumes: rancor is first stripped from their chests — "We will rip out whatever is in their breasts of resentment, [as] brothers facing each other on couches" — before the facing can mean anything. Verse 16 in our sūrah does not spell the prerequisite out, because its own 24–25 (no vain talk, only peace) already encodes it: faces that are level are what you can have once nothing in the chest has to be hidden from them.

**The Posture the Chapter Denies Everyone Else**

Read 16 against the sūrah's only other reclining, and the verse acquires its negative. The companions of the left will be, in the chapter's stark mirror, "They will be in scorching heat and boiling water, in the shade of black smoke, neither cool nor refreshing" (56:42–44) — they too get a "shade," and theirs neither cools nor shelters. The right, by contrast, recline in extended shade (30) and on raised couches, and the foremost face each other on thrones without a single discomfort itemized because none can be. Al-Wāqiʿah's technique is to distribute the same amenities between two classes and let one list read as gift and the other as parody of the first. The reclining faces of verse 16 are therefore the chapter's standard for everything "comfort" can mean: rest that is mutual, ease that has no side of the room. Shade — which for the left will mean only smoke — that here is the quiet light of a room in which nobody has to turn around.

## Verse 56:17
> They will be waited on by eternal youths

**The Same Phrase in the Neighboring Sūrah**

The Mount sūrah, whose reward-lists pair with this chapter's so closely that the two seem to be read against one another, has the identical tableau in four words: "And they will be waited on by their youthful servants like spotless pearls." (52:24) — and it then borrows this sūrah's pearl-image (23) for the servers, while here the pearls dress the maidens. The exchange of similes across the two chapters is the Qur'an's own commentary on the vocabulary: youth, pearls, eternal round-service belong to the whole scene, and which object gets which figure is a matter of the scene's lighting, not of doctrine. The sūrah of the Event says "eternal youths," the sūrah of the Mount says "like hidden pearls" — together they tell the audience what the boys are for: the sight of unspoiled, tireless, glad service is itself part of the hospitality. Being waited upon by people who cannot grow sullen is the Garden's answer to the world's most quiet fear of one's own household.

**Whose Children They Are: A Debate the Sūrah Leaves Open**

The commentaries raise a question the verse never asks — whose boys these are — and its answers show what a pastoral text is doing with its silences. One party reports a position from Salmān al-Fārisī identifying the servants as the children of the polytheists — the idea being that no child of that community is lost, and their service in the Garden is their station of nearness. Another party, reported from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, assigns them to the children of the believers, matching the hope that the faithful's little ones who died young are not dead at all. The sūrah itself says only mukhalladūn — made to last — and its neighboring promise in the chapter of the Storm, that believers will be joined with their offspring who followed them in faith (52:21 — *“As for those who believe and whose descendants follow them in faith, We will elevate their descendants to their rank, never discounting anything ˹of the reward˺ of their deeds.”*), covers the parents' grief without naming it here.

**Boys Who Never Change**

The gloss on the servants is a single claim about time: they are immortal in the sense that they never grow up, never grow old and never change in shape. The service of this verse is therefore not a station anyone passes through. In a chapter whose first business is the unmaking of everything that ages, that is not decoration — the only beings here described as fixed are the ones whose whole work is to keep giving.

## Verse 56:18
> with cups, pitchers, and a drink ˹of pure wine˺ from a flowing stream

**Three Vessels, One Sentence**

The Arabic sets the table in three nouns: kās — the glass that already holds its drink — abrīq — the ewer that pours it — and salsabīl, a word so saturated with motion that translators keep taking it as the name of a spring, when here it is "from a spring" (min maʿīn): the glass, the pitcher, and the flowing source. That ordering — served cup, refilling vessel, living wellhead — is the whole hydraulic dream of a desert people, compressed into a metonymy: the sūrah never describes the liquid in verse 18, only the containers and the plumbing, and the containers are doing the theology. In a Book that will spend verses 57–74 arguing for God's skill from milk and semen and rain, Paradise's drink arrives pre-argued: it is described by its vessels and its spring because the sūrah's point is not what the drink is like. Where it comes from and what it will never do — and verse 19 exists to complete that clause's "never.".

**The Wine the Sūrah Never Names**

The liquid is only implied: "a cup from a flowing spring," and the translator brackets "of pure wine," supplying from the wider Qur'an what this verse declines to say. That reticence is the verse's most-misunderstood property. Elsewhere the Book promises the near-rank "rivers of wine, delicious for those who drink" (47:15). The glasses of the companions in the Ranks chapter are filled "from a spring" with identical phrasing (37:45–46 — *“A drink ˹of pure wine˺ will be passed around to them from a flowing stream”*), but here in al-Wāqiʿah the noun khamr never appears — because the sūrah's interest is in what the drink will not do, and naming the wine would summon the world's associations with it. The bracketed insertions in the verse's English are the honest trace of that design: the sūrah offers the cup and its stream and leaves to the next line the whole duty of defining the drink by subtraction: "that will cause them neither headache nor intoxication" (56:19). Paradise is described in this chapter as the removal of the reason for prohibition, not as its relaxation.

**Handles, Spouts, and a Spring That Does Not Empty**

The three vessels were told apart in the glosses by their fittings: the cups have no handles and no spouts, while the jugs sometimes have them and sometimes do not. The detail that matters belongs to the third noun. All of them, glasses included, are filled from a flowing spring rather than from containers that could run out. The wine of this verse is drawn, not decanted — the same economy as the water poured at 31 and the fruit never cut off at 33.

## Verse 56:19
> that will cause them neither headache nor intoxication

**A Verse of Subtraction**

Having placed the cup, the sūrah defines the drink completely by what it will not do: neither piercing headache nor loss of mind. This apophatic delight is a deliberate rhetoric. The audience of Makkah knew wine's two bills — the skull's throb the next morning, and the mind's fog that night — and the law had already ringed the drink with restrictions because of them. The Garden's answer to the denier's complaint that God has forbidden pleasant things is not "it will be allowed to you there" but "its cost will be removed there." The pleasures of al-Wāqiʿah are described by their side-effects excised: the fruit has no season and no denial (33), the shade has no heat, the talk has no vanity, and the drink here has neither end. Every comfort in the chapter arrives with its invoice already paid, which is the sūrah's answer, in image form, to the world's only argument against God's generosity — that enjoyment is borrowed against pain.

**Ṣadāʿ: The Headache with a Spear in It**

The word for the headache, ṣudāʿ, comes from a root of piercing — ṣadʿa, to pierce, to stab — so the noun is not "a sore head" but a lance through it, the classical hangover image. Its neighbor in the Qur'an, in the Ranks sūrah, keeps the same two negatives against the same cup: no headache in them, nor will they be intoxicated (37:46–47 — *“crystal-white, delicious to drink It will neither harm ˹them˺, nor will they be intoxicated by it”*), a fixed Qur'anic pair for the drink of the near-rank that our verse repeats with new verbs. The redundancy is useful: two late Makkān sūrahs agree, clause for clause, on the precise medical profile of the forbidden thing as the measure of the promised thing.

**The Four Effects Removed**

Ad-Ḍaḥḥāk reported from Ibn ʿAbbās a list that makes the two negations exact: the wine of this world has four effects — it intoxicates, it gives a headache, it makes a person vomit and it makes him pass water often — and God described the wine of Paradise free of all four. The commentators then divided the verse's two verbs between those effects: Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, Saʿīd bin Jubayr, ʿAtiyyah al-ʿAwfī, Qatādah and as-Suddī took the first as "it does not give them a headache" and the second as "it does not take away their reason."

## Verse 56:20
> ˹They will also be served˺ any fruit they choose

**faakihah: The Ripening**

The word is faakihah, from the root that means, before it means fruit, to burst into ripeness — and the same root gives the sūrah's neighbors their verb for delight (tafakkuh, to enjoy), so that "fruit" and "enjoyment" are etymological twins in the Qur'an's hand. Al-Wāqiʿah uses the word without a tree: "and fruits" arrives as sheer produce, unburdened by orchard, harvest, season. The verse's brackets are the translator's work, and they show how spare the Arabic remains: "and fruit they choose" — noun, relative clause, done — following the vessels of verse 18 without a new verb of giving. The sūrah's syntax is a banquet at which the plates keep appearing because the Host's sentence does not end; each new noun is another course placed by parataxis, and the reader is fed by the list's mere continuation.

**The Measured Portion Said Without Measure**

The sūrah of Ranks describes the Garden's fare with a phrase that reads as a gloss of this verse: "They will have a known provision: fruits ˹of every type˺. In the Gardens of Bliss, ˹reclining˺ facing each other on thrones." (37:41–44). What our chapter borrows is the structure — provision and fruit and the removal of the hangover (19 above and 37:46–47 agree clause for clause) — while dropping its specificity of "known," the measured. That difference is the verse's quiet teaching: the near-rank's fruit is chosen, not allotted; the companions of the right receive fruit that is "abundant" and "never out of season nor forbidden" (32–33), while the foremost receive the open verb of choice.

**The Grammar of "Any"**

The relative construction — mā, "whatever," with the verb of choosing attached — is an open file in Arabic, a noun without extension limits, and the sūrah's restraint with it is the point: no list is closed by verse 20, no inventory is proposed. Compare the chapter of the All-Merciful, which promises the near neighbors of Paradise "two kinds of every fruit" (55:68) — abundance stated as doubling — while al-Wāqiʿah states it as the removal of the word "only": whatever the chooser can select is selectable. The two techniques mark the difference between a catalogue and a constitution; our chapter is writing laws of the place rather than its stock. Verse 20's bare relative is one of the strongest of them, a small function-word that outlaws the sentence "you may not have that." Later, the translators' brackets ("They will also be served") try to supply the verb of giving the Arabic withholds. But in the original, "fruit" simply stands where the sentence needs no verb — the wish is already on its way to being met, grammatically, before anyone serves it.

**The Verse Read as a Permission**

The commentators drew a practical rule out of this clause: it is evidence that a person may choose the fruit he prefers and wishes to eat. Preference, in the Garden's accounting, is not greed.

Aḥmad recorded, and Abū Yaʿlā with him, a report from Anas about a woman who came to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ with a dream. She had been taken out of Madinah into Paradise, where she heard a noise at which Paradise wept, and found twelve men from an expedition the Prophet ﷺ had sent, brought in with their wounds still bleeding. They were taken to a river called Baydakh and submerged in it, and their faces became as radiant as the full moon; then they were brought a plate of gold holding green dates, and they ate as much as they wanted, and whenever the plate was turned around they ate from whatever fruit it held. When the expedition's emissary arrived he named the same twelve men as dead. Al-Ḥāfiẓ aḍ-Ḍiyāʾ judged the report to meet the conditions of Muslim. What the tafsir keeps from it is the plate that never runs short however it is turned — verse 20's clause seen from the eater's side.

## Verse 56:21
> and meat from any bird they desire

**Flesh in an Orchard**

The menu pairs fruit and meat, the two staples of a desert diet, and it does so in the sūrah's spare style: and the meat of birds, as they desire. There is no cook, no fire, no slaughter and no market in the sentence. The missing knife is the detail the sūrah refuses to supply, since meat arrives here without the death that meat means in this world. The commentators noticed the silence and mostly filled it with reports that the birds present themselves willingly. The verse's own grammar supports the quiet, because the phrase "as they desire" places the whole apparatus between the wish and the plate and leaves it there.

**Why Meat Belongs in This Verse**

It is easy to skim these verses as decoration after the cups of the verses before, and the placement argues otherwise. The next passage defines the Garden by its sounds, where there is no idle talk and no incitement to sin, only peace, peace (56:25 — *“There they will never hear any idle or sinful talk”* and 56:26 — *“only good and virtuous speech”* give that). The verse about the birds comes immediately before it, as the last word in the body's catalogue.

Flesh, fruit and drink complete the animal functions before the sūrah moves on to speech, plants, water, shade, spouses and numbers. That is the order of a human being restored, sense by sense, to a world with none of its costs. This verse, at the hinge, does the work the others avoid. It serves meat, the one food in this world that comes through death, and it does so in a few words that mention neither animal nor knife.

**The Birds in the Qur'an's Wider Sky**

These birds are the last stop on a journey the Book makes them take. In the sūrah of Dominion they are one of the resurrection's public signs: do they not look at the birds above them, spreading their wings and folding them in? (67:19 asks it — *“Have they not seen the birds above them, spreading and folding their wings? None holds them up except the Most Compassionate. Indeed, He is All-Seeing of everything”*) — creatures whose flight is itself an argument about God's hold on what flies. In the sūrah of the Table, God commands Abraham to take four birds, cut them into pieces, lay the pieces on hills and call them, and they come to him (2:260 tells it — *“Allah said, “Then bring four birds, train them to come to you, ˹then cut them into pieces,˺ and scatter them on different hilltops.”*), the first demonstration of raising in the Qur'an's order, performed with birds. The sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān gives Jesus clay birds that live when he breathes on them (3:49 records it — *“and ˹make him˺ a messenger to the Children of Israel ˹to proclaim,˺ ‘I have come to you with a sign from your Lord: I will make for you a bird from clay, breathe into it, and it will become a ˹real˺ bird—by Allah’s Will.”*).

The dinner in this sūrah belongs to the end of the same argument. The birds that illustrated resurrection in the sign-passages are served at table here. What God raises, God also provides, and the miracle the deniers mocked turns out, in the Garden, to be the meal.

**Birds the Size of Camels**

Aḥmad recorded from Anas that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said the birds of Paradise are like Bukhtī camels grazing among the trees of Paradise. Abū Bakr said, "O Allah's Messenger, surely these birds must be wonderful," and the answer came back three times: "Those who eat them are more wonderful." The Prophet ﷺ added, "And I hope that you will be among those who eat from them." Aḥmad alone carries the report on this chain. The exchange is the logic of this verse in one line: the wonder is not in the provision but in the guest.

## Verse 56:22
> And ˹they will have˺ maidens with gorgeous eyes

**Qāṣirāt: The Gaze That Has Arrived**

In its companion chapters the Book completes the image with the detail that carries the sūrah's whole psychology of desire: these are "restraining their glances" — qāṣirāt aṭṭarf (37:48 — *“And with them will be maidens of modest gaze and gorgeous eyes”*; 38:52 — *“And with them will be maidens of modest gaze and equal age”*) — eyes that have stopped reaching, companions whose gaze is fixed on their own and who themselves look at no one else. The two directions matter together: desire in the Garden is not an intensified appetite but an appetite that has come to rest, and the "wide-eyed" of verse 22 in this sūrah and the "restrained" in its neighbors describe one condition from each side — the eye open and the eye contented. The phrase is the answer to the restlessness every husband and wife of the world knows. The sūrah promises not the addition of beauty to an envious, ranging heart but the removal of ranging itself, which is the chapter's consistent technique: cups with no headache (19), fruit with no season (20), and companions with no wander.

**The Antecedent the Sūrah Reuses at 35**

These two verses do a second piece of work that the reader only meets later. When the chapter says of the people of the right that God created them a special creation and made them virgins (35–36), the pronoun has no stated noun to lean on. Abū ʿUbaydah's answer was that the noun had already been given here, in the companions of verses 22 and 23, and al-Akhfash agreed that the later sentence implies the maidens of Paradise without naming them. Verse 22 therefore describes the companions of the near rank and supplies the subject of the middle estate's promise at the same time.

## Verse 56:23
> like pristine pearls

**Maknūn: The Beauty Nobody Has Touched**

The simile's adjective is maknūn, passive of kanana — to store, to hide away — and the image it carries is the pearl still in its shell, still in its bed on the sea-floor: a thing of value that has not been handled, aired, priced, or worn. The sūrah's neighbors use the same figure twice more, — the identical adjective that the Mount sūrah lends its youthful servants ("like spotless pearls," 52:24). The Insān sūrah likens its eternal pages to scattered pearls (76:19 — *“They will be waited on by eternal youths. If you saw them, you would think they were scattered pearls”*) — but here the effect on the verse's grammar is the whole theology: "maidens… like pearls with hidden treasure in them" says that in the near-rank the most precious beings are the untouched ones. Untouchedness — not the shine — is the property being compared. Every other pearl image in the audience's world was about extraction: diving, trading, drilling, stringing. The sūrah borrows the gem and returns it to the seabed, so that its companions' value is defined by what has never happened to them. In a chapter whose first verses strip the world of all its handled things, the hidden pearl is the one object that cannot be seized, lost, or hoarded, because it was never exposed.

**Pristine, Not Merely White**

The tafsir's gloss on the simile is plain: they are as white and pure as fresh pearls. It then ties the image to the other place where the Book uses it, in the sūrah of the Ranks, where the companions are (37:49 — *“as if they were pristine pearls”*), and to the description carried in the sūrah of the All-Merciful. The comparison is the same in all three: the pearl is valued before it is handled.

## Verse 56:24
> ˹all˺ as a reward for what they used to do

**Jazaʾ: The Root That Means Matching**

The noun is jazaʾ, from the root of going-between and equivalence — to repay a debt with its like, to settle — and the sūrah uses it here as a bare accusative, a hanging verbal noun that reads as a verdict appended to the whole preceding scene: ˹all this˺ ˹is˺ a recompense. The verse does not say the reward "exceeds" the deeds, nor that it "pays" them like a wage; the root's semantics is fittingness, the scale that matches. The classical reading of the sūrah's Paradise is built on this double awareness: what the near-rank receives is proportionate — the scene from 15 to 23 is not a fairy-tale surfeit but the exact correlate of lives spent in faith and its practices — and yet the proportionality is not arithmetic, because the matching is of kinds, not quanta: those who were brought near are near, those who chose God over the market choose fruit and birds, those who served guests recline as guests.

**The Deeds Named After the Reward**

The tafsir's paraphrase keeps the direction of the sentence intact: these delights were granted to them as a reward for the good deeds they performed in this life. The order is what the verse's grammar insists on. The whole scene from 15 to 23 is described first and its cause named last, so that the furniture of Paradise arrives with its invoice attached. Nothing here is presented as an endowment; it is paid, and the people reclining on it are the ones who paid.

## Verse 56:25
> There they will never hear any idle or sinful talk—

**Laghv: The Word for Everything Spoken for Nothing**

Laghv is not sin's big brother in the Qur'an's usage; it is the class of the useless and the foul — idle, hurtful, pointless speech, the chatter that occupies the gatherings of the proud. The Qur'an's portrait of the believers opens with "those who avoid idle talk" (23:3), and the Stories chapter has them answer slander by turning away (28:55 — *“When they hear slanderous talk, they turn away from it, saying, “We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours.”*); the verse following this ban states what replaces laghv — "only good and virtuous speech" (26), which in Arabic is the greeting of peace said twice. The near-rank's estate, then, is delimited at its edge by a noise-policy, and this is not decoration: the first thing al-Wāqiʿah says about the companions of the right, once the exclamations are done, is what their ears will never again have to endure. A hearer of 620 CE lived inside laghv — the market's slander, the poet's lampoon, the tribe's taunt — and the sūrah promises, before food or spouses, silence's opposite: a place where no one has to talk back, because no one talks at all except in greeting.

**"There": Whose Garden Is Being Quiet?**

The pronoun in the verse's Arabic — fīhā — has an antecedent the commentators debated: the gardens of verse 12, the scene of 15–24, or even the cups of 18 whose drinkers were just promised no headache (19's "in them" was the cups or the drinkers. The earlier chapters' parallels attach "lā rafath" — no obscene speech — to the Garden itself, as the Heifer verse legislates for the nights of Ramaḍān (2:187 — *“It has been made permissible for you to be intimate with your wives during the nights preceding the fast.”*) and the Ranks sūrah for its guests' cups (37:47 — *“It will neither harm ˹them˺, nor will they be intoxicated by it”*)). The debates are minute and the answer is not: whichever antecedent wins, the verse keeps the chapter's method of pairing body and word — the head that will not ache (19) and the room in which no word will wound (25) belong to one estate. Its silence is a physical good, like water and shade, distributed to the class that is described at greater length than any other. The near-rank was granted furniture; the right are granted, first of all, a protected ear.

**The Sin of Idle Talk in a Chapter about Denial**

Set the verse against the sūrah's opening: its verdict on the deniers is a speech-crime from the opening line — the Event before which "no one can deny it has come" (2) — and its transcript of their mockery comes in the middle of the chapter: "When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?" (56:47), which is laghv in the strongest sense: the joke about resurrection, the heckle at the sermon, the word about the unseen spoken for a laugh. Al-Wāqiʿah's anatomy of hell, when it comes at 41–46, will say nothing about their punishment being noisy; it says instead that they were "luxurious" and persisted in the "great sin" (45–46). Verse 25 stands between these two portraits of talk and is the hinge: the saved are defined as those whose speech got nothing out of them and whom nothing got out of, and the lost as those whose conversation about the biggest fact was a sneer. The Garden's quiet is not aesthetic; it is the final shape of a habit the verse's audience could still break.

**Nothing to Overhear**

One consequence of the ban deserves its plain word: in the Garden there is nothing worth eavesdropping on. The Qur'an elsewhere insists that every word is already recorded — "he does not utter a word but there is a watcher, ready" (50:18) — and the sūrah's verse 25 turns that cosmic surveillance into relief rather than dread: the record of the saved will contain no idle passage to be read back to them, no foul hour of talk to be weighed, because the Garden itself legislates the material out of existence.

## Verse 56:26
> only good and virtuous speech

**Salām: The Greeting That Is the Doctrine**

Peace is the word the Qur'an gives its God as a name, the word the angels use at every gate of Paradise in the chapter of thunder (13:23–24 — *“And the angels will enter upon them from every gate, ˹saying,˺”*), the greeting of Abraham's guests when they came to him in the form of men. — in the Jonah chapter's line about the believers — God's own salutation: "their greeting will be, “Peace!”" (10:10). Al-Wāqiʿah's verse stands in the middle of that family without quoting any of it: "peace, peace" is all the chapter needs to make the Garden's speech identical with its theology, because the sūrah's whole argument has been that the Event brings safety to the people of nearness and danger to no one in this room. To hear salām repeated is to have the day's verdict spoken back to you as wish rather than sentence. The exception clause of verse 26 is so the only place in the chapter where God lets the saved speak — and what He lets them say is the greeting that asks for what they have already got.

**Two Words against a Loud World**

Consider the argument the verse contains, because it is an argument against noise — the real noise of Makkah's sacred precinct, where the poets competed in the sanctuary's markets, the tribes traded lampoons under the Kaʿba's shadow. The mushrikāʾ, in the Book's own charge, "made their prayer around the House nothing but whistling and clapping" (the phrase of 8:35 — *“Their prayer at the Sacred House was nothing but whistling and clapping. So taste the punishment for your disbelief”*, whose two rare nouns the classical readers glossed exactly as avocations of the tongue). A chapter that bans laghv and then permits only salām twice is issuing, at the center of a Paradise description, a critique of the sanctuary's acoustics, and its hearers knew which rooms they were being offered instead. The near-rank's ear is the last possession the sūrah protects before it furnishes the rest. The protection is total: no vanity, no incitement to sin (25), no satire, no slogan, no claim, no rebuttal — a list of absences that describes, from the inside, what every Makkān gathering was and what the Garden will not be.

**The Greeting in Two Other Chapters**

The Book closes two other portraits of the saved with this same word. The Garden of Eternity is a place (14:23 — *“where they will be greeted with “Peace!””*), and the sūrah of the Overwhelming states the other half of the rule: a Garden (88:11 — *“where no idle talk will be heard”*). Read beside verse 25, the two make one policy — the useless word removed, the greeting left standing — and al-Wāqiʿah is the chapter that says both halves inside a single line.

## Verse 56:27
> And the people of the right—how ˹blessed˺ will they be

**The Exclamation, Third Time**

The device returns exactly as it broke out at verses 8–9 — "the people of the right, how ˹blessed˺ will they be. The people of the left, how ˹miserable˺ will they be" (56:8–9) — a phrase named and then answered about, and the sūrah has taught its audience the phrase, so that this third occurrence needs no gloss to be felt. The earlier pair of exclamations stood before any description existed; this one stands at the threshold of thirteen verses of description (28–40), which changes its meaning. The question is no longer "what will become of them?" but "what are they, now that they are what they are?" — the wonder raised at the saved person not as hope but as completed fact, the same astonishment the near-rank's verses earned from a census instead of couches. The repetition tells the audience that the Day's classes are not only three tiers on a list; each is a surprise, and even a fully described class, viewed from the inside, still defeats the sentence that began its description.

**Where the Right Hand Comes From**

The phrase aṣḥāb al-maymana, the people of the right side, is older than this sūrah, and the present translation's "people of the right" hides from English readers that the Arabic noun is aṣḥāb, owners and companions at once and is not an Arab invention. The Book's own neighbors speak of the day when the scrolls are handed — 84:7 promises the recipient of the book in the right hand a reckoning "easy," a near-relative of this verse's joy — and the older scriptures, as the chapter on the Sheep notes, knew a placing of the blessed at the right and the accursed at the left (56's own phrase at 8–9 was shown there to be borrowed currency). But "aṣḥāb" deserves attention: aṣḥāb are owners and companions at once — the people who own the right hand, the fellowship that is right-handedness — and the same noun governs the "companions of the Garden" in the Pen chapter's parable (68:17–32 — *“Indeed, We have tested those ˹Meccans˺ as We tested the owners of the garden—when they swore they would surely harvest ˹all˺ its fruit in the early morning”*, whose owners promised to harvest at dawn and lost the crop). Al-Wāqiʿah's companions of the right are, in their very title, the counter-parable to those hoarding farmers: ownership in this sūrah names a side, not an estate.

**A Rank Below the Foremost**

Maymūn bin Mihrān stated the ordering without hedging: the people of the right are lesser in rank than the foremost in faith. That is why this class gets a different inventory — no thrones woven with gold, no cups, no immortal servants, but trees, shade, water, fruit and beds — and why the census at 39 and 40 can call them a multitude on both sides. The middle estate is larger and less furnished, and the exclamation of verse 27 is the chapter's way of saying that this is still a wonder.

## Verse 56:28
> ˹They will be˺ amid thornless lote trees

**Sidr: The Tree at the Edge of Everything**

The lote tree carries, in the audience's own scripture-memory, the weight of a boundary. The Star chapter sets at the far end of the Prophet's ascent the lote tree of the ultimate boundary (53:14 — *“at the Lote Tree of the most extreme limit ˹in the seventh heaven˺”*), beside which — in the reports the collections preserve of that night — the Garden of the Refuge is described. And the Saba chapter tells of a lote-lined garden of the most famous hydraulic miracle in Arabia, the Marib gardens of the two right-hand and left-hand plots, which the people of Sheba lost when the dam broke and whose replacement, the verse says, was bitter fruit, fruitless bushes. A few sparse thorny trees (34:16 — *“But they turned away. So We sent against them a devastating flood, and replaced their orchards with two others producing bitter fruit, fruitless bushes, and a few ˹sparse˺ thorny trees”*) — tamarisk in place of lote. Al-Wāqiʿah's companions of the right stand, verse 28 says, among lote trees that have no thorns — the boundary-tree restored to them as plain garden stock. The resonance is double: the tree no human ascent may pass is, for the saved majority, a hedge in an orchard, and the tree that the ruined South Arabian paradise lost is returned in a form that cannot be dammed, flooded, or forfeited.

**The Left's Menu, Read Backward**

Set the lote tree against the only other tree the chapter names and verse 28 becomes argument, not botany. The people of the left "will certainly eat from ˹the fruit of˺ the trees of Zaqqûm, filling up ˹their˺ bellies with it" (56:52–53), then drink boiling water on top of it (56:54 — *“Then on top of that you will drink boiling water”*). The Ranks sūrah asks which lodging is better, the blessed's or "the tree of Zaqqûm" (37:62) — and the right-hand's lote, thornless and honeyed, is the direct negation of that menu's every property: fruit with no defense versus a fruit that is the defense, food that nourishes versus food that scalds. The sūrah's plants, like its water and shade, are distributed in pairs. The pairing is its most patient form of preaching: it tells the audience that nature is not innocent, that the same earth grows a lote and a zaqqūm. That what each plant will be on the Day — provision or punishment — is decided, in this chapter's physics, by which class you are standing in.

**Thornless, and Laden**

The glosses on "thornless" run in two directions and the tafsir keeps both. A long roster — Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿIkrimah, Mujāhid, Ibn al-Aḥwaṣ, Qasāmah bin Zuhayr, aṣ-Ṣaʿb bin Nusayr, al-Ḥasan, Qatādah, ʿAbdullāh bin Kathīr, as-Suddī and Abū Ḥazrah — read it as the kind without thorns, while Ibn ʿAbbās is also reported as reading it as the tree laden with fruit. The verdict is that both apply, and the reason is given: the lote tree of this world is thorny and carries little fruit, and in the hereafter the opposite holds, thornless and heavy with a load that strains the trunk.

## Verse 56:29
> clusters of bananas

**Bananas in the Qur'an: The Translation's Decision**

The Arabic says ṭalḥ, a thorny tree of the Ḥijāz whose pods the Arabs knew well. The English here says "clusters of bananas" — a decision with a long pedigree rather than an eccentricity. The classical collections transmit from ʿAbd al-Razzāq's circle and others a report from Ibn ʿAbbās, through Mujāhid, that the ṭalḥ of the Garden is the banana tree (mawz), and al-Ṭabarī records the same identification alongside the plain-botanical reading. The translator brackets nothing, which means the rendering leans toward the exotic gloss. The reader should register both: pods stacked like beads on the thorny desert tree, or bananas piled one above another in their hands, either image saying the same thing about the fruit of Paradise — it comes already clustered, countable, and made for gathering. The verse is two words in Arabic and this is how briefly the chapter builds its orchard.

**Clusters and the Chapter's Economics of Enough**

One arithmetic point closes the verse. The right-hand list (28–34) counts: two trees, a shade, a water, a fruit that is "abundant" (32), beds (34) — seven items, and not one of them owned. Verse 29's stacked clusters are the smallest unit of the chapter's anti-economy: fruit gathered is fruit spent, and fruit stacking means the supply outruns the picking. The sūrah that will soon reproach the traders of Makkah for a life spent in "rivalry in increase" (102:1) sets before them, for the right, fruit that increases without rivalry, hanging in ropes that no one cut, in a grove no one fenced. Clusters are abundance the hand can see, and verse 29 gives the audience the quantity of the Garden measured in what their own eyes could count: not gardens, but bananas, stacked, for the taking, forever on schedule.

**What the Thorns Were Replaced With**

ʿUtbah bin ʿAbd as-Sulamī reported that he was sitting with the Messenger of Allah ﷺ when a bedouin came and asked about the tree in Paradise that has more thorns than anything else — meaning the ṭalḥ. The Prophet ﷺ said that for every place a thorn had stood, God put fruit in its stead, like a castrated, tight-skinned ram, a food of seventy colours, each different from the other. In this verse's tree the thorn is not merely removed; it is converted.

**A Tree Quraysh Used to Sit Under**

The word ṭalḥ named a large thorny shrub that grew in the Ḥijāz, and Mujāhid glossed the adjective as fruit piled one on top of another. The tafsir adds a historical reason for the choice: God is reminding Quraysh of the very trees whose shade they used to enjoy. The identification with the banana is old and widely reported — Ibn Abī Ḥātim records it from Abū Saʿīd and notes the same from Ibn ʿAbbās, Abū Hurayrah, al-Ḥasan, ʿIkrimah, Qasāmah bin Zuhayr, Qatādah and Abū Ḥazrah — and Ibn Zayd gave the linguistic ground for it: the people of Yemen call the banana tree ṭalḥ. Ibn Jarīr recorded no other explanation for the word.

## Verse 56:30
> extended shade

**The Only Luxury the Desert Could Not Refuse**

Shade, in the sūrah's world, was not comfort; it was infrastructure — the date-palm was grown for it as much as for fruit, the tent was engineered for it, and the midday rest (qaylūlah, the sun's ceasefire woven into the day's prayer-rhythm) was a survival tactic. To promise "extended shade" to the people of the right is therefore to promise the middle class of the saved their own highest good, stretched. The sūrah's next line about the left will prove how load-bearing the gift is: "in the shade of black smoke, neither cool nor refreshing" (43–44) — the lost get a shade too, in the chapter's grim parallel. The comparison shows that what makes Paradise paradise, in al-Wāqiʿah's register, is not the presence of the exotic but the absence of the noon.

**Shade Against the Sun of That Day**

The Qur'an's the teaching about the Last Day runs on one meteorological fact: the sun of the Standing is close. "When the sun is wrapped up" (81:1) and humanity sweats toward its chin in the gatherings — this sūrah itself quotes no such line. Its audience needed no quote, and "extended shade" enters the inventory of the saved where the heat is the chapter's unspoken adversary (the left's "scorching heat," 42, names it at last). The shade is so Last-Day furniture: what the Day's weather makes of you depends on whose shade you are under. The Book's most consoling image of God's own protection — the seven sheltered in His shade on the Day with no other shade, preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī's book of charity from the Prophet's list — belongs to the same economy. Al-Wāqiʿah does not cite it; it simply places extended shade among the middle class's goods and lets the audience draw the inference that shade, on that Day, is not meteorology at all.

**The Shade a Rider Cannot Pass**

This is one of the few verses in the Qur'an that the Prophet ﷺ quoted by name. Al-Bukhārī recorded from Abū Hurayrah that he said: "In Paradise there is a tree that a rider could travel for a hundred years in its shade and not be able to pass it. Recite, if you will: extended shade." Muslim collected it as well, and the version in Aḥmad is carried by Muslim, al-Bukhārī and ʿAbd ar-Razzāq. The shade of verse 30 is therefore not a metaphor the commentators stretched; it was stretched in the recitation itself, within the lifetime of the first audience.

## Verse 56:31
> flowing water

**Maskūb: Water That Pours Itself**

The participle s-k-b is the verb of pouring — an intransitive image made passive: water poured out, running, ungarrisoned. Arabic's ordinary Paradise-hydrology is different: rivers "flowing beneath" the gardens, tajrī min taḥtihā, the settled irrigation-phrase of an agrarian afterlife, and the choice of maskūb instead of any form of that phrase shifts the water from canals to cascade. The sūrah's neighbors exploit the same verb for the opposite face of the Day: the seas "poured forth" in the overwhelming day (81:6 — *“and when the seas are set on fire”*). The people of the left will, at verse 42 of this chapter, get "boiling water" in the same family of images (hamīm, a different word — but the chapter keeps water hot where they are, poured where the right are). Verse 31, in other words, is not a stream; it is water in the act of giving itself, three lines after fruit that gave itself off the tree, and the passive participle is the whole verse, as in verse 30, a roofless verb.

**The Flood Inverted**

Arabic's most violent water is also its most nourishing. The Book keeps the two faces together: Noah's drowning was a rain "poured down" in the Samaritan chapter's words — the heavens opened "with water pouring in torrents" (54:11) — and verse 31's maskūb cannot be heard by any trained ear without that resonance: the water that erased a civilization is. The right, poured for drinking and irrigation in a Garden where it can no longer rise. The sūrah's theology of rain elsewhere is explicit — "We send down water in measured amounts and then give it to you to drink, and you are no hoarders of it" (23:18) — and the clause "you are no hoarders" legislates the exact miracle of verse 31: water no cistern can claim. Extended shade overhead, ungoverned water below; the two passive nouns of 30–31 describe a landscape without the two things that made landscape a contest at Makkah — sun and storehouse.

## Verse 56:32
> abundant fruit—

**Never Out of Season: Killing the Calendar**

The completion is verse 33's pair — "never out of season nor forbidden" (33) — and the first negation does the larger work. Maqṭūʿ means cut off, interrupted, and in the date-country it names the one fact every farmer knows: harvest is a season, and the rest of the year is storage and trade. To promise fruit that is "lā maqṭūʿah" is to abolish the calendar for the middle class of the saved — no glut and no famine, no "out of it," and therefore no preservation, no hoarding by the one who has it in February, no price. The Qur'an elsewhere describes this very blessing as the miracle given to Quraysh — the winter and summer journeys that feed the town (106:1–3 — *“secure in their trading caravan ˹to Yemen˺ in the winter and ˹Syria˺ in the summer”*) — and al-Wāqiʿah removes the journeys from the Garden completely, because the journeys existed for what the trees of the right will not need. Time in verse 32 is no longer a ration; it is a season that never ends, and the abundance line is the sūrah's longest answer to short years.

**Nor Forbidden: Killing the Taboo**

The second negation, lā muḥarramah, landed in the audience's ears with a specific clatter. The pre-Islamic sanctity-laws had made whole categories of food "forbidden" by vow and cult — the beasts and crops dedicated away from tables by the saʾiba, the wāʿila and the ḥāmī of the Heifer chapter's catalogue (5:103 — *“Allah has never ordained the ˹so-called˺ baḥîrah, sâ'ibah, waṣîlah, and ḥâm camels. But the disbelievers just fabricate lies about Allah, and most of them lack understanding”*), with their rulings on who could eat what in what months. The verse's muḥarramah is the same root's adjective, and "never forbidden" promises the right-hand people an orchard without cult: fruit owed to no one, sacred to no altar, restricted to no gender, householder, or month. It is the mirror-image of verse 18's cup, which the world's law had made forbidden and whose Garden-version removes the harm instead (19).

**Abundance in the Sūrah's Mouth**

Kathīr, the adjective's root, is the word the Qur'an uses when the world's numbers impress it and disappoint it: the party that said "we are more in number" (54:45, of the defeated host; the claim echoed by the Day-of-Judgment crowds), and the hoard-counting sūrah's rivalry in increase. That is why 32 can end the orchard's middle without ending its argument: the many who eat the many fruits are the many whom the chapter's arithmetic was counting from verse 6, and the sūrah has quietly changed what its own word "multitude" means.

**The Fruit That Looks Familiar and Is Not**

The tafsir reads the abundance of these verses against the Qur'an own description of the Garden's fruit in the Heifer (2:25 — *“Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,” for they will be served fruit that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺”*). The shape will be familiar and the taste will not be. That detail keeps the supply of verse 32 from turning into monotony, which is the one danger an endless orchard would otherwise carry.

Aḥmad recorded a longer exchange from ʿUtbah bin ʿAbd as-Sulamī on the same subject. A bedouin asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ whether Paradise has fruit, and was told that it does, and that it has a tree called Ṭūbā. Asked whether it resembles the trees of his own country, he was told that nothing in his land resembles it, though it is like a tree of ash-Shām called al-Jawzah, standing on one trunk with its branches spread all around. The bedouin asked how large its cluster is, and was told: the distance a crow flies in a month without resting. Asked how thick its trunk is, he was told that a four-year-old she-camel from his own family's herd could travel it and not get around it before her shoulders gave out with age. When he asked about the grapes, the Prophet ﷺ asked whether his father had ever slaughtered a ram and handed the skin to his mother to make into a hanging bucket: one such grape, and it would be enough for the man, his family and his clan.

Two further reports were read beside these verses. In the Two Ṣaḥīḥs the Lote Tree of the utmost boundary is described with leaves like the ears of elephants and fruits like the clay jugs of Ḥajar. And in the Two Ṣaḥīḥs, from Ibn ʿAbbās, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said of the eclipse prayer that he saw Paradise and reached out for a cluster of its fruit — "had I taken it, you would have gone on eating from it for as long as the life of this world lasts."

## Verse 56:33
> never out of season nor forbidden—

**What the Sacred Months Taught the Ear**

"Mutaharram" in the audience's usage was not only food-law; the same root named the months in which war was forbidden (9:5 — *“But once the Sacred Months have passed, kill the polytheists ˹who violated their treaties˺ wherever you find them, capture them, besiege them, and lie in wait for them on every way. But if they repent, perform prayers, and pay alms-tax, then set them free. Indeed, Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”*; 2:194 — *“˹There will be retaliation in˺ a sacred month for ˹an offence in˺ a sacred month, and all violations will bring about retaliation. So, if anyone attacks you, retaliate in the same manner. ˹But˺ be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is with those mindful ˹of Him˺”*) — a truce imposed on time itself, when roads were safe and markets held their fairs. That resonance means verse 33's "nor forbidden" is not simply the abolition of taboo but the generalization of the truce: the Garden is the sacred month that never exits the calendar, a place where the rules that made eating an occasion for peace or for priestly veto have been universalized into permanent permission. If the pre-Islamic sanctities fenced food by cult, and the months fenced travel by treaty, the right-hand orchard is the state in which both fences run out of business: nothing is barred, nowhere is unsafe. The sūrah that borrowed the "people of the right hand" phrase from ancient judgment-lore finishes by borrowing the oldest Arab institution of protected time and extending it without end.

The clause is built the way verse 19 built the cup: lā plus the first noun, wa-lā plus the second, a double denial on indefinite forms that shuts down two whole categories rather than two particulars. The parallel is close at hand in the chapter of The Raising of Hands, where the rewarded are told their fruit "will hang within reach" (69:23) — fruit that cannot be denied because it cannot be kept from the hand — and the sūrahs of the Mercy and the Event keep the same formulaic pair of negations for the Garden's produce: the two words always travel together because together they exhaust what scarcity is.

**Joseph's Seven Years, Answered**

The Book's most exact scene of "out of season" sits in Joseph, where Egypt stores grain through seven lean years after seven fat, and famine is the plot's hinge (12:47–49 — *“Joseph replied, “You will plant ˹grain˺ for seven consecutive years, leaving in the ear whatever you will harvest, except for the little you will eat”*). Every hearer of al-Wāqiʿah lived, in the market's price of a measure of wheat, the memory of a season that cut food off, and lā maqṭūʿah speaks first to the stomach's calendar — the fruit never interrupted — before it speaks to law. The verse's genius is that it requires no miracle to be understood: no reader in the room missed that a garden without a lean month is a garden without a king, without granary-policy, without the rich buying up the year. Joseph's storehouse existed because seasons did; cancel the seasons and the whole apparatus of who eats when dissolves by itself.

**A Note on the Verse's Afterlife**

The pair of negations outlived the sūrah in Islamic law's own debates about the Garden: the jurists asked, reading 33 against the Heifer's food-rules, whether any ruling of the world — the prohibitions of carrion, blood. Swine flesh (2:173 — *“He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah.”*) — has a mirror in Paradise, and the standard answer took this verse as its proof-text: the Garden abrogates prohibition itself, not its permissibility-by-exception that the world knows, where what is barred is licensed only in hunger. Verse 33 so became, in the commentaries, the locus classicus for a theology of law's end: the saved majority eats with no mufti between them and the tree. The sūrah, which had spent its courtroom half attacking tabus of cult (5:103's saʾiba was the audience's own inventory — *“Allah has never ordained the ˹so-called˺ baḥîrah, sâ'ibah, waṣîlah, and ḥâm camels. But the disbelievers just fabricate lies about Allah, and most of them lack understanding”*), closes its orchard by unhallowing hallowing. "Never forbidden" is the shortest sentence in the chapter for the shortest definition of the hereafter the Qur'an ever gives: a place where the only sacred is the safe.

**Nothing in the Way, and a Replacement at Once**

Qatādah's gloss on the second clause is practical: nothing will keep them from the fruit — no branch, no thorn, no distance. The tafsir adds a report it had cited earlier in the same passage: whenever someone takes a fruit in Paradise, another grows in its place. That is the mechanism behind "never cut off." The supply is not a stock being drawn down but a tree that keeps answering.

## Verse 56:34
> and elevated furnishings

**Furush: The Bed Before It Was Furniture**

The noun furush, plural of firāsh, is the Qur'an's own word for the earth as mattress — the verse of Ta-Ha praises the Lord "who made for you the earth a bed (firāshan) and the sky a canopy" (20:53), and this sūrah's opening cataclysm takes that bed away under the feet (4). What the chapter does at 34, accordingly, is not add a piece to an inventory but restore the primal good: the firm thing you lie on, back to being spread, and this time raised (marfūʿah), safe from the shake that unmade mountains. The root's domestic range is telling — firāsh is bedding, the saddle-cushion under the rider, anything spread to receive a body's weight — and the Garden's "elevated furnishings" keeps all of it: the saddle's rest after travel, the mattress after the day's work, the marriage-bed after the long betrothal. Al-Wāqiʿah gives the middle saved class not couches of state but the whole vocabulary of being received by something solid that stays solid.

**High, Soft, and Comfortable**

The gloss on the raised couches is three adjectives: high, soft and comfortable. The tafsir's note on the verses that follow explains why the beds are named before anyone is said to be on them — the women of these couches are not mentioned by name at all, and the sentence about creating them a special creation (35) has to reach back for its subject. The beds of verse 34 are the hinge: the furniture is described, and the people who will be on it are understood.

## Verse 56:35
> Indeed, We will have perfectly created their mates

**The First-Person Plural at the Center of the Orchard**

After seven lines of passive, agentless nouns — stacked, stretched, poured, raised — the verse breaks into "Indeed, We created": the chapter's only direct "We" between the opening's "We" of 57 and the argument's, and the royal plural lands on the softest subject in the passage, the spouses. The switch of voice is the sūrah's own commentary on 28–34: the trees and beds were described as if self-assembling because that is how guests receive hospitality — nobody sees the host's hands — and when the text finally shows the hands, it shows them for the marriage. The emphatic inna and the perfect verb together answer the audience's probable question about the source of all this: the list has a maker, the maker is introducing Himself at the moment He introduces the intimate. The sūrah so binds, in one sentence, its theology of creation to its promise of companions — the same bind that the argument-half (57) will use for the first creation's proof.

**"Their Mates": The Possessive That Keeps Marriages**

The pronoun attaches the wives to the husbands as property of relation, azwājuhum in the phrase of the Book (2:25 — *“Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow. Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,” for they will be served fruit that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺. They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever”*; 23:5 — *“those who guard their chastity”*) — "their spouses," the same possessive that the Thunder chapter uses when the believers enter the Gardens "and the righteous among their fathers, spouses, and descendants" (13:23). That continuity is a doctrine: the sūrah of the Event does not dissolve the married into the angelic, and the middle class of the saved are promised, not replacement companions, but their own — newly made. The promise of 13:23 — *“the Gardens of Eternity, which they will enter along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants.”*'s family-reunion is not quoted here, but its logic stands behind the possessive; the audience who buried spouses in Makkah heard, at the center of an orchard-promise, a pronoun that does not let death file the marriage. Commentators noted the verse's tact: the near-rank got companions described by beauty, the right by making — the middle estate, whose marriages were worn with age and loss, receives the text's one explicit note of re-creation, as if to say: the love you knew is the love that will be, grown again from the seed of what God first sprouted (56:57–58 — *“It is We Who created you. Will you not then believe ˹in resurrection˺ Have you considered what you ejaculate”*).

**Brought Back After Age**

The gloss on the special creation answers a question the verse does not raise: these are women who grew old in this world and are brought back in the other as virgins, youthful, loving toward their husbands, beautiful, kind and cheerful. The re-creation of verse 35 is therefore not the manufacture of strangers. It is the same persons restored, which is what the possessive of "their mates" at the head of the verse was already implying.

## Verse 56:36
> making them virgins

**Aṭhār: The Word, Its Range, and Its Grammar**

The noun is abkār, plural of ʿadhrāʾ, and the sūrah's sentence puts it in the accusative as a state (ḥāl): "We made them" — in what condition? "virgins." The grammar matters for how the verse should be read: the word describes the manner of God's making, not a status imposed on persons with histories, since the sentence's subject is the Creator who, at 35, sprouted the spouses anew. The present line's single word carries a whole aesthetic of firstness, and al-Wāqiʿah applies it, in the middle estate's passage, to the most intimate of the saved goods — the firstness, the chapter's orchard implies, of a condition that God's fresh making alone can give (35).

**The All-Merciful's Verse Beside This One**

The chapter of Mercy, the sūrah that stands before al-Wāqiʿah in the codex and shares its reward-lists, describes the Garden's companions with a formula close to this verse's: "maidens of modest gaze, who no human or jinn has ever touched before" (55:56; 55:74 — *“No human or jinn has ever touched these ˹maidens˺ before”*), the negative of mass (touching) repeated as pure fact." The pair of chapters is instructive, because Mercy's formula adds what our verse suppresses — the prior husband — and adds the gaze, the contentedness — while the Event keeps only the bare state, abkār. Between the two, the tradition's question surfaces: are these new women other than the wives of the world, and who, if remarried, is hers forever?

**The Strength Given with the Garden**

Aṭ-Ṭayālisī recorded from Anas that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said the believer in Paradise will be given such-and-such strength for women; Anas asked whether anyone could manage that, and the answer was the strength of a hundred men. At-Tirmidhī collected the report as well and called it ṣaḥīḥ gharīb. Aṭ-Ṭabarānī carries a parallel from Abū Hurayrah in which the question is asked outright — will we be with our wives in Paradise? — and the answer is that a man will be able to be with a hundred virgins in a day; al-Ḥāfiẓ Abū ʿAbdullāh al-Maqdisī judged that report to meet the conditions of the ṣaḥīḥ. Both were read against this verse because its single word is what raises the question they answer.

## Verse 56:37
> loving and of equal age

**ʿUruban: The Noun That Means Being In Love**

The first adjective is ʿurub (plural ʿuruban, as here) or ʿarīb. The old lexicography — the lexica preserve it carefully — glosses it as the woman inflamed in love for her husband, ʿāshiqah in plain terms, the same root giving the verb for a wine that "goes to the head." Al-Wāqiʿah therefore places, between "virgins" and "of equal age," the one word in the orchard-passage that names a feeling rather than a property. The sūrahs' most repeated note on it is this: the adjective is mutual in the real case, since the spouses' list began at 35 with "their mates" — a possessive of reciprocity — and ʿuruban supplies the interior the sūrah will not describe elsewhere even for the near-rank. The All-Merciful sūrah, whose companions are "of modest gaze, who no human or jinn has ever touched before" (55:56), supplies the other half of the psychology: restraint toward all else, flame toward the one. This is the sūrah's only love-word in its two Paradise portraits, and it sits on the middle class's spouses. The line drew the question the sūrah could not avoid provoking: if the saved are re-mated, whom does a remarried widow belong to in the Garden? The verse's equal-aged mutuality was read, by the commentators with the widows in mind, as the end of rivalry for a place, not the reassignment of it.

**Why Love Is in the List at All**

A final observation about the sūrah's place in the Book. Al-Wāqiʿah is recited in the tradition for provision and in the madhhabs for the dying, and its Paradise has — uniquely among the late Meccan pairs of chapters like it — a word of feeling in the reward-list. The Ranks sūrah describes its companions by the eye (37:48 — modest gaze — *“And with them will be maidens of modest gaze and gorgeous eyes”*), the Rising by faces (75:22–23 — bright, looking at their Lord — *“On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright looking at their Lord”*), the chapter of Man by drink and garments. Here the middle estate's passage closes on "loving." Whatever the codex's arrangement was meant to teach, the reciter who reaches verse 37 at a bedside finds the sūrah promising, to the family of ordinary believers, not scenery but the re-found tenderness itself, and equal footing for its exercise.

**Thirty-Three, and of One Age**

The glosses on the two adjectives are specific. Saʿīd bin Jubayr reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that the first means women infatuated with their husbands — "have you not seen a she-camel in heat? She is like that" — and ad-Ḍaḥḥāk reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that these women and their husbands are each other's sweethearts. The same sense comes from ʿAbdullāh bin Sārijis, Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, Abū al-ʿĀliyah, Yaḥyā bin Abī Kathīr, ʿAtiyyah, al-Ḥasan, Qatādah and ad-Ḍaḥḥāk. On "of equal age," ad-Ḍaḥḥāk reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that they will be of one age, thirty-three years old; Mujāhid said equal, and in another report similar; ʿAtiyyah said comparable.

The equal age is the verse's own answer to the worry the commentators raised at the end of this section. Where nobody is older than anybody else, nobody is ranked by age.

## Verse 56:38
> for the people of the right

**The Preposition That Dedicates the Orchard**

Li, the little dative, opens this verse — "for the people of the right" — and after ten lines of passive scenery it is the sentence in which the sūrah finally tells the reader who the scenery was made for, even though the answer has been announced since 27. The preposition of benefit and assignment does work the audience understood intimately: in the gift-language of the Arabs, "this is for you" is what turns an object into a given. The sūrah now says it, formally, of the whole estate: the mates with their three epithets (36–37), the beds, the water, the thornless grove — all of it, for them. The bracketing of the present translation ("˹They will be˺" back at 28) anticipated the pronoun; the Arabic saves the dedication until now, so that the list stands between announcement and assignment, and the reader's question "who gets all this?" is answered exactly once, at the end of the answer. That is not redundancy; that is how hospitality is staged.

**"A Promise Made in Public: The Grammar of Assignment**

The translators print 38 as a fragment attached to what precedes, and the Arabic is exactly that exposed: no verb governs the phrase — the list simply turns toward its owners. Exegetes asked why the sūrah does not say "We gave this to them," and the best answer is that the bare dative keeps the giving continuous: a past-tense gift would locate the reward in one act. "For them" states a permanent relation — the estate is theirs the way a promised dower is owed, without expiry. It is also, once noticed, the grammar of the whole chapter's hope: the saved are those for whom things are said — "for those who fear the Standing" is the All-Merciful sūrah's refrain (e.g., 55:46, 62, whose two gardens are both "for" someone). Al-Wāqiʿah, recited beside the same deathbeds, places its longest "for them" here, in the middle estate's dedicatory clause, where the ordinary believer, who is not the near one, learns the sentence is addressed to them.

**Created for Them: Ibn Jarīr's Reading**

The tafsir takes the little dative as an assignment: these women were created, or kept, or given in marriage for the people of the right — the view of Ibn Jarīr, who reads verses 35 to 38 as one sentence whose last word is the beneficiary. The alternative is narrower: that the phrase belongs to the adjective before it, so that it describes their being of equal age for the people of the right. On the first reading the dedication covers the whole orchard; on the second it covers one quality of its companions. The verse leaves both open, and the wider reading is the one this section has followed.

## Verse 56:39
> ˹who will be˺ a multitude from earlier generations

**The Census Returns, and Changes Its Noun**

The chapter's counting device comes back for the second time, and the nouns are different in a way the translations hide at their edges. The near-rank were "a swarm from earlier generations" and "a few from later" (13–14), the swarm-word laḥq and the few-word qalīl; the right are kathrah, a multitude, "from earlier generations" and again "from later" (39–40) — the same min-partitive, the same paired halves, now filled with the opposite magnitude. Al-Wāqiʿah so teaches its audience to read the census as a scale: the reader who heard 13–14 and flinched ("only the few?") arrives at 39 and is handed the many. The bracketed "˹who will be˺" is the translator's patch over the Arabic's ellipse — the line is a bare accusative of state — and the bareness is the style of these lists: classes as quantities, quantities as mercy, the arithmetic done by God.

**The Multitude of the Ancient Peoples**

"Earlier generations" — the same phrase that carried the near-rank's swarm — now does new work, because the right's multitude from the old nations is, in the sūrah's own theology of history, the crowd the old prophets actually saved. The near-rank's "swarm from the former" named the champions of each age; the right's multitude is the congregations: the followers of Noah, the rank and file of Abraham's household, the ordinary Israelites of the Exodus, the believing remnant of every destroyed city. The Qur'an elsewhere warns that "most of people" will not believe (12:103 — *“And most people will not believe—no matter how keen you are”*). The sūrah's own courtroom half will call the left "the misguided deniers" (51) — but nowhere does this chapter quote a count of the damned against a hope of the saved, and verse 39's multitude from the former is the quiet surprise that balances the census.

**Why Repetition Preaches Better Than Novelty**

The chapter could have described the right's numbers with new language, and chose instead to re-run the device of 13–14 with the adjective inverted. That is repetition with a purpose the audience of a recited text feels: the second census lands faster, because the first taught the grammar, and the inversion is felt as a gift — the mechanism that said "few" for the heroes says "many" for you. The census even shares its halves — former and later — with the Day's whole gathering at 49–50 ("Most certainly, earlier and later generations will surely be gathered ˹together˺ for the appointed Day" (56:49–50)), so that the saved classes' counts are cut from the same cloth as the total roll-call.

**Multitude as Mercy, Not Majority Argument**

One caution the verse's defenders should keep: "a multitude" is not, in this chapter, an appeal to numbers as proof. The Qur'an's own charge against the crowds is sharp — "if you obey most of those on earth, they will lead you away from God's path" (the sense of 6:116 — *“˹O Prophet!˺ If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah’s Way. They follow nothing but assumptions and do nothing but lie”*) — and al-Wāqiʿah has just finished telling its hearers that the majority of history sat with the left (51–52 will say so in so many words). Verse 39's comfort is therefore not "most people make it". It is that the saved middle is no elite club, that belonging to a multitude, not a spiritual aristocracy, is the normal shape of deliverance, and that the same warning chapters which say "few are My grateful servants" (34:13) here say the right-hand crowd is vast.

**The First Company, Described**

Al-Bukhārī and Muslim recorded from Abū Hurayrah the description of that first company: they will enter Paradise shining like the moon when it is full, and those after them like the brightest star in the sky; they will not urinate, relieve themselves, spit or blow their noses; their combs will be of gold, their sweat musk, and their censers aloeswood; their wives will be of the ḥūr al-ʿayn; and their form will be one form, the form of their father Ādam, sixty cubits tall. The multitude of verse 39 is a crowd of people who look alike because nothing about them is left to compete over.

## Verse 56:40
> and a multitude from later generations

**The Second Half of the Pair**

Verse 39's multitude from the earlier generations leans on this line the way the near-rank's "swarm" (13) leaned on their "few" (14): the census is one statement with a hinge, and the hinge opens toward the audience. "Later generations" — al-ākhirūn — is the sūrah's own term for you: not the centuries of the prophets whose ruins the Makkān trade routes passed, but the communities the chapter's recitation would itself create. The sūrah's grammar keeps the later half in the accusative of state, a bare phrase appended to the scene. The effect is a sentence the reader finishes by existing in it: every congregation that ever heard al-Wāqiʿah was, at the moment of hearing, a candidate for the multitude its final census line reserves. The text's temporality is a loop — a book about the end of time whose verse about "later" includes all readers to the Hour.

**What the Word "Kathrah" Has Learned to Mean**

The multitude here answers the chapter's own use of abundance since verse 32, where fruit's adjective was kathīrah, "abundant," and it completes the quiet re-education of the sūrah's favorite root. In the audience's life, kathrah was competitive — the rivalry in increase named by the short sūrah "You are diverted by rivalry in increase, until you visit the graves" (102:1–2), the counting of numbers as a civic sport. Al-Wāqiʿah now attaches the same word to a census of the saved (39–40) and to fruit (32) — multitude without rivalry, quantity that is received, not racked up — and verse 40 is the human end of that lesson: the later generations arrive as many. Their "many" cannot be credited to anyone's strategy, because the census is spoken by God before the Day. The verse's one-word adjective, repeated from 32, is the sūrah's way of converting the community's vice into its comfort.

**The Prophets Shown, and the Seventy Thousand**

Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded from ʿAbdullāh bin Masʿūd the night the prophets were displayed to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ: a prophet passed with a company of men, another with three, and another with nobody at all — and Qatādah, one of the report's narrators, recited at that point the line from Hūd, "is there not among you a single right-minded man?" (11:78 — *“Is there not ˹even˺ a single right-minded man among you?”*). Then Mūsā passed with a great crowd from the Children of Israel. The Prophet ﷺ was told to look to his right on the hill, and to the horizon on his left, and at each he was asked whether he was pleased, and said that he was; and with them, he was told, are seventy thousand who will enter Paradise without reckoning.

ʿUkkāshah bin Miḥṣan of Banī Asad, a man of Badr, asked to be made one of them, and the Prophet ﷺ prayed that he would be. A second man asked for the same and was told that ʿUkkāshah had got there first. Then came the arithmetic that belongs to this verse: "I hope that you will be a quarter of the people of Paradise," and the Companions said Allāhu akbar; "a third," and they said it again; "half," and they said it again — and the Messenger of Allah ﷺ then recited these two verses, a multitude from the earlier generations and a multitude from the later. Asked afterwards who the seventy thousand were, the Companions guessed that they were those born into Islam who never associated anything with God; when their guess reached him he gave the real definition: those who are not cauterised, who do not ask for ruqyah, and who take no omens, but put their trust in their Lord. The report has many chains, in the Ṣaḥīḥs and elsewhere.

## Verse 56:41
> And the people of the left—how ˹miserable˺ will they be

**The Third Exclamation, and the Last**

The chapter now pays the full price of its own rhetoric: the exclamation that opened at verses 8–9 — "the people of the right, how ˹blessed˺ will they be. The people of the left, how ˹miserable˺ will they be" (56:8–9) — and re-run at 27 for the blessed, re-runs for the lost, and the third iteration is the last time the sūrah will use its signature device. The repetition has by now taught the audience to lean forward at the formula. The lean is the sermon: the saved passages rewarded the expectation with orchards and companions, and verse 41 sets up the identical musical figure to do the opposite — the listener's body learns the pattern as comfort, and the sūrah strikes it as loss. That is the moral beauty of the verse's placement: the sūrahs of the commentators, long accustomed to this sūrah's repetitions, note how often re-echoed structures soften the ear before the blow falls, and here the softening is the sermon.

**"How Miserable": The Absence That Replaces the Answer**

Verse 27's withheld predicate, "how ˹blessed˺," was filled by the readers with lote and shade. 41's ˹miserable˺ will be filled by scorching wind, boiling water, smoke — but the sūrah's design keeps both classes, in their intro-lines, unspeakable: the saved are "too blessed to say," the lost "too miserable to name," and the identical grammar for both refuses the audience a voyeur's advantage in either direction. The present translation's brackets make the refusal uniform — two adjectives, one for each — and the verse's real teaching is in that uniformity: on the Day there will be classes whose condition outruns the chapter's own vocabulary on both sides. Al-Wāqiʿah, which has made its entire argument out of what the solid world cannot be trusted for, says here what it says in 56:74 — *“So glorify the Name of your Lord, the Greatest”*: God makes things, and the verdicts are His, and the human words for them (blessed, miserable) are bracketed inserts doing their best.

"Left-hand people" completes the phrase this commentary traced at verse 8: the Book of Matthew places the goats at the left hand as the sheep at the right (25:33 — *“Whenever they bring you an argument, We come to you with the right refutation and the best explanation”*), the ancient Near East knew the left as the inauspicious side (the left hand's eating and cleaning duties, the right hand's covenant-feasts). The Arabic mashʾama, from sh-m-l — the shmal, the north wind's cold and leftward side — is already unlucky in the pre-Islamic poet's mouth.

## Verse 56:42
> ˹They will be˺ in scorching heat and boiling water

**Samūm: The Wind That Is a Verdict**

The word rendered "scorching heat" here is samūm in the Arabic — the wind of piercing, from a root of burning through and through, the sirocco that the audience fled at midday and that their poets made the counter-shade to the garden's. The sūrah's punishment is therefore not invented weather; it is the region's worst day raised to an estate, and the selection tells you everything about al-Wāqiʿah's method of hell: it subtracts from the known, it does not add to it. The near-rank's delights were their goods perfected — cups with no headache, beds elevated, shade extended — and the left's torments are their miseries, kept exactly. Verse 42's "scorching wind" is also, in the chapter's system, the answer to verse 30: the right are in stretched shade; the left are in moving air with no relief in it, and wind in a desert is, precisely, what shade is not.

**Hamīm: The Water That Cannot Be Water**

The second noun, maʾ, boiling water, ḥamīm, is the Book's fixed word for the drink of the lost. The wider Qur'an supplies its exact profile so consistently that this verse needs no adjectives of its own: the Cave sūrah's parallel has them cry for relief and be answered with water like molten brass that sears the face, an evil drink and an evil companion (18:29 — *“When they cry for aid, they will be aided with water like molten metal, which will burn ˹their˺ faces.”*) — and the Muhammad sūrah's rivers-passage ends by placing, opposite the four promised rivers, "water that tears their insides" (47:15, quoted above at this chapter's cup-section). Al-Wāqiʿah sets its own poured water — flowing, ungoverned, free — at verse 31. That the two "waters" of the chapter, thirty verses apart, are the same noun family's two ends: maskūb poured for the right, ḥamīm boiling for the left, and the sūrah's hydrology is its theology of what a gift becomes when the Giver is refused. The translator's bracket ˹They will be˺ supplies, again, the bare locative: in these two elements, the verse says; they live inside weather that will not nourish.

**Three Words, Three Glosses**

The glosses on the three nouns are short and they agree. Samūm is a fierce hot wind. Hamīm is boiling water. Yaḥmūm is the shadow of smoke — reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, Abū Ṣāliḥ, Qatādah and as-Suddī. Nothing in this inventory is invented weather; it is the region's worst day named in the region's own words, and the next verse takes the third noun apart.

## Verse 56:43
> in the shade of black smoke

**ʿIhab: The Word for a Sky That Has Caught Fire**

The noun ʿihāb carries, in Arabic's usage, both the leaping column of fire from a blaze and the dense smoke rising over it — the beacon the Bedouin read at distance, smoke meaning household or danger. The line itself says ẓill, "shadow," attached to the smoke. The old glosses read the sūrah's companion-word yaḥmūm — which the Cliff chapter uses for scalding water: "except boiling water and ˹oozing˺ pus" (78:25) — as black because intensely hot. The present translation prints the color it infers, "in the shade of black smoke." Within our chapter, the domestic sense — smoke above a fire that burns — makes the punishment's skyline exact: the lost stand in the shadow of their own cooking-fires. The near-rank sit beside water poured for them (31). The sūrah that denied the world any lasting solidity has, at minimum, given every class a skyline; the left's is made of what burning leaves behind, low enough to press on the head. And the "shade" here is, of course, verse 30's word minus its properties; verse 44 exists to do the subtracting.

**Blackness: The Sūrah's Palette**

Al-Wāqiʿah has kept its colors sparse, and the few it uses cluster here at the end of the description. The face of the near-rank was bright in the neighbors' Paradise (75:22–3, "On that Day some faces will be bright, looking at their Lord" — the chapter's own counter-light, quoted earlier at verse 16). The left's sky is yaḥmūm-adjacent black, the smoke's color; between the two the sūrah painted fire's whiteness ("crystal-white" is the Ranks' wine, 37:46 — *“crystal-white, delicious to drink”*, this chapter's neighbor) and the green of brocade nowhere. The palette is so moral, not decorative: light and color in this chapter belong to description, black to the undescribed, and the smoke at 43 is the sūrah's only painted darkness — the shadow it throws is, the next verse says, unqualified by anything that would make it weather. The chapter that opened by pulverizing the mountains' color into "scattered dust" (6) ends its two estates with a grove (28) and a soot-cloud (43), and the two images are the whole the teaching about the Last Day it offers: growth or smoke.

**The Shade in Three Columns**

The sūrah of the Sent Forth describes the same destination in the same three moves, and its address is aimed at the denial this chapter opened with: the disbelievers are told to (77:30 — *“Proceed into the shade ˹of smoke˺ which rises in three columns”*), and the verse after it denies that shade its coolness and its shelter in the words verse 44 uses here. Al-Wāqiʿah gives the same weather without the columns; the Sent Forth gives the columns and the command.

## Verse 56:44
> neither cool nor refreshing

**The Verse That Unbuilds a Word**

Ẓalla means to shade, and what it provides is two things the audience's proverbs never failed to enumerate: coolness (barad — "the coolest of shades," as the all-Merciful sūrah describes the Garden's best ẓill, 55:56 — *“In both ˹Gardens˺ will be maidens of modest gaze, who no human or jinn has ever touched before”*) and benefit. Verse 44 denies both of the smoke's shadow, and the mechanism is the sūrah's signature: the left receive the word for relief minus the contents of relief, a shade that is neither cool nor useful. The two negated adjectives, barīd and karīm, are classical and debated — barīd is the cool a shade strikes the body with, karīm the pleasant, the noble, the worth having — and whichever etymology the reader prefers, the sense is single: the smoke-shade is a form of blessing preserved as a museum of blessing, everything it used to do for the body canceled. The cancellation stated in two adjectives at the end of the estate's entire description.

**"Refresh": What the Water and the Shade Were For**

Nafʿ, benefit, has been the sūrah's hidden standard since its first description. The right's shade is extended (30), the near-rank's cups do not harm (19), the fruit is never cut off (33) — every amenity in this chapter exists for its use-value, stated in passive participles that promise the body will find them. The left's smoke-shade is the only "place" in the chapter whose properties are enumerated as absences. The absent quality, benefit, belongs to the commercial vocabulary of profit — the same root the sūrah of the Iron spends its half on, the trade that does not lose its goods (35:29; 61:10-11, "a commerce that will save you"), the nafʿ that hoarded wealth never bought for its owner. In al-Wāqiʿah's economy the damned are not tortured so much as disinvested: the elements that profit everyone else are, for them, held out at zero yield, and "neither cool nor refreshing" is the ledger-line of an estate in which nothing pays.

**The Small Word That Ends the Scene**

The last noun of the left's first inventory is "benefit," and that is a ledger-word — the sūrah's whole introduction has been about gain and loss ever since it distinguished those handed their record in the right hand, who face "a reckoning, easy" (84:7, quoted at this chapter's own verse-8 commentary), from everyone else. Verse 44 closes the left's scenery on the idea of benefit and then the chapter, remarkably, refuses to escalate: the mockery (47–48), the gathering (49–50), the zaqqūm menu (51–55), the accommodation (56) all swim inside a condition defined twice as unprofitable, and the punishment is not enlarged, only witnessed.

**Neither Sun-Blaze Nor Bitter Cold: The Chiasmus**

The chapter of Man sets the Garden's climate in the mirror of this verse: in the Garden "you will see neither scorching heat nor bitter cold" (76:13) — the saved lack both extremes, because the shade is balanced. The lost have neither the sun's harm nor the cold's harm, but equally neither the sun's rising nor the cold's relief, because their medium is the in-between that smoke makes. Al-Wāqiʿah's verse 44, ending on "nor refreshing," so states the classical definition of hell in this literature: climate without season, exposure without end, a sky that neither rules nor serves.

**Karīm: the Glosses on the Second Negation**

Al-Ḥasan and Qatādah read "nor refreshing" as a statement about appearance: its sight is not pleasant. Ad-Ḍaḥḥāk put it in the register of drink: every drink that is not fresh is not generous. The tafsir's summary of the pair is that this shadow brings no soft, cool breeze and shows no clarity — the two properties shade exists to have.

## Verse 56:45
> Indeed, before this ˹torment˺ they were spoiled by luxury

**Mutraf: The Passive Man of Pleasure**

The Arabic's word for the luxury here is takrafuf, the verb ismutrafa — "made soft, dandled, put at ease" — and the morphology is the whole sermon: the damned are not described as people who seized pleasure but as people who were laid into it, formed by it, the passive object of a life of ease, which is how this Book's political vocabulary habitually reads the rich of the old cities. The prophets of the Qur'an hear the phrase before this sūrah: when a messenger summons a town's assembly, the same title — "the eminent" — speaks from prosperity in the accounts of Nūḥ, Hūd. Ṣāliḥ (e.g., 11:27), and the sūrah of the Heights says that God takes such people "by degrees, from ways they do not perceive" (7:182). That class-worried phrase stands behind verse 45, and when al-Wāqiʿah calls the left "mutrafūn in life," it therefore charges them with a condition, not a hobby: a world arranged so that nothing ever asked anything of them, and a self arranged by that world to find asking unbearable.

**Why "Indeed" Opens the Verse**

The near-rank's opposite property is not austerity but vigilance: "men whom neither trade nor sale diverts from the remembrance of God" (24:37, whose commerce clause this chapter's own verse 25 answers in kind) — and verse 45's mutrafūn are that verse inverted: people diverted by nothing, because everything already belonged to them.

## Verse 56:46
> and persisted in the worst of sin

**Istihlāʾ: The Verb of Keeping a Claim Alive**

The second charge's verb is stronger than "persisted" — its root carries pressing, insisting, holding on to a claim, and the sūrah's construction — an imperfect held inside a past plural — makes the sin a posture maintained across time, not a single act. That fits the chapter's entire psychology: verse 45's spoilage was a condition, verse 46's persistence is a decision made again and again under that condition. The two kānū-lines together are the sūrah's complete anatomy of hardening — circumstance, then repetition — the same grammar the saved were given at 24 ("what they used to do") with the sign reversed. The sūrah has no concept of an eternal punishment that is not also a persistent will; verse 46 is where that conviction sits, in one Form VIII imperfect.

**"The Worst of Sin": Which Sin Is Meant?**

Classical readers asked what al-ithm al-ʿaẓīm names here and answered from the sūrah's own sequence: the immediate context supplies mockery of resurrection at 47–48, and the chapter's opening supplied denial of the Day at 2 (the Day of Recompense whose truth "no one can deny" when it arrives). Many sūrahs preferred the widest reading — the unbelief that the Qur'an elsewhere calls the one sin God does not "overlook," the shirk the Book asserts directly as a wrong of greatness (31:13, Luqmān's charge to his son — *“And ˹remember˺ when Luqmân said to his son, while advising him, “O my dear son! Never associate ˹anything˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺, for associating ˹others with Him˺ is truly the worst of all wrongs.”*) — and the "worst" here (al-ʿaẓīm) is the superlative's plain work: within every sin this audience knew, the one that arranges all others against God. The translation's "worst of sin" captures the Arabic's definite-plus-superlative exactly; and the sūrah's strategy lets both readings stand: the persisted-in denial is, in the chapter's own file, at once the resurrection-joke and the theology that made the joke possible.

**ʿAẓīm: The Adjective Shared with the News**

The word "great" is not idle in this sūrah's world. The Qur'an's other late chapter calls resurrection "the tremendous News (anbaʾ al-ʿaẓīm) about which people disagree" (78:2–3). The sūrah's own last verse puts the superlative on God as its final verb: "So glorify the Name of your Lord, the Greatest." (56:96) The sūrah so builds a small ladder with one word: the News is great, the sin of mocking it is "the great sin," and the Lord whose name closes the chapter is the Majestic — and the left, in verse 46, are the people who attached ʿaẓīm to the wrong rung: they held fast to what is small in the face of what is not.

**The Sin Named, and the Repentance Not Intended**

Two glosses fill in the charge. On "they persisted," the tafsir reads that they went on and did not intend to repent. On "the worst of sin," Ibn ʿAbbās identified it as idolatry — disbelief in God together with the claim that idols and rivals were gods beside Him — and the same identification is reported from Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah, ad-Ḍaḥḥāk, Qatādah and as-Suddī. The verse holds both halves together: a sin persisted in is a decision, and the decision named here is the one that arranges all the others.

## Verse 56:47
> They used to ask ˹mockingly˺, “When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected

**Dust and Bones: The Sūrah's Own Cosmology Turned on It**

The objection's materials are the chapter's: "When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones" borrows — the sūrah's audience would have caught the exact phrasing — the vocabulary of 4–6, where the mountains, crushed, "became scattered dust." The mockers speak as if pulverization proved impossibility: we will come apart into turāb and ʿiẓām, and who reassembles sawdust? The chapter has already answered at the level of physics: the same "becoming dust" they cite as their defense is, in the sūrah's telling, the Day's own opening movement — disassembly is not the denial of resurrection but its schedule. The God who flattens mountains (5) does it to re-compose, which is why verse 57 opens the argument-half with "It is We Who created you. Will you not then believe?" The quotation at 47 and the cataclysm at 4 are so one argument in two moods, the sūrah's audience able to hear the skeptics' dust as the Event's dust without a word of commentary.

**"The First [Life]" — What the Arabic's Question Actually Duplicates**

Readers of the English should know that the sūrah's line contains a word the present translation folds into "resurrected": the baʿth, sending forth, raising. The object of the wonder — "will we really be sent forth?" — is the same verb the chapter uses for recreation at 61 ("We may transform you and bring you forth (yunshiʾkum) in a creation you do not know"). The mockers' noun is not resurrection as doctrine but resurrection as the word in the prophet's mouth, thrown back doubled. And the sūrah's placement makes the exchange grammatically exact: verse 47's interrogative (a-inna) is answered at 49 with the plain "qul: inna al-awwalīna wal-ākhiriina la-jāmiʿūn" — "Say: most certainly, earlier and later generations will surely be gathered" (56:49–50) — the chapter matching their questioning particles with a command to answer by decree, the two words (baʿth/jamʿ) the only religious vocabulary the sūrah chooses to concede them.

**The Ranks Sūrah's Identical Line**

Late sūrahs record the same objection again and again — 17:49, 25:15, 36:78, 37:16, 79:23 among them — and the closest in form and music is the Ranks sūrah's: "What! When we are dead and become dust and bones, will we really be resurrected? And our forefathers as well?" — in the present translation, essentially identical to ours — so that a listener moving between the two chapters heard one fixed quotation, a liturgical refrain of the skeptical, preserved verbatim. That the Qur'an reuses the sentence shows how real and how tired the objection was. How the sūrah's answer differs by chapter: the Ranks answers with the single command "qul: al-awwalūna wal-ākhirona la-jāmiʿūna ilā mīqādi yawmin maʿlūm" — the same gathering-clause our sūrah borrows — while al-Wāqiʿah, at 57–74, adds its four interrogatives of semen, seed, water, and fire. The refrain belongs to the audience's history; the answers belong to the hour of each sūrah.

## Verse 56:48
> And our forefathers as well?”

**One Line, Its Own Verse: The Art of the Follow-Up Sneer**

The sūrah gives the left's second breath its full verse-mark — wa-ābāʾunal-awwalūn — five words, an addendum, and in its brevity the most vivid line of their quotation. Grammatically it is not a new sentence but a tacked-on dative: "will we really be raised — and our forefathers as well?" The incredulity is all in the "and also": the first question mocked the individual's return, the addendum mocks the logistics, the sheer implausible scope — that the buried centuries, the ancestors of the audience's own pride, would have to be recalled too. And the sūrah's placement — immediately before "Say: most certainly, the earlier and the later will be gathered" (56:49–50) — answers the addendum first: the forefathers are in, the scope is exactly the objection, and the answer is one breath long.

**The Forefathers: Makkah's Strongest Noun**

To the first audience, ābāʾ were not merely ancestors but authority — the religion of the fathers was the standard defense of the sanctuary's order ("We found our fathers upon a path," the sūrah's own summary of the Makkān creed-formula in the Verse chapter's words, 43:22–23 — *“In fact, they say, “We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are following in their footsteps.” Similarly, whenever We sent a warner to a society before you ˹O Prophet˺, its ˹spoiled˺ elite would say, “We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are walking in their footsteps.”*). The Karbala of Arabic memory ran backward through them. The left's sneer therefore reveals, while dismissing resurrection, everything else they hold: the ancestors are the ones who must not be troubled. The dead of the old ways should stay in their honored ground; the claim that God re-raises the generations is offensive because it would summon, to the same questioning Day, the fathers who are supposed to be above questioning. Al-Wāqiʿah obliges: 49–50 gathers "the earlier and the later" without exception, and the sūrah's longer argument will insist, in its dying-scene, that "We are nearer to him than you, but you do not see" (56:85), a nearness no forefather has. Verse 48 is so the chapter's quietest demolition of ancestor-culture: it lets the mockers name their idol in a subordinate clause and then books him into the census.

**"Bring Our Forefathers If You Are Truthful": The Book's Taunt Returned**

The Qur'an elsewhere turns this very word back on its users. To the claim that paradise belongs to Israel alone the Heifer chapter says: "Bring your forefathers, if you are truthful" (2:111) — produce your ancestors as witnesses — and in the Poets chapter Moses, defying Pharaoh's court, names the Judge by their own oldest title — the God of their Lord and of their forefathers, Lord of the East and the West (26:26–29 — *“Moses added, “˹He is˺ your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers.”*) — the forefathers not as defense but as summons. Al-Wāqiʿah's 48 rhymes with both usages without quoting either: the sneer's own logic — "and our forefathers as well?" — assumes the fathers stand outside the summons, and the sūrah's theology of the gathering has already made them the first names on the roll. The audience who heard 48 in Makkah heard themselves, precisely where they felt unexamined: the dead whom no one questions will be questioned first, and the question-mark the mockers put on the fathers will, the sūrah implies, be turned back on the sons.

## Verse 56:49
> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Most certainly, earlier and later generations

**The Certainty-Grammar of Verse 49**

The English "most certainly" stands for two Arabic particles working together — the sentence's inna (indeed) and the lam of emphasis (la-jāmiʿūn) in its predicate — a construction Qur'anic recitation reserves for verdicts: not "the gathered will come," but "they are, assuredly, gathering-to-be." The sūrah, which has been describing, now swears. The shift in register is the listener's cue: everything from 50 to 56 hangs on this lam. Commentators noted that the verse carries no oath-formula (no "by the Lord of such-and-such") — the certainty is asserted, not sworn — while the chapter's only sworn passage is its own great oath to the Book — "So I do swear by the positions of the stars" (56:75). The design places two unshakable statements side by side: the Day's gathering, without an oath. The Book's truth, with one — as if the sūrah's architecture wanted its audience to notice that both rest on the same God's word and no human evidence, the resurrection and the revelation, each "most certainly," and each asked to be taken before being seen.

**No One Left Out of the Roll**

The tafsir's paraphrase supplies the scope that the verse's two words carry: the earlier and the later generations of the children of Ādam will be gathered for the Day of Resurrection, and none of them will be left out. The Qur'an states the same completeness where it explains the delay (11:104 — *“We only delay it for a fixed term”*), and the verse after it adds that no one will speak on that Day except by His leave, some of them miserable and others joyful. The addendum of verse 48 — "and our forefathers as well?" — is answered by a roll-call with no exceptions in it.

## Verse 56:50
> will surely be gathered ˹together˺ for the appointed Day

**Mīqāt: Contract-Language for the End of Time**

The sūrah's word is mīqāt, from w-q-t, the noun of measured appointment. The present line's "the appointed Day" renders mīqāti yawmin maʿlūm, a day with a date, and the phrase is the audience's own: a mīqāt is a fixed deadline agreed between parties — the same noun family that carries, in the Heights, the forty nights of Mūsā's tryst with his Lord (7:142 — *“We appointed for Moses thirty nights then added another ten—completing his Lord’s term of forty nights.”*). The sūrah so speaks of resurrection in the register of contracts kept: the Day is not a spontaneous eruption but an appointment the deniers themselves have been served papers for, and the mockers' incredulous "will we really?" (47) lands, grammatically, on a date already set. That is the quiet terror of verse 50: the Hour behaves like a creditor's patience — nothing is rushed because nothing can be renegotiated.

**"The Known Day": Why the Arabic Insists**

Mīqāti yawmin maʿlūm — for an appointed, known Day — and the "known" (maʿlūm) is one of the Qur'an's most theologically loaded adjectives: the Day is known to God, known through the preachers, "known" the way a court date is known to both parties. Al-Wāqiʿah keeps the plain force: this Day is knowable — announced by preachers, calendared in revelation, "known" the way a tryst is known to both parties — and the Ranks sūrah, whose Garden-list our chapter keeps beside its own, attaches the identical adjective to the reward ("a known provision," 37:41). That Day and recompense share one epithet within the same pair of late texts. The Day's "knownness" is so part of the evidence: an appointment the accused can produce no excuse for having missed.

**Three Words for the Date, and a Fourth Already Spoken**

The sūrah's small library of Last-Day nouns could be read as a lesson in the audience's calendar: maʿlūm, the known day. Mawʿūd, the promised, which the Qur'an's skeptics mock in the same breath they mock the dust — "when is this promise, if you are truthful?" asks the sovereign chapter of the dominion of the same doubters (67:25 — *“˹Still˺ they ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?”*); and mīqāt, the appointment that does not require their agreement to bind them. Al-Wāqiʿah's contribution to the set is its deployment of all three registers inside two verses — "appointed," "known," and, at 56, "the Day of Judgment" (yawm al-dīn, the Day of Recompense of verse 2 by another name) — a single event named four ways: promised, dated, known, and owed. That quadruple naming is not stylistic surplus. The sūrah is answering verse 47's "really?" from every angle the audience's own contract-vocabulary could supply: you were told, it is on the book, it is a fixed date, and the payment is being withheld by no one — the debt is yours.

The verb's root is the same as the Qur'an's name for Friday's gathering and for the end of the world it foreshadows — the sūrah of Hud calls it the day on which all people will be gathered, a day of witnessing (11:103 — *“Surely in this is a sign for those who fear the torment of the Hereafter. That is a Day for which humanity will be gathered and a Day ˹that will be˺ witnessed ˹by all˺”*). The sūrah of Consultation names it the Day of Assembly about which there is no doubt (42:7 — *“And so We have revealed to you a Quran in Arabic, so you may warn the Mother of Cities and everyone around it, and warn of the Day of Gathering—about which there is no doubt—˹when˺ a group will be in Paradise and another in the Blaze”*) — while our verse's form, la-jāmiʿūna, says it plainly: they will be gathered. The sūrah adds no place, only the Day, because in al-Wāqiʿah's geography the place is the weather and the address is a class.

**Neither Early Nor Late**

One gloss on the "known Day" is worth keeping on its own: the time is precisely designated, and it will not come late or early, and it will not increase or decrease. That is the difference between an appointment and a threat. A threat can be put off; a fixed term cannot be moved by the party it binds, which is why this sūrah answers the mockers' "when?" with a date rather than with a warning.

## Verse 56:51

> Then you, O misguided deniers

**The Turn from "They" to "You"**

Through verses 41–50 the left were talked about; at 51 the sūrah turns and talks to them — "Then you, O misguided deniers" — and the shift of person, what the sūrahs' rhetorical science calls iltifāt, is the verse's whole argument. Report addresses a jury; summons addresses the accused. The audience, who have been listening to a description of some third group's smoke, suddenly stand inside the address, because the "you" has no quoted speaker other than the God the chapter has been asserting since 49, and every hearer must decide which "you" they are in. Classical readers of the Qur'an's rhetoric list this turn among its most forceful devices precisely because it happens without warning — no verse-divider announces that the court now speaks to the defendants — and al-Wāqiʿah's placement at the hinge of its second half makes 51 the loudest single line of the left's passage, a line consisting, in Arabic, of nothing but a conjunction, a pronoun, and two nouns.

**"Misguided": The Verb of Losing the Road**

The second title, al-ḍāllīna, is the sūrah's word for going astray, and the root is travel-language: ḍalāla is losing the road, missing the way across open ground — the worst thing that can befall a people whose survival is navigation. The Qur'an uses the same word for its messenger's own prehistory, in the chapter of the Morning-Hours, "He found you unaware of the Scripture and the faith. He guided you" (93:7) — guidance and being-guided are the pair the word lives inside — and so verse 51's insult is, at bottom, directional: these people are not the wicked but the lost. The Day they denied is, in the sūrah's physics, the point at which being lost becomes a fixed address.

## Verse 56:52

> will certainly eat from ˹the fruit of˺ the trees of Zaqqûm

**The Fruit That Answers the Mockery**

Context gives the verse its logic: the left denied being raised (47), the gathering was affirmed (49–50), and the address comes (51), and now, at 52, the verdict is not fire as such — it is eating. The Ranks sūrah runs the same menu earlier in the Book — a tree sprouting in the bottom of the Hell, fruit compared to the heads of devils, bellies filled with it, a drink of hamīm after the eating. A return to the Blaze (37:64–68 — *“The evildoers will certainly ˹be left to˺ eat from it, filling up their bellies with it”*) — five notes in the same order our 52–56 will play. The point of a food-punishment is that the deniers made their bodies their argument: "when we are dust and bones, will we really be raised?" The answer is a body that eats forever and is nourished by nothing — resurrection granted in its most terrible form, flesh restored only to the function of swallowing. Al-Wāqiʿah's mockers get their resurrection, in the first tense it will be felt: the throat.

**The Certainty-Lam Returns, Third Time**

One reception-note completes the entry: zaqqūm became, in the literature of warning-sermons across the centuries, the Qur'an's emblem for the punishment of appetite. The preaching literature turned the tree into the food of every worldly injustice the audience could name — the devourer of orphans above all, after the Women chapter's line that such a man eats fire into his own belly (4:10 — *“Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies. And they will be burned in a blazing Hell”*).

## Verse 56:53

> filling up ˹your˺ bellies with it

**Filling the Belly With What Cannot Feed**

The verse says that the men on the left will fill their bellies from the tree of bitter fruit. The Book's punishment here is unusual: the wish to be full is granted, and what fills them cannot nourish anybody.

**What the Tree Is**

The tree in the verse is the tree the Book names elsewhere for the food of the fire, and it is described as growing in the bottom of the fire and having heads like the heads of devils. The verse in front of the reader says simply that the men will eat from it until their stomachs are full. The picture is not of a man who is denied food; it is of a man who eats and eats and gets nothing from it.

**The Comparison in Another Sūrah**

The Book describes the same food in the sūrah of the Overwhelming, where it says that the man on that day will have no food except a bitter thorn plant, which neither nourishes nor satisfies hunger (88:6 — *“They will have no food except a foul, thorny shrub”* and 88:7 — *“neither nourishing nor satisfying hunger”* name the plant and both failures). The sūrah here makes the point without adding the sentence, because the image carries it: a belly filled with something that was never food. The Book's punishments match the offences its own way, and the offence in this passage is a life spent filling appetites.

**What the Verse Says About Appetite**

The men being described had lived for what they could take. The sūrah earlier describes them as the men drawn to what is easy and comfortable, who asked whether they would be raised again and treated the warning as a story. The punishment in front of the reader answers the appetite directly. The one pleasure that never fails a person in this world, the comfort of a full stomach, is turned into the thing that torments him, and the sūrah leaves the reader to notice what that means.

**What the Sūrah Sets Against It**

The same sūrah describes the food of the men on the right a few verses away: fruit they choose, the meat of birds as they wish, and rivers of drink that leave no headache and give no dullness (56:20 — *“˹They will also be served˺ any fruit they choose”* through 56:21 — *“and meat from any bird they desire”* name the fruit and the meat, and 56:19 — *“that will cause them neither headache nor intoxication”* describes the drink). The two meals sit in the same passage, and the reader is meant to compare them. The verse in front of the reader is a single sentence about a tree, and it explains the whole difference between a life ordered around what a person wants and a life ordered around what God has given.

## Verse 56:54

> Then on top of that you will drink boiling water—

**"On Top of That": The Sequence of a Host's Care, Inverted**

Thumma ʿalā dhālik — "then, upon that" — is the sūrah's way of keeping the manners of serving in the scene of punishment: in the etiquette the audience knew, the drink comes after the food, the host does not let the guest's throat go dry at the end of the meal. The Ranks sūrah, narrating the same menu, says it with the same phrase — "Then on top of that they will be given a blend of boiling drink" (37:67), the line our verse paraphrases. That courtesy, exercised upon the damned, is the verse's chosen irony: the left are served, hosted, attended to — their estate is, as 56 will name it, an "accommodation" (nuṭub, hospitality) — and the sūrah refuses to depict hell as neglect. Nothing here is forgotten, withheld, or left to chance. The punishment is administered with the precision of a host who has planned the whole meal, which is far more terrible than abandonment, because it means the left are as certainly in God's hands as the near-rank are, and are being attended to with the same intentionality.

**"Like Molten Brass": The Neighboring Description**

What this verse declines to say about the drink, the Book says elsewhere. The sūrahs line the passages up as commentary on each other: the Cave chapter's hell-folk, when they cry for aid, "will be aided with water like molten metal, which will burn ˹their˺ faces" (18:29). The Dazzling chapter's "boiling in the bellies" image for the tree-food of the sinner (44:45–46 — *“Like molten metal, it will boil in the bellies”*) — the same belly that 56:53 — *“filling up ˹your˺ bellies with it”* filled, boiling with what the neighbors say is molten brass. The present sūrah keeps all such comparison back; 54 states only "of ḥamīm," the noun it has already defined in the estate's air (42), and lets the hearer's memory of the other chapters do the work. The restraint fits the chapter's method throughout the left's passage: scenery in three nouns, cause in two lines, and no gore added to what the audience's own experience of heat, thirst, and bad water already supplies — al-Wāqiʿah's punishment is, repeatedly, the familiar made inescapable.

---

## Verse 56:55

> and you will drink ˹it˺ like thirsty camels do.”

**Ḥīm: The Image the Desert Needed No Gloss For**

The comparison's noun — hum, plural ahyām, from the root of the thirst that unmakes the brain — means the camel driven mad by thirst, the animal that will not leave the trough, drinks and drinks and comes up empty, in the old lexicography because the beast's disorder prevents its body from receiving water at all. To an audience whose wealth walked on four legs and whose worst day was the camel's death by thirst, the sūrah needed no further word: "you will guzzle like him" is the entire sentence of punishment, and the translators' bracket ˹it˺ marks the object their verb carries on its own. The simile performs the classic degradation of the Qur'an's damning imagery — humans reduced, not to demons. To livestock: the chapter of Muhammad says the deniers "eat and enjoy as cattle eat and enjoy" (47:12), in a plain assertion of the same zoology — and here the very act of drinking, which the near-rank perform from ewers with no harm (18–19), is given to the left in its beastly form.

**The Sūrah's Similes, Counted**

Al-Wāqiʿah uses similes four times, and their distribution is its aesthetics. For the cosmos: the mountains elsewhere become like carded wool (70:6 — *“They truly see this ˹Day˺ as impossible”*), while this chapter's mountains are crushed to pieces and become scattered particles of dust (5–6) — and at 75–76 the stars' positions carry an oath instead of a comparison. For the near-rank: "like hidden pearls" (23), beauty defined by untouchability. For the left: "like molten brass" is not here, it is the neighbor's. This chapter gives them only "like the him." The pattern is precise: the saved get a simile from the treasury (the pearl, the thing hoarded and safe), the lost a simile from the pasture (the animal, the thing perishing at the water). Between them the sūrah's oath-sworn stars — the middle of the chapter's sky, unowned. Verse 55, the last of the left's images, is therefore the chapter's lower term: the audience's most cherished livestock, rendered in their most humiliating death, handed to the mockers of resurrection as their likeness forever, and the sūrah's economy of comparison is closed.

---

## Verse 56:56

> This will be their accommodation on the Day of Judgment

**Ḍiyāfa: The Word for Hosting, Used as a Sentence**

The noun the present translation renders "accommodation" is ḍiyāfah — hospitality, the guest-right, the whole etiquette of receiving the traveler — and its choice, once felt, changes the verse's temperature completely. The sūrah calls the Fire's menu a hospitality. The Book uses the same noun, with the same irony, in the Ranks chapter's sequence: "that is their ḍiyāfa. Let them taste it" (37:68), and — for the other side — "Is the accommodation of Paradise better, or the tree of Zaqqūm?" (37:62), where ḍiyāfa appears at the head of the blessed estate's description too. The two usages prove that the word belongs to the chapter's moral vocabulary: hosting is what one does to the arrived. The Day of Judgment is, in al-Wāqiʿah's design, an arrival at what was always being prepared — the near-rank's "elevated furnishings" (34) and the left's ḥamīm are both, in this sūrah's grammar, things a guest is given. Nothing could be more offensive to Arab honor-usage than calling the Fire a ḍiyāfa, which is precisely why the sūrah calls it that.

## Verse 56:57

> It is We Who created you. Will you not then believe ˹in resurrection˺

**The "We" Comes Back and Stands Up**

The sūrah described creation in polite passives for several verses: water poured down, shade stretched out, mates made in pairs. It saved the first person plural for special moments, and this is one of them. It is We who created you, says the verse. The pronoun comes first, before the verb, and that order makes the claim exclusive. Whoever else the audience credited with making them, whether the stars their fortune-tellers watched, the ancestors their family trees honoured, or luck they blamed for their troubles, is being answered before the sentence reaches its verb.

The next clause asks whether they will believe, and the belief it asks for is not belief in creation. They already accepted that. It asks for belief in the second act, the raising of the dead, which the first act makes easy. The whole sūrah argues from what the sceptics already accepted to the thing they denied.

## Verse 56:58

> Have you considered what you ejaculate

**"Have You Considered": The Verb That Sees**

The formula a-fa-raʾaytum — the present line's "have you considered" — uses the Arabic verb of sight for an act of thought, and the sūrah's account of knowledge is in the pun: the way to know is to look at what you already do, closely, as though for the first time. Late Makkān preaching is full of this imperative to see (the Neighbor chapter's "have you not seen what your Lord did," 89:6, the Elephant's "have you not seen how your Lord dealt," 105:1). Al-Wāqiʿah turns the eye inward: its four "consider" questions will scan semen, seed, water, fire — the four substances of the household — without once asking the audience to contemplate the stars, which the oath at 75 will do for the Book's sake. The verse is so the argument's manifesto of method: no cosmic subtlety, only the nearest body-function, held up for inspection.

**Maniyy: The Euphemism That Is Clinical**

The verb tamnū — "you pour out" — is Arabic's own discretion: the fluid (umniyyah) named by its spilling, no anatomy, no stage, no clot. The Qur'an speaks about semen without shame and without detail elsewhere — "from a fluid ejected, issuing from between the backbone and the ribs" (86:6–7, a description that is functional, not physiological) and "did he not used to be a drop of ejected fluid?" (75:37) — and 56:58 — *“Have you considered what you ejaculate”* stands in the same register: what is asked is not a biology lesson but the admission that the fluid is not, at the moment of its pouring, anyone's achievement. Commentators of the sūrahs read 58's bluntness as pedagogical: the audience's boasting was of lineage (nasab), and the sūrah reaches into the very act from which all their genealogies are made and asks the one question pride cannot answer — did you make that drop? The euphemism makes the question possible for public recitation; the question, once asked in the mosque, unmakes the boast.

Equally instructive is the verse's silence: no embryology, no "wonders of development," nothing the defensive literature of later centuries loves to supply. Modern readers are often tempted to hear in "what you ejaculate" a foreshadowing of histology — and the temptation should be named and let go: the sūrah's argument needs the drop to remain what the audience knew it to be, a lost bit of the body, because its logic is about agency, not about complexity. The Qur'an describes the stages in its own places (23:12–14 — *“then placed each ˹human˺ as a sperm-drop in a secure place”*, 22:5 — *“O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh—fully formed or unformed—in order to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you.”*). Those descriptions serve the reminder of formation; here the point is narrower and deeper: the fluid is your only raw material, and even it arrives in you from what you do not control — you eat, you drink, you pour, and none of it was manufactured by your will. Argument from the mundane, kept mundane, is the verse's discipline.

## Verse 56:59

> Is it you who create ˹a child out of˺ it, or is it We Who do so

**"Or": The Question With No Third Door**

The Arabic's disjunctive am ("is it you… or is it We") frames a choice the sūrah refuses to widen: there is no third answer in which the fluid makes itself, or chance does it, or the ancestors do it — the question's shape is the argument's whole content. Al-Wāqiʿah's theology of creation is, at this point in the chapter, radically simple. The simplicity is the Makkān point: the audience's gods were sharers, partners "who own" and "who intercede," and the Qur'an's recurring demonstration is that partnership requires, at minimum, one act anywhere that another agent performed — the drop being the case in point, because no claimant — not al-Lāt, not al-ʿUzzā, not the family's own memory — has ever put a soul in semen or taken one out. The verse does not enumerate what God did in the embryo; it asks who did it, and its two-option grammar is how the sūrah closes every loophole at once without arguing a single loophole.

**"Create It": The Verb That Exceeds the Fluid**

To the audience's own craft-vocabulary, "creating" (kh-ل-q) meant what a maker does: shaping leather, weaving cloth, forging the head of a lance — and the sūrahs record that Arab poets could call a fine poet "the best of those who shape (khāliq)" in the phrase of making, which is why the verse's verb stings: it asks whether their making reaches into the making of a person. Quietly answers no maker, however skilled, has ever made what he worked with. The Believers sūrah ends its embryological sequence with exactly this coronation: "blessed be God, the best of shapers" (23:14), a verse that knows the audience's praise-formula and redirects it — and 56:59, in its plainer Makkān accent, does the same work by question: "is it you who create it?" The craft is honored. The creation is claimed; the sūrah never says the maker is nothing, only that making is not the same as giving being, and the fluid's first being is the proof.

---

## Verse 56:60

> We have ordained death for ˹all of˺ you, and We cannot be prevented

**Qaḍāʾ: Death as a Completed Act**

The verb is qaḍā — to decree, and also to finish, to carry through to the end (the same root gives the Day "of Decision," yaum al-faṣl) — and the sūrah does not say "We will make you die" but "We have ordained death among you": a settled matter, filed and closed, its grammar as certain as the decree it states. The audience's own poet had written, as poets did, that death is the one thing a man cannot outrun, avert, or buy. The sūrah converts that fatalism into a personal appointment with a first-person verb, and the conversion — fate to decree, decree to address — is verse 60's quiet revolution in the Arabia of "fortune (dahr) does it.".

**"Cannot Be Prevented": The Image of the Race**

The Arabic's last word, masbūqūn, is from racing — "We are not to be outrun, forestalled, got ahead of" — and the metaphor fits a people who measured honor in the speed of their camels and the escape of their clients: no one has ever arrived anywhere before God's decision about them. The Heifer chapter's line about those who fled their towns "in their thousands for fear of death" — and God caused them to die, then gave them life (2:243 — *“Allah said to them, “Die!” then He gave them life.”*) — is the same doctrine told as story. And the verse 60 here tells it as the unlosable race whose every entry is already decided: you cannot be first to evade what is ordained to you. — the sūrah's next word — the defeat is productive, not terminal, which is the one sentence the fatalist's Arabic never had. Read against the mockers' "when we are dust, will we really be raised?" (47), "We cannot be outrun" means: your dust is, specifically, our turf; the race you imagined against annihilation, you have already lost on our terms, and our terms say re-creation.

---

## Verse 56:61

> from transforming and recreating you in forms unknown to you

**The Verse's Quiet Argument Against the Dust-objection**

Set 61 back inside the mockers' sentence and it answers them at their own hinge. Their objection at 47 was: dust and bones do not reassemble — the materials are too ruined. Verse 61 concedes the ruin completely: you will not reassemble; you will be transformed and brought up new, in forms not in your inventory of materials at all. The sūrah's resurrection is not archaeology, and 61 lets no reader pretend it is: the "re-creation" (nashʾ) verb, chosen in the same chapter where God "made the mates anew" (35), makes the risen body, like the Garden's spouses, a fresh sprouting rather than a repair — and the mockers' physics is so answered not by a stronger physics but by a change of category: what God does with dust is not gather it but graduate it. The argument-half's logic (58–59: the drop you did not make) generalizes here: just as the body's origin was not an assembly you performed, its end will not be one you can object to.

---

## Verse 56:62

> You already know how you were first created. Will you not then be mindful

**Tadhakkur: Forgetting as the Disease the Sūrah Treats**

The closing verb, tadhdhakkar, is the root of dhikr — remembrance — and in the Qur'an's anthropology the sin of the deniers is rarely ignorance; it is inattention, the un-remembering of what is conceded. Al-Wāqiʿah has already staged it: the Event "no one can deny" (2), the forefathers the mockers cite as if they were beyond summons (48), the Book they "take lightly" (81). Verse 62's "will you not then be mindful" prescribes against that disease with the sūrah's own name as medicine — the Qur'an, in its self-designation, is "the Reminder (dhikr)," sent so that what the soul already knows is not lost (the Book's claim, paraphrased in the Heifer and the Qāf chapters: "a Reminder to whoever has a heart" — the Qāf verse's exact phrase, 50:37) — and the verse's placement right after the "unknown forms" of 61 makes the logic complete: the future body cannot be known. The past origin can be remembered, and remembering the lesser is the sūrah's way of making the greater credible.

**The All the more Engine of 57–74**

Read 62 as the argument's thesis-key: if you were made once from nothing you did not prepare, the second making is not harder but easier, and the mockers' difficulty ("dust and bones?") is therefore not an objection but a forgetting. The Book states this syllogism aloud elsewhere, in the chapter of Ya-Sin, where the man who crumbles the bones is answered: "Who will give life to bones while they are decayed? Say: He will give them life who produced them the first time" (36:78–79, cited in plain paraphrase because the sūrahs count them as one argument) — and al-Wāqiʿah, which never quotes Ya-Sin, runs the identical demonstration inside its own four "consider" questions: first creation (57), drop (58–59), decree of death (60), transformation (61). The reminder of the knowing (62), five rings of one chain.

**The Verse at the End of the Census It Began**

The Ranks chapter will later press the point in the Book's own terms — man created "from a dust, then a drop of fluid" (the sūrah of Prostration's reminder, 32:7–8 — *“Who has perfected everything He created. And He originated the creation of humankind from clay Then He made his descendants from an extract of a humble fluid”*, in plain paraphrase) — and verse 62 declines to cite even that: your knowing, it says, is pre-scriptural, lodged in the fact that you exist and were born.

## Verse 56:63

> Have you considered what you sow

**The Second Consideration: From the Body to the Field**

The argument-half runs on one repeated imperative — "have you considered" — applied to what you eject (58), what you sow (63), what you drink (68). What you kindle (71), and the sequence moves outward from the body through the field to the sky and the hearth, the four domains a Makkān household actually inhabited. The second, at 63, is the one the audience's wealth was built on: grain was the south's money as much as its food, the storehouse was the bank, and the sūrahs of the South Arabian terraces are a civilization's autobiography told in irrigation. By placing sowing next to semen the sūrah has made both into the same class of evidence — acts every man performs and no man completes — and the verse's brevity ("what you sow," two words in Arabic, the verb zaraʿa and its object) does the rest: the sermon will not allegorize the seed here, though later readers loved to. It simply puts the furrow beside the womb as second witness to verse 57's thesis, "We created you," in a court of one exhibit per room.

**The City Without Fields**

One contextual fact sharpens the verse: Makkān scripture is honest that the sanctuary-town was agricultural nonsense — the Heifer chapter's own account of Ibrāhīm's prayer makes the valley's barrenness the premise of the settlement ("a land without cultivation," 14:37). The city's fame was the trade that carried grain into it, not the grain it grew. "Have you considered what you sow" therefore addresses, at 63, an audience for whom sowing was a profession of neighbors (the Ṭāʾif farmers, the Yemeni terraces) and an import. The sūrah's argument reaches them through the thing they bought weekly: to live at Makkah was to depend, every loaf, on rain and growth you could not station guards around.

**Seeds Elsewhere in the Book**

Al-Wāqiʿah cites no seed-parables here, but the audience's scripture was thick with them. The verse's "what you sow" is a compressed intertext: the Heifer's "a seed that splits the sprout" (6:95, plain paraphrase), the Heifer's own parable of the grain that puts forth seven ears, a hundred in each (2:261, paraphrased — *“The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains.”*). The Victory chapter's sower whose shoot "He sprouts, then makes it grow strong" (48:29, in plain paraphrase), and the Kafrūn sūrah's devastating "you certainly know" structure — "not are you worshippers of what I worship" — which verse 68's format will imitate. The Qur'an's agriculture is, across these passages, a single argument: growth is the clearest case of un-owned power, and the seed, because it must leave the hand to live, is the object that best dramatizes trust. Verse 63 places that whole scriptural stock, without quotation, behind four words — "have you considered what you sow" — and lets the listener's own familiarity with the Book's farming do the exegesis.

---

## Verse 56:64

> Is it you who cause it to grow, or is it We Who do so

**Nashz: The Root That Grows Crops, Mates, and Bodies**

The verb of the challenge — "is it you who cause it to grow?" — carries the sūrah's own root for sprouting: n-sh-ʾ, to bring up, to cause to rise. The same root has already done duty twice in this chapter and does so once more below: God "made the mates in a [new] making" (inshaʾan, 35), "We could transform and bring you up in a form you do not know" (nunshiʾakum, 61). Here, the growth of what the farmer buries (tunshīʾūna? — the sūrahs read the verb as the raising of vegetation). One root, therefore, runs from the re-creation of spouses to the resurrection of bodies through the daily sprouting of grain. The sūrah is making its case with morphology as much as with questions: in al-Wāqiʿah's vocabulary, marriage-freshness, harvest, and the risen body are the same act of God, one verb wearing three contexts. The earth's sprouting is also, elsewhere in the Book, the earth's speech — the Quake chapter has the ground "tell its news, because your Lord has inspired it" (99:4–5) — and the mockers at 47, who dismissed the reassembly of dust, are answered by the dust that rises every spring on their own terraces, speaking, testifying, sending up what nobody put there.

**"We Cause It to Grow": The Continuity of Creating**

The Book's hoarding-laws elsewhere — the hoarding of gold and silver that is not spent in God's way, with its tidings of a painful torment (9:34, in plain paraphrase — *“Give good news of a painful torment to those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in Allah’s cause”*), a theme the Iron sūrah's hardening-verse sounds at 57:16 — *“Has the time not yet come for believers’ hearts to be humbled at the remembrance of Allah and what has been revealed of the truth, and not be like those given the Scripture before—˹those˺ who were spoiled for so long that their hearts became hardened. And many of them are ˹still˺ rebellious”*, already noted in this commentary — provide the moral half of the argument. 64 provides the metaphysical one: growth is not a thing you do. The increase you priced was never yours to begin with, and the Day's census (8–14) sorts people by what they did with what was given, which is the only ownership the sūrah ever admits.

## Verse 56:65

> If We willed, We could simply reduce this ˹harvest˺ to chaff, leaving you to lament

**Ḥuṭāmah: The Sound of the Thing Crushed**

The word the present translation renders "chaff" is ḥuṭam — an object of the root meaning to crumble, to bruise to fragments, the same word used for dates crushed in the hand and, in the Sijjīl passage of the Elephant chapter. The birds' stones that make the army "like eaten straw" (105:5, plain paraphrase — the Book's master image for a harvest or an army reduced to fodder). Ḥuṭam is not a botanical state but an aftermath: what remains when the crop is broken down past use, and the sūrah's threat is therefore not drought, not blight, but the specific horror of the granary-age — labor completed, produce present, and then crushed to stuff no animal will take. For an audience whose calendar, debts, and dowries all hung on the harvest's yield, the verse names the total loss available to a God who "willed," and the word's sound — the emphatic ḥ, the heavy stops — performs the breaking the meaning describes.

**The Verse the Surah Answers with Gratitude**

Finally, 65 belongs to the chapter's economy of thanks. Its last argument-question, at 70, ends "will you not then give thanks?" — shakūr, the grateful servant of whom the Saba chapter said few are (34:13, cited already at this chapter's verse 14 — *“˹Only˺ a few of My servants are ˹truly˺ grateful”*) — and verse 65 is the verse that defines what gratitude is for: not for the harvest enjoyed (that is 64's growth, conceded) but for the harvest not yet crushed. Al-Wāqiʿah has, from its thornless lote trees to its never-cut fruit, described the Garden as provision with the "if We willed" clause spent forever — and verse 65 stands in the argument-half like the world's one paragraph on why the clause has not yet been spent on you: read it as mercy, the sūrah says. It is still the condition and not the outcome. Let the lament be only a rehearsal, keening over standing grain, the sound of a people God chose not to make an example.

## Verse 56:66

> “We have truly suffered a ˹great˺ loss

**A Loss Named in the Grammar of Certainty**

The cry carries, in Arabic, the full asseveration — inna plus the lam of emphasis — "truly, we are the utterly-bested," and the sūrah has just used the identical particles for its own decree: "Most certainly, earlier and later generations will surely be gathered" (56:49–50). The parallel is unmissable once named: the lament of the lost speaks, under threat, in the very grammar of certainty God uses over them. Their "truly" will be as confident then as the oath was now — the sūrah implies that on the Day even the ruined will believe, and believe with emphasis, their unbelief having been the temporary, not this confession. The bracketed ˹great˺ supplies an intensifier the Arabic's root carries contextually, and readers should hear the double emphasis, divine and human, echoing across seventeen verses: what God swears, the loss swears too, and the sūrah's audience is being asked which "truly" they will be the first to accept.

**Loss as the Sūrah's Recurring Ledger-Word**

Khasāra — the cry's noun — is the chapter family's commercial term. Al-Wāqiʿah, which has priced the whole Day in trade-language (shade "extended" like credit, "benefit" denied to the left — 44, "for what they used to do" as payment — 24), puts on the farmers' lips the one word a merchant cannot mishear: loss, the verdict on a tijāra, a transaction that failed. The Book reserves the same noun for its gravest bankruptcies — "they bought error for guidance. Their trade did not turn to profit" (2:16, plain paraphrase), "the losers are those who lose themselves and their families" (the sense of the Az-Zumar tally, 39:15 — *“Say, “The ˹true˺ losers are those who will lose themselves and their families on Judgment Day.”*) — and the lament at 66 is, in that phrase, the smallest case of the largest: a field is the classroom. The loss they keeningly confess is rehearsal for the one the sūrah is really counting. The keening of the farmers, in a chapter recited in a market-town, is translated without remainder; Makkah understood a bad trade better than it understood resurrection, and the sūrah says so by letting it use the traders' own arithmetic for the Day.

## Verse 56:67

> In fact, we have been deprived ˹of our livelihood˺.”

**The Root That Unites 33 and 67**

Read across the chapter, verse 67 answers verse 33 at the level of letters. The people of the right were promised fruit "never out of season nor forbidden (lā maqṭūʿah wa-lā muḥarramah)" — fruit on which no taboo has ever been passed, the root ḥ-r-m negated as a bar — and the keening farmers end their sentence as "deprived (maḥrūmūn)," the same root passed against them as exclusion. Al-Wāqiʿah's theology of provision lives in that rhyme: ḥarām in this chapter means, both times, the power to withhold — the Garden is what no hand is forbidden from. The Field-world is what any hand may be cut from — and the sūrah has placed the two uses to bracket its argument-half, so that the saved orchard (28–34) and the lost lament (63–67) are spelled from one consonant-root. The keening, heard in Arabic, is therefore unintelligible to the believer except as a definition by contrast: they mourn. The mourned estate is the one whose fruit "no one is barred from," a sentence recited forty verses earlier in the same breath the sūrah now asks the lameters to complete: "we — we are the barred.".

**The Deprived and the Pride of Sanctuary**

Makkāns lived under a name that made ḥurmah their claim to the world: their town was the sanctuary (ḥaram), its truce-months the heritage of Ibrāhīm's prayer ("a land without cultivation" endowed with safety, in the Abrahamic petitions the Book preserves — 14:35 — *“˹Remember˺ when Abraham prayed, “My Lord! Make this city ˹of Mecca˺ secure, and keep me and my children away from the worship of idols”*, 2:126 — *“And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord, make this city ˹of Mecca˺ secure and provide fruits to its people—those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day.” He answered, “As for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves for a little while, then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire. What an evil destination!”*, plain paraphrase). Their pride was that of people no one could touch at the House. The lament's word, maḥrūm, takes that pride apart at its root: the same ḥ-r-m whose sacred security they cited, whose fruit-taboos they kept for their altars (5:103's — *“Allah has never ordained the ˹so-called˺ baḥîrah, sâ'ibah, waṣîlah, and ḥâm camels. But the disbelievers just fabricate lies about Allah, and most of them lack understanding”* cultic refusals, at this chapter's verse 33), becomes in their cry the noun of being shut out — and the sūrah so converts the city's master-concept into its warning: the sanctuary protects nobody from deprivation, the taboo feeds nobody. "we are barred" is the confession Makkah's entire sanctity-culture was built to prevent its people from ever having to say. To recite 67 in that town was to speak its vocabulary back to it with the sign reversed.

**Silence After the Cry: How the Sūrah Closes the Sowing**

Rizq, across the Book, is what a creature is given to live on. The Qur'an's fixed answer to every anxiety about it is that the Giver and the grower are one: "there is no creature on earth but that upon God is its provision" (11:6, in plain paraphrase), a verse whose grammar of guarantee (ʿalā) matches, in reverse, the lament's "we are the deprived (maḥrūmūn)" — those cut off from what was owed, in their imagination.

## Verse 56:68

> Have you considered the water you drink

**The Third Consideration, and the Only Passive One**

The argument-half's four questions are built on four things — what you eject, what you sow, what you drink, what you kindle — and the third breaks the pattern in a way the reciter feels before the analyst names it. Semen, seed, and fire are things the audience does: verbs of human making run all three ("what you ejaculate," "what you sow," "you kindle"). But the water verse says "the water you drink" — the only clause of the four in which the listeners' contribution is reception. The sūrah has moved from what you make to what you merely take in, and the escalation of the argument is in the grammar: nobody is required to pretend they did not sow, and it is almost impossible to pretend you did not need to drink. Verse 68 is so the point at which the sūrah stops contesting credit and simply asks, out loud, about the one commodity that has never belonged to anyone in Makkah — and does it in the flat phrase of "have you considered," as if reminding, not teaching, which is this chapter's whole manner.

## Verse 56:69

> Is it you who bring it down from the clouds, or is it We Who do so

**"From the Clouds": Muduj, the Rain-Word the Sūrah Chooses**

The Arabic's noun for the source is muduj (plural of madfa, in the sūrahs' lexica: the cumulus, the heaped rain-clouds that "drive" forward). The present translation's "clouds" keeps the concrete sky-thing the audience watched — not "the sky" (samāʾ), which the Qur'an elsewhere uses for rain's origin ("He sends down rain from the sky," 14:32, 2:22 — *“˹He is the One˺ Who has made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy; and sends down rain from the sky, causing fruits to grow as a provision for you.”*, plain paraphrase). The sūrah's word here, in the classical readings, marks a specific phenomenology: the white heap on the horizon that the whole town watches for, the cloud as the desert's rumour. That choice lets verse 69 stage its disjunction at the audience's own observation-post — you see the clouds, you do not drive them; your eyes track the heaping, your hands have no handle on the descent — and the rain's familiar mechanics become, in five words, a demonstration of un-owned power. The argument-half's most concrete line is also its most meteorological, and the sūrahs enjoyed noting that the very plural of the word pictures what the people called, in their proverb, the sky's "driving" of water to the valley-beds they alone could claim.

**"Do So": The Ellipsis That Keeps the Rain Unmechanized**

The present translation's "or is it We Who do so" renders an Arabic ellipsis — the line does not repeat the verb's object or its instrument. The sūrah, which could have said "We send it down, weighed, timed, guided to the wells it chose," simply answers "or We," and the bareness is deliberate. The sūrahs of the water-verses elsewhere carry the mechanics ("We send down water in measured measure," 23:18, plain paraphrase; "the fertilizing winds," 15:22, the same book's phrase), while al-Wāqiʿah declines the detail here because its argument needs only authorship: rain is the case in which every theory of the world concedes at least one god — the audience's own al-Anwāʾ, the star-lot oracles of rain, prove how seriously everyone took the descent's contingency — and the sūrah's question is which. To keep "We do so" unexplained is to deny the listener the comfort of a mechanism halfway to God: the rain's how is left to the other chapters, while the who, in this one, stands exposed in two words and no third option.

## Verse 56:70

> If We willed, We could make it salty. Will you not then give thanks

**Ujāj: The Salty That Is the Sweet's Reverse**

The word ujāj — "salty," in the older glosses also bitter, scalding, the undrinkable — is Qur'anic only in this chapter and in the Furqan's twin-seas verse: "one fresh and palatable and the other salty and bitter, placing between them a barrier they cannot cross" (25:53) — the two-seas verse whose barrier-phrase the All-Merciful sūrah also invokes (55:19–20 — *“yet between them is a barrier they never cross”*). The pair of usages shows what verse 70's threat costs: al-Wāqiʿah does not say "We could make it poison" — no invented evil — it says We could make your drinking-water taste like the sea you already know, and the horror is precisely at home. The sūrah's the teaching about the Last Day works the same way everywhere (a shade that is smoke, cups without headache, a spring without a cistern): the punishment and the promise are each built from one familiar ingredient with one property changed. 70 is the formula at its cleanest — take the sweet (furat), apply the will, and Makkah has no vocabulary for the loss that follows.

**The Verse the Chapter Answers with Fire**

Gratitude, in this chapter's country, is not a mood but a survival technology. The audience's own scripture had taught them what its failure costs: the people of the two garden-terraces were a grateful folk, said the Sabaʾ chapter, until ingratitude (kufr, here in its root-meaning of un-thanking) and the dam-sent flood turned their groves to bitter-fruited substitutes (34:15–17 — *“But they turned away. So We sent against them a devastating flood, and replaced their orchards with two others producing bitter fruit, fruitless bushes, and a few ˹sparse˺ thorny trees”*) — the exact reversal of verse 70, sweet to salt, watered to destroyed. The sūrah reciting "will you not then give thanks" in the hearing of Makkah carries the Sabaeans' aftermath as its unspoken second clause.

## Verse 56:71

> Have you considered the fire you kindle

**Fire as the Chapter's Double-Edged Element**

Al-Wāqiʿah has used fire before this verse and will use it after. 71 is the joint: "the blazing-fire" (al-jahīm) is the left's lodging — 48 — their drink is boiling (hamīm, 42, 54). Their doom is "a blazing-fire" at 94; meanwhile the near-rank have no fire in their inventory at all, and the Event itself melts mountains but does not burn them — punishment in this chapter is thermal, but fire as fuel is never once called punishment. Verse 71 stands in the middle of that distribution and does the sūrah's real work with the element: it lifts fire out of the courtroom into the argument — the same substance that houses the lost is the one each hearer coaxes from wood every evening — and the "consider" asks whether the kindler can keep treating, as a tool, what his own book makes a verdict. The sūrah will draw the line itself at 73, but the line runs, first, through the hearth.

**Kindling, the Qur'an's Verb for Human Effort**

Lexically, ārā/yūrī is the verb of what a person does to make a fire. The Book uses the same root when it describes, with quiet irony, humans "kindling" what they cannot create — the listeners' phrase at 71 will therefore echo a wider Qur'anic habit of granting the act's name to the actor and its substance to God, the way the Cave chapter lets a man say "I do not think the Hour will ever come" while the garden's ownership stays, line by line, with its real Owner (18:35–42 — *“And he entered his property, while wronging his soul, saying, “I do not think this will ever perish”*, the parable this commentary has already cited at the chapter's gardens).

## Verse 56:72

> Is it you who produce its trees, or is it We Who do so

**"Its Trees": Fire's Forgotten Family**

The possessive is the verse's hidden hand: "its tree" — the fire's tree — and the sūrah, asked for the origin of flame, does not answer "lightning" or "flint" but "tree": the kindler's fire belongs, etymologically, to vegetation. Every fire in this audience's world was stored sunlight in tamarisk roots, dried dung over twigs, the desert's only fuel two steps from a plant. That arboreal answer closes a ring the chapter has been building since 27–28 — lote-tree and thorn-apple as Garden and Hell's own flora, seed and sprout as the argument's middle act (63–65) — and 72 now makes the fire, too, a botanical fact: the flame is a tree's hour. The question "is it you who produce its tree?" quietly relocates the hearth to the field, where the sūrah has already proved nobody is master. The Arabic's singular (shajaratahā) keeps the riddle compact — the fire's tree is any tree, every tree, and none of them theirs.

**36:80 — The Sūrah's Twin Verse on Green Fire**

Readers of the sūrahs know the pairing, and it belongs in this entry: "He who produces fire for you from the green tree, and from it you kindle" (the Yā-Sīn sūrah 36:80 — "Who gives you fire from green trees, and — behold! — you kindle fire from them" (56:71's own verb for kindling, re-echoed there) — its parallel line about fire from the wet wood — the ʿarfaj and marakh whose damp kindles when struck, per the sūrahs' botanical glosses). The two chapters say the same thing with the same root, and al-Wāqiʿah's "is it you who produce its tree" is the question whose answer Ya-Sin pronounces as miracle: water in wood, fire out of water, the element's nativity in the driest of lands. A Makkān hearer had no need of philosophy to test it — every tinder-box was the argument, and the green tree's secret combustion was known by name, season, and strike. The sūrah's question does not explain the phenomenon but files it, like the rest of the four considerations, under "We," and its partner-chapter's verse proves that this was a standing Qur'anic move, the hearth as a case in the Book's court of origins.

## Verse 56:73

> We have made it ˹as˺ a reminder ˹of the Hellfire˺ and a provision for the travellers

**Matāʿ: The Sūrah's Own Economic Word for Mercy**

Provision (matāʿ) is not filler; it is the chapter's trade-vocabulary at its most religious, and its near-twin sits in the Abasa sūrah's inventory of care — "a means of sustenance for you and your animals" (80:32), where the same noun names the harvest's purpose across species. Al-Wāqiʿah's matāʿ shares that range: the verb-world of Makkah's market — goods, brief enjoyment, the wayfarer's kit — is conscripted into the sermon to say that what can be sold is, first, what has been given, and that the sūrah's "provision" is a mercy-word wearing a merchant's coat. The commentary has traced the chapter's commercial register through the shade "extended" like credit and the payment-language of 24. Verse 73 finishes the job by pricing the unpriceable: the fire's usefulness is not a resource discovered but a commodity issued, and "travellers" are, in that ledger, not owners — the sūrah's audience of traders hear themselves, for once, described the way every trader really is, carrying what was handed out.

## Verse 56:74

> So glorify the Name of your Lord, the Greatest

**The Courtroom Breaks Into Praise**

The argument-half, which began with "We created you — will you not then believe?" (57), ends without a verdict: the sūrah's fourth consideration has been answered, its fire given its double commission (73). What follows is not a sentence on the deniers but an address to the Messenger — "So glorify." The fāʾ, "so," is the whole verse's logic: praise is the proper close of evidence, the courtroom's last act when the hearts have not yet delivered.

**The Same Sentence in the Sūrah of the Most High**

The verse's twin stands one short sūrah away: "Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High" (87:1) — the opening of the Aʿlā cycle — and the two lines share almost their whole diction, differing only in the superlative: "the Greatest" here, "the Most High" there, and in position: al-Aʿlā begins where al-Wāqiʿah pauses mid-course. The pairing is the recitation-tradition's own way of teaching this verse — a doxology that opens one sūrah closes twice another — and it lets the commentator name the genre: imperatives of tasbīḥ framed, in this Book, around arguments rather than inside them, as though praise were appointed to stand at the joints where proof ends. Makkans who knew both sūrahs by heart, as the nightly reciters of this one evidently did, would have felt 74 as the same hinge they met at the start of the Most High: the Name, the Lord, the superlative — and the difference between "High" (of His station) and "Greatest" (of His claim) lands, in al-Wāqiʿah's context, on the courtroom: what can be subpoenaed about the world's Maker, after four considerations of semen, seed, water. Fire, is only this — that the Name is set above every share in it.

## Verse 56:75

> So I do swear by the positions of the stars—

Readers of the sūrahs know the dispute embedded in the line's first syllables: the particle lā before "I swear" has been taken, by the majority of the commentators, as an oath-intensifier of classical Arabic (the phrase's "lā" of emphasis, leaving the sense "So, indeed, I swear"). By others as a genuine negation — "I do NOT swear" — directed at the audience's own swearing by the stars, their anwāʾ, the rain-lot of the pre-Islamic calendar that the Qur'an elsewhere confronts (53:19–20's idols and their clientele, at this chapter's verse 59, and the Sijjīl-of-stars theology the Star sūrah answers from its opening: "By the stars when they fade away," 53:1).

**Stars in This Chapter: The Only Sky of the Argument-Half**

Within al-Wāqiʿah itself, verse 75 is the stars' only appearance in the argument — the chapter's skies have been, until here, Last-Day (mountains dusted, the sky split, 5–6 — the sūrah's near-rank dwell under "the expanded shade" and no constellation. Its left under "a column of smoke," 43 — a sky of weather, not of fate) — and the sudden introduction of the stars at the oath is therefore conspicuous: the sūrah admits the stars, briefly and only as witnesses, into a chapter that otherwise refuses them every office, destiny, intercession, or calendar. The structural reading is exact: al-Wāqiʿah has just argued the elements (seed, crop, water, fire) away from the audience's control. Verse 75 takes the last domain of pre-Islamic control — the sky's timetable — and turns it into an oath-slot: the stars do not rule; they testify, and the sūrah's audience, who read fortunes in settings, hear their own science sworn with. From 75 on, the chapter's only vertical reference is to "the Lord of the worlds" (80). The heavens are reduced, as they are so often in this Book, to furniture with a liturgical function: positions that glorify (74's tasbīḥ includes theirs, in the sense of the Musabbiḥāt's whole grammar, 57:1 — *“Whatever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies Allah, for He is the Almighty, All-Wise”*) — and nothing else.

## Verse 56:76

> and this, if only you knew, is indeed a great oath—

**"If Only You Knew": The Chapter's One Concession of Scale**

The parenthesis "law taʿlamūn — if only you knew" is, in the present translation's wording, unique in this sūrah, and its placement is its meaning: the sūrah measures its own oath and finds the audience's knowledge too short for the instrument. The phrase "if only you knew" in this Book opens hoods over facts whose full weight the hearer cannot see — it is the Book's phrase for facts whose full weight a hearer reaches only afterward, the verse-by-verse inventory of the Day given to those who will believe it too late for the knowledge to help — and here it tells the room, before any doctrine, that swearing itself has mass: the sūrah's audience, expert in oaths, in the solemnities of the sanctuary and of the trade-contract, is told that their expertise is the one thing they do not bring to this line. Al-Wāqiʿah's court of evidence so closes with a confession calibrated to the witnesses' pride — you know oaths; you do not know this one.

**Oaths in This Book: The Grammar of Sworn Worlds**

The Qur'an swears, as its audiences did — by the night when it envelops, the forenoon, the city and its security, the wind in its measure (the short-sūrah register, in paraphrase of the opening oaths of Sūrah al-Layl, al-Ḍuḥā, al-Balad, and the Adhkariyyāt's "the scattering winds," 51:1) — and the objects of its oaths are the same sky and earth the audience read for luck, profit, and weather, retaken for witness. Verse 76 stands inside that habit and names it once: "this is indeed a great oath," the only place in the chapter that comments on the instrument of swearing itself. The commentary can add the classical observation that the sūrah's oaths are addressed to a people whose oaths were sacred commerce — a tribe that broke a covenant swore. A town that trusted the stars swore, and a Book that swears by their star-positions inverts both practices at once: God's asseveration is sworn not by the stars' power but by their positions, which is 75's anti-mantic move, here given its liturgical weight: the oath is great because the sworn thing — a noble Book in a guarded Record — is. The first time in this audience's experience, a fact that cannot be lost, broken, or outvoted.

## Verse 56:77

> that this is truly a noble Quran

**Majīd: The Word the Sūrah Applies to Its Own Book**

Noble translates majīd — a root of glory that in classical usage belongs to what is magnificient in itself, not to what is generous toward others (karīm), and the Qur'an uses it of the Book only here and twice beside: "Qāf. By the glorious Quran!" (50:1) and "In fact, this is a glorious Quran" (85:21) — two other late-Makkān sūrahs, two other courtrooms, the same adjective sworn (50) or asserted against the deniers' "glorious" (85, the Broken-Pattern chapter, whose "glorious Quran" follows its oath by "the guarded Tablet" in a sequence this verse and the next will replicate). Al-Wāqiʿah's choice of majīd at the hinge is so not a compliment but a claim of self-sufficiency: the Book's nobility is intrinsic, unloaned from its audience's reception — and the sūrah, whose entire middle has argued that nothing in the world is owed, applies the same theology to its own text: it is noble. Its honor is the Maker's, touched by none except the purified (79).

**The Nobility the Argument-Half Prepared**

It is worth measuring how 77's claim was earned: the sūrah has spent seventeen verses (57–73) removing the audience's ownership of the world's processes. A book that can do that, in a room, by being said, carries its majīd as an effect of its operation — the nobility asserted at 77 is the sūrah's own CV. The structure answers the mockers' challenge — "will we really be resurrected?" (56:47, in plain echo of the verse's own translation, already argued at this chapter's verses 41–44): they asked for resurrection on their terms. The sūrah's reply at 77 is procedural — the book that unmans your weather and your wealth is the evidence you actually have, and it is noble. Classical readers of this chapter liked to add the intertext: when the Qur'an elsewhere calls the same Book "a firm rope" and "the decisive speech, not a jest" (the sense of 86:13 — *“Surely this ˹Quran˺ is a decisive word”*'s four-word contrast, in plain paraphrase), it is al-Wāqiʿah's own register — speech that separates classes, that the near-rank will hear as news from their Lord. That the left will, too late, have found undecidable. Nobility here is not aesthetic; it is jurisdictional, and the sūrah's argument-half has just handed the court to the Book.

## Verse 56:78

> in a well-preserved Record

**The Record with Its Exact Book-Names**

The family-words — Umm al-Kitāb, Lawḥ Maḥfūẓ, Kitāb Makhnūn — carry one claim with different vault-doors: the recited book has, above recitation, a kept original. Verse 78, in al-Wāqiʿah's courtroom, enters that original as evidence: the oath (75) was sworn "that this is a noble Quran" (77), "in a well-preserved Record" (78), a prepositional clause that says the nobility is not the copy's polish but the archetype's keeping. The sūrah, by adding the location, answers the charge its audience was living to make — that the Qur'an is Muḥammad's trade (next verses: "how can you take this message lightly, and make denial your livelihood?" — 81–82, in plain echo of their translation) — with provenance: a book with a Record is not a book with a vendor.

**The Only Record in a Chapter Without Books**

The sūrah's phrase for the Book's location — a well-preserved Record (kitāb makhnūn) — belongs to a small family of Qur'anic self-names for revelation's archive: "in the Master Record with Us — highly esteemed, rich in wisdom" (43:4, the Orchid sūrah's phrase of "Mother of the Book" here supplied with its "with Us"), and, in the Broken-Pattern chapter that swears like this one, "in a Preserved Tablet, recorded" (85:21–22, whose "glorious Quran" line this commentary cited at 77).

The Lote-tree of the Utmost Boundary, which the Star sūrah sets near revelation's descent (53:13–16, in the other chapter's plain account — *“while the Lote Tree was overwhelmed with ˹heavenly˺ splendours”*), does duty in the neighbor-chapter where al-Wāqiʿah keeps silence — and the effect on a Makkān ear is measurable: the sky that cast rain-lots (this commentary at 59 and 69) is here demoted to a shelving-system belonging to the Lord who sends, and nothing in it dispenses anything at all.

## Verse 56:79

> touched by none except the purified ˹angels˺

**"None Touch It": The Negative That Protects the Exhibit**

The clause's function in the courtroom is evidentiary before it is ritual: the sūrah has sworn by the stars' positions that "this is a noble Quran" (75–77), lodged it in "a well-preserved Record" (78). Verse 79 now forecloses the only remaining attack on the exhibit's integrity — that it was touched by what it denies, jinn, soothsayers, or the marketplace. The sūrah has already told that story once: "Shall I inform you upon whom the devils descend?

Verse 79 now says what those scenes assumed: the holy and its bearers are in contact only with the clean, and the boys "like preserved pearls (luʾluʾ maknūn)" (18) — the same adjective as the Record (makhnūn, 78) — are themselves kept, veiled, untouched by the world's dust.

**Purity as the Chapter's Recurring Grammar**

Read for the ˹angels˺ gloss alone, verse 79 completes a scene the chapter has drawn twice before in other words: the near-rank are served by "young boys round about them, like preserved pearls" (17–18). The sūrah's own phrase for the heavenly touch already ran, at this commentary's verses 7–10, through the angels' charge over every soul (in the sense of the Rays' "appointed in succession," 13:10–11 and 86:4 — *“There is no soul without a vigilant angel ˹recording everything˺”* — plain paraphrases, cited earlier).

They reveal a whisper to most of the liars" (26:221–222, in the Poets' plain account — this chapter's oracle-denunciation at 59's environment). 79 gives the flip-side: the Qurʾān's channel is, by contrast, touch-proof. The sūrah's audience — who judged every poem by the jinn at its poet's elbow, the familiar jinn at the poet's elbow, a literary forensics of the age this commentary names but does not quote — hear their own literary forensics turned back on them: you know how to detect a tainted book. This one is certified by the manner of its untouchedness.

## Verse 56:80

> ˹It is˺ a revelation from the Lord of all worlds

**"The Lord of All Worlds": The Chapter's One Full Title**

In al-Wāqiʿah the phrase appears exactly once — at this verse, on the present translation's text — and its placement turns the whole argument-half into an exercise in lordship: every consideration was a question about who does what ("is it you… or is it We"). 80 names the answer's office in the sūrah's only complete divine title: "Lord of all worlds." The sūrah's other addresses are intimate (the repeated "your Lord" of 74 and 96, the doxologies). This is the single moment the chapter widens — the Event (1) has, by the hinge, been shown to be the act of the Lord, not of a tribe's god. The four considerations' world (semen to fire) is the property of all the worlds, which is why the sūrah's classes (near, right, left) run the whole of humanity and not Makkah alone. Readers who have followed the chapter from its first "when" will feel the title as a summing: the day that levels mountains and sorts every soul into three lists is, at its middle, declared to be the jurisdiction of the Lord of all worlds. The sūrah's self-description at 80 is so also its verdict on the mockers' parochial afterlife (the left's "and our forefathers as well?" — 48, in the chapter's own quotation of its mockers) — their hour belonged to their clan; this one belongs to every world.

**26:192–194: The Verse's Unfinished Mechanism, Completed Next Door**

The Poets' sūrah carries this sūrah's line almost letter for letter — "This is certainly a revelation from the Lord of all worlds, which the trustworthy spirit [Gabriel] brought down into your heart" (26:192–194, in the present translation's phrase) — and the pairing is the sūrah's own commentary: al-Wāqiʿah's bare "revelation from" is, in the neighbor, given its bearer and its destination (the heart). That the two chapters together state the doctrine in full: origin in the Lord of all worlds, channel in the faithful spirit, terminus in the Prophet's heart, purpose in warning. The shared wording is also a shared context of accusation — both chapters speak it where the audience suspects commerce or invention — and the classical readers loved to note that tanzīl verses of this form cluster precisely at the points of attack, as if the Qur'an's self-certification were, structurally, a wound-dressing: here at 80, the chapter's exhibit is given, in one line, the sender and the manner, before the sermon turns, at 81, to the audience's unacceptable response ("how can you take this message lightly. Make denial your livelihood?" — 81–82, plain quotation of the two lines' translation), and the reader who compares the chapters will find al-Wāqiʿah's 80 the shorter cousin, which is its own kind of force: the hinge of this sūrah has no room for the mechanism, only for the origin — and Makkah's accusation, "he fabricated it," was answered best, in the chapter's scale of values, by the fact of descent itself.

**The Verse Between Doxology and Indictment**

The word (tanzīlun) is a verbal noun of the intensive pattern of "sending down in stages," and its choice matters: not the bare "sent" (arsalā, the apostle's verb, which the Qur'an reserves for messengers rather than books) but the layered descent that its own audience's complaint had made a technical term — "if only an angel had been sent down with it" (the objection, in the phrase of 25:7 — *“If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner”*, plain paraphrase). The sūrahs note that tanzīl answers the how: a book sent in pieces, verse by verse, over years, is not a book dropped whole from a desk in heaven, and the sūrah, by preferring the word of gradual descent, makes its own mode of delivery part of the claim of origin.

## Verse 56:81

> How can you then take this message lightly

**Ḥadīth: The Sūrah's Self-Name, Here and at the Star-Sūrah's Rebuke**

The present translation's "message" is the Book's word ḥadīth — in its ordinary Arabic, "new talk," and in the Qur'an's own usage, regularly the Qur'an speaking about itself as an event of speech (the phrase is decisive at 52:34 and, for this verse's exact neighbor, at 53:59, where the Star sūrah asks "Do you find this revelation astonishing?" — the same deictic phrase "this ḥadīth," in the present translation's diction, and the same rebuke-context). Al-Wāqiʿah's 81 so borrows a self-designation the sūrahs had already made accusatory: the Book is a ḥadīth, a new thing said. The verb that follows — "take it lightly," rendered in the older sūrahs as "treat it as trivial," a verb whose sūrahs' glosses run to making a thing sport and distraction — describes, in the classical glosses, precisely the Makkān manner of meeting the sūrah: not refutation but deflection, the laugh that will not answer. The sūrah's audience is indicted, in 81, not for disbelief argued but for demeanor: the chapter that spent seventeen verses asking them to consider the world has arrived, at the Book itself, at the simplest charge — you handle this as you handle nothing you believe.

**The Crime Is a Manner**

Read against the chapter's opening courtroom (2: "no soul will deny its truthfulness"), 81 shows what disbelief costs in this sūrah's anthropology: not a creed rejected in good faith but a tone adopted toward a message — "taking lightly" is a register of the self, the shrug. The sūrah, which has described the left's whole sin as mockery at dinner (47, "they used to ask mockingly," in the present translation's bracket), returns, at the hinge, to its first charge against the audience's manners. This is late-Makkān preaching at its characteristic best: no philosophical objection is entertained because none was the real problem; the sūrah's hearers did not disprove resurrection, they yawned at it — and the commentary may cite, in this sūrah's own phrase, the Book's sharpest parallel, the "will not weep. Laugh" (53:60, in the Star sūrah's plain sequence just quoted): al-Wāqiʿah and al-Najm, side by side, put the human problem not in theology but in the face's choice between tears and the smile at news of that weight.

## Verse 56:82

> and repay ˹Allah for˺ your provisions with denial

**"Make Your Livelihood Denial": The Verse's Devastating Idiom**

The present translation's "repay… with denial" renders an Arabic more merciless than its English: tajʿalūna rizqa-kum a tukadhdhibū — "you make your rizq (your livelihood, your portion, your daily bread) to be that you call the Book a lie," and the sūrah, which has spent its whole middle arguing rizq through the grain (63–65), the water (68–70). Fire's provision for travelers (73), arrives here at the sūrah's final exchange-rate: these people have bartered sustenance itself for denial — they eat provision and pay for it with kufr, the root's double sense (ingratitude and disbelief) fused in a way the commentary has been tracking since verse 14. The phrase answers, from the other side, the keening farmers of 66–67, who called the lost harvest "great loss" and themselves "deprived": the living Makkān, says 82, are making deprivation their daily profession — they receive rizq and render back denial. The bracket ˹Allah for˺ in the present line names, correctly, the one creditor they are actually dealing with. Verse 82 is so the market-sūrah's closing invoice, the chapter's commercial register (shade like credit, payment "for what they used to do," 24) arriving at its last and worst transaction.

**Rizq as the Chapter's Buried Subject, Now Named**

The chapter's entire argument-half has been a rizq-tract without the word until now: "We made it a provision (matāʿ) for the travellers" (73), the farmers' ˹livelihood˺ bracket at 67, the "fruit and fodder" theology the Abasa sūrah shares with 73's provision-language (80:31–32 — *“and fruit and fodder”*) — and at 82 the noun rizq arrives, unannounced, in the mouth of the Book's accusation, which is the sūrah's subtlest move: it reveals, at the hinge's end, that its long argument about semen, seed, rain. Fire was not about "nature" but about rizq, provision, the daily givenness of what the audience had made light of as news. To "make your livelihood denial" is, in that light, the most literal description the chapter ever gives of the audience's religion: they are, at breakfast, eating kufr.

**"Denial," Not Unbelief: The Sūrah's Charge Is Verbal**

What the sūrah's verb indicts is takdhīb — calling it a lie, the act of the tongue that 81's "take lightly" set up — and not a concluded denial of the heart. That verbal nuance is the chapter's final pastoral severity: the audience's problem began as manner (the shrug at 81) and ends as speech-act ("He fabricated it," the charge recorded elsewhere of this exact audience, 25:4 and 46:7's "this is nothing but a fabrication of earlier times" in plain paraphrase — the same noun, in the other chapters' mouths, that 82's tukadhdhibūn is answering). Al-Wāqiʿah, uniquely, declines to quote the charge and instead names its verb — you make denial your livelihood — and the effect is that the sūrah never lets its hearers the dignity of a proposition rejected. Their position is described as a transaction, repeated daily, in which the commodity is their bread and the coin is "lie," and the commentary has already seen, at 41–48, that the left's sins were listed in the same commercial order (devouring, denying, until the certainty came, 46–48's sequence).

## Verse 56:83

> Why then ˹are you helpless˺ when the soul ˹of a dying person˺ reaches ˹their˺ throat

**"Why Then": The Question That Ends the Court**

The chapter's last interrogative is the shortest form Arabic has for a rebuke: fa-law lā — "why then do you not" — a compound of the conditional and the negation that the sūrahs' grammarians gloss as "then why do you not do it," and the sūrah, which has asked in turn about belief (57), mindfulness (62). Thanks (70), now asks the one question that convicts by grammar alone: if this Book were nothing, and you were nothing but yourselves, why do you not, at this throat, do something? The scene is chosen for the exactness of its helplessness: at no moment in the human calendar is the audience's advertised autonomy — the self-possession implicit in "is it you who create it, or We?" (59) — so visibly suspended as at the deathbed, where the strongest hand present does nothing but hold the wrist. Verse 83 is therefore the argument-half's final verdict, delivered as a question: the courtroom of semen, seed, water. Fire closes at the throat, because it is the throat where every plea the chapter refused to entertain — that persons own their lives — comes to trial honestly, once, for everybody, and loses.

**The Soul at the Throat: The Sūrah's One Anatomy**

The phrase (al-nafs balagha al-ḥulqūm, "the soul reached the throat") is the Qur'an's fixed image for dying. It names a location the living body knows from the inside — the lump, the constriction, the swallow that fails — and al-Wāqiʿah, whose whole argument has been built from what Makkah knows by heart — the ejected fluid of 58, the reaped harvest of 63, the drunk water of 68 — ends on the throat's last passage. The sūrahs' readers of the verse kept the physical vocabulary: al-ḥulqūm is the windpipe itself, and the soul's "reaching" it is, in the sūrahs' tellings, the moment of the taking, the point at which the departing becomes observable and no longer reversible. The sūrah's choice of this anatomy is also its final theology of the body: after the mockers' demand to be "restored to our former state, even after decayed bones" (79:10–11, in the other sūrah's plain report), the sūrah says: your first restoration is happening, hourly, in the room next to this one — no bones required, only breath failing. The throat as the last door every argument about resurrection must pass through, whether the debater likes it or not.

## Verse 56:84

> while you are looking on

**"Looking On" and "You Cannot See": The Chapter's Twin Verbs of Sight**

Verse 84 hands the audience sight and verse 85 takes it away — "but you cannot see" — and the pair is al-Wāqiʿah's final word on knowledge: looking on is the humans' true report (you are watching the body), not-seeing is their true limit (you are watching a taking you do not see). The sūrah lets both stand in consecutive lines as a single lesson about what presence is. The sūrahs' glosses supply the standard explanation of the contrast: the bystanders see the dying and do not see the angels, the soul, the scene of the taking — "you see what is happening to the body. You do not see who is doing it" — and the rhetorical gain for the sermon is decisive: the mockers' sneer at the unseen ("will we really be resurrected?" — 47, in the chapter's own quotation) dies at the throat not by proof but by proximity, because the room at every death contains exactly one unseen fact. Everyone in the room knows, hourly, that it is there, being executed by hands they cannot name. Al-Wāqiʿah's resurrection-argument, its most contested claim, ends, at 84–85, with the ordinary witness of rooms that the unseen is real.

**What the Verse Withholds: No Angels Here, Yet**

The commentators must also mark an absence: verse 84 does not yet say who is taking the soul — that is 85's "We are nearer" — and several sūrahs note that the angels of death, familiar elsewhere in the Book ("It is the angels who take the souls of the disbelievers, striking their faces and their backs," in the plain sense of 8:50 — *“If only you could see when the angels take the souls of the disbelievers, beating their faces and backs, ˹saying,˺ “Taste the torment of burning”*. "Our messengers take him, and they are not negligent," at 6:61 — *“When death comes to any of you, Our angels take their soul, never neglecting this duty”*, quoted already at this chapter's 83), are, in al-Wāqiʿah, left completely behind the verse's curtain: the sūrah's dying-room has human eyes only, and its unseen is unnamed until the next line. The restraint serves the argument: the chapter has spent its middle insisting that the audience's own sciences (semen, harvest, rain, fire) are agencies of God they do not see. 84 keeps the same method to the last — the death scene is told as the body's fact, not the angels' spectacle — so that when 85 finally introduces the nearer We, it arrives not as mythology added to medicine but as the medicine's own missing half, the sentence the watching was already shaped to receive. The sūrahs of the afterlife's details (the striking, the questioning in the grave, the long reports of the collections) are, here, respectfully, completely absent: al-Wāqiʿah's the teaching about the Last Day is a sūrah of bare, terrible proximities, not of wonders.

## Verse 56:85

> And We are nearer to such a person than you, but you cannot see

**"But You Cannot See": The Chapter's Final Epistemic Clause**

The closing words — "but you do not see" — repeat, at the throat, the refrain the sūrah has run under every consideration: "a form you do not know" (60), "if only you knew" (76). Now "you cannot see" — and their cumulative function is to teach the audience the shape of the word "near" in this Book: nearness without sight is the normal condition of the world (61–64), and death adds only the degree. The clause also completes 84's pair — "you are looking on… but you cannot see" — and the paradox is doctrine, not poetry: the room sees the body and does not see the taking. The sūrah's hearers, who wanted resurrection argued in dust and bones (47's sneer), are answered with the plainest fact available, that the unseen is not at the far end of a syllogism but at the near end of a bed. The "but" of the clause is the chapter's last, quiet word about human instruments: your eyes were open at the right time and are still, here, useless — which al-Wāqiʿah treats as news about seeing, not about dying.

**Two Nearnesses: The Sūrahs' Own Readings**

The first reads "We are nearer" by way of the angels — the "Our" of the taking is God's command executed by the angels of death, whose office the Book states elsewhere in plain terms ("Our messengers take him. They are not negligent," 6:61, quoted here as earlier at 83, and the striking hands of 8:50 — *“If only you could see when the angels take the souls of the disbelievers, beating their faces and backs, ˹saying,˺ “Taste the torment of burning”*, in the Spoils sūrah's plain account) — a reading that suits the sūrah's refusal elsewhere to name machinery (at 84 above, the angels are unseen here too). Either way the verse's function in al-Wāqiʿah is identical — it transfers the action of the deathbed from the watchers to the Owner of the soul — and that transfer, not the metaphysics of proximity, is what the sermon has been building its whole middle toward.

**The Predicate That Replaces the Challenge**

"Qarībun aqrabu minkum" — nearer than you — is the sūrah's only comparative of divine station. Its precision should be felt: the measure is not "nearer than his jugular vein," which is the other sūrah's figure for God's relation to a person in life (50:16, "We are nearer to him than his jugular vein," quoted here in the present translation's phrase). Nearer than you — nearer than the closest family hand, the physician, the son holding the pillow — and the dying man is, at 85, the point of comparison rather than the object.

## Verse 56:86

> Now, if you are not subject to Our Will ˹as you claim˺

**The Conditional at the Bedside**

Al-Wāqiʿah's sermon had run on questions since verse 57. At 86 it abandons even questioning for a conditional: "Now, if you are not subject to Our Will — then bring it back (87)" — the challenge-form of the Book's other great disputes (the Cow's "produce a sūrah like it… if what you say is true," 2:23, and the Jonah chapter's shorter dare, 10:38 — *“Or do they claim, “He made it up!”? Tell them ˹O Prophet˺, “Produce one sûrah like it then, and seek help from whoever you can—other than Allah—if what you say is true!”*, both quoted here in the present translation's phrase), transported from the literary arena to the dying-room, where no one has ever performed it. The grammar matters for the whole chapter's trajectory: the mockers had demanded resurrection be proved to their taste (47). The sūrah now grants them, hypothetically, the one doctrine under which no proof is needed and none is possible — that they answer to no one — and stakes everything on its corollary: if no Lord, no reckoning. No reckoning, then the soul at the throat is yours to call back, and the verse hangs its "if" open in front of a room that cannot fill it, the gentlest way a book has of executing an argument by experience.

## Verse 56:87

> bring that soul back, if what you say is true

**An Imperative Nobody Has Ever Obeyed**

Urdidūhā — "bring back that soul" — is the only imperative the sūrah ever addresses to its deniers. Its content is a command to reverse the one process the chapter has spent its whole middle declaring sovereign: if you own nothing in the making (57–73) but your claim to be unaccounted for (86), exercise that ownership at the throat, now, on this one soul. Return it to the body it has just left. The sūrahs' readers marked the form: divine challenges in this Book come in escalating generosity — produce a sūrah like it (2:23 — *“And if you are in doubt about what We have revealed to Our servant, then produce a sûrah like it and call your helpers other than Allah, if what you say is true”*), ten (the Hūd chapter's dare), or call witnesses to help (10:38, both in the present translations' wording — *“Or do they claim, “He made it up!”? Tell them ˹O Prophet˺, “Produce one sûrah like it then, and seek help from whoever you can—other than Allah—if what you say is true!”*) — and al-Wāqiʿah's is the shortest and the most local: no literary performance, no assembly, only the reversal of a dying in the room you are standing in. A challenge measured to the strength of the opponents' actual position — "if what you say is true" — and the sūrah, by accepting no substitute deed, makes the verse's silence (no one ever has, anywhere) the last testimony in the courtroom: the chapter does not need to refute the claim of the unaccountable. It hands the claim the only instrument its own doctrine recognizes, the will, at the only scene where the will is on parade, and lets the failure speak.

## Verse 56:88

> So, if the deceased is one of those brought near ˹to Us˺

**The Participle of the Brought-Near, Read After the Verse of Nearness**

The double vocabulary is the hinge of the whole chapter's theology of proximity: near to all as Lord, near to some as welcome — and the dying person of 83–87, whose throat was about to be the scene of the universal nearness, is now, at 88, addressed in the possibility of the particular: which nearness did your life befriend? The sūrah does not say "if he was noble" or "if he was pious". It says "of the brought-near," a phrase whose passive forces the reader to ask by Whom — and the whole argument-half already answered: only the One whose first-person verbs owned the semen, the crop, the rain, and the fire can bring near, and can refuse to.

**"If He Was": The Conditional That No Longer Challenges**

The sorting-clauses of 88, 90, and 92 all open with "fa-ammā in kāna" — "as for, if he was" — and the conditional particle, at this late point in the chapter, is doing something new with its old work: 86 had used "if" to test a claim ("if you are not subject to Our Will, bring the soul back"). 88–92 use "if" to sort a file — the difference is between a dare and a verdict, and the sūrah's grammar marks its own transition: the room where the challenge rang (87) is the room whose occupant is now conditionally classified, and nobody is being asked to perform anything anymore. The commentators will note that the Arabic of these verses contains no verb but "was" (kāna) — the copula of settled identity, not of act — and that this is the last verb the sūrah will lend its classes: everything they did has collapsed, at the throat, into what they were. The sūrah's ethics of habit (it has repeated "they used to" (kānū) again and again, in the left's "they used to ask mockingly" at 47 and the right's "they used to do" at 24) arrives at its tense: past, complete, and, on this side of the verdict, final. "If he was" is the sūrah's plainest and most terrible question, because it asks what has, by definition, stopped being answerable by new action.

## Verse 56:89

> then ˹such a person will have˺ serenity, fragrance, and a Garden of Bliss

**Three Nouns With No Verbs: The Welcome as Pure Gift**

The apodosis of the near-rank branch is three words — fa-riḥun wa-rayḥānun wa-jannatu naʿīm — and the sūrah, which has spent its whole final third attaching rewards to verbs of past service ("a reward for what they used to do," 24. "We shall requite," in its repeated phrase of giving-back), gives its highest class, at the throat, three bare nouns and no sentence: rest, fragrance, garden — a welcome, not a wage. The grammarians of the sūrahs read the nominal clause as immediacy: the dying muqarrab does not receive after the Day. What arrives at 89 arrives now, at the moment the soul reaches the throat (83), before reckoning, in the sūrah's own compressed time in which the Event and the dying are the same verb. And the near-rank's estate, unlike the right's in the chapter's earlier panorama (28–34), has no furniture listed here at all — the sūrah reserves its orchards, spouses. Cushions for a different register — because the entry of the nearest is a greeting scene, and the commentary's long observation at this chapter's first description of them (11–14) found the same subtraction: the nearer the class, the fewer the nouns.

The word rayḥān has stood, in this sūrah's earlier passage, at the cups of the near-rank — "and fragrance (rayḥān), a Garden of Bliss" — and this commentary weighed it at verses 18–19, where its double sense (the sweet plant and the ease of soul) divided the sūrahs: the present verse, repeating the noun at the bedside, decides nothing and doubles the gift: the nearest dying are welcomed with a scent that is also relief. The sūrah, whose left receive "a column of smoke" (43 — samūm in the other chapters' word, ṣaʿīr here and samūm at 43's neighborhood) a drink of hamīm (93) — and in the other chapter of the mounting hour, the striking wind called samūm paired with hamīm itself (52:16, plain paraphrase — *“Burn in it! It is the same whether you endure ˹it˺ patiently or not. You are only rewarded for what you used to do.”*) — defines its near-rank by olfaction as sharply as it defines the lost: two economies of smell, the garden's rayḥān against the smoke and the scorched wind.

The pairing completes the chapter's economics: it indicted making denial one's livelihood (82, at this commentary's verse), promised gratitude as the rarer harvest (70). Now gives the near-rank the naʿīm whose every hour the unthankful spent pretending was theirs — and the sūrahs' hearers would have known, at the word, the companion-verse they recite when the garden's name is spoken: the Rivalry chapter ends by promising that every person will, that Day, be questioned about the naʿīm — the delights they were given (102:6–8, in plain paraphrase — *“Then, on that Day, you will definitely be questioned about ˹your worldly˺ pleasures”*).

## Verse 56:90

> And if the deceased is one of the people of the right

**The Middle Branch of the Dying**

"Fa-ammā in kāna min aṣḥāb al-yamīn" — the second of the chapter's three bedside clauses (88, 90, 92) — reopens the census the sūrah took in its opening third ("and you will become three classes… the people of the right — what are the people of the right?" — 7–9, in the chapter's own repeated formula). The repetition of the census-formula is the device by which the final third claims to be the first third, spoken at a nearer microphone: what the chapter narrated cosmically at 7–40 it now whispers individually at 88–96. The middle class, largest of the saved, is where its hearers hoped to be listed. The verse supplies nothing new about the people of the right — no gardens, spouses, or cushions (the panorama's furniture at 28–34) — and the restraint is the point: at the throat, the saved are not re-described; they are re-counted. The sūrah, which opened its Garden-material at 12 with "in the Gardens of Bliss" for the fore-runners and gave the right their whole paragraph only later, now inverts that generosity — the branch of the middle is nearly all hinge and no content — until verse 91 hands them a single greeting, which is, in this chapter's arithmetic of nearness (traced at 88 and 89), the correct dose: the nearer the class, the shorter the sentence. The people of the right, not the nearest, receive exactly one line and a welcome inside it.

## Verse 56:91

> then ˹they will be told,˺ “Greetings to you from the people of the right.”

**The Whole Reward, in One Sentence of Speech**

The brackets matter for the grammar: the sūrah's own text says "fa-salāmul-lakum" — peace/greetings to you — without naming the speaker. The present translation's ˹they will be told˺ supplies the passive that Arabic conceals, which the sūrahs' readers mostly filled with the angels ("the angels will enter upon them from every gate, 'peace upon you'"). Others took the verse's own phrase — "from the people of the right" — literally: the saved greeting the saved, mutual welcome at the throat, and the commentary records both without choosing, because the verse's design tolerates either and the second is the sūrah's plain surface.

**What 91 Withholds, and Why That Is the Praise**

One final negative observation belongs to the entry: the verse promises nothing — no garden re-listed, no spouses, no fruits — and that withholding, at the chapter's end, is not a lacuna but a judgment about the value of the greeting: having described Paradise's furniture for its panorama, the sūrah decides. The bedside, that the saved dying one needs to hear only that they belong — that the company exists, that it greets, and that its welcome is the reward in its executable form. The commentary has argued (at 64–74, 77–82) that this sūrah's whole middle is an instruction in receiving — the world as gift, the Book as gift, gratitude as the only rational response — and 91 completes the instruction: the class of the many is received, at the end, by a greeting they neither earned nor arranged, only kept being part of. The sūrah, whose mockers had "asked mockingly" (47) at their dinners and whose farmers would "lament" (66) in their fields, gives its saved. Their eternal audition, a single sentence whose content is that someone said "to you." Every embellishment the panorama could supply is, at the throat, unnecessary — the sūrah's last word about the saved is not what they will get but who will welcome them — and the doxology that follows two verses later (96) is therefore, in this architecture, not an addition but the only possible response: if the end of the saved is a greeting from the saved, the end of the sermon is "glorify the Name," because the God who arranges welcomes has, at last, nothing left to say about the welcome itself.

## Verse 56:92

> But if such person is one of the misguided deniers

**They Become What They Did: The Verb Named Back at Its Owners**

"Fa-ammā in kāna min al-mukadhdhibīn" — "the deniers-of-lie" — and the participle is the chapter's final receipt: at 82 the audience was charged with making denial (takdhīb) their livelihood — "you repay your provisions with denial" — and at 92, at the throat, the doing has become the name: the one who called the Book a lie is, eternally, "of the deniers," his act fossilized into his class. The sūrah's whole anthropology has been this morphology in motion — you are what you repeatedly do to the given world — and the near-rank branch, by contrast, was named by what was done to them ("the brought-near," 88, passive, divine verb). The middle branch was named by position ("the people of the right"). Only the lost are named by their own transitive verb, active, unrelieved: the three branches' three nominal forms are the chapter's last, silent sermon on how names are acquired on this side of dying, and the denier's, alone, is self-made.

## Verse 56:93

> then their accommodation will be boiling water ˹to drink˺

**The Bracket's Verb: "˹To Drink˺" and the Gift That Is Not Taken**

The translators' ˹to drink˺ marks a verb the Arabic scene has already supplied at 54 ("then upon that, you will drink") and which 93 leaves to the noun — and the small bracket is the key to the verse's theology of shukr: the water of 68–70 was drinkable, sweet, unearned. Had to be received with gratitude to be received at all ("will you not then give thanks?" — 70, argued above at length); the water of 93 is drinkable only in the physical sense, ingested without reception, a rizq that cannot be thanked because it cannot be refused or enjoyed. Al-Wāqiʿah's entire hydrology, from "is it you who bring it down" (69) to this cup, says that the difference between the two waters is not chemistry but the drinker's history — and verse 93, placed one verse after the class was named "misguided deniers" (92), spells the class out as the condition of being handed a gift in a form that registers only as weight, the final drink as the final answer to a life of not-thanking: the sūrah's last hydrological word is that the ungrateful, at the end, still drink. That is exactly what they made of all the water they were given — an universe in which everything is poured and nothing is received.

## Verse 56:94

> and burning in Hellfire

**Ṣalīr: The Noun of Roasting, the Chapter's One Occurrence**

The word "burning" (ṣalīr) belongs, in the Arabic's phrase, to the roasting of what is exposed — the sūrahs' lexica gloss it as the blazing fire's heat upon the surface, the searing. It occurs in this chapter only here, in the present translation's rendering (73's bracketed "of the Hellfire" being supplied for the reminder-verse, as noted above). Its rarity is worth the note: the Book's commonst nouns for the abode — the "Jahannam" of the Medinan detail-passages and much of the Meccan, the "blazing" of the neighbors — are, in al-Wāqiʿah, absent or bracketed. The chapter reaches its final word about the lost with a rare, searing concrete noun: not a place-name but a happening, "a burning," the sūrah's last grammatical judgment that hell, in this book's telling, is an event the self is inside of, not a geography one travels to. Commentators of the sūrahs, who counted such things, read the choice against the near-rank's three nouns — welcome, scent, garden, two states and a place — and found the left's two: a drink and a state, no place at all: "burning in," with the "in" hanging loose, the sūrah declining even to let the lost own a habitation, which is the whole poetics of verse 94 in one preposition's ellipsis.

## Verse 56:95

> Indeed, this is the absolute truth

**"This": The Demonstrative That Certifies the Whole Chapter**

The present translation's "this" (hādhā) is, at 95, the most contested finger in the sūrah — pointing back at the two verses of burning and drink, at the whole three-branch sorting, or at the entire chapter from the Event's first fall — and the sūrahs' exegetes preserved the range without closing it, most settling on the broadest: the sūrah ends its verdicts by swearing that everything just said is true, the near-rank's welcome and the left's cup alike. The formula has an exact twin elsewhere in the Book: "And indeed, this ˹Quran˺ is the absolute truth" (69:51, the Haqqah's line, in the present translation's wording) — a late-Makkān sūrah that also opens with its Day's name (al-Ḥāqqah, "The Reality") and also ends its description by certifying the Book itself. The pair shows what 95 does: the demonstrative at the end of al-Wāqiʿah's last verdict behaves exactly as the Haqqah's behaves at its close — the chapter about the Day says, of itself, this is the truth — which is to say that in this register of sūrah, the final sentence about the teaching about the Last Day is always also the final sentence about the book that speaks it. No reader should be surprised that the chapter whose hinge-oath swore the recitation noble (75–77) ends by calling its contents the truth that certainty deserves.

**The Sūrah's Self-Referral, Read Against Its Bedside History**

The phrase — truth of certainty, ḥaqq al-yaqīn — is a genitive of kind. The sūrah's audience had heard its family before: "if you had known with knowing-certainty (bi-ʿilmi l-yaqīn)," the Rivalry chapter warns, "you would have seen the blazing-fire, then you would have seen it with the eye of certainty (ʿayni l-yaqīn)" (102:5–7, the sūrahs' plain account). The tradition of this vocabulary grades knowledge into levels — know it, see it, be there at it — which is the ladder the commentary has been tracing in this chapter since its certainty-lam at 49–50, "most certainly… will surely be gathered," where certainty first entered as God's own register.

## Verse 56:96

> So glorify the Name of your Lord, the Greatest

**The Repeat, and What the 22 Verses Between Did**

Verse 96 is verse 74 — "So glorify the Name of your Lord, the Greatest" — the present translation prints the two lines identically. The sūrah's whole design turns on what the interval did to them: at 74 the command closed an argument about the world's making (57–73). The hearer praised what he had been shown — seed, rain, fire, all of it unowned. At 96 the command closes the bedside sorting (88–95). The same words now fall on a hearer who has been walked through the Book's four predicates (77–80), his own triviality indicted (81–82), a soul at its throat (83–87), and three final doors (88–94) — the second doxology is the first one, priced. This is the sūrah's last lesson in the difference between the two acts of praise the chapter has trained: you can glorify the Maker of crops, which the argument-half could reach, or glorify the Lord whose name is Greatest because He is the one whose welcome at 89 and greeting at 91 you intend to arrive inside — and al-Wāqiʿah, which has been educating its audience's vocabulary all along (the rayḥān re-weighted at 18–19 and 89, the shade at 30 and 43, the certainty-lam at 50, 52, 54. 95), ends by handing the reciter back the sentence he heard at the hinge and letting him find, in it, all the chapter between.

**The End, and the Next Beginning**

Finally — the canon's quiet grace: in the received sequence this chapter's last word, "the Greatest," stands immediately before the opening of Sūrah al-Ḥadīd: "Whatever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies Allah. He is the Almighty, All-Wise" (57:1, the present translation verbatim) — the imperative al-Wāqiʿah gave the Prophet becoming, in the next sūrah's first word, the indicative of all creation: you are told to glorify; the heavens and the earth already do.
