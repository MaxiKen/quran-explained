# Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ (Chapter 112) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Pure Monotheism and the Absolute Unity of God**

Al-Ikhlāṣ, "The Purity of Faith" or "Sincerity"—also titled classically as *Sūrat al-Tawḥīd* (The Chapter of Divine Unity), *Al-Najat* (The Deliverance), and *Al-Ṣamad*—is the one-hundred-and-twelfth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 4 verses of early Makkan revelation. In the spiritual hierarchy of the Qur'anic revelation, Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ holds an exalted, peerless station: in authentic hadith recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (5015) and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (811) by Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ affirmed that reciting Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ is equivalent to *one-third of the entire Qur'an* (*taʿdilu thulutha al-Qur'ān*)!

**The Structural Symmetry of the Four Divine Attributes**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Ikhlāṣ is a fourfold diamond of divine theology:

1. Al-Aḥad (1): *“Say: He is Allah, the One (*Qul Huwa Allāhu Aḥad*)!”* While the word *Wāḥid* means one in numerical quantity, *Aḥad* means unique, indivisible, incomparable oneness—a singular unity that has no parts, no divisions, no second, and no equal.

2. Al-Ṣamad (2): *“Allah, the Eternal Refuge (*Allāhu al-Ṣamad*)!”* Ibn ʿAbbās and classical lexicographers explained *Al-Ṣamad* as the Absolute Master upon Whom all creation depends for every need, breath, and heartbeat, while He is completely self-enough, needing nothing and no one. The Negation of Lineage (3): *“He neither begets nor is born (*Lam yalid wa-lam yūlad*)!”* Negates both biological ancestry and biological descent: refuting the polytheists who claimed angels were the daughters of God, the Christians who claimed Jesus was the Son of God, and the ancient dualists who claimed gods were born of primordial chaos.

4. The Negation of Equivalence (4): *“Nor is there to Him any equivalent (*Wa-lam yakun lahū kufuwan aḥad*)!”* Concluding with the absolute metaphysical boundary: nothing in creation—no angel, no prophet, no human intellect, and no cosmic force—can ever share, match, or approximate the Essence of God.

It takes its title from *Ikhlāṣ*—meaning that it purifies the human soul from every speck of idolatry, anthropomorphism, and religious error, distilling the entire metaphysical reality of God into four brief, perfect sentences: *“Say, 'He is Allah, [who is] One; Allah, the Eternal Refuge; He neither begets nor is born, nor is there to Him any equivalent!'”* (112:1–4).

The historical occasion of al-Ikhlāṣ is recorded in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī (3364) and Musnad Aḥmad from Ubayy ibn Kaʿb.

## Verse 112:1

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “He is Allah—One ˹and Indivisible˺

**The Chapter Begins With an Order to Speak**

*Qul* — say. The first word of the sūrah is a command addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, and it puts the whole chapter in the register of dictation. What follows is not a description the messenger offers of what he has understood; it is a text he is told to utter, word for word, to whoever asked.

A minority of the Companions is reported to have recited the chapter without the *qul*, on the ground that the Prophet ﷺ did not need to be instructed in something he already knew. Keeping it is also better sense: the verse is a public answer to a public question, and "say" is the direction for delivering it.

**Allah: The Name That Gathers All the Others**

Between the pronoun and the predicate stands the name: *Allāh aḥad*. In the tradition Allāh is called the gathering name, *al-ism al-jāmiʿ*, because every other divine name and attribute is gathered inside it in principle. The Qur'an says as much in several places: *“Allah has the Most Beautiful Names. So call upon Him by them”* (7:180); *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Call upon Allah or call upon the Most Compassionate—whichever you call, He has the Most Beautiful Names.’”* (17:110); *“Allah—there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him.

Al-Rāzī used that structure to read this verse as a complete statement of theology in two words. Allāh conveys the totality of the positive attributes — Creator, Knower, Powerful; *aḥad* conveys the totality of the negative ones — without body, without substance, without accident. Affirmation and negation together, in a phrase short enough to say in one breath, are what make the chapter a creed rather than a description.

**The Question This Verse Was Answering**

The occasion of revelation is reported from Ubayy ibn Kaʿb: the idolaters said to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, "Trace the lineage of your Lord for us," and this sūrah came down in answer. The report is in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3364, which Dārussalām grades *daʿīf* on its chain, though its content has been accepted as the occasion by the commentators across the schools. Other accounts put the same question to a group of Jews, and a third to a group of Christians.

**Equivalent to a Third of the Qur'an**

Two reports in the Ṣaḥīḥ collections state the chapter's weight. Abū al-Dardāʾ narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said: "Is any one of you incapable of reciting a third of the Qur'an in a night?" When the Companions asked how that could be done, he answered that *"Say: He is Allah, One"* is equivalent to a third of the Qur'an — Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 811a. Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī reported that a man heard another reciting this sūrah over and over, thought the recitation insufficient, and mentioned it in the morning; the Prophet ﷺ said, "By Him in Whose Hand my soul is, it equals one-third of the Qur'an" — Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6643.

A third report shows the chapter inside daily prayer. A commander sent on an expedition used to close every unit of his prayer with this sūrah; asked why, he said it describes the Most Merciful and he loved to recite it. The Prophet ﷺ answered: "Tell him that Allah loves him" — Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7375.

## Verse 112:2

> Allah—the Sustainer ˹needed by all˺

**Al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm: The Qur'an's Own Gloss on the Word**

Because *ṣamad* occurs once, its meaning has to be reconstructed from parallel expressions, and the Qur'an has a formula that does exactly the same work in two words. *Al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* — the Ever-Living, the All-Sustaining — appears in the three great verses of lordship: *“Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining.”* (3:2); the same phrase opening the Verse of the Throne (2:255); and *“And all faces will be humbled before the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining.”* (20:111).

**The One Who Remains When Everything Else Stops**

A third reading takes *al-Ṣamad* as the eternal, self-enough Being who continues after creation has ceased — the One who has always been and will always be. This is the sense in which the word is often translated "the Everlasting" or "the Eternal Refuge," and it is supported by the Qur'an's own statements elsewhere.

*“He is the First and the Last, the Most High and Most Near, and He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.”* (57:3) — first without beginning, last without end. *“Everything is bound to perish except He Himself. All authority belongs to Him. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.”* (28:88). And in the sūrah of the Most Merciful: *“Every being on earth is bound to perish. Only your Lord Himself, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain ˹forever˺.”* (55:26–27).

Reading 112:2 beside 2:255 is the most reliable way to fix the meaning of the rare word. Where the Throne Verse explains at length — drowsiness does not overtake Him, His seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, their preservation does not tire Him — this verse says it in one word and moves on.

**God Recites This Chapter Back**

A ḥadīth qudsī in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī puts verses two to four into the mouth of God. Abū Hurayrah reported the Messenger of Allah ﷺ as saying: "Allah said: The son of Adam tells a lie against Me, and he has no right to do so; and he abuses Me, and he has no right to do so. His lying against Me is his saying that I will not recreate him as I created him the first time. And his abusing Me is his saying that Allah has begotten children, while I am the self-enough Master, Whom all creatures need, Who begets not nor was He begotten, and there is none like unto Me." That is Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4975.

**Two Words That Carry the Whole Claim**

*Allāh al-Ṣamad* is the shortest complete religious sentence in the Qur'an. It names God and states His relation to everything that is not God, and it does both without a verb. Arabic allows that economy; the verse uses it.

The density explains the chapter's place in practice. It is recited in the obligatory prayers and in the supererogatory ones, it is the sūrah most often memorized after the Fātiḥah. It is part of the nightly practice reported from ʿĀʾishah: the Prophet ﷺ would join his hands, breathe into them, recite this sūrah and the two that follow it, and wipe over his body as far as he could, three times — Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5056, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī. A text that short can be said in the dark, by anyone, in any state, and it says everything the longer chapters say about who God is.

## Verse 112:3

> He has never had offspring, nor was He born

**Two Negations Pointing in Opposite Directions**

*Lam yalid wa lam yūlad* — He has not begotten, and He was not begotten. The particle *lam* negates a past action with continuing force, which is why the translation renders it as "has never": the statement is not about a period in God's history but about the whole of it. Nothing that could be called His child has ever existed, and nothing that could be called His father ever did.

*Yalid* is active and *yūlad* passive, and they are built from the same root, so the sentence is a single word turned around. The Qur'an often makes its negations symmetrical in this way — 57:3, *"He is the First and the Last"* — and the symmetry is the argument.

**The Claim Being Answered Was a Meccan One**

The assertion this verse denies had a specific shape in the Prophet's audience. The Quraysh and the tribes around them ascribed offspring to God, and the offspring were daughters: the angels. The Qur'an argues with that claim repeatedly and with evident irritation. *“Ask them ˹O Prophet˺ if your Lord has daughters, while the pagans ˹prefer to˺ have sons.”* (37:149); *“‘Allah has children.’ They are simply liars.”* (37:152); *“Has He chosen daughters over sons?”* (37:153).

The same charge appears in al-Anʿām: *“Yet they associate the jinn with Allah ˹in worship˺, even though He created them, and they falsely attribute to Him sons and daughters out of ignorance. Glorified and Exalted is He above what they claim!”* (6:100). And the verse that follows states the argument this sūrah compresses: *“˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and earth. How could He have children when He has no mate? He created all things and has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of everything.”* (6:101).

**The Word "Son" Does Not Mean the Same Thing in the Creed**

The verse has been read for centuries as a denial of Christian teaching. The reading is not baseless: the Qur'an elsewhere rejects the claim explicitly, as in *“The Jews say, ‘Ezra is the son of Allah,’ while the Christians say, ‘The Messiah is the son of Allah.’”* (9:30) and *“They say, ‘The Most Compassionate has offspring.’ You have certainly made an outrageous claim”* (19:88–89).

**Begetting Requires a Peer, and a Peer Divides God**

The Qur'an presses the same point cosmologically: *“Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted. So Glorified is Allah, Lord of the Throne, far above what they claim.”* (21:22). Plurality in the divine produces conflict; conflict produces disorder; the world is ordered; therefore the divine is not plural.

The jinn make the same confession in their own chapter, and their wording is almost identical to this one: *“˹Now, we believe that˺ our Lord—Exalted is His Majesty—has neither taken a mate nor offspring”* (72:3), immediately after admitting that *“the foolish of us used to utter ˹outrageous˺ falsehoods about Allah”* (72:4). The denial of mate and offspring is, in the Qur'an's own presentation, what a rational being concludes about God once the foolishness is dropped.

The Qur'an supports the gloss in the passage already quoted (6:101) and in the accusation against those who built kinship between God and the jinn: *“They have also established a ˹marital˺ relationship between Him and the jinn. Yet the jinn ˹themselves˺ know well that such people will certainly be brought ˹for punishment˺.”* (37:158).

The Qur'an's most careful statement about Jesus belongs beside this verse, because it makes the same distinction in the same compressed way: *“It is not for Allah to take a son! Glory be to Him. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is!”* (19:35).

Al-Nisāʾ states it at length: *“The Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary, was no more than a messenger of Allah and the fulfilment of His Word through Mary and a spirit ˹created by a command˺ from Him. So believe in Allah and His messengers and do not say, ‘Trinity.’ Stop!—for your own good. Allah is only One God. Glory be to Him! He is far above having a son!”* (4:171).

## Verse 112:4

> And there is none comparable to Him.”

**Beyond Likeness and Yet Near**

The tradition resolved the pair by distinguishing God as He is in Himself from God as He is disclosed. With respect to His essence, nothing is comparable to Him and nothing perceives Him; about His manifestation in the world, He is nearer than the world is to itself — *“and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein”* (50:16). Transcendence and nearness are not competing claims on this account but claims about different relations.

**Another Verse Already Said This, and This One Stops Earlier**

Al-Shūrā contains the Qur'an's other great denial of likeness: *“˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and the earth. He has made for you spouses from among yourselves, and ˹made˺ mates for cattle ˹as well˺—multiplying you ˹both˺. There is nothing like Him, for He ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Seeing.”* (42:11). The two verses agree in content and differ in what follows the denial.

42:11 continues into attributes. Having said that nothing is like Him, it immediately says that He hears and sees — the two faculties creatures also have, named in the same words used of creatures. The verse therefore performs the difficult operation the tradition calls affirmation without likening: the names are shared, the reality is not.

112:4 stops at the denial. No attributes follow, no qualification, no continuation. The chapter ends on the refusal of comparison, which makes it the sharpest statement of transcendence in the Qur'an and also the one most in need of being read beside 42:11. Taken alone, a bare denial could be mistaken for an emptying of God; the sūrah avoids that by putting the affirmation first, in verses one and two.

**The Word Order Is the Argument**

The Arabic runs *wa lam yakun lahu kufuʾan aḥad* — literally, "and there has not been, for Him, an equal, anyone." The predicate *kufuʾan* is advanced before the subject *aḥad*, and Arabic advances what it wants to emphasize. The fronting puts the emphasis on "equal" rather than on "anyone," so that the sentence denies the category before it denies the members of it.

The verse raises an obvious difficulty: if nothing resembles God, in what sense is He present to the world? The Qur'an answers by holding two statements together. *“Everything is bound to perish except He Himself.”* (28:88). And: *“To Allah belong the east and the west, so wherever you turn you are facing ˹toward˺ Allah. Surely Allah is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing.”* (2:115).

**Why the Chapter Ends on Unlikeness**

A creed that finished on "He is one" would leave the hearer with a number; a creed that finishes on "nothing is comparable to Him" leaves him with a silence that has been earned rather than assumed. The four verses can be recited in a single breath. The tradition treats them as enough: this is the sūrah a man recited in every prayer out of love. Was told that Allah loved him for it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7375); it is one of the three chapters the Prophet ﷺ recited into his palms each night before wiping them over his body, as reported by ʿĀʾishah in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5056.

Al-Ikhlāṣ is usually the second chapter a Muslim child memorizes after the Fātiḥah.
