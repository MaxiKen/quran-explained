# Sūrah al-ʿAlaq (Chapter 96) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Cave of Ḥirā' and the Choke of Abū Jahl**

The historical canvas of al-ʿAlaq spans two dramatic moments: the dawn of revelation at Ḥirā' and the confrontation with pagan tyranny in the courtyard of the Kaʿbah. In the first moment, recorded by ʿĀ'ishah in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (3), the Angel Gabriel seized the forty-year-old Muḥammad in the cave, embracing him with crushing intensity three times, commanding each time: *“Iqra'!”* (Read!), to which the unlettered prophet replied: *“I cannot read!”*, before the first five verses were inscribed forever upon his heart. The second historical moment (verses 9–19) occurred years later in Makkah, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim (2797) about Abū Jahl, who swore by the idols that if he ever saw Muḥammad praying beside the Kaʿbah, he would trample the Prophet's neck into the dust. When the Prophet stood to pray, Abū Jahl approached him, but suddenly recoiled in blind terror, his hands shielding his face. When asked what happened, Abū Jahl gasped: *“Between me and him was a ditch of blazing fire, terrors, and wings!”* The Prophet ﷺ remarked: *“Had he drawn one step closer, the angels would have torn him limb from limb!”*

**The Structural Architecture and the Crushed Forelock**

The architecture of Sūrah al-ʿAlaq moves across three powerful movements:

1. The Primordial Epiphany of Knowledge (1–5): The command to read in the Name of the Creating Lord, the humble origin from a clinging clot, the infinite generosity of God (*al-Akram*), the divine pedagogy of the Pen (*ʿallama bi-l-qalam*), and the illumination of human ignorance.

2. The Pathology of Tyranny: Al-Ṭughyān (6–8): Unveils the timeless psychological root of human oppression: *“No! Indeed, man transgresses (*inna al-insāna la-yaṭghā*) because he sees himself self-enough (*a ra'āhu istaghnā*)!”* It shatters this illusion with the sobering reality of verse 8: *“Indeed, to your Lord is the return (*Inna ilā Rabbika al-rujʿā*)!”*

3. The Confrontation at the Sanctuary and the Call to Prostration (9–19): Stages the arrogant tyrant who tries to forbid a servant when he prays (*ara'ayta al-ladhī yanhā ʿabdan idhā ṣallā*). Verse 14 delivers the piercing question: *“Does he not know that Allah sees?”* It threatens the tyrant with humiliation in verse 15: *“No! If he does not desist, We will surely drag him by the forelock—a lying, sinning forelock (*nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭi'ah*)!”* Let him summon his tribal council (*nādiyah*); God will summon the violent angels of punishment (*al-Zabāniyah*)! The chapter concludes with the triumphant, defiant liturgical command to the Prophet: *“No! Do not obey him. But prostrate and draw near (*wa-sjud wa-qtarib*)!”*

It takes its title from verse 2, which commemorates the biological miracle of human embryogenesis: *“Read in the name of your Lord who created—created man from a clinging clot (*min ʿalaq*)! Read, and your Lord is the Most Generous, who taught by the pen, taught man that which he knew not!”* (96:1–5).

## Verse 96:1

> Read, ˹O Prophet,˺ in the Name of your Lord Who created—

**The First Revealed Words Were an Instruction, Not a Statement**

These five verses are the earliest revelation the Prophet ﷺ received, and the fact is not a pious tradition but a documented account. ʿĀʾishah described the beginning of revelation in detail: it started as true dreams that came like the breaking of daylight, then a love of solitude was given to him, and he would withdraw to the cave of Ḥirāʾ for nights of worship before returning to his family. Then the angel came and said *iqraʾ*, read. He answered, *mā anā bi-qāriʾ* — I am not one who reads. The angel pressed him until he thought he would die, released him, and repeated the command three times, then recited these opening verses. The account stands at the head of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī's collection, numbered 3, and again in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 160. What the placement signals is that the whole revelation begins with a command addressed to a man who could not carry it out.

**An Unlettered Man Ordered to Read**

The tension between the command and the answer is the hinge of the passage. He did not say he was unwilling; he said he was not a reader. The Qur'an insists on this condition repeatedly and treats it as evidence rather than embarrassment: those who follow the unlettered Messenger (7:157 — *“˹They are˺ the ones who follow the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, whose description they find in their Torah and the Gospel.”*). You did not recite any book before it, nor did you write one with your right hand, for then those who falsify would have doubted (29:48 — *“You ˹O Prophet˺ could not read any writing ˹even˺ before this ˹revelation˺, nor could you write at all. Otherwise, the people of falsehood would have been suspicious”*); He is the One who sent among the unlettered a Messenger from themselves (62:2 — *“He is the One Who raised for the illiterate ˹people˺ a messenger from among themselves—reciting to them His revelations, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom, for indeed they had previously been clearly astray”*). A man who cannot read is commanded to read, and what he receives is a book. The arrangement removes the most obvious natural explanation for the text. If the recipient had been a scholar, the content could be attributed to his learning; because he was not, the content must come from elsewhere. The command was not a request for literacy but the installation of a revelation.

The prepositional phrase *bi-smi rabbika* does real grammatical and religious work. The command to read is immediately qualified: reading happens in a name, and the name is not the reader's. Everything that follows in the sūrah develops this single point. Knowledge is not acquired by the knower's power but received from a Lord, and the Lord is named as *rabb* — the one who nourishes, raises, sustains and completes — before any other description. The choice is significant for a man who had just been told to do something he could not do.

## Verse 96:2

> created humans from a clinging clot

**ʿAlaq: The Word and What It Names**

The term *ʿalaq* is a plural or collective noun from the root meaning to cling or hang. Classical usage covers a leech, a blood clot, and anything that attaches itself to something else. Translators choose between "clinging clot," "clot of blood" and "embryo," and the ambiguity is in the original rather than in the translation. What all the senses share is attachment and dependence: a thing that has no independent existence and is held in place by another. The Qur'an uses the same word in the same sequence elsewhere: We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging clot, then from a lump of flesh, formed and unformed (22:5 — *“O humanity! If you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then ˹know that˺ We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh—fully formed or unformed—in order to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you.”*). And in Sūrat al-Mu'minūn: then We made the drop into a clinging clot, then We made the clot into a lump of flesh (23:14 — *“then We developed the drop into a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then developed the clot into a lump ˹of flesh˺, then developed the lump into bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, then We brought it into being as a new creation.”*). The word is not a poetic flourish but a term of art in the Qur'an's account of development.

**Why the Embryo and Not the Adult**

Every account of creation could have begun with the finished human being — the upright posture, the express speech, the reasoning mind. The Qur'an consistently chooses the beginning instead, and the choice is argumentative. A finished human being invites admiration; an embryo invites the question of who made it. The sūrah of al-Qiyāmah puts the same point with unmistakable directness: was he not a drop of emitted fluid, then a clinging clot, then He fashioned and proportioned him and made of him the two pairs, male and female? Is He not able to give life to the dead? (75:37–40 — *“Were they not ˹once˺ a sperm-drop emitted”*). Beginning with the clot forecloses the possibility of self-congratulation. Whatever a person becomes, he became it from something that had no power, no will and no shape, and the one who gave him shape has not relinquished that role.

**The Second Making Is Easier Than the First**

The verse does one further piece of work, which the Qur'an states explicitly elsewhere. God is the one who originates creation and then repeats it, and it is easier for Him (30:27 — *“And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it—which is even easier for Him.”*). The comparison is not a claim that God finds things difficult; it is a way of speaking to human reasoning, which measures possibility by precedent. If the first assembly happened from nothing — from dust, from a drop, from a clot — then the reassembly is not a harder problem but a solved one. Al-ʿAlaq plants the premise in its second verse without drawing the conclusion, because the conclusion is not what this sūrah is about. What it needs here is only the fact of dependence: the man who will be told, six verses later, that he exceeds all bounds, is a man who began as a clinging thing and was made into what he is.

## Verse 96:3

> Read! And your Lord is the Most Generous

**Al-Akram: The Superlative Placed Where the Reader Needs It**

The word *akram* is the elative of *karīm*: the most generous, the most noble, the most bountiful. It appears in the Qur'an as a divine description in a small number of places, and here it is attached to the command to read rather than to a statement about creation. The placement is the point. A man told to do what he cannot do is not first told that God is powerful — power would only underline his incapacity — but that God is generous. Generosity is the attribute that bridges the gap between the command and the ability to fulfil it. The same word is used of God elsewhere with the same logic: O humanity, what has deluded you about your Lord, the Most Generous (82:6 — *“O humanity! What has emboldened you against your Lord, the Most Generous”*), where the question is precisely why a creature would doubt a Lord described in these terms. Al-ʿAlaq answers the question before it is asked, by naming generosity as the reason the command can be given at all.

## Verse 96:4

> Who taught by the pen—

**The First Created Thing Was a Writing Instrument**

The Prophet ﷺ connected the pen to creation itself. ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit reported that he heard him say: the first thing Allah created was the pen; He said to it, write; it asked, what should I write, my Lord; He said, write what has been decreed about everything until the Hour comes. The report is recorded in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4700 and graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī. The claim is not that a physical reed existed before the heavens; the interpretive tradition understands the pen as the instrument by which the decree was inscribed. What matters for this verse is the association the report establishes between the pen and divine knowledge. The same instrument that fixes God's decree in the unseen is the instrument by which human beings fix what they know in the seen. Teaching by the pen is therefore not an incidental metaphor but a description of how knowledge is transmitted at both ends of the scale.

**Nūn. By the Pen and What They Write**

The Qur'an swears by the pen elsewhere, and the oath is worth reading beside this verse. Sūrat al-Qalam opens: Nūn. By the pen and what they inscribe. You are not, by the grace of your Lord, a madman (68:1–2 — *“Nũn. By the pen and what everyone writes”*). The oath serves to vindicate the Prophet against a charge of insanity, and it does so by appealing to the written word. The same instrument appears here as an instrument of divine teaching. Together the two passages give the pen a double role in the Qur'an's self-understanding: it is how God teaches, and it is how the truth of the message is preserved and defended. A community formed around a recitation was also, from its first verses, a community told to write things down — and the earliest Muslims did, keeping written records of the revelation alongside the memorised text.

**Three Pens: Decree, Record, and the Human Hand**

Commentators distinguish several kinds of pen behind the single word. The first is the pen of the decree, by which everything that will happen until the Hour was written. The second is the pen of the recording angels, who write down what human beings do: there are indeed keepers over you, noble scribes who know whatever you do (82:10–11 — *“honourable angels, recording ˹everything˺”*); and the two receivers, seated to the right and to the left, noting every word (50:17–18 — *“As the two recording-angels—˹one˺ sitting to the right, and ˹the other to˺ the left—note ˹everything˺”*). The third is the ordinary pen in a human hand. The three are not competing meanings but three instances of one principle: knowledge that matters is fixed in writing. The verse's use of the definite article — *bi-l-qalam*, by the pen — allows all three to stand behind it, and the breadth is appropriate to a statement about how God teaches a species.

**Writing and the Responsibility Attached to It**

The verse does not stop at describing a gift; it implies an obligation. If God taught by the pen, then what a person writes is not a neutral act performed with a divine instrument. The Qur'an makes the connection explicit when it warns against writing something with one's own hand and then claiming it comes from God (2:79 — *“So woe to those who distort the Scripture with their own hands then say, “This is from Allah”—seeking a fleeting gain! So woe to them for what their hands have written, and woe to them for what they have earned”*), and again when it describes the record laid open on that Day, with the wicked crying that it does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted (18:49 — *“What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?”*). A pen in a human hand carries the same weight as a tongue: it fixes a claim, and the claim is answerable. The early Muslim community understood this. The written word was used for treaties, for the revelation itself, and for the transmission of the Prophet's sayings, and disputes over attribution were settled by reference to chains and written records rather than by assertion. To be taught by the pen is to be trusted with something that outlives the writer, and the sūrah's generosity is a trust before it is a gift.

## Verse 96:5

> taught humanity what they knew not

**Adam Was Taught the Names**

The Qur'an locates the origin of human knowledge in a specific act. God taught Adam the names, all of them, then presented the things to the angels and asked them to name them if they were truthful; when they could not, Adam was told to inform them of the names (2:31–33 — *“Allah said, “O Adam! Inform them of their names.”*). The angels' reply is worth keeping in view: glory be to You, we have no knowledge except what You have taught us (2:32 — *“They replied, “Glory be to You! We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise.”*). The verse is a decisive statement about the direction of teaching in the Qur'an's cosmos. Knowledge flows downward. Even beings of light and obedience have only what has been given to them, and the human being's advantage at the beginning was not power but a taught vocabulary. Al-ʿAlaq compresses the same claim into three words, and the commentators connect the two passages explicitly, taking *al-insān* here either as humanity generally or as Adam specifically.

**You Knew Nothing When You Came Out of the Womb**

The Qur'an states the individual case with equal bluntness. God brought you out of your mothers' wombs knowing nothing, and gave you hearing, sight and hearts, so that perhaps you would be grateful (16:78 — *“And Allah brought you out of the wombs of your mothers while you knew nothing, and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect so perhaps you would be thankful”*). The three faculties named are instruments of acquisition, not sources of content: hearing to receive, sight to observe, and the heart to process. All three are gifts, and the verse ties them to gratitude. Al-ʿAlaq makes the same point without the list, and the two together describe a coherent picture. A human being arrives empty, is equipped with means, and is then taught — by parents, by teachers, by books, and ultimately by God, who is the one who arranged that any of it should work. The claim is not that effort is worthless. It is that effort operates on equipment and material that were given.

**Taught What You Did Not Know: The Prophet's Own Case**

The verse has a specific application to the man who received it. God has sent down to you the Book and the wisdom, and taught you what you did not know; and the bounty of God upon you is great (4:113 — *“Allah has revealed to you the Book and wisdom and taught you what you never knew.”*). The wording is almost identical to this verse, and it is addressed to the same person. That parallel is important for how the sūrah should be read: the one commanded to read is himself included in the category of those taught what they did not know. He is not a teacher standing outside the process.

**Knowledge as a Gift That Rules Out Boasting**

The verse closes the sūrah's first movement, and the way it closes it determines what the second movement can say. If knowledge is received, then the person who has it is not its source, and his possession is a position of trust rather than an achievement. The Qur'an presses this repeatedly: the person who knows is not equal to the one who does not know (39:9 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Are those who know equal to those who do not know?”*), yet knowledge does not make the knower superior in God's reckoning except through what he does with it — the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most mindful of Him (49:13 — *“Surely the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous among you.”*). The connection between verse five and the transgression described in verse six is therefore not a change of subject but a consequence. The creature described here as taught what he did not know is the same creature who, in the very next breath, exceeds all bounds. The sūrah sets the gift and the ingratitude side by side without comment, and lets the contrast speak.

## Verse 96:6

> Most certainly, one exceeds all bounds

**Kallā: The Particle That Stops an Argument**

The sūrah turns on a single word. *Kallā* is one of the most forceful particles in Arabic and among the hardest to translate: no, on the contrary, certainly not, far from it. It does not merely negate; it rejects a premise and refuses to let the discussion continue on that footing. The Qur'an uses it as a marker of strong disapproval, and al-ʿAlaq uses it twice, here and in the closing verse, which brackets the second half of the sūrah between two refusals. What is being refused is not stated in the verse itself; the particle assumes the reader knows. After five verses describing a creature made from a clot and taught what he did not know, the unspoken premise is obvious: that any of this is his own doing. *Kallā* cuts it off, and the statement that follows explains what happens to a creature who entertains it.

**Pharaoh: The Qur'an's Own Example of the Same Verb**

The sūrah does not name an example, but the Qur'an supplies one in a passage that uses the identical verb. When God sent Mūsā to Pharaoh, the instruction was: go to Pharaoh, for he has exceeded all bounds (79:17 — *“˹commanding,˺ “Go to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺”*). The same form, *ṭaghā*, describes the ruler who declared himself the highest lord (79:24 — *“saying, “I am your lord, the most high!”*) and whose punishment the Qur'an presents as the standing case of boundary-crossing. The parallel is instructive because it shows what the excess consists of. Pharaoh's transgression was not mainly cruelty but a claim about himself: that he was above the limits that apply to other creatures. Al-ʿAlaq describes the same movement in an ordinary person, without the armies and without the throne, which is precisely what makes the verse disturbing. The condition does not require power to appear. It requires only the feeling described in the verse that follows.

## Verse 96:7

> once they think they are self-sufficient

**Qārūn's Claim, and the Answer to It**

The Qur'an gives the feeling a named example. Qārūn was given treasure whose keys alone burdened a group of strong men, and when his people told him not to exult, he answered: I was given it only because of knowledge I have (28:78 — *“He replied, “I have been granted all this because of some knowledge I have.” Did he not know that Allah had already destroyed some from the generations before him who were far superior to him in power and greater in accumulating ˹wealth˺? There will be no need for the wicked to be asked about their sins”*). The claim is the exact form of *istighnāʾ* the verse describes — an achievement read as self-generated, with the source of the achievement attributed to the achiever. The verse that follows asks whether he did not know that God had destroyed, before him, generations stronger than he and greater in accumulation (28:78 — *“Did he not know that Allah had already destroyed some from the generations before him who were far superior to him in power and greater in accumulating ˹wealth˺?”*), and the story ends with the earth swallowing him and his house (28:81 — *“Then We caused the earth to swallow him up, along with his home. There was no one to help him against Allah, nor could he even help himself”*). The narrative is a worked example of the two verses of al-ʿAlaq put together: a man who exceeded his bounds because he considered himself enough, and the end that follows. What Qārūn forgot is what this sūrah states in its second line — that he was made from a clot.

**The Test the Qur'an Applies to Any Possession**

There is a practical way to tell whether a possession has produced this feeling, and the Qur'an supplies it. Ask where the credit was directed when the thing was acquired. Qārūn attributed his treasure to his own knowledge (28:78 — *“He replied, “I have been granted all this because of some knowledge I have.” Did he not know that Allah had already destroyed some from the generations before him who were far superior to him in power and greater in accumulating ˹wealth˺? There will be no need for the wicked to be asked about their sins”*); the owner of the two gardens in Sūrat al-Kahf attributed his estate to himself and doubted that it would ever perish (18:35–36 — *“And he entered his property, while wronging his soul, saying, “I do not think this will ever perish”*). Both men had, in ordinary terms, legitimate achievements. The failure was in the accounting, not in the achievement. A person who attributes a success to God and works for the next one has not fallen into *istighnāʾ*; a person who attributes it wholly to himself has, whatever the size of the success. The sūrah's five opening verses are the correct accounting laid out in order — created, sustained, taught — and the feeling described here is what happens when that order is reversed and the creature is placed at the head of it.

The verse is often read as a critique of riches, and the reading loses the point. *Istighnāʾ* is not having; it is a judgement about having. The Qur'an praises wealthy believers elsewhere without qualification — the sūrah of al-Baqarah describes those who spend from what they have been provided (2:3 — *“who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and donate from what We have provided for them”*), and the promise of increase is attached to gratitude rather than to poverty: if you are grateful, I will certainly give you more (14:7 — *“And ˹remember˺ when your Lord proclaimed, ‘If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful, surely My punishment is severe.’”*). What the verse identifies as dangerous is the conclusion a person draws from his possessions, not the possessions themselves. That distinction has practical consequences. A person cannot remove the risk by giving everything away, because the feeling can attach to asceticism, to learning, or to reputation as easily as to money. Nor is a rich person automatically in the condition described. The test is the direction of the credit and the sense of accountability that follows from it, and both are available to be examined by anyone at any time.

O humanity, you are those in need of God, and God is the Free of Need, the Praiseworthy (35:15 — *“O humanity! It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy”*).

## Verse 96:8

> ˹But˺ surely to your Lord is the return ˹of all˺

**One Sentence Undoes the Whole Illusion**

Three Arabic words — *inna ilā rabbika al-rujʿā* — answer the previous verse, and they answer it by changing the frame rather than by arguing. The person who feels self-enough is imagining a life with no end and no accounting. The verse supplies both. There is a return, it is to your Lord, and it is certain, marked by the emphatic particle *inna*. Nothing is said about punishment, about fire, or about reward; the verse simply states that the independence is temporary because the direction of travel is fixed. The rhetorical economy is remarkable. A person convinced he needs nothing can be argued with for hours, but he can be silenced in a line by being told where he is going. The verse does not refute the feeling so much as place it in a context that dissolves it.

**Al-Rujʿā: A Fixed Term With a Fixed Direction**

*Rujʿā* is a verbal noun meaning return, and the Qur'an uses the concept as one of its constants. The phrase *innā li-Llāhi wa-innā ilayhi rājiʿūn* — we belong to God and to Him we return (2:156 — *“who, when faced with a disaster, say, “Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return.”*) — is the formula Muslims say at a death, which means the idea is recited at the moment of loss rather than debated in the abstract. The same concept underlies the statements that to God is the final return (3:83 — *“Do they desire a way other than Allah’s—knowing that all those in the heavens and the earth submit to His Will, willingly or unwillingly, and to Him they will ˹all˺ be returned”*) and that to your Lord is the destination (53:42 — *“and that to your Lord ˹alone˺ is the ultimate return ˹of all things˺”*). The direction is always the same and always singular: not to a place, not to a state, but to a person, your Lord. Al-ʿAlaq adds the possessive — *rabbika*, your Lord — which makes the return personal. The one being addressed is not returning to an impersonal order of things but to the one who made him from a clot and taught him what he did not know.

**The Return as the Answer to an Entire Order of Life**

The verse is usually read as a statement about individual death, and it is that. Its range is wider. Meccan society was organised around permanence of another kind: lineage that went back generations, alliances that lasted, wealth that accumulated, honour that was defended in poetry long after the person died. The Qur'an addresses that order directly in the sūrah that follows this one in the standard arrangement, asking whether the one who amasses wealth and counts it supposes that his wealth will make him live forever (104:2–3 — *“who amasses wealth ˹greedily˺ and counts it ˹repeatedly˺”*). Against that whole system, three words assert a single fixed point. Everything a person builds is provisional, and the return is not a metaphor for legacy but a destination with an address. The verse is short because the claim does not need elaboration. A person who accepts it will read the rest of the sūrah — and most of the Qur'an — differently, and a person who does not accept it will find the threats that follow merely rhetorical.

## Verse 96:9

> Have you seen the man who prevents

**The Sūrah Turns From a Principle to a Scene**

Eight verses have laid out a general account of the human being: made from a clot, taught by the pen, prone to excess, bound to return. Now the sūrah narrows to a person and an event. The shift is signalled by *a-raʾayta*, "have you seen," a formula the Qur'an uses to introduce something the listener is meant to look at closely. What follows is not a parable. The commentators identify the man as Abū Jahl — ʿAmr ibn Hishām of the Banū Makhzūm, one of the leading opponents of the Prophet ﷺ in Mecca — and the incident as his try to stop the Prophet from praying at the Kaʿbah. The reports describing this passage were revealed, they say, years after the opening verses, attached to a specific confrontation. The sūrah records the confrontation in the third person and leaves the name out, which is characteristic: the Qur'an names almost none of its opponents, preferring to describe the act.

**A-Raʾayta: Seeing With the Mind**

The formula does not ask whether the listener has literally witnessed the scene. *Raʾā* in this construction means to perceive or to consider, and the Qur'an uses it to force an evaluation: have you considered the one who does such a thing? The same opening appears in Sūrat al-Māʿūn: have you seen the one who denies the Recompense? That is the one who repels the orphan (107:1–2 — *“That is the one who repulses the orphan”*), where the question introduces a character study rather than an event. Here it works the same way. The listener is being asked to form a judgement about a type of person, using a real case as the example. The rhetorical effect is to make the listener the judge.

**The Verb That Names the Offence**

The word translated as prevents is from the root n-h-y, which means forbidding or restraining someone from an act. The Qur'an builds much of its moral vocabulary on this root: forbidding what is wrong is paired throughout with enjoining what is right, and the pair defines a community's responsibility toward itself (3:104 — *“Let there be a group among you who call ˹others˺ to goodness, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil—it is they who will be successful”*). What the verse describes is the same machinery turned around. The man is doing what societies do to enforce norms — restraining a person from a public act — except that the act being restrained is prayer and the norm being enforced is the city's own preference. The inversion is the offence. Nothing suggests that Abū Jahl acted from a sincere conviction that prayer was harmful; the reports describe annoyance, rivalry and contempt. Recording the act with a verb that belongs to moral enforcement sharpens the charge: this was not a private dislike but a try at coercion.

The Qur'an treats the obstruction of worship as a grave matter elsewhere too, asking who does greater wrong than one who prevents the mosques of God from being used for His name to be remembered, and strives to ruin them (2:114 — *“Who does more wrong than those who prevent Allah’s Name from being mentioned in His places of worship and strive to destroy them?”*).

## Verse 96:10

> a servant ˹of Ours˺ from praying

**The Word ʿAbd, and the Honour Hidden in It**

The object of the obstruction is called *ʿabd* — servant, slave. In the Qur'an the word is not a term of degradation but the highest title a human being can hold, because it names a relationship rather than a rank. God describes His messenger with it in the sūrah of the Night Journey: glory be to the One who took His servant by night (17:1 — *“Glory be to the One Who took His servant ˹Muḥammad˺ by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque whose surroundings We have blessed, so that We may show him some of Our signs.”*), and the same word opens the account of revelation itself: blessed is He who sent down the Criterion upon His servant (25:1 — *“Blessed is the One Who sent down the Standard to His servant, so that he may be a warner to the whole world”*). To be God's *ʿabd* is to belong to Him, and the sūrah uses it here at the exact moment the man is being threatened. The construction in Arabic attaches the servanthood to God rather than leaving it bare, which is why the translation supplies "of Ours." The man being obstructed is not a rival to Abū Jahl; he is the property of the one Abū Jahl is defying.

**The Threat, as Recorded by Ibn ʿAbbās**

The confrontation is documented in the prophetic commentary on this sūrah. Ibn ʿAbbās reported that Abū Jahl said: if I see Muḥammad praying at the Kaʿbah, I will tread on his neck. When the Prophet ﷺ heard of it he said: if he did that, the angels would snatch him away. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4958, in the Book of Tafsīr, placed under the chapter heading for verse 96:15–16 — *“But no! If he does not desist, We will certainly drag him by the forelock— a lying, sinful forelock”* and transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās through ʿIkrimah. What the report adds is the physical detail the verse leaves out: the threat was not to argue or to fine but to trample. The answer given to it is equally physical — angels, not words — and it is the same answer the sūrah gives in its own voice.

**Servanthood and the Sūrah's Understanding of Freedom**

The title ʿabd answers the earlier diagnosis as directly as it answers Abū Jahl. The transgression described in verses six and seven was a claim of independence; the man being defended here is described as a servant. The two positions are exhaustive in the Qur'an's scheme — a person is either a servant of God or a servant of something else, and Sūrat al-Kāfirūn makes the exclusivity plain in its closing line: to you your religion, and to me mine (109:6 — *“You have your way, and I have my Way.”*). Servanthood is therefore not a lesser state that the Prophet ﷺ is described as despite his rank; it is the correct state, and the only one that is not bondage to a created thing. Abū Jahl's clan gave him a different identity — lineage, honour, numbers — and the verse offers the alternative in a single word. The man at prayer belongs to God, and the man obstructing him belongs to his assembly.

## Verse 96:11

> What if this ˹servant˺ is ˹rightly˺ guided

**A Hypothetical That Is Not Really Hypothetical**

The verse opens with *a-raʾayta in kāna ʿalā al-hudā* — have you seen if he is upon guidance. Grammatically it is a conditional; rhetorically it is a challenge with the answer assumed. The particle *in* introduces a condition whose fulfilment is not in doubt, and the Qur'an uses the construction to make the listener supply the obvious consequence. If the man being obstructed is guided, what does that make the man doing the obstructing? The verse does not finish the thought, and the omission is the method. The listener completes it himself, and a conclusion the listener reaches on his own is harder to argue with than one handed to him. The three conditional verses that follow build a small case: guided, commanding righteousness, and — on the other side — denying and turning away.

**ʿAlā al-Hudā: Upon Guidance, Not Merely Holding It**

The preposition matters. The phrase is not "if he has guidance" but "if he is *upon* guidance" — *ʿalā al-hudā* — which places the person on top of something stable, as a rider is on a mount or a traveller on a road. The same construction appears in the opening of Sūrat al-Baqarah: those who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and spend from what We have provided for them are upon guidance from their Lord, and it is they who will be successful (2:5 — *“It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful”*). The same preposition is used of its opposite: those who traded guidance for misguidance are described as wilfully deaf, dumb and blind, so that they will never return (2:18 — *“They are ˹wilfully˺ deaf, dumb, and blind, so they will never return ˹to the Right Path˺”*). The image is of a path with a direction. Guidance in the Qur'an is not a feeling but a position on a route, and being upon it implies both that the route exists and that the person has been placed on it. The sūrah's first verse already told us who does the placing.

## Verse 96:12

> or encourages righteousness

**A Second Condition, and It Is About Other People**

The first conditional asked whether the servant is guided. This one asks whether he commands or encourages *taqwā*. The addition changes the category. Personal guidance is an inward state with outward effects; enjoining *taqwā* is an act directed at others, with social consequences. The sūrah is describing a man who is not only right but who is calling other people to be right, and that is precisely what made him intolerable to the man obstructing him. A private believer can be ignored. A person who tells a city that its practices are wrong cannot. The verse identifies the real object of Abū Jahl's hostility: not the prayer as such, but the public claim that there is a right way and that the city is not on it.

**Taqwā: The Word and Its Weight**

*Taqwā* comes from the root *w-q-y*, to shield or protect oneself, and it means a state of guarded care before God — mindfulness that shapes conduct. Translators use piety, God-consciousness, righteousness, or reverence, and none captures the whole. The Qur'an treats it as the substance of the religious life rather than one virtue among many: the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most mindful of Him (49:13 — *“Surely the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous among you.”*); and provision for the journey, and the best provision is taqwā (2:197 — *“Take ˹necessary˺ provisions ˹for the journey˺—surely the best provision is righteousness.”*). To command *taqwā* is therefore not to promote a particular rule but to call people to the orientation from which all rules follow. A man who does that is doing the most consequential thing available in his society, and the sūrah's question is asking whether the obstructer has considered that possibility before acting.

**Commanding the Good as a Defined Obligation**

The Qur'an makes this activity an explicit duty rather than an optional virtue. Let there be a group among you who call to good, command what is right and forbid what is wrong (3:104 — *“Let there be a group among you who call ˹others˺ to goodness, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil—it is they who will be successful”*); you are the best community brought forth for humanity, enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong (3:110 — *“You are the best community ever raised for humanity—you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah.”*). The duty is communal and it is named as the mark of the community's distinction. Al-ʿAlaq's question sits inside that framework. When the sūrah asks whether the servant encourages righteousness, it is asking whether he is performing the function the Qur'an elsewhere assigns to the believers as such. The obstructer is therefore not merely interfering with one man's prayer; he is interfering with the mechanism by which a community keeps itself honest. The sūrah treats that interference as the graver offence, which is why it names it separately.

**The Public Character of the Call, and Its Cost**

Enjoining righteousness is not a safe activity, and the sūrah knows it. The man who tells his society that its practices are wrong makes enemies by definition, and the Qur'an records the cost in the accounts of every previous messenger. Noah's people covered their ears and wrapped themselves in their garments and persisted in their refusal (71:7 — *“And whenever I invite them to be forgiven by You, they press their fingers into their ears, cover themselves with their clothes, persist ˹in denial˺, and act very arrogantly”*); Hūd's people told him they saw him as a fool and thought he was a liar (7:66 — *“The disbelieving chiefs of his people responded, “We surely see you as a fool, and we certainly think you are a liar.”*); Shuʿayb was threatened with expulsion from his own land unless he returned to their faith (7:88 — *“We will certainly expel you and your fellow believers from our land, unless you return to our faith.”*). Al-ʿAlaq compresses the pattern into a single question. By asking whether the servant encourages righteousness, the sūrah places the Prophet ﷺ inside the sequence without naming a predecessor, and it identifies the reason for the hostility he faced. The obstruction of the prayer was not a spontaneous act of dislike; it was the predictable reaction of a society to a man who had told it that it was wrong.

## Verse 96:13

> What if that ˹man˺ persists in denial and turns away

**Kadhdhaba and Tawallā: Denial and Withdrawal Together**

Two verbs are used, and they cover two domains. *Kadhdhaba* is the intensive form of lying or calling something a lie; it means an active rejection of a truth one has been shown, not mere absence of belief. *Tawallā* means to turn away, to turn one's back, to withdraw from a thing. The pairing is common in the Qur'an and always deliberate: the denial is internal, the turning away is physical. Together they describe a person who has both refused the claim and removed himself from the situation in which it could reach him. That is a more complete state than either verb alone. A person who doubts but remains present can still be addressed; a person who denies and turns away has closed the channel. The Qur'an treats this as the most dangerous condition a human being can be in, and it is the condition the sūrah attributes to the man threatening a person at prayer.

**The Referent Question, and Which Reading Holds**

Commentators differ on whether *kadhdhaba wa-tawallā* refers to Abū Jahl or continues to refer to the Prophet ﷺ in the hypothetical frame of the previous verses — as if to say, have you considered what would follow if he were one who denied and turned away? The majority take the referent as Abū Jahl, and the reasons are textual. The two previous conditionals were favourable descriptions of the servant. A third in the same series would be redundant; the tone shifts from favourable to condemnatory, which signals a change of subject; and the verses that follow address the one who denies with a direct threat, which only makes sense if that person is the obstructer. The minority reading is not impossible in grammar, but it leaves the passage without a subject for the warning. The majority reading is the coherent one.

**The Same Pairing Across the Qur'an**

Denial paired with turning away is a recurring portrait, and its recurrence shows that the Qur'an treats it as a type rather than an incident. And then describes the man who neither believed nor prayed, who persisted in denial and turned away, and then went to his own people walking boastfully (75:31–33 — *“but persisted in denial and turned away”*).

## Verse 96:14

> Does he not know that Allah sees ˹all˺

**The Object of Seeing Left Unstated**

The verb *yarā* has no object in the Arabic, and the omission is comprehensive. God sees — everything, everyone, at all times, including this. Nothing is specified because nothing is excluded. The same absolute use appears in God's assurance to Mūsā and Hārūn: fear not, I am with you both, hearing and seeing (20:46 — *“Allah reassured ˹them˺, “Have no fear! I am with you, hearing and seeing”*), where the two faculties are stated without objects for the same reason. Commentators note that the unqualified form is stronger than any qualified one would be, since specifying an object implies a limit. The verse is answering the man who obstructed a prayer performed in a public square: that prayer was seen, the threat was seen, the boast was seen. Nothing about the scene was private, and nothing about it has been lost.

**Knowledge and Seeing: Why Both Verbs Are Needed**

The verse joins two verbs that do different work. *Yaʿlam* is knowledge; *yarā* is sight. The man is asked whether he *knows* that God *sees*, which means the deficiency is located in his knowledge rather than in God's perception. God's seeing is not in question; the man's awareness of it is. That is a precise diagnosis, and it explains why the warning that follows takes the form it does. A person who does not know he is watched behaves differently from one who does, and the difference is not in the watching but in the behaviour. The Qur'an makes the same point in its description of the righteous: those who are in awe of their Lord without seeing Him, and are fearful of the Hour (21:49 — *“who are in awe of their Lord without seeing Him, and are fearful of the Hour”*). The unseen observer is the fact that makes conduct real rather than performative.

**What the Question Assumes About the Man**

The form of the question tells us something about its target. A-lam yaʿlam — does he not know — assumes that knowledge was available to him. The verse does not ask whether God has made Himself known; it asks whether the man knows something that has been shown. The Meccan audience had the sūrah's own first verses, the public preaching, the repeated arguments from creation, and the visible fact of a man praying at the Kaʿbah in defiance of threats. The material was not hidden. This is the basis on which the Qur'an holds people accountable: knowledge must reach a person before responsibility attaches, a principle stated plainly in the verse that God does not punish until a messenger has been sent (17:15 — *“And We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺”*). The man addressed here had received the message and rejected it, so the question is not about his information but about his attention.

## Verse 96:15

> But no! If he does not desist, We will certainly drag him by the forelock—

**The Forelock in Arab Life and in the Qur'an**

The *nāṣiyah* is the forelock, the hair at the front of the head, and in Arab usage it stood for the person himself. To seize a man's forelock was to humiliate him completely; to place one's forelock in someone's hand was an act of total submission. The Qur'an uses the image elsewhere with the same force: the guilty will be known by their marks and seized by their forelocks and their feet (55:41 — *“The wicked will be recognized by their appearance, then will be seized by ˹their˺ forelocks and feet”*). More fully, it states that God holds every creature by its forelock: there is no moving creature but He holds it by its forelock; my Lord is upon a straight path (11:56 — *“I have put my trust in Allah—my Lord and your Lord. There is no living creature that is not completely under His control. Surely my Lord’s Way is perfect justice”*). That verse supplies the theology behind this one. The forelock is not only a convenient grip; it is the Qur'an's symbol for the fact that no creature is outside divine control, and the threat here is the application of that control to a specific man who denied it.

**Why the Forelock Rather Than the Hand or the Neck**

The choice of body part carries meaning beyond mechanics. Abū Jahl had threatened to trample the Prophet's neck; the answer names the aggressor's own head. The forelock is the front of the head, the visible part of the face's upper region, the part that leads when a person walks. Seizing it is therefore seizing the leading part of a man — his pride, his direction, his public self. The Qur'an's use of the same image in 11:56 — *“I have put my trust in Allah—my Lord and your Lord. There is no living creature that is not completely under His control. Surely my Lord’s Way is perfect justice”* confirms the reading: the forelock stands for the whole creature under control. There is also a symmetry the verse does not point out but does not hide. The man intended to put his foot on the neck of a servant at prayer; he will be taken by the front of his own head. The sūrah leaves the comparison to the listener, which is more effective than stating it.

## Verse 96:16

> a lying, sinful forelock

**Kādhibah and Khāṭiʾah: Two Different Charges**

The two adjectives are not synonyms, and the pairing covers two domains. *Kādhibah*, from *kadhib*, concerns speech and claim: the forelock lies, meaning the man lied — in his denial of the message, in his threats, in his boast about the number of his supporters. *Khāṭiʾah*, from *khaṭiʾa*, concerns action and fault: the forelock is sinful, meaning the man committed wrong. The Qur'an distinguishes the two elsewhere, and the distinction is meaningful: a person can lie without acting wrongly, and can act wrongly without lying. To be described with both terms is to be indicted on both counts. The verse therefore says, in the shortest available form, that this man's speech was false and his conduct was corrupt, and that both are known. Nothing about the indictment requires a trial; the description is delivered as established fact.

**The Forelock as the Leading Part of the Man**

Commentators note that the forelock is the front of the head, and the front of the head is where a person's decisions are visibly registered — the face turned toward or away from something, the eyes directed, the expression of contempt or assent. The Qur'an's wider use of the image supports this: God holds every creature by its forelock (11:56 — *“I have put my trust in Allah—my Lord and your Lord. There is no living creature that is not completely under His control. Surely my Lord’s Way is perfect justice”*), which places the grip at the point of direction rather than at the point of strength.

**The Forelock and the Punishment Described Elsewhere**

The image connects to a wider description of the punishment of the Fire. The guilty will be recognised by their marks and seized by their forelocks and their feet (55:41 — *“The wicked will be recognized by their appearance, then will be seized by ˹their˺ forelocks and feet”*), a verse that pairs the same body part with the feet and assigns the seizure to the angels of punishment. Al-ʿAlaq uses the forelock alone, and the choice concentrates the image on the leading part of the person. There is also a connection to the sūrah's own vocabulary: the man who threatened to trample a neck is answered with the seizure of a head, and the symmetry is exact. The Qur'an's punishments are frequently fitted to the crime in this way, and the fitting is not incidental. It makes the punishment legible as a response rather than as an arbitrary severity.

## Verse 96:17

> So let him call his associates

**An Imperative That Is a Challenge, Not a Permission**

The form *fa-lyadʿu* is a third-person command: let him call. Arabic uses this construction for challenge and derision as readily as for permission, and the context here removes any ambiguity. Nothing is being granted. The man has been told that his forelock will be seized, and the answer to that threat is an invitation to bring his help. The imperative is rhetorical, of the same kind as the Qur'an's challenge elsewhere: call upon those you claim besides Him, and they will not remove your hardship nor transfer it (17:56 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Invoke those you claim ˹to be divine˺ besides Him—they do not have the power to undo harm from you or transfer it ˹to someone else˺.”*). The verse is daring the man to do the one thing he thinks will save him, and the daring is safe because the sūrah knows the outcome. What follows in the next verse is the answer to the call before it has been made.

**The Boast, and the Occasion of the Verse**

The scene is described in the same report. The Prophet ﷺ was praying when Abū Jahl came and said, have I not forbidden you from this — three times. The Prophet turned and scolded him. Abū Jahl answered with his boast, and the two verses were revealed. Ibn ʿAbbās added: by God, had he called his council, the wardens of God would have seized him. The wording and the occasion of revelation are therefore given in a single graded report (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3349), not assembled from later commentary. Whether or not every detail is exact, the reports agree on the substance: the confrontation was about numbers, and the Qur'an answered it with a comparison of numbers. That is why the two verses are paired as they are — let him call his assembly; We will call the wardens. The structure is a direct reply, in the man's own terms, to the man's own argument.

**The Limits of the Assembly in Qur'anic Perspective**

The Qur'an takes tribal solidarity seriously as a fact and dismisses it as a resource. An assembly can protect a man in this world, and the text does not pretend otherwise; the Prophet ﷺ himself lost his protection when Abū Ṭālib died, and the years that followed were the hardest of the Meccan period. What is denied is that the assembly has standing beyond this world. The accounts of earlier peoples in Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ show the same elites answering their messengers with threats of expulsion and stoning, and being destroyed: Shuʿayb's people told him they would stone him if not for his clan (11:91 — *“Were it not for your clan, we would have certainly stoned you, for you are nothing to us.”*). Al-ʿAlaq compresses the point into four words: let him call them. The invitation is safe because the assembly has no jurisdiction over what is coming, and the verse exists to make that limit visible to everyone listening.

## Verse 96:18

> We will call the wardens of Hell

**Al-Zabāniyah: The Wardens Named**

*Zabāniyah* is a plural with no ordinary singular in use, and the commentators derive it from a root meaning to push away or repel — the ones who push people back, or drive them forward. The Qur'an uses the term here and describes the same function elsewhere: over it are angels, stern and severe, who do not disobey God in what He commands them and who do as they are commanded (66:6 — *“O believers! Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, overseen by formidable and severe angels, who never disobey whatever Allah orders—always doing as commanded”*). The description answers a question the verse itself does not raise: these are not independent agents but functionaries, and their severity is obedience. The word carries no mythology with it; it names a role. That matters for how the verse should be read. The threat is not that a monster will be unleashed but that an appointment will be kept, by beings whose only characteristic is that they do what they are told.

**Nineteen, and the Argument About the Number**

The Qur'an gives a number for the keepers of the Fire in Sūrat al-Muddaththir: over it are nineteen (74:30 — *“It is overseen by nineteen ˹keepers˺”*), followed immediately by the statement that they are made none other than angels, and that their number is a trial for those who disbelieve (74:31 — *“We have appointed only ˹stern˺ angels as wardens of the Fire.”*). The Meccan response, as the same passage records, was to treat nineteen as a small number that could be overcome. The answer given is that strength is not measured by counting. Al-ʿAlaq makes the same point without a number: let the man summon his assembly, and God will summon the wardens. The comparison the Quraysh wanted to make — how many against how many — is refused at the level where it would matter. A clan can be counted; the wardens cannot be counted in a way that helps the person facing them.

## Verse 96:19

> Again, no! Never obey him ˹O Prophet˺! Rather, ˹continue to˺ prostrate and draw near ˹to Allah˺

**The Third Kallā, and a Sūrah That Ends With an Instruction**

The particle of refusal appears for the third time, and after it comes the only direct command in the second half of the sūrah. The structure is worth noting: eleven verses about a man who tried to stop a prayer end with an instruction to keep praying. Nothing is said about retaliation, about answering the threat, or about the outcome of the confrontation. The final words of the first revelation in Islam are *wa-sjud wa-qtarib* — prostrate, and draw near. Whatever else the sūrah establishes, its last statement is about the act that was being obstructed, and it is addressed to the one who was obstructed. The refusal and the command belong together: no, do not obey him, and yes, continue.

**Do Not Obey Him: The Refusal Stated Without Argument**

*Lā tuṭiʿhu* — do not obey him — is the plainest possible rejection, and it is given without reasons. No case is made against Abū Jahl, no comparison of consequences is offered, no negotiation is proposed. The Qur'an uses the same formula elsewhere: so do not obey the deniers (68:8 — *“So do not give in to the deniers”*), where the command is followed immediately by the statement that they wish the Prophet would compromise so that they might compromise in return (68:9 — *“They wish you would compromise so they would yield ˹to you˺”*). The pattern is consistent. Obedience to a person who obstructs worship is not a matter for calculation, because the thing being calculated away is the worship itself. The verse treats the demand for compliance as illegitimate on its face. A man who tries to stop a servant from praying has no standing to be obeyed, and the sūrah declines to argue the point.

**Wa-Sjud: The Act That Answers the Threat**

The command is to prostrate, and the choice of act is precise. Prostration is the posture in which the forehead — the front of the head, the region the previous verses named — is placed on the ground. The man who threatened to trample a neck is answered with an instruction to put the highest part of the body at the lowest point. The Qur'an treats prostration as the definitive act of submission: the true believers in God's revelation are those who fall into prostration when it is recited to them and glorify their Lord. Who are not too proud (32:15 — *“The only ˹true˺ believers in Our revelation are those who—when it is recited to them—fall into prostration and glorify the praises of their Lord and are not too proud”*), and the Prophet ﷺ said that the nearest a servant comes to his Lord is when he is prostrating, so make supplication in that state; the report is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 482. The verse's command therefore does not merely resume the interrupted prayer. It prescribes the specific posture that most directly contradicts the transgressor's claim of independence.
