# Sūrah al-ʿAlaq (Chapter 96) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:1

> Read, ˹O Prophet,˺ in the Name of your Lord Who created—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Revealed Words Were an Instruction, Not a Statement**

These five verses are the earliest revelation the Prophet ﷺ received, and the fact is not a pious tradition but a documented account. ʿĀʾishah described the beginning of revelation in detail: it started as true dreams that came like the breaking of daylight, then a love of solitude was given to him, and he would withdraw to the cave of Ḥirāʾ for nights of worship before returning to his family. Then the angel came and said *iqraʾ*, read. He answered, *mā anā bi-qāriʾ* — I am not one who reads. The angel pressed him until he thought he would die, released him, and repeated the command three times, then recited these opening verses. The account stands at the head of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī's collection, numbered 3, and again in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 160. What the placement signals is that the whole revelation begins with a command addressed to a man who could not carry it out.

**An Unlettered Man Ordered to Read**

The tension between the command and the answer is the hinge of the passage. He did not say he was unwilling; he said he was not a reader. The Qur'an insists on this condition repeatedly and treats it as evidence rather than embarrassment: those who follow the unlettered Messenger (7:157); you did not recite any book before it, nor did you write one with your right hand, for then those who falsify would have doubted (29:48); He is the One who sent among the unlettered a Messenger from themselves (62:2). A man who cannot read is commanded to read, and what he receives is a book. The arrangement removes the most obvious natural explanation for the text. If the recipient had been a scholar, the content could be attributed to his learning; because he was not, the content must come from elsewhere. The command was not a request for literacy but the installation of a revelation.

**In the Name of Your Lord, Not in the Name of a Skill**

The prepositional phrase *bi-smi rabbika* does real grammatical and theological work. The command to read is immediately qualified: reading happens in a name, and the name is not the reader's. Everything that follows in the sūrah develops this single point. Knowledge is not acquired by the knower's power but received from a Lord, and the Lord is named as *rabb* — the one who nourishes, raises, sustains and completes — before any other description. The choice is significant for a man who had just been told to do something he could not do. The capacity to carry out the command is not in him; it belongs to the one who gave the command. This is why the sūrah can move so quickly from creation to teaching. The same Lord who brought the man into existence is the one who now brings knowledge into him.

**Who Created: The Object Left Deliberately Unstated**

The verse ends with the participle *alladhī khalaqa*, "the one who created," with no object attached. Arabic allows this, and here it produces breadth: created — everything, without specification. The commentators note that the omission makes the statement maximal, and the next verse then narrows it to the human being, which shows that the narrowing is a second move rather than the first. The sequence is the standard Qur'anic order: God as creator in general, then God as the one who made this particular creature from a clot. The rhetorical effect on the listener is to place him inside a much larger fact before attending to him. A man told to read in the name of the one who created is being told that the authority behind the command is the authority behind existence itself.

**Why the Revelation Opens With a Command Rather Than a Creed**

Most scriptures begin with a statement about God. This one begins with an imperative addressed to a person. The difference is not stylistic. A statement invites assent; a command invites obedience, and obedience is what the whole of the sūrah's second half will be about, down to the closing instruction to prostrate. The Prophet ﷺ was not asked first to believe something but to do something, and the thing he was asked to do was the one thing he was least equipped to do. That arrangement sets the pattern for the community that would form around him: the response to revelation is not primarily an opinion but a practice. The sūrah's own structure confirms it, since it ends where it began — with a command, and with an act of the body rather than a proposition of the mind.

**The Cave, the Solitude, and What Came Before the Command**

The circumstances recorded with these verses are part of their meaning. The man in the cave was not a teacher, not a priest, not a poet, and not seeking an office. He had withdrawn from a society he found corrupt, and the withdrawal itself was a habit he had built. The revelation arrived in that state, which the tradition describes as preceded by months of true dreams. The detail matters because it separates this event from every other kind of religious claim. Nothing was performed to induce it, no technique was used, no audience was assembled. A solitary man in a cave on a mountain outside Mecca was given a command in a language he spoke, in words he could repeat, and the words described his own origin in terms he could check. The sūrah opens with the command, but the command only makes sense against that silence. Nothing about the command was provisional, and nothing about the answer cancelled it. The sūrah holds both facts in the same line, which is why the verse can be read as the summary of a whole vocation in five words.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:2

> created humans from a clinging clot

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Universal Creator Narrowed to One Creature**

The first verse named the creator without an object; this one supplies the object and chooses the most unlikely candidate. Not the heavens, not the mountains, not the angels — the human being, and not the human being at his best but the human being at his earliest and least impressive. The narrowing is the argument. A creator general enough to have made everything is brought down to a single, verifiable fact about the listener: you were once a clot. The sūrah does not ask the listener to accept anything he cannot inspect. Every human being alive came through the state described here, and the description is offered as the ground for everything that follows. If God made this, then the command in the previous verse is not an arbitrary demand from a distant power but an instruction from the one who assembled the creature receiving it.

**ʿAlaq: The Word and What It Names**

The term *ʿalaq* is a plural or collective noun from the root meaning to cling or hang. Classical usage covers a leech, a blood clot, and anything that attaches itself to something else. Translators choose between "clinging clot," "clot of blood" and "embryo," and the ambiguity is in the original rather than in the translation. What all the senses share is attachment and dependence: a thing that has no independent existence and is held in place by another. The Qur'an uses the same word in the same sequence elsewhere: We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging clot, then from a lump of flesh, formed and unformed (22:5); and in Sūrat al-Mu'minūn: then We made the drop into a clinging clot, then We made the clot into a lump of flesh (23:14). The word is not a poetic flourish but a term of art in the Qur'an's account of development.

**The Stages as a Structured Argument**

The Qur'an returns to these stages often enough that they form a deliberate pattern rather than scattered imagery. The fullest account, in Sūrat al-Ḥajj, is explicitly framed as a response to doubt about resurrection: O humanity, if you are in doubt about the Resurrection, then We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from a clinging clot, then from a lump of flesh (22:5). The logic is comparative. Whoever assembled these stages once, without consultation and without assistance, is not incapable of assembling them again. Al-ʿAlaq uses the same material for a different purpose: here the stages are not an argument against doubt but a reminder of dependence. A creature that began as something clinging has no standing from which to declare independence, which is exactly the declaration the sūrah will address six verses later.

**From the Best Form to the Clot**

The previous sūrah in the Qur'anic order describes the same creature from the opposite end. Al-Tīn states that human beings were created in the best form and then warns of a fall to the lowest of the low. Al-ʿAlaq begins at the bottom of the same scale: the creature who will reach the best form started as a clot. Read together, the two sūrahs describe a single arc, and the arc is the reason the middle matters. Neither origin nor destination determines a person's outcome; the outcome is decided by what he does between the two. This is why the sūrah can move from embryology to arrogance in four verses. The distance between a clot and a man who considers himself self-sufficient is the distance the Qur'an wants the listener to measure, because that distance was covered by God's action and not by the man's.

**Why the Embryo and Not the Adult**

Every account of creation could have begun with the finished human being — the upright posture, the articulate speech, the reasoning mind. The Qur'an consistently chooses the beginning instead, and the choice is polemical. A finished human being invites admiration; an embryo invites the question of who made it. The sūrah of al-Qiyāmah puts the same point with unmistakable directness: was he not a drop of emitted fluid, then a clinging clot, then He fashioned and proportioned him and made of him the two pairs, male and female? Is He not able to give life to the dead? (75:37–40). Beginning with the clot forecloses the possibility of self-congratulation. Whatever a person becomes, he became it from something that had no power, no will and no shape, and the one who gave him shape has not relinquished that role.

**The Second Making Is Easier Than the First**

The verse does one further piece of work, which the Qur'an states explicitly elsewhere. God is the one who originates creation and then repeats it, and it is easier for Him (30:27). The comparison is not a claim that God finds things difficult; it is a way of speaking to human reasoning, which measures possibility by precedent. If the first assembly happened from nothing — from dust, from a drop, from a clot — then the reassembly is not a harder problem but a solved one. Al-ʿAlaq plants the premise in its second verse without drawing the conclusion, because the conclusion is not what this sūrah is about. What it needs here is only the fact of dependence: the man who will be told, six verses later, that he exceeds all bounds, is a man who began as a clinging thing and was made into what he is.
**The Clot as the Answer to Every Claim of Ownership**

The verse has a use beyond argument. A person who has wealth, followers, learning or strength tends to experience those things as his, and the experience is the root of the self-sufficiency the sūrah names later. The clot is the permanent counter-evidence. There was a moment when this person had no property, no memory, no will and no shape, and everything he now calls his was added afterwards by someone else. The Qur'an returns to that moment whenever the claim of ownership appears, and it does so without malice: the point is not to humiliate but to correct an accounting error. The man who considers himself beyond need has simply forgotten the first entry in his own ledger. Al-ʿAlaq places that entry in the second line of the first revelation, before any command has been fully carried out, which suggests that the correction was considered urgent enough to be delivered at the very start.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:3

> Read! And your Lord is the Most Generous

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command Is Repeated Before Any Reading Has Taken Place**

*Iqraʾ* returns, unchanged and emphatic. Between the two occurrences nothing has been read; the man in the cave said he was not a reader, and the account records no compliance. The repetition is therefore not a restatement but an insistence, and insistence of this kind has a function: it establishes that the command does not depend on the addressee's capacity. The angel does not withdraw the instruction when it is met with an honest statement of inability. He repeats it. That pattern governs the entire prophetic career that follows — a man without letters, without an army and without a tribe of consequence is told repeatedly to deliver a message, and the telling does not stop when the obstacle is real. The verse holds the two halves of the situation together: an impossible command, and a Lord whose generosity supplies what the command requires.

**Al-Akram: The Superlative Placed Where the Reader Needs It**

The word *akram* is the elative of *karīm*: the most generous, the most noble, the most bountiful. It appears in the Qur'an as a divine description in a small number of places, and here it is attached to the command to read rather than to a statement about creation. The placement is the point. A man told to do what he cannot do is not first told that God is powerful — power would only underline his incapacity — but that God is generous. Generosity is the attribute that bridges the gap between the command and the ability to fulfil it. The same word is used of God elsewhere with the same logic: O humanity, what has deluded you concerning your Lord, the Most Generous (82:6), where the question is precisely why a creature would doubt a Lord described in these terms. Al-ʿAlaq answers the question before it is asked, by naming generosity as the reason the command can be given at all.

**Karīm in the Speech of the Arabs and What the Qur'an Changed**

Before this revelation, *karīm* belonged to the vocabulary of tribal honour. A *karīm* man was one of unblemished lineage, open-handed at the fire, unshakeable in protection of a guest, and the poets used the word of men. Applying it to God in the superlative moved the term into a register it had not occupied. The exegetes note the shift and treat it as significant: the Arabs understood generosity as the highest of virtues and measured it in camels and slaughter, and the Qur'an takes the same measure and applies it to the one who gives existence and knowledge. Nothing about the word's force is lost in the transfer; what changes is its ceiling. The listener hears a familiar virtue and is told that the one addressing him possesses it without limit, which converts a tribal ideal into a theological claim.

**Generosity as the Frame Around Two Gifts**

The verse sits between two descriptions of giving, and the middle position is deliberate. Before it: the creator who made the human being from a clot. After it: the one who taught by the pen and taught humanity what they did not know. Generosity names what those two acts have in common. Creation is a gift given to a recipient who did not exist to request it; knowledge is a gift given to a recipient who cannot produce it himself. Neither is earned, and neither can be repaid. The commentators draw out the pairing explicitly, noting that God blessed humanity with the two greatest blessings there are, existence and understanding, and that both are acts of the same generosity. The consequence for the reader is uncomfortable and immediate: a creature whose being and whose mind are both received has nothing left from which to claim credit.

**Why Generosity Rather Than Power or Majesty**

The sūrah could have named any divine attribute at this point, and the choice tells us what the passage is doing. Majesty would have answered the man's fear; power would have answered his incapacity. Generosity answers something else — his expectation about what is being asked of him. A generous lord does not issue a command in order to catch the recipient failing; he issues it because he intends to supply the means. That is the assumption the verse installs, and the sūrah spends its next two lines justifying it. The teaching by the pen is not a test of the reader's resources but a provision for them. Read in that light, the repetition of *iqraʾ* stops being pressure and becomes a promise: read, because the one who tells you to read has already arranged that you will be able to.
**Generosity That Does Not Wait to Be Earned**

The verse names the attribute before the recipient has done anything. The man in the cave had not read, had not obeyed, had not yet delivered a message; he had said he was not a reader. The response to that admission was not a condition but a description of the one commanding. This ordering is characteristic of the Qur'an's account of God's dealings: the gift precedes the obligation, and the capacity precedes the command. The commentators draw the same point from the sequence of the sūrah itself — creation, then generosity, then teaching — noting that a person is made before he is asked for anything, and taught before he is required to act. The consequence for the reader is that generosity cannot be bargained with. It is not a reward for reading well; it is the reason reading is possible at all. Every later demand in the sūrah, including the command to prostrate, rests on this prior provision.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:4

> Who taught by the pen—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Teaching Is Attributed to God, and the Instrument Is Named**

The verse does two things in three words. It makes God the teacher, and it names the pen as the means. Both moves are unusual. Ancient literatures credit teachers, sages, kings and gods with wisdom, but the Qur'an places the act of teaching itself in God's hands and then supplies the tool. The participle *allama* — the one who taught — is the same verb used of Adam's instruction and of the Prophet's own, and it will be completed in the next verse with its object. What is striking here is the instrument. Not the tongue, not memory, not the intellect, but the *qalam*: the reed pen, cut and split at the nib, dipped in ink, used to fix speech on a surface. The most oral of revelations begins by honouring the technology of writing, in a sūrah revealed to a man who could not write.

**The First Created Thing Was a Writing Instrument**

The Prophet ﷺ connected the pen to creation itself. ʿUbādah ibn al-Ṣāmit reported that he heard him say: the first thing Allah created was the pen; He said to it, write; it asked, what should I write, my Lord; He said, write what has been decreed concerning everything until the Hour comes. The report is recorded in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 4700 and graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī. The claim is not that a physical reed existed before the heavens; the exegetical tradition understands the pen as the instrument by which the decree was inscribed. What matters for this verse is the association the report establishes between the pen and divine knowledge. The same instrument that fixes God's decree in the unseen is the instrument by which human beings fix what they know in the seen. Teaching by the pen is therefore not an incidental metaphor but a description of how knowledge is transmitted at both ends of the scale.

**Nūn. By the Pen and What They Write**

The Qur'an swears by the pen elsewhere, and the oath is worth reading beside this verse. Sūrat al-Qalam opens: Nūn. By the pen and what they inscribe. You are not, by the grace of your Lord, a madman (68:1–2). The oath serves to vindicate the Prophet against a charge of insanity, and it does so by appealing to the written word. The same instrument appears here as an instrument of divine teaching. Together the two passages give the pen a double role in the Qur'an's self-understanding: it is how God teaches, and it is how the truth of the message is preserved and defended. A community formed around a recitation was also, from its first verses, a community told to write things down — and the earliest Muslims did, keeping written records of the revelation alongside the memorised text.

**Three Pens: Decree, Record, and the Human Hand**

Commentators distinguish several kinds of pen behind the single word. The first is the pen of the decree, by which everything that will happen until the Hour was written. The second is the pen of the recording angels, who write down what human beings do: there are indeed keepers over you, noble scribes who know whatever you do (82:10–11); and the two receivers, seated to the right and to the left, noting every word (50:17–18). The third is the ordinary pen in a human hand. The three are not competing meanings but three instances of one principle: knowledge that matters is fixed in writing. The verse's use of the definite article — *bi-l-qalam*, by the pen — allows all three to stand behind it, and the breadth is appropriate to a statement about how God teaches a species.

**Writing as Generosity Extended Into Time**

The verse belongs to a sūrah whose third line declared that God is the Most Generous, and the pen is the mechanism by which that generosity outlasts the moment of giving. Speech dies with the breath; a written line does not. A teacher who speaks reaches the people in the room, and a teacher who writes reaches everyone who comes after. To teach by the pen is to make knowledge available to the absent, the unborn, and the poor — those who cannot sit with a sage or travel to a school. The Qur'an's own history is the clearest illustration: a revelation given orally in a cave became a written text that has been copied by hand for fourteen centuries, and the copies are why any reader today can check a verse against another. The generosity described in the previous verse is not merely given; it is preserved, and the pen is what preserves it.
**The Pen and the Survival of This Very Sūrah**

There is a concrete sense in which the verse describes its own fate. These were the first verses revealed, spoken to a man alone on a mountain, with no scribe present and no audience. They survive today in a fixed written form, identical in every printed copy from Morocco to Indonesia, and they were written down within the lifetime of the people who heard them. The scribes of the revelation were appointed during the Meccan and Medinan periods, and the written record was compiled after the Prophet's death from both the written fragments and the memories of those who had learned them. The instrument named in this verse is therefore not a metaphor in the history of the text but a fact about it. God taught by the pen, and the pen is why the teaching is still available to be read.
**Writing and the Responsibility Attached to It**

The verse does not stop at describing a gift; it implies an obligation. If God taught by the pen, then what a person writes is not a neutral act performed with a divine instrument. The Qur'an makes the connection explicit when it warns against writing something with one's own hand and then claiming it comes from God (2:79), and again when it describes the record laid open on that Day, with the wicked crying that it does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted (18:49). A pen in a human hand carries the same weight as a tongue: it fixes a claim, and the claim is answerable. The early Muslim community understood this. The written word was used for treaties, for the revelation itself, and for the transmission of the Prophet's sayings, and disputes over attribution were settled by reference to chains and written records rather than by assertion. To be taught by the pen is to be trusted with something that outlives the writer, and the sūrah's generosity is a trust before it is a gift.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:5

> taught humanity what they knew not

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence That Completes the Opening Argument**

Five verses form a single movement, and this is where it lands. Created — from a clot. Generous — the Most Generous. Taught — by the pen. The object of the teaching is now supplied: humanity, and the content is everything it did not already have. The construction is emphatic in Arabic, using a relative clause with a negative: *ʿallama al-insāna mā lam yaʿlam*, taught the human being what he did not know. Nothing is excluded. Not some knowledge, not a particular science, but the whole of what was previously absent. The verse is the sūrah's statement of the human condition before God: a creature who begins with nothing in his mind and ends with something, and who did not put it there himself. Everything that follows in the sūrah — the transgression, the self-sufficiency, the threat — is a response to a person who has forgotten this verse.

**Adam Was Taught the Names**

The Qur'an locates the origin of human knowledge in a specific act. God taught Adam the names, all of them, then presented the things to the angels and asked them to name them if they were truthful; when they could not, Adam was told to inform them of the names (2:31–33). The angels' reply is worth keeping in view: glory be to You, we have no knowledge except what You have taught us (2:32). The verse is a decisive statement about the direction of teaching in the Qur'an's cosmos. Knowledge flows downward. Even beings of light and obedience possess only what has been given to them, and the human being's advantage at the beginning was not power but a taught vocabulary. Al-ʿAlaq compresses the same claim into three words, and the commentators connect the two passages explicitly, taking *al-insān* here either as humanity generally or as Adam specifically.

**You Knew Nothing When You Came Out of the Womb**

The Qur'an states the individual case with equal bluntness. God brought you out of your mothers' wombs knowing nothing, and gave you hearing, sight and hearts, so that perhaps you would be grateful (16:78). The three faculties named are instruments of acquisition, not sources of content: hearing to receive, sight to observe, and the heart to process. All three are gifts, and the verse ties them to gratitude. Al-ʿAlaq makes the same point without the list, and the two together describe a coherent picture. A human being arrives empty, is equipped with means, and is then taught — by parents, by teachers, by books, and ultimately by God, who is the one who arranged that any of it should work. The claim is not that effort is worthless. It is that effort operates on equipment and material that were given.

**Taught What You Did Not Know: The Prophet's Own Case**

The verse has a specific application to the man who received it. God has sent down to you the Book and the wisdom, and taught you what you did not know; and the bounty of God upon you is great (4:113). The wording is almost identical to this verse, and it is addressed to the same person. That parallel is important for how the sūrah should be read: the one commanded to read is himself included in the category of those taught what they did not know. He is not a teacher standing outside the process. This is one reason the Qur'an can be so severe about boasting later in the same sūrah. The man who receives revelation is a student of the same Lord as everyone else, and the sūrah's warning against self-sufficiency applies to him first and to his community after him.

**Knowledge as a Gift That Rules Out Boasting**

The verse closes the sūrah's first movement, and the way it closes it determines what the second movement can say. If knowledge is received, then the person who possesses it is not its source, and his possession is a position of trust rather than an achievement. The Qur'an presses this repeatedly: the person who knows is not equal to the one who does not know (39:9), yet knowledge does not make the knower superior in God's reckoning except through what he does with it — the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most mindful of Him (49:13). The connection between verse five and the transgression described in verse six is therefore not a change of subject but a consequence. The creature described here as taught what he did not know is the same creature who, in the very next breath, exceeds all bounds. The sūrah sets the gift and the ingratitude side by side without comment, and lets the contrast speak.
**The Verse That Removes the Excuse**

Taken with the four before it, this verse closes off a particular kind of defence. A person who claims independence usually argues from capacity: I built this, I learned this, I earned this. The sūrah's answer is not that the capacity is unreal but that it was supplied. The clay was fashioned by another; the clot was developed by another; the knowledge was taught by another; the instrument of writing was provided by another. What remains for the person to claim is the use he made of the provision, and the sūrah will hold him to exactly that. The argument is not a denial of human effort — the Qur'an insists on effort everywhere, and its whole legal and moral order presumes it — but a denial of human origination. The line between the two is where the sūrah's second half operates, and the line is drawn here, in three words, before the transgression is even named.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:6

> Most certainly, one exceeds all bounds

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kallā: The Particle That Stops an Argument**

The sūrah turns on a single word. *Kallā* is one of the most forceful particles in Arabic and among the hardest to translate: no, on the contrary, certainly not, far from it. It does not merely negate; it rejects a premise and refuses to let the discussion continue on that footing. The Qur'an uses it as a marker of strong disapproval, and al-ʿAlaq uses it twice, here and in the closing verse, which brackets the second half of the sūrah between two refusals. What is being refused is not stated in the verse itself; the particle assumes the reader knows. After five verses describing a creature made from a clot and taught what he did not know, the unspoken premise is obvious: that any of this is his own doing. *Kallā* cuts it off, and the statement that follows explains what happens to a creature who entertains it.

**Ṭaghā: The Root Behind Ṭāghūt**

The verb *ṭaghā* means to go beyond a limit, to overflow, to exceed what is proper. Water that *ṭaghā* has flooded its banks. Applied to a person, it denotes transgression of a boundary that was set by someone else, and the Qur'an builds an entire vocabulary on the root. *Ṭāghūt* — usually rendered false god, tyrant or idol — is the intensive form, the thing that has itself been exceeded into. *Ṭughyān* is the act. The word choice is precise: the problem described here is not wickedness in the abstract but boundary-crossing. A limit existed, the creature knew it, and he crossed it. That is why the sūrah pairs the verb with the feeling of self-sufficiency in the next verse. Boundaries are only crossed by someone who no longer recognises the authority that set them.

**The Ingratitude Built Into the Transgression**

Commentators read this verse against the five before it and find the transgression defined by its context. Creation was given, knowledge was given, generosity was named — and the response was excess. The particle *kallā* therefore carries the sense of "despite all that." The transgression is not a neutral fact about human nature; it is a reaction to a gift. That is what makes it serious in the Qur'an's accounting. A person who has received nothing and takes what he can is acting out of need; a person who has been made, taught and provided for, and then exceeds his bounds, is acting out of something else. The Qur'an names that something in the next verse, and the naming is what gives the sūrah its diagnosis. The disease is not desire but the illusion of independence.

**Pharaoh: The Qur'an's Own Example of the Same Verb**

The sūrah does not name an example, but the Qur'an supplies one in a passage that uses the identical verb. When God sent Mūsā to Pharaoh, the instruction was: go to Pharaoh, for he has exceeded all bounds (79:17). The same form, *ṭaghā*, describes the ruler who declared himself the highest lord (79:24) and whose punishment the Qur'an presents as the standing case of boundary-crossing. The parallel is instructive because it shows what the excess consists of. Pharaoh's transgression was not primarily cruelty but a claim about himself: that he was above the limits that apply to other creatures. Al-ʿAlaq describes the same movement in an ordinary person, without the armies and without the throne, which is precisely what makes the verse disturbing. The condition does not require power to appear. It requires only the feeling described in the verse that follows.

**Taught by the Pen, Then Beyond Bounds**

The juxtaposition in this sūrah is one of the sharpest in the Qur'an. The creature addressed here was made from a clot and taught what he did not know, and the next thing he does is exceed his limits. The irony is not accidental. Knowledge is the very thing that most easily produces the illusion it is meant to prevent. A person who has learned to write, to calculate, to diagnose and to build can look at his own achievements and conclude that the capacity was his. The sūrah places the gift and the transgression five lines apart so that no reader can mistake one for the other. What the verse describes is not ignorance but a misuse of understanding, and the Qur'an treats that as the more dangerous condition, since an ignorant person can be taught while a person convinced of his own sufficiency does not think he needs teaching.
**The Boundary That Was Actually Crossed**

It is worth being precise about what limit the verse has in view, because the word can be read too broadly. The transgression described here is not ordinary wrongdoing — theft, cruelty, dishonesty — which the Qur'an treats under other headings. *Ṭughyān* is specifically the crossing of a limit set by God, and the paradigm case in the Qur'an is a claim to authority that belongs to God alone. Pharaoh's excess consisted in saying I am your lord, most high (79:24). The sūrah's transgressor makes a smaller version of the same claim, without words: by treating himself as beyond need, he treats himself as beyond jurisdiction. The boundary is the one between creature and creator, and it is crossed silently, in the mind, long before it is crossed in action. That is why the verse pairs the excess with a feeling rather than with a deed.
**The Order of the Sūrah Is the Argument**

Five verses of gift, then one word of refusal, then the transgression. Nothing in the arrangement is accidental. If the sūrah had begun with the excess, it would have read as a condemnation of human nature; beginning with creation and teaching, it reads as a diagnosis of ingratitude. The difference matters for how the verse should be applied. The Qur'an is not saying that human beings are inevitably rebellious; it is saying that rebellion is a response to provision, and that the response is perverse. The particle *kallā* is what makes the perversity audible — a word of refusal placed directly after a list of gifts. Read aloud, the sequence produces its own rebuke: created, generous, taught by the pen, taught what you did not know — no, on the contrary, the human being exceeds his bounds. The listener hears the disproportion without being told about it, which is why the verse needs no elaboration and why the sūrah moves on immediately to the cause.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:7

> once they think they are self-sufficient

**Expanded Commentary**

**Istighnāʾ: The Feeling That Ends Dependence**

The verb is *istaghnā*, form ten of the root *gh-n-y*, which carries wealth, sufficiency and freedom from need. Form ten adds the sense of seeking or considering: to deem oneself sufficient, to regard oneself as beyond need. The Qur'an's diagnosis of transgression rests on this single word, and the diagnosis is psychological before it is moral. The person does not first commit a crime and then feel independent; he feels independent, and the crime follows. The sequence in the two verses is causal — the human being exceeds all bounds *because* he considers himself self-sufficient — and the particle *an* in Arabic can carry that causal force. Read this way, the verse is not describing two facts but one fact and its effect. The transgression is the symptom; the sense of sufficiency is the disease.

**Why This Feeling Is the Root of Every Rebellion**

The exegetes are unusually unanimous here: *istighnāʾ* is the origin of all rebellion against God. The reasoning is straightforward. Every commandment presupposes a commander with authority over the one commanded, and authority over a person is only felt by someone who knows he is dependent. A creature who believes he needs nothing has no reason to accept a limit, no reason to pray, no reason to give, and no reason to stop. The whole apparatus of religion — obligation, gratitude, repentance — is built on a prior recognition of need. Remove that recognition and the apparatus does not merely weaken; it becomes unintelligible. This is why the Qur'an returns to the point so often, and why the sūrah places the word immediately before its warning. The verse is not condemning wealth. It is identifying the state of mind that wealth, knowledge, health and status all tend to produce.

**Qārūn's Claim, and the Answer to It**

The Qur'an gives the feeling a named example. Qārūn was given treasure whose keys alone burdened a group of strong men, and when his people told him not to exult, he answered: I was given it only because of knowledge I have (28:78). The claim is the exact form of *istighnāʾ* the verse describes — an achievement read as self-generated, with the source of the achievement attributed to the achiever. The verse that follows asks whether he did not know that God had destroyed, before him, generations stronger than he and greater in accumulation (28:78), and the story ends with the earth swallowing him and his house (28:81). The narrative is a worked example of the two verses of al-ʿAlaq put together: a man who exceeded his bounds because he considered himself sufficient, and the end that follows. What Qārūn forgot is what this sūrah states in its second line — that he was made from a clot.

**The Actual Condition: Poverty Toward God**

Against the feeling, the Qur'an sets a fact. O humanity, you are those in need of God, and God is the Free of Need, the Praiseworthy (35:15). The verse is addressed to all humanity without exception, and it uses the same vocabulary the sūrah uses for the transgressor. The word *fuqarāʾ* — the poor, the needy — is applied to everyone, including the wealthy, because the need in question is not financial. Every human being depends on air, on water, on sleep, on the continued functioning of organs he does not control and did not design. Al-ʿAlaq states the same dependence in embryological terms: the creature began as a clinging thing held in place by another. The self-sufficiency described in this verse is therefore not an exaggeration of a real independence but a complete misreading of the situation. Nothing about the human condition supports it.

**Wealth Is Not the Only Route to the Feeling**

It would be a mistake to read this verse as a warning to rich people alone. The feeling arises wherever a person finds a resource he did not create and mistakes it for an endowment. Intelligence produces it in scholars; health produces it in the young; a stable country produces it in citizens who have never seen a famine; a working mind produces it in anyone who has solved problems. The Qur'an's own examples span the range — Qārūn with his treasure, Pharaoh with his kingdom, and the man in Sūrat al-Kahf with his two gardens who said, I do not think this will ever perish, and I do not think the Hour will occur (18:35–36). What unites them is not the size of the possession but the direction of the credit. The sūrah's five opening verses exist to correct that direction, and this verse names the error they correct.
**The Test the Qur'an Applies to Any Possession**

There is a practical way to tell whether a possession has produced this feeling, and the Qur'an supplies it. Ask where the credit was directed when the thing was acquired. Qārūn attributed his treasure to his own knowledge (28:78); the owner of the two gardens in Sūrat al-Kahf attributed his estate to himself and doubted that it would ever perish (18:35–36). Both men had, in ordinary terms, legitimate achievements. The failure was in the accounting, not in the achievement. A person who attributes a success to God and works for the next one has not fallen into *istighnāʾ*; a person who attributes it wholly to himself has, whatever the size of the success. The sūrah's five opening verses are the correct accounting laid out in order — created, sustained, taught — and the feeling described here is what happens when that order is reversed and the creature is placed at the head of it.
**What the Verse Does Not Say About Wealth**

The verse is often read as a critique of riches, and the reading loses the point. *Istighnāʾ* is not having; it is a judgement about having. The Qur'an praises wealthy believers elsewhere without qualification — the sūrah of al-Baqarah describes those who spend from what they have been provided (2:3), and the promise of increase is attached to gratitude rather than to poverty: if you are grateful, I will certainly give you more (14:7). What the verse identifies as dangerous is the conclusion a person draws from his possessions, not the possessions themselves. That distinction has practical consequences. A person cannot remove the risk by giving everything away, because the feeling can attach to asceticism, to learning, or to reputation as easily as to money. Nor is a rich person automatically in the condition described. The test is the direction of the credit and the sense of accountability that follows from it, and both are available to be examined by anyone at any time.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:8

> ˹But˺ surely to your Lord is the return ˹of all˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Sentence Undoes the Whole Illusion**

Three Arabic words — *inna ilā rabbika al-rujʿā* — answer the previous verse, and they answer it by changing the frame rather than by arguing. The person who feels self-sufficient is imagining a life with no end and no accounting. The verse supplies both. There is a return, it is to your Lord, and it is certain, marked by the emphatic particle *inna*. Nothing is said about punishment, about fire, or about reward; the verse simply states that the independence is temporary because the direction of travel is fixed. The rhetorical economy is remarkable. A person convinced he needs nothing can be argued with for hours, but he can be silenced in a line by being told where he is going. The verse does not refute the feeling so much as place it in a context that dissolves it.

**Al-Rujʿā: A Fixed Term With a Fixed Direction**

*Rujʿā* is a verbal noun denoting return, and the Qur'an uses the concept as one of its constants. The phrase *innā li-Llāhi wa-innā ilayhi rājiʿūn* — we belong to God and to Him we return (2:156) — is the formula Muslims say at a death, which means the idea is recited at the moment of loss rather than debated in the abstract. The same concept underlies the statements that to God is the final return (3:83) and that to your Lord is the destination (53:42). The direction is always the same and always singular: not to a place, not to a state, but to a person, your Lord. Al-ʿAlaq adds the possessive — *rabbika*, your Lord — which makes the return personal. The one being addressed is not returning to an impersonal order of things but to the one who made him from a clot and taught him what he did not know.

**Return as a Fact Before It Is a Threat**

Readers often hear eschatological verses as warnings, and this one is usually included in that category. Its grammar, however, is declarative. It does not say you will be punished; it says the return exists. The distinction matters for how the verse functions in the argument. A threat can be resisted or discounted, but a fact about the structure of things can only be ignored. The sūrah's use of it here is therefore structural: it establishes the ground on which the following eleven verses will stand. The threats against the one who prevents prayer only carry weight because a return has been announced. Without this verse, the promise to seize him by the forelock would be rhetoric; with it, the promise is the working out of something already settled.

**Why the Verse Comes Before the Account of Abū Jahl**

The placement is not incidental. Verses nine to nineteen describe a specific conflict — a man preventing a servant of God from praying, and the answer given to him. That account would read as a historical incident if it were not preceded by this verse. Set after it, the incident becomes an instance of a general law. The man who boasted about his clan and his power was operating inside the same frame as everyone else: his independence was temporary and his return was certain. The sūrah therefore moves from the universal to the particular with the hinge already installed. What happened at the Kaʿbah was not an exception to the rule described in this verse but a demonstration of it, and the demonstration is what makes the general statement concrete for the listener.

**Two Words That Dismantle Self-Sufficiency**

The verse achieves its effect with minimal material, and the minimalism is the method. *Rabb* establishes a relationship of nurture and lordship — the one who raised you, provided for you, taught you. *Rujʿā* establishes a destination. Together they produce a picture the self-sufficient person cannot accommodate: a life that began with a giver and ends with a return to the same giver. Everything in between — the wealth, the clan, the intelligence, the status — is bracketed. The Qur'an does not deny that those things exist; it denies that they determine the outcome. This is why the verse sits exactly where it does, between the diagnosis of the disease and the case study of a man who had it. The reader is given the frame before being shown the man, so that when the man appears he is already understood.
**The Return as the Answer to an Entire Order of Life**

The verse is usually read as a statement about individual death, and it is that. Its range is wider. Meccan society was organised around permanence of another kind: lineage that went back generations, alliances that lasted, wealth that accumulated, honour that was defended in poetry long after the person died. The Qur'an addresses that order directly in the sūrah that follows this one in the standard arrangement, asking whether the one who amasses wealth and counts it supposes that his wealth will make him live forever (104:2–3). Against that whole system, three words assert a single fixed point. Everything a person builds is provisional, and the return is not a metaphor for legacy but a destination with an address. The verse is short because the claim does not need elaboration. A person who accepts it will read the rest of the sūrah — and most of the Qur'an — differently, and a person who does not accept it will find the threats that follow merely rhetorical.
**The Verse in the Architecture of the Sūrah**

Al-ʿAlaq divides cleanly into two halves, and this verse is the hinge. The first half — five verses — describes what God has done: created, been generous, taught by the pen, taught what was unknown. The second half — eleven verses — describes what a particular man did, and what will be done about it. Between them stands a single declarative sentence about the return, and the sentence is what makes the transition possible. Without it, the move from embryology to a street confrontation in Mecca would be abrupt; with it, the confrontation is an instance of the rule. The verse also does something quieter: it converts the sūrah's first half from a description of gifts into a statement of obligations. A creature made, sustained and taught, who is returning to the one who did all three, is a creature with an account to settle. The eleven verses that follow are the beginning of that settlement.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:9

> Have you seen the man who prevents

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Turns From a Principle to a Scene**

Eight verses have laid out a general account of the human being: made from a clot, taught by the pen, prone to excess, bound to return. Now the sūrah narrows to a person and an event. The shift is signalled by *a-raʾayta*, "have you seen," a formula the Qur'an uses to introduce something the listener is meant to look at closely. What follows is not a parable. The exegetes identify the man as Abū Jahl — ʿAmr ibn Hishām of the Banū Makhzūm, one of the leading opponents of the Prophet ﷺ in Mecca — and the incident as his attempt to stop the Prophet from praying at the Kaʿbah. The reports describing this passage were revealed, they say, years after the opening verses, attached to a specific confrontation. The sūrah records the confrontation in the third person and leaves the name out, which is characteristic: the Qur'an names almost none of its opponents, preferring to describe the act.

**A-Raʾayta: Seeing With the Mind**

The formula does not ask whether the listener has literally witnessed the scene. *Raʾā* in this construction means to perceive or to consider, and the Qur'an uses it to force an evaluation: have you considered the one who does such a thing? The same opening appears in Sūrat al-Māʿūn: have you seen the one who denies the Recompense? That is the one who repels the orphan (107:1–2), where the question introduces a character study rather than an event. Here it works the same way. The listener is being asked to form a judgement about a type of person, using a real case as the example. The rhetorical effect is to make the listener the judge. The sūrah does not pronounce on the man in this verse; it asks the listener what he thinks of someone who would do this, and the answer is obvious before the sentence is finished.

**What Kind of Act Preventing Prayer Is**

Preventing prayer is not a minor offence in the sūrah's framing, and the reason is structural. Prayer is the act by which a human being acknowledges dependence — the very dependence the previous verses described and the very thing the transgressor denies. To stop someone praying is therefore not to interfere with a private habit but to attack the recognition on which the whole relationship rests. It is the practical form of the transgression described in verses six and seven: the man who considers himself self-sufficient does not merely decline to pray himself, he wants the act of praying removed from view. The Qur'an treats the obstruction of worship as a grave matter elsewhere too, asking who does greater wrong than one who prevents the mosques of God from being used for His name to be remembered, and strives to ruin them (2:114).

**Why the Qur'an Records the Insult at All**

A shorter revelation could have issued the threat and omitted the scene. The Qur'an keeps the scene, and the keeping has a purpose. First, it dignifies the target: the Prophet ﷺ is not described as a man being bullied but as a servant of God at prayer, and the account survives because God chose to record it. Second, it exposes the aggressor without naming him, which is more damaging than a name would be — the description outlives the man and applies to anyone who repeats the act. Third, it establishes a precedent for the community that would follow. Opposition to the Prophet took this specific form, and the verses tell later believers what such opposition actually is: not a difference of opinion but an attempt to interrupt the connection between a human being and his Lord.

**The Question Left Hanging**

The verse is incomplete as it stands, and the incompleteness is deliberate. "Have you seen the man who prevents" stops mid-thought, with the object of the prevention arriving in the next verse. Arabic allows this suspension, and it produces a moment of held attention: the listener is left waiting for the object. The effect is to make the act of preventing arrive as a shock rather than as a label. If the verse had said "have you seen the man who prevents prayer," the phrase would have been processed as information. Instead the listener holds the image of a man engaged in obstruction and is then told what is being obstructed. The construction enacts the interruption it describes, which is a small instance of the Qur'an's habit of making form carry meaning.
**A Named Man and an Unnamed Type**

The Qur'an's decision to leave Abū Jahl unnamed deserves attention, because it had alternatives. The text names other opponents — Pharaoh, Hāmān, Qārūn, Abū Lahab in the sūrah that bears his title. Here the man is described only by his act. The effect is to make the description transferable. Anyone in any period who uses position to stop another person from worshipping occupies the same place in the verse, and the warning that follows applies to him without amendment. This is a deliberate feature of Qur'anic narrative rather than an oversight: the named cases are fixed in history, while the unnamed ones remain open. A reader who encounters the verse is not learning about seventh-century Mecca so much as being shown a type he will recognise. The type is not confined to persecutors. It includes anyone who mocks, obstructs or penalises another person's prayer, and the sūrah's severity about it is measured by the response promised in the verses that follow.

**The Verb That Names the Offence**

The word translated as prevents is from the root n-h-y, which denotes forbidding or restraining someone from an act. The Qur'an builds much of its moral vocabulary on this root: forbidding what is wrong is paired throughout with enjoining what is right, and the pair defines a community's responsibility toward itself (3:104). What the verse describes is the same machinery turned around. The man is doing what societies do to enforce norms — restraining a person from a public act — except that the act being restrained is prayer and the norm being enforced is the city's own preference. The inversion is the offence. Nothing suggests that Abū Jahl acted from a sincere conviction that prayer was harmful; the reports describe annoyance, rivalry and contempt. Recording the act with a verb that belongs to moral enforcement sharpens the charge: this was not a private dislike but an attempt at coercion.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:10

> a servant ˹of Ours˺ from praying

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Word ʿAbd, and the Honour Hidden in It**

The object of the obstruction is called *ʿabd* — servant, slave. In the Qur'an the word is not a term of degradation but the highest title a human being can hold, because it names a relationship rather than a rank. God describes His messenger with it in the sūrah of the Night Journey: glory be to the One who took His servant by night (17:1), and the same word opens the account of revelation itself: blessed is He who sent down the Criterion upon His servant (25:1). To be God's *ʿabd* is to belong to Him, and the sūrah uses it here at the exact moment the man is being threatened. The construction in Arabic attaches the servanthood to God rather than leaving it bare, which is why the translation supplies "of Ours." The man being obstructed is not a rival to Abū Jahl; he is the property of the one Abū Jahl is defying.

**Prayer as the Act That Could Not Be Hidden**

The verse specifies the act: *idhā ṣallā*, when he prays. Prayer in Mecca was performed in the open, at the Kaʿbah, in full view of the city. It could have been done privately, in a house, at night. The Qur'an's own account of the early community shows that concealment was possible and sometimes practised, yet the prayer described here is public, and that publicness is what provoked the confrontation. Abū Jahl's objection was not to belief as an interior state but to its visible performance. The distinction is the one that matters in the sūrah. A faith that never appears in the body is not what the Qur'an is describing, and the threat in the following verses is directed at the man who tried to make the act disappear. Prayer is the point at which dependence becomes visible, which is why it is the first thing the self-sufficient man wants stopped.

**The Threat, as Recorded by Ibn ʿAbbās**

The confrontation is documented in the prophetic commentary on this sūrah. Ibn ʿAbbās reported that Abū Jahl said: if I see Muḥammad praying at the Kaʿbah, I will tread on his neck. When the Prophet ﷺ heard of it he said: if he did that, the angels would snatch him away. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4958, in the Book of Tafsīr, placed under the chapter heading for verse 96:15–16 and transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās through ʿIkrimah. What the report adds is the physical detail the verse leaves out: the threat was not to argue or to fine but to trample. The answer given to it is equally physical — angels, not words — and it is the same answer the sūrah gives in its own voice.

**A Servant, Not a King: The Titles Chosen**

The sūrah could have called the Prophet by name, or as messenger, or as prophet. It calls him a servant. The choice answers Abū Jahl's objection at its root. Abū Jahl's grievance, on every account of him, was about status: that this man was being followed, that he was being obeyed, that he was attracting attention that belonged elsewhere. The Qur'an's reply does not raise the Prophet's rank in worldly terms; it lowers it deliberately, to the position of a servant, and then makes that position unassailable. There is no status to compete with, so there is nothing to envy, and the one who attacks the servant is not attacking a rival but the master. The verses that follow work entirely on that basis. The threat is answered not by defending the servant's honour but by announcing who owns him.

**What the Verse Says About the Worth of Prayer**

Read against the whole sūrah, this verse assigns prayer a value it never argues for. Nothing here explains what prayer does or why it matters; it is simply the thing worth obstructing, and the obstruction is treated as a capital offence. The Qur'an often establishes value this way — by showing what someone was willing to fight over. A man who risks a confrontation with the divine to stop an act has testified to the act's power more convincingly than any defence of it could. That testimony is preserved in the text. Fourteen centuries later the verse still records the fact that prayer in Islam was considered, by its enemies, worth trying to eliminate, and by its Lord, worth defending with angels.
**Why the Scene Was Written Into the Revelation**

Revelations are not obliged to record humiliations. The Prophet ﷺ suffered worse than a threat, and most of it is not in the text. This one is, in detail, with the threat answered verse by verse. The preservation has a function for the community that came after. Believers in later centuries would face the same procedure — ridicule at prayer, obstruction of worship, pressure to make the act private — and the sūrah gives them a precedent in which God intervened in the strongest terms available. The verses are therefore a permanent statement about the value of the act being obstructed. Prayer is not defended here with arguments about its spiritual benefits; it is defended with a threat against the one who interferes with it. That is the sūrah's way of ranking the act, and the ranking is unambiguous. Nothing else in this passage receives that treatment.

**Servanthood and the Sūrah's Understanding of Freedom**

The title ʿabd answers the earlier diagnosis as directly as it answers Abū Jahl. The transgression described in verses six and seven was a claim of independence; the man being defended here is described as a servant. The two positions are exhaustive in the Qur'an's scheme — a person is either a servant of God or a servant of something else, and Sūrat al-Kāfirūn makes the exclusivity plain in its closing line: to you your religion, and to me mine (109:6). Servanthood is therefore not a lesser state that the Prophet ﷺ is described as despite his rank; it is the correct state, and the only one that is not bondage to a created thing. Abū Jahl's clan gave him a different identity — lineage, honour, numbers — and the verse offers the alternative in a single word. The man at prayer belongs to God, and the man obstructing him belongs to his assembly.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:11

> What if this ˹servant˺ is ˹rightly˺ guided

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Hypothetical That Is Not Really Hypothetical**

The verse opens with *a-raʾayta in kāna ʿalā al-hudā* — have you seen if he is upon guidance. Grammatically it is a conditional; rhetorically it is a challenge with the answer assumed. The particle *in* introduces a condition whose fulfilment is not in doubt, and the Qur'an uses the construction to make the listener supply the obvious consequence. If the man being obstructed is guided, what does that make the man doing the obstructing? The verse does not finish the thought, and the omission is the method. The listener completes it himself, and a conclusion the listener reaches on his own is harder to argue with than one handed to him. The three conditional verses that follow build a small case: guided, commanding righteousness, and — on the other side — denying and turning away.

**ʿAlā al-Hudā: Upon Guidance, Not Merely Holding It**

The preposition matters. The phrase is not "if he has guidance" but "if he is *upon* guidance" — *ʿalā al-hudā* — which places the person on top of something stable, as a rider is on a mount or a traveller on a road. The same construction appears in the opening of Sūrat al-Baqarah: those who believe in the unseen, establish prayer, and spend from what We have provided for them are upon guidance from their Lord, and it is they who will be successful (2:5). The same preposition is used of its opposite: those who traded guidance for misguidance are described as wilfully deaf, dumb and blind, so that they will never return (2:18). The image is of a path with a direction. Guidance in the Qur'an is not a feeling but a position on a route, and being upon it implies both that the route exists and that the person has been placed on it. The sūrah's first verse already told us who does the placing.

**Why the Question Is Addressed to the Obstructor's Judgement**

Commentators read these verses as a rebuke directed at Abū Jahl, and the rebuke has a specific shape. The man who tried to stop the prayer is being asked whether he has examined the thing he is trying to stop. Has he determined that the one praying is misguided? Has he tested the claim? The question implies that he has not. Obstruction of this kind is not usually based on investigation; it is based on annoyance. The sūrah exposes the procedure by putting it in the form of an inquiry: show me your finding, if you have one. The device is common in the Qur'an's arguments with the Quraysh, which repeatedly ask whether they have been given knowledge, whether they have seen, whether they were witnesses when the decree was made. None of the questions expects information; each one reveals an absence.

**Guidance as the Ground of the Whole Passage**

The verse supplies the standard by which the rest of the passage will be judged. If the servant is upon guidance, then the obstruction is not a disagreement between two opinions but an attack on a position held by divine appointment. Everything that follows depends on this: the command to ignore the threat, the announcement that God sees, the promise of the forelock. None of it would make sense if the two men were merely rival claimants. The sūrah establishes the asymmetry early and economically. One man is upon a path set by God; the other is standing in the way of it. The reader who accepts the first verse of the sūrah — that the Lord who created has commanded — has already accepted this verse as well, and the remaining verses simply draw the consequences.

**The Suspended Answer, and What the Listener Must Supply**

The verse ends without stating what happens if the condition is met, and the next verse adds a second condition rather than an answer. The accumulation of unanswered conditionals produces a specific effect: the listener is left holding three open questions and one obvious conclusion. This is characteristic of Qur'anic rhetoric in argument, where the conclusion is withheld precisely because it is unavoidable. The technique respects the listener. It does not tell him what to think; it arranges the material so that only one thought is available. By the time the fourth question arrives — does he not know that God sees — the listener has already answered the first three, and the fourth is no longer a question about Abū Jahl but a question about anyone who obstructs a person at prayer.
**The Listener Placed in the Position of Judge**

The rhetorical strategy of these verses is to hand the judgement to the listener rather than deliver it. Three questions are asked and none is answered, which means the listener must supply the verdict himself. The method has a specific advantage: a conclusion a person reaches is harder for him to abandon than one imposed on him. The Qur'an uses the device throughout its arguments with the Quraysh, and it is particularly effective here because the material is simple. Is the man praying guided? Does he encourage righteousness? Is the man obstructing him denying and turning away? Any listener who answers honestly has already condemned the obstruction, and the verses that follow merely formalise what he has concluded. The technique also protects the Prophet ﷺ from the appearance of defending himself. The case is not argued by the accused; it is put to a third party, and the third party is whoever reads the sūrah.

**Three Conditionals, and the Order They Are In**

The arrangement of the questions is itself an argument. First: is he guided? Second: does he enjoin righteousness? Third: is the other man denying and turning away? Two favourable conditions about the servant, then one damning condition about the obstructer. Nothing is asserted; everything is posed. The effect is to make the comparison without stating it, and the comparison is between a man who is right and calls others to be right, and a man who denies and withdraws. The listener draws the conclusion, and the conclusion is that the obstruction is not a dispute between equals. The Qur'an uses this method elsewhere in its arguments with the Quraysh, where a series of questions replaces a series of claims. The advantage is that the listener cannot accuse the speaker of assertion, since nothing has been asserted. What has been arranged is material, and the material supports only one verdict.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:12

> or encourages righteousness

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Second Condition, and It Is About Other People**

The first conditional asked whether the servant is guided. This one asks whether he commands or encourages *taqwā*. The addition changes the category. Personal guidance is an inward state with outward effects; enjoining *taqwā* is an act directed at others, with social consequences. The sūrah is describing a man who is not merely right but who is calling other people to be right, and that is precisely what made him intolerable to the man obstructing him. A private believer can be ignored. A person who tells a city that its practices are wrong cannot. The verse identifies the real object of Abū Jahl's hostility: not the prayer as such, but the public claim that there is a right way and that the city is not on it.

**Taqwā: The Word and Its Weight**

*Taqwā* comes from the root *w-q-y*, to shield or protect oneself, and it denotes a state of guarded care before God — mindfulness that shapes conduct. Translators use piety, God-consciousness, righteousness, or reverence, and none captures the whole. The Qur'an treats it as the substance of the religious life rather than one virtue among many: the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most mindful of Him (49:13); and provision for the journey, and the best provision is taqwā (2:197). To enjoin *taqwā* is therefore not to promote a particular rule but to call people to the orientation from which all rules follow. A man who does that is doing the most consequential thing available in his society, and the sūrah's question is asking whether the obstructer has considered that possibility before acting.

**Commanding the Good as a Defined Obligation**

The Qur'an makes this activity an explicit duty rather than an optional virtue. Let there be a group among you who call to good, enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong (3:104); you are the best community brought forth for humanity, enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong (3:110). The duty is communal and it is named as the mark of the community's distinction. Al-ʿAlaq's question sits inside that framework. When the sūrah asks whether the servant encourages righteousness, it is asking whether he is performing the function the Qur'an elsewhere assigns to the believers as such. The obstructer is therefore not merely interfering with one man's prayer; he is interfering with the mechanism by which a community keeps itself honest. The sūrah treats that interference as the graver offence, which is why it names it separately.

**Why the Two Conditions Are Kept Apart**

Guidance and the enjoining of *taqwā* could have been combined in a single clause. Keeping them separate allows the sūrah to make a point about the difference between being right and saying so. The first condition concerns the servant's own state; the second concerns his effect on others. A person might be guided and silent, and the obstruction of such a person would be a smaller matter. What the sūrah describes is a guided person who speaks, and the speech is what generates the conflict. The distinction is useful beyond this passage. Most of the opposition the Qur'an records against its prophets is not directed at their private worship but at their public call. Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ and Shuʿayb were each opposed for the same reason: they told their people that their way was wrong. Al-ʿAlaq places the Prophet ﷺ in that sequence without naming any of them.

**The Question the Obstructer Never Asked**

The rhetorical force of the verse lies in what it implies about the obstructer's procedure. Nobody asks whether a man encourages righteousness before trying to stop him. The question is not on the agenda of someone who has already decided that the man must be stopped. By raising it, the sūrah reveals that the decision was made without examination, and that is the indictment. The verse does not accuse Abū Jahl of cruelty, though he was cruel; it accuses him of not having looked. The three conditionals together — guided, enjoining righteousness, and then, on the other side, denying and turning away — form a comparison the obstructer refused to make. The sūrah makes it for him, in public, in the text that will be read for as long as the text exists.
**The Public Character of the Call, and Its Cost**

Enjoining righteousness is not a safe activity, and the sūrah knows it. The man who tells his society that its practices are wrong makes enemies by definition, and the Qur'an records the cost in the accounts of every previous messenger. Noah's people covered their ears and wrapped themselves in their garments and persisted in their refusal (71:7); Hūd's people told him they saw him as a fool and thought he was a liar (7:66); Shuʿayb was threatened with expulsion from his own land unless he returned to their faith (7:88). Al-ʿAlaq compresses the pattern into a single question. By asking whether the servant encourages righteousness, the sūrah places the Prophet ﷺ inside the sequence without naming a predecessor, and it identifies the reason for the hostility he faced. The obstruction of the prayer was not a spontaneous act of dislike; it was the predictable reaction of a society to a man who had told it that it was wrong.

**Righteousness as the Measure of a Whole Society**

The verse treats the enjoining of taqwā as a measurable contribution rather than a personal virtue. A society in which nobody calls anyone to righteousness has no internal correction; its practices harden and its injustices become ordinary. The Qur'an describes that state as a consequence of abandoning the practice, and identifies the abandonment among the reasons previous communities were destroyed. The servant described here is performing a function the city needs and does not want, which is a familiar position for the prophets. Al-ʿAlaq puts the point in a question rather than a sermon, and the question is aimed at the man who objected. Has he considered whether the person he is obstructing is calling people to what is right? If so, the obstruction is not only an offence against one man but an injury to everyone the call would have reached. The verse leaves that consequence standing behind the question instead of developing it.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:13

> What if that ˹man˺ persists in denial and turns away

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Conditional Turns on the Obstructer Himself**

The first two questions were about the servant. This one is about the man doing the obstructing: have you seen if he denies and turns away? The shift is the pivot of the passage. After establishing what the servant is — guided, enjoining righteousness — the sūrah asks the listener to consider what the obstructer is. The demonstrative and the change of subject make the contrast explicit: that man, the one who has been acting as though he held authority to decide. The question is not whether he denies; the sūrah takes the denial as known. It asks whether the listener has grasped the combination — denial in the heart together with turning away in conduct — and what that combination produces in a person who holds power.

**Kadhdhaba and Tawallā: Denial and Withdrawal Together**

Two verbs are used, and they cover two domains. *Kadhdhaba* is the intensive form of lying or calling something a lie; it denotes an active rejection of a truth one has been shown, not mere absence of belief. *Tawallā* means to turn away, to turn one's back, to withdraw from a thing. The pairing is common in the Qur'an and always deliberate: the denial is internal, the turning away is physical. Together they describe a person who has both refused the claim and removed himself from the situation in which it could reach him. That is a more complete state than either verb alone. A person who doubts but remains present can still be addressed; a person who denies and turns away has closed the channel. The Qur'an treats this as the most dangerous condition a human being can be in, and it is the condition the sūrah attributes to the man threatening a person at prayer.

**Why the Two Verbs Explain the Obstruction**

The combination accounts for the behaviour described in the previous verses. A man who merely disagreed would argue. A man who was merely indifferent would ignore. The man who denies and turns away does something else: he removes the thing that reminds him of what he has rejected. Prayer performed in public is a standing reminder, and the reminder is unbearable to someone who has turned his back. Hence the attempt to stop it. The sūrah's diagnosis is psychologically exact. The aggression is not confidence; it is the reaction of a person who cannot tolerate the presence of what he has refused. The verse explains the scene without excusing it, and the explanation makes the subsequent warning intelligible rather than merely severe.

**The Referent Question, and Which Reading Holds**

Commentators differ on whether *kadhdhaba wa-tawallā* refers to Abū Jahl or continues to refer to the Prophet ﷺ in the hypothetical frame of the previous verses — as if to say, have you considered what would follow if he were one who denied and turned away? The majority take the referent as Abū Jahl, and the reasons are textual. The two previous conditionals were favourable descriptions of the servant, and a third in the same series would be redundant; the tone shifts from favourable to condemnatory, which signals a change of subject; and the verses that follow address the one who denies with a direct threat, which only makes sense if that person is the obstructer. The minority reading is not impossible in grammar, but it leaves the passage without a subject for the warning. The majority reading is the coherent one.

**The Same Pairing Across the Qur'an**

Denial paired with turning away is a recurring portrait, and its recurrence shows that the Qur'an treats it as a type rather than an incident. The sūrah of al-Qiyāmah asks: does man think he will be left neglected? and then describes the man who neither believed nor prayed, who persisted in denial and turned away, and then went to his own people walking boastfully (75:31–33). The same figure appears in the accounts of previous peoples, who were told, turned away, and were then overtaken. Al-ʿAlaq contributes the specific case: the man who denied and turned away in Mecca was the same man who threatened to trample a praying servant, and the sūrah's warning to him is the working out of a pattern the Qur'an has already described many times.
**What Denial Plus Turning Away Produces in Practice**

The two verbs together describe a procedure, and the procedure explains the scene the sūrah is describing. A person who denies a claim but stays in its presence remains answerable to it; the claim continues to reach him, and he must keep refusing it, which is uncomfortable. The efficient solution is to remove the source. That is what the obstructer attempts: not to refute the prayer but to stop it, not to answer the call but to silence the caller. The Qur'an shows the same procedure in its accounts of earlier peoples, whose response to their messengers was consistently some form of removal — expulsion, threat, mockery, or a demand that the messenger stop speaking. Al-ʿAlaq's contribution is to name the internal state that produces the procedure. Denial alone would be an intellectual position. Denial combined with turning away is a strategy, and a strategy of that kind escalates, which is why the sūrah answers it with a warning rather than with an argument.

**A Portrait of Settled Opposition**

The two verbs describe a condition rather than a moment. Denial that is repeated becomes settled; turning away that is maintained becomes a way of life. The sūrah is not describing a man who had a bad day or who raised an objection in the heat of an argument. It is describing someone who has organised himself around a refusal, and the description explains the persistence of the opposition the Prophet ﷺ faced in Mecca. Thirteen years of rejection were not thirteen years of repeated consideration and repeated rejection; they were the working out of a position taken early and never re-examined. That is what denial plus turning away names, and it is why the verse is followed by a warning rather than by an appeal. A person in this condition is not reachable by argument, because he has removed himself from the situations in which arguments reach people.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:14

> Does he not know that Allah sees ˹all˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Shortest Verse in the Passage Carries Its Whole Argument**

Six Arabic words — *a-lam yaʿlam bi-anna Allāha yarā* — and the passage turns from description to warning. The question is not whether God exists; the man addressed is not an atheist in any modern sense. It is whether God *sees*, and whether the man knows it. The distinction is what gives the verse its bite. A person can acknowledge a deity and still act as though his own conduct were unobserved, and that is precisely the condition the sūrah has been describing. The self-sufficiency of verse seven is a practical doctrine: I am beyond need. This verse names the assumption underneath it: I am beyond observation. The two collapse together. Once the watching is removed, the need goes with it, and the boundaries described in verse six stop applying.

**The Object of Seeing Left Unstated**

The verb *yarā* has no object in the Arabic, and the omission is comprehensive. God sees — everything, everyone, at all times, including this. Nothing is specified because nothing is excluded. The same absolute use appears in God's assurance to Mūsā and Hārūn: fear not, I am with you both, hearing and seeing (20:46), where the two faculties are stated without objects for the same reason. Commentators note that the unqualified form is stronger than any qualified one would be, since specifying an object implies a limit. The verse is answering the man who obstructed a prayer performed in a public square: that prayer was seen, the threat was seen, the boast was seen. Nothing about the scene was private, and nothing about it has been lost.

**Knowledge and Seeing: Why Both Verbs Are Needed**

The verse joins two verbs that do different work. *Yaʿlam* is knowledge; *yarā* is sight. The man is asked whether he *knows* that God *sees*, which means the deficiency is located in his knowledge rather than in God's perception. God's seeing is not in question; the man's awareness of it is. That is a precise diagnosis, and it explains why the warning that follows takes the form it does. A person who does not know he is watched behaves differently from one who does, and the difference is not in the watching but in the behaviour. The Qur'an makes the same point in its description of the righteous: those who are in awe of their Lord without seeing Him, and are fearful of the Hour (21:49). The unseen observer is the fact that makes conduct real rather than performative.

**The Verse as the Ground of Everything After It**

The structure of the passage depends on this question. What follows — the seizure by the forelock, the summoning of the wardens, the command not to obey — is enforcement, and enforcement presupposes a witness. If God did not see, the threats would be empty. The verse installs the witness in four words, and the sūrah then proceeds as though the matter were settled. This is a common Qur'anic method: a single foundational statement, then a series of consequences drawn from it without further argument. The reader is not asked to be persuaded at each step. He is asked to accept one premise and follow it, and the premise here is the most basic one in the Qur'an's moral order — that nothing is unobserved.

**Addressed to a Man, Applicable to Everyone**

The verse is about Abū Jahl, and it is not only about him. The Qur'an's habit is to use a named case as the instance of a general rule, and the general rule here is stated in the negative interrogative form that invites the listener to apply it to himself. Every person who acts differently when he believes he is unseen has accepted the premise the verse rejects. The sūrah does not moralise about this; it states the fact and moves on to the consequence. That economy is what makes the passage effective. No sermon is delivered about hypocrisy, no list of behaviours is produced. A single question establishes that the watching is continuous, and the listener is left to notice how much of his own conduct depends on the opposite assumption.
**The Watching as the Basis of the Whole Moral Order**

The question in this verse is doing more than threatening one man. It is asserting the condition without which the Qur'an's moral system cannot function. Commandments addressed to a person who believes he is unobserved are enforced only by other people, and other people are absent for most of a life. The Qur'an's ethics therefore rests on a prior fact: the observation is continuous, and it includes what no other person can see. That is why the verse uses the verb of seeing rather than the verb of knowing or judging. Judgement comes later; seeing is happening now. Every instruction in the Qur'an about sincerity, about intention, about what a person does when alone, presupposes this verse. The man in Mecca who threatened a praying servant had not considered it, and the sūrah asks him whether he knows it — a question that is really addressed to every reader who behaves differently in private.

**What the Question Presupposes About the Man**

The form of the question tells us something about its target. A-lam yaʿlam — does he not know — presupposes that knowledge was available to him. The verse does not ask whether God has made Himself known; it asks whether the man knows something that has been shown. The Meccan audience had the sūrah's own first verses, the public preaching, the repeated arguments from creation, and the visible fact of a man praying at the Kaʿbah in defiance of threats. The material was not hidden. This is the basis on which the Qur'an holds people accountable: knowledge must reach a person before responsibility attaches, a principle stated plainly in the verse that God does not punish until a messenger has been sent (17:15). The man addressed here had received the message and rejected it, so the question is not about his information but about his attention. He had the facts and did not act on them.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:15

> But no! If he does not desist, We will certainly drag him by the forelock—

**Expanded Commentary**

**Kallā a Second Time, and Then a Physical Image**

The particle of refusal returns, and this time it is followed by an action. The first *kallā* rejected the transgressor's premise; this one closes the discussion and introduces the consequence. What follows is the most concrete image in the sūrah: a hand taking hold of the hair at the front of a man's head and dragging him. The Qur'an often moves from abstraction to body — from decree to seizure, from warning to grip — and the move is not decoration. A threat expressed in physical terms cannot be spiritualised away. The man who boasted about his clan is being told, in the idiom of the street he boasted in, exactly what will happen to him. The sūrah has been arguing with ideas for fourteen verses; at the fifteenth it stops arguing.

**La-Nasfaʿan: A Verb of Violent Seizure**

The verb *nasaʿa* means to pull or drag forcibly, and the form used here is doubly emphatic: the prefixed *lām* of oath and the suffixed *nūn* of emphasis. Arabic grammar reserves this combination for statements the speaker intends as sworn and certain. Commentators gloss it as taking hold of something and pulling it hard, and the image is of a man seized and hauled, not gently led. The emphasis particles matter because they answer the transgressor's assumption. A person who considers himself beyond need assumes that consequences are uncertain or remote. The grammatical form removes the uncertainty: this will happen, it is sworn, and there is no ambiguity in the verb. The sūrah gives the man one condition to avoid it, and the condition is stated before the threat rather than after — if he does not desist.

**The Forelock in Arab Life and in the Qur'an**

The *nāṣiyah* is the forelock, the hair at the front of the head, and in Arab usage it stood for the person himself. To seize a man's forelock was to humiliate him completely; to place one's forelock in someone's hand was an act of total submission. The Qur'an uses the image elsewhere with the same force: the guilty will be known by their marks and seized by their forelocks and their feet (55:41). More comprehensively, it states that God holds every creature by its forelock: there is no moving creature but He holds it by its forelock; my Lord is upon a straight path (11:56). That verse supplies the theology behind this one. The forelock is not merely a convenient grip; it is the Qur'an's symbol for the fact that no creature is outside divine control, and the threat here is the application of that control to a specific man who denied it.

**Why the Forelock Rather Than the Hand or the Neck**

The choice of body part carries meaning beyond mechanics. Abū Jahl had threatened to trample the Prophet's neck; the answer names the aggressor's own head. The forelock is the front of the head, the visible part of the face's upper region, the part that leads when a person walks. Seizing it is therefore seizing the leading part of a man — his pride, his direction, his public self. The Qur'an's use of the same image in 11:56 confirms the reading: the forelock stands for the whole creature under control. There is also a symmetry the verse does not point out but does not hide. The man intended to put his foot on the neck of a servant at prayer; he will be taken by the front of his own head. The sūrah leaves the comparison to the listener, which is more effective than stating it.

**The Condition Attached: If He Does Not Desist**

The threat is conditional, and the condition is genuine. The verse does not say the man will be seized; it says he will be seized if he does not stop. The Qur'an is consistent on this point — a warning is issued so that it may be avoided, and the door remains open until the moment closes. The historical outcome is known: Abū Jahl did not desist. He continued to lead the opposition in Mecca, was among the chief figures at Badr, and was killed there in the second year after the emigration. The sūrah's warning was fulfilled in the man's own lifetime, within a few years of its revelation, and the fulfilment is part of why the passage was preserved. A conditional threat that is met with refusal and then carried out establishes the seriousness of every other conditional in the Qur'an.

**The Sworn Form of the Threat**

The grammatical construction carries the certainty the verse claims. Arabic expresses emphasis by combining the prefixed lām of oath with the suffixed emphatic nūn on the verb, and the two together produce a statement that is both sworn and stressed. The translation renders it as "We will certainly drag him," which is accurate but flat; the original is closer to a sworn declaration made in the moment of speaking. The Qur'an reserves this form for statements it intends to be beyond negotiation, and its use here answers the specific assumption the man was operating under — that threats coming from the Prophet's side carried no force. A person who considers himself self-sufficient discounts consequences he does not expect to be enforced. The form of the verb removes the discount, and it does so before the image that follows has even been described.

**What the Image Would Have Meant in Mecca**

The image is drawn from the world its audience lived in. Seizing a man by the forelock was a public humiliation performed on captives and on those utterly defeated, and it appears in the poetry of the period as the mark of a man whose power has been taken from him. The verse therefore describes no abstract punishment but a specific social outcome, and it describes it to a man whose entire standing rested on never being in that position. The threat is calibrated to its target. Abū Jahl feared loss of face more than he feared death, and the verse names the loss of face in the most concrete terms available. The historical record shows the calibration was not wasted: he died at Badr in the second year after the emigration, and the accounts of that day describe his end as one of exposure rather than of honour.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:16

> a lying, sinful forelock

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Body Part Described With Moral Adjectives**

The verse does something grammatically unusual, and the unusualness is the point. A forelock is a piece of hair; hair does not lie and hair does not sin. Yet the verse attaches two moral adjectives to it — *kādhibah*, lying, and *khāṭiʾah*, sinful — and does so without explanation. Arabic permits this transfer freely, and the Qur'an uses it to concentrate an indictment into a single phrase. The forelock is named because the forelock will be seized, and the description tells us what kind of person is attached to it. The effect is a portrait in two words. Everything the previous verses established about the man — his denial, his turning away, his assumption that he is unobserved — is compressed into the description of the thing that will be grabbed.

**Kādhibah and Khāṭiʾah: Two Different Charges**

The two adjectives are not synonyms, and the pairing covers two domains. *Kādhibah*, from *kadhib*, concerns speech and claim: the forelock lies, meaning the man lied — in his denial of the message, in his threats, in his boast about the number of his supporters. *Khāṭiʾah*, from *khaṭiʾa*, concerns action and fault: the forelock is sinful, meaning the man committed wrong. The Qur'an distinguishes the two elsewhere, and the distinction is meaningful: a person can lie without acting wrongly, and can act wrongly without lying. To be described with both terms is to be indicted on both counts. The verse therefore says, in the shortest available form, that this man's speech was false and his conduct was corrupt, and that both are known. Nothing about the indictment requires a trial; the description is delivered as established fact.

**Arabic Idiom: Attributing to the Part What Belongs to the Whole**

The construction has a name in the rhetorical tradition — attribution of a quality to a part of a person in place of the person himself — and it is common in classical Arabic poetry, where a man's hand, eye, or forelock is described with the man's own attributes. The Qur'an uses the idiom with precision rather than ornament. By naming the forelock instead of the man, the verse does three things at once: it identifies the point of seizure, it strips the man of his name and his clan, and it reduces him to a part of himself. The boast recorded in the reports about this passage was about numbers and supporters; the answer mentions none of that. A man who claimed to be the most powerful person in Mecca is described in the text as a lying, sinful forelock. The reduction is the punishment before the punishment.

**The Forelock as the Leading Part of the Man**

Commentators note that the forelock is the front of the head, and the front of the head is where a person's decisions are visibly registered — the face turned toward or away from something, the eyes directed, the expression of contempt or assent. To describe the forelock as lying and sinful is to locate the man's falsehood and his fault in the part of him that leads. The Qur'an's wider use of the image supports this: God holds every creature by its forelock (11:56), which places the grip at the point of direction rather than at the point of strength. Modern readers sometimes observe that the region of the brain behind the forehead is associated with planning and decision, and while that observation should not be read back into the text as its meaning, it is consistent with the image the verse selects. The leading part of the man is the part that is taken.

**Why the Description Falls on the Organ Rather Than the Man**

There is a final reason for the construction, and it is the most severe. A person's name can be defended, his lineage can be invoked, his supporters can be summoned — the following verses show the man attempting exactly that. A forelock has none of those resources. By transferring the indictment to a body part, the verse removes every avenue of defence the man actually had in Meccan society. He cannot call on his clan to vindicate a forelock. He cannot claim that the hair was misunderstood. What is left is the bare fact: a lying, sinful thing, held by God. The sūrah's method throughout this passage is to strip the transgressor of what he relied on — first his assumption of independence, then his clan, then his own person — and this verse completes the third step in two words.

**Two Words That Function as a Verdict**

The brevity of the description is judicial. A court pronounces a finding in a formula, and the formula here is two adjectives attached to a body part. No defence is heard, no evidence is listed, no procedure is described, because in the Qur'an's account the evidence is not in dispute: the denial and the turning away were stated in the previous verses, and the watching was affirmed in the verse before those. The verdict simply follows. This is characteristic of the Qur'an's eschatological language, which often states outcomes as though they were already accomplished. The sūrah uses the same technique in compressed form: the man is described as lying and sinful before any judgement scene has taken place, because the judgement has already been made by the one who sees.

**The Forelock and the Punishment Described Elsewhere**

The image connects to a wider description of the punishment of the Fire. The guilty will be recognised by their marks and seized by their forelocks and their feet (55:41), a verse that pairs the same body part with the feet and assigns the seizure to the angels of punishment. Al-ʿAlaq uses the forelock alone, and the choice concentrates the image on the leading part of the person. There is also a connection to the sūrah's own vocabulary: the man who threatened to trample a neck is answered with the seizure of a head, and the symmetry is exact. The Qur'an's punishments are frequently fitted to the crime in this way, and the fitting is not incidental. It makes the punishment legible as a response rather than as an arbitrary severity.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:17

> So let him call his associates

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Imperative That Is a Challenge, Not a Permission**

The form *fa-lyadʿu* is a third-person command: let him call. Arabic uses this construction for challenge and derision as readily as for permission, and the context here removes any ambiguity. Nothing is being granted. The man has been told that his forelock will be seized, and the answer to that threat is an invitation to bring his help. The imperative is rhetorical, of the same kind as the Qur'an's challenge elsewhere: call upon those you claim besides Him, and they will not remove your hardship nor transfer it (17:56). The verse is daring the man to do the one thing he thinks will save him, and the daring is safe because the sūrah knows the outcome. What follows in the next verse is the answer to the call before it has been made.

**Al-Nādī: The Assembly of the Clan**

The word *nādī* denotes the gathering place of a tribe or clan and, by extension, the men gathered there — the council, the assembly, the people of standing who sit together and decide. In Meccan society the *nādī* was the institution that mattered. It was where protection was granted, where disputes were settled, where a man's worth was counted in the number of those who would stand with him. Abū Jahl's clan, the Banū Makhzūm, was among the strongest in Quraysh, and his own claim, on the account of Ibn ʿAbbās, was that no one in the valley had more people to call for assistance than he did. The report is in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3349, graded ṣaḥīḥ by Dār al-Salām; al-Tirmidhī himself called it ḥasan gharīb ṣaḥīḥ. The verse names that institution precisely because it is the man's whole basis of confidence.

**The Boast, and the Occasion of the Verse**

The scene is described in the same report. The Prophet ﷺ was praying when Abū Jahl came and said, have I not forbidden you from this — three times. The Prophet turned and scolded him. Abū Jahl answered with his boast, and the two verses were revealed. Ibn ʿAbbās added: by God, had he called his council, the wardens of God would have seized him. The wording and the occasion of revelation are therefore given in a single graded report (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3349), not assembled from later commentary. Whether or not every detail is exact, the reports agree on the substance: the confrontation was about numbers, and the Qur'an answered it with a comparison of numbers. That is why the two verses are paired as they are — let him call his assembly; We will call the wardens. The structure is a direct reply, in the man's own terms, to the man's own argument.

**A Challenge Issued by God, Not by the Prophet**

The voice in this verse is not the Prophet's. Nothing in the wording suggests that Muḥammad ﷺ is challenging Abū Jahl to a contest of supporters; the challenge comes from the one who will call the wardens. The distinction matters for how the passage was received. A prophet out-numbered in a city where his clan's protection had been withdrawn would have had no standing to issue a challenge of this kind, and the Quraysh would have treated it as bravado. The verse is presented as a statement from a party the Quraysh did not recognise, which is why it functioned as a test rather than as an insult. Those who accepted the sūrah as revelation heard a promise; those who did not heard a claim they could mock. The historical outcome settled which reading was correct.

**The Clan as the Only Power the Man Recognised**

The verse is also a diagnosis. Abū Jahl is told to call his assembly because that is the only resource he believes in. He has no argument against the prayer, no evidence against the message, and no answer to the question of whether God sees. What he has is a council of men, and the sūrah allows him that resource in order to display its limits. The method is characteristic: the Qur'an frequently lets an opponent state his strongest claim and then answers it on its own ground. Here the ground is collective strength, and the answer in the next verse is a different collective. The comparison is not between God and a clan; it is between the men the man can summon and the beings God will summon. That is the man's own frame of reference, and inside it he loses.

**The Limits of the Assembly in Qur'anic Perspective**

The Qur'an takes tribal solidarity seriously as a fact and dismisses it as a resource. An assembly can protect a man in this world, and the text does not pretend otherwise; the Prophet ﷺ himself lost his protection when Abū Ṭālib died, and the years that followed were the hardest of the Meccan period. What is denied is that the assembly has standing beyond this world. The accounts of earlier peoples in Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ show the same elites answering their messengers with threats of expulsion and stoning, and being destroyed: Shuʿayb's people told him they would stone him if not for his clan (11:91). Al-ʿAlaq compresses the point into four words: let him call them. The invitation is safe because the assembly has no jurisdiction over what is coming, and the verse exists to make that limit visible to everyone listening.

**What the Verse Leaves Unanswered**

The challenge is issued and the sūrah does not wait for a response. No outcome is described, no scene of the assembly being convened is narrated, and nothing in these verses reports whether Abū Jahl attempted it. The silence is deliberate. The Qur'an frequently issues a challenge and moves on, leaving fulfilment to events the listeners will witness. The effect is to keep the verse open: it was addressed to a man in Mecca, and it remains addressed to anyone who relies on a group rather than on God. A reader who belongs to a powerful family, a party, a profession or a nation is being asked the same question in the same terms. Call them. The verse does not need to describe the result, because the result is identical in every case and the reader already knows it.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:18

> We will call the wardens of Hell

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Answer Arrives in the Same Verb**

The reply repeats the man's own construction. He will call; We will call. The parallel is exact and deliberate, and it converts the man's challenge into a contest he has already entered. The first-person plural — *sana-dʿu al-zabāniyah*, We will call the wardens — places God as the summoner, which changes the scale of the comparison without the verse needing to say so. A man calls men from his clan's council; God calls beings whose function is the punishment of the Fire. The sūrah does not argue that God is stronger than Quraysh. It simply sets the two calls side by side in identical grammar and lets the difference in the objects do the work. Rhetorically this is one of the tightest exchanges in the Qur'an: eight words in Arabic answering nine.

**Al-Zabāniyah: The Wardens Named**

*Zabāniyah* is a plural with no ordinary singular in use, and the commentators derive it from a root meaning to push away or repel — the ones who push people back, or drive them forward. The Qur'an uses the term here and describes the same function elsewhere: over it are angels, stern and severe, who do not disobey God in what He commands them and who do as they are commanded (66:6). The description answers a question the verse itself does not raise: these are not independent agents but functionaries, and their severity is obedience. The word carries no mythology with it; it names a role. That matters for how the verse should be read. The threat is not that a monster will be unleashed but that an appointment will be kept, by beings whose only characteristic is that they do what they are told.

**Nineteen, and the Argument About the Number**

The Qur'an gives a number for the keepers of the Fire in Sūrat al-Muddaththir: over it are nineteen (74:30), followed immediately by the statement that they are made none other than angels, and that their number is a trial for those who disbelieve (74:31). The Meccan response, as the same passage records, was to treat nineteen as a small number that could be overcome. The answer given is that strength is not measured by counting. Al-ʿAlaq makes the same point without a number: let the man summon his assembly, and God will summon the wardens. The comparison the Quraysh wanted to make — how many against how many — is refused at the level where it would matter. A clan can be counted; the wardens cannot be counted in a way that helps the person facing them.

**The Contrast of Two Gatherings**

The pairing of verses seventeen and eighteen produces a picture, and the picture is the argument. On one side, a council of Meccan notables, convened by a man who is proud of their number, sitting in a city that has expelled the message. On the other, the wardens of the Fire, called by the one who owns the Fire. The sūrah does not describe the second gathering because it does not need to; the name is enough. The contrast is between a human institution, which depends on agreement, interest and fear, and an appointment already made, which depends on nothing in the created order. Every resource Abū Jahl had — his clan, his standing, his allies — operates inside the world that will end. The wardens operate outside it. That is the whole of the answer, and it takes one verse.

**Why the Answer Is Not Immediate**

The verse promises a calling, not an event. The man is not struck down where he stands, and the sūrah does not claim he will be. The delay is consistent with the Qur'an's treatment of threats elsewhere: the respite is real, and it is part of the test. The historical record shows the outcome arriving within a few years — Abū Jahl died at Badr — but the text itself leaves the timing with God. The effect on the original listeners was double. For those who mocked, the absence of an immediate sign confirmed their mockery. For those who believed, the promise was a statement about the future that they had to hold without evidence. The Qur'an's threats work this way as a rule, and the sūrah of al-ʿAlaq ends not with a demonstration but with a command to keep praying.

**The Wardens and the Obedience of Angels**

The Qur'an's description of the keepers of the Fire removes any suggestion that they are independent powers. Over it are angels, stern and severe, who do not disobey God in what He commands them and who do as they are commanded (66:6). The emphasis on obedience is theologically important. The Fire and its wardens are not a rival kingdom inside the Qur'an's cosmos; they are part of the same administration as the angels who bring mercy, and their severity is a function of their compliance. This matters for how the threat in al-ʿAlaq should be read. The man is not being threatened with the release of a force that might be bargained with or appeased. He is being told that an order exists and will be executed by agents who do not deviate from it. The certainty of the threat rests on the obedience of the executors.

**The Answer in the Man's Own Terms**

The rhetorical achievement of pairing verses seventeen and eighteen is that it accepts the opponent's frame entirely. The man argued in numbers; the reply is in numbers. He claimed an assembly; the reply names a different assembly. No appeal is made to a standard the man would reject, and no theological preamble is offered. The sūrah meets him inside his own system of measurement and shows him losing there. The method runs through the Qur'an's arguments: to those who demanded a sign, signs are named; to those who boasted of wealth, wealth is compared; to those who relied on their clan, the clan is counted against something larger. The verse is therefore not a threat from outside the man's world but a demonstration inside it, which is why it needed so few words.

## Sūrah al-ʿAlaq 96:19

> Again, no! Never obey him ˹O Prophet˺! Rather, ˹continue to˺ prostrate and draw near ˹to Allah˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Third Kallā, and a Sūrah That Ends With an Instruction**

The particle of refusal appears for the third time, and after it comes the only direct command in the second half of the sūrah. The structure is worth noting: eleven verses about a man who tried to stop a prayer end with an instruction to keep praying. Nothing is said about retaliation, about answering the threat, or about the outcome of the confrontation. The final words of the first revelation in Islam are *wa-sjud wa-qtarib* — prostrate, and draw near. Whatever else the sūrah establishes, its last statement is about the act that was being obstructed, and it is addressed to the one who was obstructed. The refusal and the command belong together: no, do not obey him, and yes, continue.

**Do Not Obey Him: The Refusal Stated Without Argument**

*Lā tuṭiʿhu* — do not obey him — is the plainest possible rejection, and it is given without reasons. No case is made against Abū Jahl, no comparison of consequences is offered, no negotiation is proposed. The Qur'an uses the same formula elsewhere: so do not obey the deniers (68:8), where the command is followed immediately by the statement that they wish the Prophet would compromise so that they might compromise in return (68:9). The pattern is consistent. Obedience to a person who obstructs worship is not a matter for calculation, because the thing being calculated away is the worship itself. The verse treats the demand for compliance as illegitimate on its face. A man who tries to stop a servant from praying has no standing to be obeyed, and the sūrah declines to argue the point.

**Wa-Sjud: The Act That Answers the Threat**

The command is to prostrate, and the choice of act is precise. Prostration is the posture in which the forehead — the front of the head, the region the previous verses named — is placed on the ground. The man who threatened to trample a neck is answered with an instruction to put the highest part of the body at the lowest point. The Qur'an treats prostration as the definitive act of submission: the true believers in God's revelation are those who fall into prostration when it is recited to them and glorify their Lord, and who are not too proud (32:15), and the Prophet ﷺ said that the nearest a servant comes to his Lord is when he is prostrating, so make supplication in that state; the report is in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 482. The verse's command therefore does not merely resume the interrupted prayer. It prescribes the specific posture that most directly contradicts the transgressor's claim of independence.

**Wa-Qtarib: Drawing Near as the Purpose of the Act**

The second verb gives the reason for the first. Prostration is not an end in itself; it is a means of proximity, and the Qur'an names the proximity as the goal. The sequence in the verse is the sequence in the act: the body goes down, the distance closes. This is why the sūrah can end here. A man threatened with violence, mocked by his society, and told to stop praying is given a two-word instruction whose content is not defence but approach. The verse reorders the whole situation. The threat assumes that the important thing is the man's power; the command asserts that the important thing is the distance between a servant and his Lord. Everything else in the passage — the forelock, the clan, the wardens — is arranged around that distance, and the command tells the Prophet ﷺ which side of it to work on.

**The Prostration of Recitation**

This verse is one of the places in the Qur'an where a prostration of recitation is prescribed: a reader or listener who reaches it performs a single prostration. The practice is established in the reports of the Companions and is treated in Islamic law as a recommended act at the fifteen such verses. The placement of the prostration at the end of this sūrah is appropriate to its content. The reader who has just gone through the account of a man who tried to prevent a prayer is invited to perform the prayer's defining posture immediately, in the act of reading. The sūrah does not merely describe the interrupted worship; it produces it. That is a small instance of the Qur'an's method — a text that expects to be enacted, not merely read — and it closes the first revelation with the body rather than with a proposition.

**The Command to the Prophet and to the Community After Him**

The instruction is addressed in the second person singular, and the immediate addressee is the Prophet ﷺ. Its scope is wider. The situation described in the sūrah — pressure to stop praying, mockery, a threat from someone with more social power — was not confined to Mecca in the first years of revelation. It recurred throughout the history of the community, and the command was preserved for those recurrences. The Qur'an regularly states a ruling in a specific case and leaves the generality to be understood, and the exegetes treat this verse as an instance of that method. The practical content is simple: when someone with power tells you to stop worshipping, the answer is not negotiation but the continuation of the act, coupled with the nearness the act produces. The verse offers no strategy beyond that, which suggests it did not think one was needed.

**Prostration as the Refutation of Self-Sufficiency**

The sūrah began by diagnosing a disease: the human being exceeds his bounds when he considers himself self-sufficient. It ends by prescribing the opposite posture. Prostration is the physical denial of self-sufficiency — the head, the highest part of the body and the seat of the pride in question, placed on the ground. The connection between the two halves is therefore not merely structural but therapeutic. The cure for the feeling of independence is not an argument; it is an act performed repeatedly, in a fixed form, at fixed times, in which the body rehearses the truth the mind has forgotten. The verse's two verbs supply both the act and its purpose, and the sūrah's last words leave the reader doing rather than concluding.
