# Sūrah Nūḥ (Chapter 71) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Named for a Preacher, Not for a Flood**

Sūrah Nūḥ is the seventy-first chapter of the Qur'an, twenty-eight verses long, revealed in Makkah. It takes its name from the prophet it reports at length, and it is one of four chapters named after a prophet from the earlier scriptures — the others being Yūnus, Yūsuf, and Ibrāhīm. The name promises a story and delivers something narrower and harder: not the biography of Noah, but the record of his preaching.

There is no ark in this sūrah. No boat is built, no wood is cut, no water rises, no mountain is climbed, no son drowns on screen. The flood appears as half a clause in verse twenty-five, and the fire that follows it as the other half. Everything else — twenty-six of the twenty-eight verses — is speech: the commission, the call, the sermon of provision and creation, the report of refusal, and the closing prayers. The sūrah is a document about what preaching costs when nobody listens.

**A Story Told in Pieces, and Why**

The Qur'an returns to Noah in at least nine other places, and no two accounts are the same: 7:59–64, 10:71–73, 11:25–48, 21:76–77, 23:23–30, 26:105–121, 29:14–15, 37:75–82, and 54:9–15. Some give the dialogue with the chiefs, some the building of the vessel, some the drowning of the son, some the rain and the oven. Together they do not add up to a continuous chronicle, and they are not meant to. The Qur'an states its own reason for telling prophet stories at all: *“And We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ the stories of the messengers to reassure your heart. And there has come to you in this ˹sûrah˺ the truth, a warning ˹to the disbelievers˺, and a reminder to the believers.”* (11:120). The function is guidance, not history, and each account selects the phase of the story that serves the audience hearing it.

This sūrah selects the phase of exhaustion. Noah has called day and night, openly and privately, for a thousand years less fifty (29:14). Every method has been tried. The audience has answered with stopped ears, covered heads, an organized plot, and enforced idolatry. What remains is a report filed with God and a petition for the case to be closed. A Makkan listener in the years of the boycott and the persecution heard his own situation described in the oldest voice available.

**Its Place Between Two Messengers**

Sūrah Nūḥ sits immediately before Sūrah al-Jinn, and the pairing is not accidental. The tradition reports Noah as the first messenger sent to the people of the earth — the title given to him by humanity itself in the intercession narration recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3340 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 194 — while al-Jinn reports a delegation of non-human listeners arriving at the last messenger's recitation. The first chapter of the pair describes a mission that ends in total refusal and a destroyed town; the second describes a mission whose audience includes beings the preacher never saw, who believed on hearing a single recitation.

Both chapters also carry the same promise in the same image: abundant water given to a people on condition that they hold to the way (71:11; 72:16). Read together, the two sūrahs set out the two possible outcomes of one message — the society that refuses for a millennium and is removed from the earth, and the listeners who hear once and turn. The warning of the first is made intelligible by the hope of the second, and the sequence places the Prophet ﷺ, who was being told both stories in Makkah, between the first messenger and a mission that had not yet reached its audience.

**What the Sūrah Argues**

Three claims run through the chapter. The first is that warning precedes punishment, always, and that the interval between them is the whole space in which a human being is free. The second is that the refusal of a message is rarely a failure of evidence; Noah's people were given provision promised, a body described, a sky inventoried, and a road laid down, and they covered their heads. The third is that error is organized — that the resistance to a prophet is built by men of standing whose wealth and children increase them only in loss, and whose institutions transmit the refusal to the next generation.

The sūrah ends where it began: with a painful punishment named at the start and a prayer for destruction at the close, and between them four petitions for mercy, widening from the prophet himself to every believing man and woman who would ever live.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:1

> Indeed, We sent Noah to his people ˹saying to him˺, “Warn your people before a painful punishment comes to them.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Career Summarized in One Imperative**

The sūrah named for Noah does not begin with the flood, the boat, or a man building timber walls in dry ground. It begins with a commission, and the commission is a single verb in the imperative: *andhir*, warn. God sends Noah to his people and hands him one sentence to carry. Everything else in these twenty-eight verses — the pleading, the fingers in the ears, the rain and gardens promised to penitents, the drowning — grows out of that opening command.

The compression is deliberate. Elsewhere the Qur'an measures this mission in centuries: *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them for a thousand years, less fifty. Then the Flood overtook them, while they persisted in wrongdoing.”* (29:14). A thousand years of work reduced here to a charge and a deadline. That is because the sūrah is not a chronicle. It is the record of a preacher and of what preaching costs when nobody answers. The first verse fixes the frame: a messenger is defined by the message he is told to deliver, and Noah is a warner before he is a survivor, a father, or a captain. Warning is not one of his tasks. It is the task.

**The Word "Before" Puts an Expiry on Mercy**

Warning only works while there is time left, and the verse says so by placing the punishment after the call: warn them *before* a painful punishment comes to them. The gap between the two is the whole space of human freedom. Inside that gap, refusal can still be reversed; the moment it closes, the same words become an epitaph.

The Qur'an returns to this gap repeatedly, and always with the same unease. On the Day, the wrongdoers ask for exactly what Noah offered and refused to receive: *“Our Lord! Delay us for a little while. We will respond to Your call and follow the messengers!”* (14:44). The request is denied because the deadline was announced in advance and ignored. Noah's people were warned with the sequence intact — first the call, then the consequence — which is what makes their destruction a judgment rather than an ambush. A punishment that arrives after a thousand years of invitation is not a trap. This is why the timing particle carries so much weight: it converts the flood from catastrophe into verdict, and it converts Noah from a doomsayer into a witness. He told them when. He told them what. He told them for how long they could still change it.

**The Identical Charge Given to the Last Messenger**

Noah is not commissioned alone in this way, and the sūrah's first audience knew it. The same command to warn, before a punishment arrives, is issued to Muḥammad ﷺ throughout the Makkan revelation: *“˹This is˺ a Book sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺— do not let anxiety into your heart regarding it—so with it you may warn ˹the disbelievers˺, and as a reminder to the believers.”* (7:2); *“And warn the people of the Day when the punishment will overtake ˹the wicked among˺ them”* (14:44); *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘I warn you only by revelation.’ But the deaf cannot hear the call when they are warned!”* (21:45); *“Warn them ˹O Prophet˺ of the approaching Day when the hearts will jump into the throats, suppressing distress.”* (40:18).

The parallel is the point of telling the story at all. The messengers are one line of work, and the work is warning. Stories of the prophets are not antiquarian material in the Qur'an; they are the résumé of the office. *“And We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ the stories of the messengers to reassure your heart.”* (11:120). A man being called a madman in Makkah hears that a man was called the same thing for nine and a half centuries, and that the second man was right.

**Painful, but Not Yet Described**

The punishment is named by its quality — *alīm*, painful — and by nothing else. No water, no sky opening, no oven boiling over. The reader who knows the story from other sūrahs supplies the scene; this one withholds it. When the drowning finally appears it is half a clause: they were drowned because of their sins (71:25).

That economy is a choice, not an omission. Compare the accounts that do stage the event: *“So We opened the gates of the sky with pouring rain,”* (54:11) followed by the vessel of planks and nails (54:13), or the detailed instructions of 11:37–44 where the ark is built under divine direction and the oven bursts. Those passages make the catastrophe visible. This one keeps it abstract on purpose, so that attention stays where the sūrah wants it: on the warning, on the response to it, and on the interior of a man who keeps talking to people who keep leaving. The adjective *alīm* also does quiet work. It describes the punishment as felt — pain located in a body and a heart — which raises the stakes of the warning without staging a spectacle. The reader is told enough to fear and nothing at all to be entertained.

**The First Messenger, and a Title He Shares**

Islamic tradition places Noah at the head of the prophetic line sent to the earth. In the long report of the intercession on the Day of Resurrection, recorded in both Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3340 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 194 from Abū Hurayrah, humanity goes to Adam, is turned away, and then goes to Noah with these words: you are the first of the messengers to the people of the earth, and God named you a grateful servant. The name is scriptural: *“˹O˺ descendants of those We carried with Noah ˹in the Ark˺! He was indeed a grateful servant.”* (17:3).

Two things follow for this sūrah. First, Noah belongs to the group the Qur'an calls the messengers of firm resolve: *“So endure patiently, as did the Messengers of Firm Resolve.”* (46:35). Second, the whole human population after the flood descends from him, so the address in verse one — *his people* — is, in a sense, everyone. The sūrah that opens with a warning to one town is the oldest warning on record for the species that survived it.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:2

> Noah proclaimed, “O my people! I am truly sent to you with a clear warning

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Warner Introduces Himself Before He Warns**

Noah's first public act is not the warning itself but a statement about who is speaking: I am a warner sent to you, and the warning is clear. He establishes the office before the content, because the content will be unbearable unless the authority behind it is settled. A stranger shouting about punishment is a nuisance; an envoy with credentials is a matter of state.

The self-introduction is a standing feature of prophetic speech in the Qur'an. Noah says the same thing in Hūd: *“Indeed, I am sent to you with a clear warning”* (11:25), and again in al-Shuʿarāʾ: *“I am only sent with a clear warning.”* (26:115). Muḥammad ﷺ is told to say it in his own voice over and over — *“What is revealed to me is that I am only sent with a clear warning.”* (38:70); *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘O humanity! I am only sent to you with a clear warning.’”* (22:49); *“Signs are only with Allah. And I am only sent with a clear warning.”* (29:50); *“That knowledge is with Allah alone, and I am only sent with a clear warning.”* (67:26). The formula does two jobs at once. It claims a commission from God, and it disclaims everything else: not a miracle worker on demand, not a ruler, not a fortune teller. Only a clear warner.

**"O My People" — Kinship Retained While Delivering a Threat**

The address *yā qawm*, O my people, keeps the tie he is about to strain. He does not say "you deniers" or "you enemies"; he says the words a man uses for his own clan, the people whose blood and history are his. The threat that follows is therefore not a declaration of separation but an act of loyalty: I warn you because you are mine.

This is why the address keeps returning across the prophetic accounts, and always at the hardest moments. Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Shuʿayb and Mūsā all open with it; Noah uses it here and again in the complaint of verse twenty-one and in the sermon of verse ten. The Qur'an elsewhere draws out the psychology of the audience who hear it: his people's answer is that he is only a human like them, and that only the lowliest follow him (11:27), and the chiefs tell the crowd he wants to be superior to them (23:24). Kinship is precisely what makes the warning hard to swallow. A stranger's threat can be dismissed; a brother's threat has to be answered. By holding on to the word *qawm*, Noah refuses to let the conversation become a debate between opponents. He keeps it a family matter, and he keeps himself accountable to it — his grief in the verses that follow is the grief of a man whose own people are walking away.

**mubīn — Clarity as a Property of the Warning**

The adjective *mubīn* does not mean "obvious to everyone." It means the warning makes its object plain: it separates, exposes, and articulates. The root b-y-n is the Qur'an's word for the difference between two things that could otherwise be confused — guidance and error, the true and the borrowed, the road that arrives and the road that ends.

Applied to a warner, *mubīn* describes what he does to the future. He removes the ambiguity from it. Before him, punishment is a rumor; after him, it is a stated event with a stated cause and a stated way out. That is why the same adjective is attached to the Book itself, to signs, and to the messengers: *“We have surely sent you with the truth as a deliverer of good news and a warner. There is no community that has not had a warner.”* (35:24). Clarity is a moral achievement here, not a rhetorical one. A man can be perfectly audible and still leave his listeners confused about what is at stake; the *nadhīr mubīn* is the one who makes the choice intelligible.

The word also carries a quiet burden for the speaker. If the warning is clear and it is rejected, the responsibility moves entirely to the hearer. Noah is establishing, in his own first sentence, that whatever happens next cannot be blamed on a poorly worded message.

**An Open Sentence Waiting for Its Content**

Grammatically the verse stops mid-thought. The colon at its end in the translation is honest to the Arabic: the statement of the warning is not finished, and what the warning actually contains comes in what follows. Noah names his office, then hands over the terms.

That structure is characteristic of Qur'anic prophetic speech. The commission is announced, then delivered as a list of concrete requirements rather than as an abstraction. Warning is not a mood or a tone of voice; it has content, and the content can be obeyed or disobeyed. The unfinished sentence also produces a specific effect on the reader: it holds the verse in suspension, the way a man holds his breath before saying something his family does not want to hear. Everything the sūrah will report — the refusal, the fingers pressed into ears, the arrogance — is already implied by the fact that he has to announce himself as a warner first. A man who expected to be welcomed does not open by establishing his credentials.

**Every Community Has Had Its Warner**

The claim Noah makes about himself is not a personal privilege but an instance of a general law. The Qur'an states that law without exception: a warner has come to every people. *“We have never destroyed a society without warners”* (26:208); *“We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, ‘Worship Allah and shun false gods.’”* (16:36); *“You ˹O Prophet˺ are only a warner. And every people had a guide.”* (13:7).

Two consequences follow, and they are uncomfortable in opposite directions. For Noah's people, the presence of a warner means the destruction that comes later is deserved: no community is destroyed without warning, so a destroyed community is a warned one. For the warner, it means he is never the exception and never the last. He belongs to a long series of men who said the same thing to the same kind of audience and were answered the same way. That knowledge is what makes endurance possible rather than merely heroic.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:3

> worship Allah ˹alone˺, fear Him, and obey me

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Duties That Contain the Whole of Religion**

The content of the warning, when it finally arrives, is three commands: worship God, fear God, obey me. Al-Rāzī's reading of the triad is the traditional one, and it holds up — these three are not a sample of religion but its complete structure. Worship fixes the object, fear fixes the inner posture, obedience fixes the practice. Everything else in Islamic law and spirituality is an elaboration of one of the three.

The order is not arbitrary and cannot be rearranged. Worship comes first because a religion that begins with ethics has no ground; fear comes second because worship without reverence collapses into habit; obedience to the messenger comes third because it is derived, not original. Put the third item first and you have a cult of the leader, which the Qur'an explicitly refuses: *“It is not appropriate for someone who Allah has blessed with the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood to say to people, ‘Worship me instead of Allah.’ Rather, he would say, ‘Be devoted to the worship of your Lord ˹alone˺’”* (3:79). Noah's sentence is the opposite of that forbidden sentence, and its grammar makes the difference visible. Obey *me* is a subordinate clause attached to worship *Allah*. The messenger is the route, never the destination.

**ʿIbādah — Worship as Exclusive Service**

The verb is *uʿbudū*, from ʿ-b-d, the same root as *ʿabd*, servant. Worship in Qur'anic Arabic is not primarily an emotion or a ritual performance; it is the condition of being owned and of acting accordingly. The bracketed *˹alone˺* in the translation is doing real work, since the sentence is addressed to a people who already worshipped, and worshipped a great deal — they simply did not restrict it.

That is the standard shape of the prophetic quarrel. Noah's people were not atheists, and neither were the Quraysh. The dispute is about exclusivity, which is why the same call is reported in the other accounts with the second half spelled out: *“O my people! Worship Allah ˹alone˺. You have no god other than Him.”* (23:23), and *“O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous Day.”* (7:59). Service divided between several masters is not partial service; it is no service at all, because each master is obeyed only when convenient. The demand for exclusive ʿibādah is therefore a demand for a life with a single ordering principle — one authority that cannot be traded against another when the two disagree.

**Taqwā — Fear That Functions as Care**

*Ittaqūhu* is the imperative of the same root as *taqwā*, usually rendered as fear, God-consciousness, or mindfulness. The literal sense of the root w-q-y is to shield oneself, to put something between you and harm. A man with taqwā has taken cover.

This is why "fear" is not quite the right English word and yet survives translation. The fear in question is not terror at a tyrant; it is the alertness of someone who knows where the edge is and does not want to fall over it. It is the disposition of a person who expects consequences. Al-Rāzī's triad needs this middle term because worship can be performed externally by a man whose heart is elsewhere, and obedience can be imitated by a man who has not accepted that anything is at stake. Taqwā is what makes both real. It is also the faculty Noah's people lacked, and the sūrah names that lack later with a rhetorical question about majesty and awe (71:13). The connection between the two verses is direct: he tells them to fear God, they respond by refusing to hold Him in weight, and the diagnosis of the whole story is that the missing organ was never knowledge. It was reverence.

**Obey Me — The Messenger as the Practical Form of the Message**

The third command is the one that provokes. It asks for obedience to a human being, and the Qur'an anticipates the objection by recording it: his people say he is only a man like them who wants to be superior (23:24), and the chiefs insist they will not believe in a man followed only by the lowest of the low (26:111).

The answer is structural rather than personal. Obedience to a messenger is not a separate loyalty added to loyalty toward God; it is how loyalty toward God is enacted in time. Revelation arrives as speech, and speech requires a speaker, a context, and an application. A man who claims to worship God while refusing the terms on which God's message reached him has not protected the purity of his worship. He has replaced a living instruction with an abstraction of his own design.

The command is also, quietly, a test of character. It asks the listener to accept direction from someone who offers him no power, no wealth, and no status — a man whose only credentials are that he was sent. People who obey on the basis of advantage will not obey this. People who obey on the basis of truth will.

**A Single Program, Repeated by Every Messenger**

Read together, the three commands form the shortest complete statement of what the Qur'an asks of a human being, and it recurs in every prophetic mouth with minor variation. The consistency is itself an argument the sūrah leaves implicit: the message has not changed because the One who sends it has not changed.

What changes between prophets is circumstance, vocabulary, and law — not the triad. Noah says it in three clauses; the same three appear in the call of Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Shuʿayb, and in the Makkan preaching of Muḥammad ﷺ, who is told to announce the identical exclusion: *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘Should I seek a lord other than Allah while He is the Lord of everything?’”* (6:164). The triad's compactness is why it can survive nine and a half centuries of repetition without wearing thin. It is also why it is so easily refused: there is nowhere in it to negotiate. Not one of the three clauses admits a compromise, and Noah's people, as the following verses show, tried every kind.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:4

> He will forgive your sins, and delay your end until the appointed time. Indeed, when the time set by Allah comes, it cannot be delayed, if only you knew!”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Promises Attached to Three Commands**

The previous verse issued the demands; this one supplies the wages. If you worship, fear, and obey, two things follow: your sins will be forgiven, and your end will be postponed to a term appointed. Forgiveness deals with the past, delay with the future. Between them they cover the whole of a guilty life — the record already written and the time still available.

The pairing is instructive because forgiveness alone would leave the convert in an odd position: pardoned, but with no stated horizon. Delay alone would be a reprieve without a pardon. Together they describe the Qur'an's standard account of repentance, which is never merely emotional. Repentance changes a person's standing and it changes the shape of the remaining life. The promise is also carefully limited in what it does not offer: no guarantee of prosperity as such, no exemption from death, no reversal of history. God forgives and He postpones; He does not undo. A man who repents at ninety is forgiven, and he still dies — but he dies with his term complete rather than cut short, which is the specific blessing named here.

**"Your Sins" — The Question of the Partitive**

The Arabic reads *min dhunūbikum*, literally "from your sins," and the translation drops the particle and renders it as *your sins*. Whether *min* is partitive — some of your sins — or explicative — that is, your sins, with *min* merely introducing the object, is a genuine exegetical question with a real stake.

If it is partitive, the forgiveness offered here is incomplete, and the natural supplement is that the sins not covered are those owed to other people, or those committed after conversion, or the rights God chooses to retain. If it is explicative, the offer is total: embracing the message and obeying the messenger wipes the previous record clean. The second reading is stronger, for two reasons. First, Arabic regularly uses *min* in this introductory way, and the Qur'an itself elsewhere ties conversion to a full cancellation of what came before. Second, a partial pardon would undercut the verse's own argument, which is that turning to God is worth everything a person currently has. Noah is making the largest offer available to him; reading it as a discount assumes he mispriced it. The traditional preference for the expansive sense rests on that logic rather than on grammar alone, which is why both readings continue to be reported.

**Delay Until a Term Appointed**

*Yu'akhkhirkum ilā ajalin musammā* — He will postpone you to a named term. *Ajal* is the fixed span allotted to a person or a people; *musammā* adds that it is specified, written, and known to the One who wrote it. The blessing is therefore not longevity in the abstract but the completion of a span.

This is a distinctively Qur'anic idea, and it appears wherever the subject of time and judgment comes up. Nations have terms as individuals do: *“For each community there is an appointed term. When their time arrives, they can neither delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it.”* (7:34); the same sentence is repeated in 10:49. God's forbearance is described in the same vocabulary: *“If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for what they have committed, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term.”* (16:61; cf. 35:45). The commentators differ on what the term means in Noah's offer — an individual's natural death, the arrival of a punishment in this world, the end of the age, or the Day of Judgment — and the disagreement is not resolvable from the text. What all the readings share is more important than what separates them: the span is fixed, it is named, and it can be reached rather than truncated.

**The Clock That Cannot Be Moved**

Then the verse turns on itself. When God's term arrives, it will not be postponed. The same word for delay that carried the promise now carries the threat, and the shift is what gives the sentence its edge.

The structure is deliberate. Postponement is available now, on a condition; postponement will not be available then, at any price. The two halves of the verse describe one clock with two settings. While the offer stands, time is the mercy — it is the room in which a decision can still be made. Once the term arrives, time is the sentence. This is the doctrine the Qur'an presses against every audience that assumes it has more years coming: *“Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment.”* (3:178). Delay is neutral. What it produces depends entirely on the person holding it, and the sūrah's own ending shows the alternative outcome in the starkest form — drowning, then Fire (71:25). The difference between the two outcomes was not the amount of time. Both parties had time.

**If Only You Knew**

The verse closes with four words that locate the failure precisely: *law kuntum taʿlamūn*, if only you knew. Not "if you were able," not "if you were permitted" — if you knew. The obstacle is placed inside the hearer's understanding, not in his circumstances.

That placement is consistent with everything the sūrah says about this people. They are not described as weak, oppressed, deceived by a stronger power, or born into an impossible situation. They are described as refusing: fingers in ears, garments pulled over the head, persistence, arrogance (71:7). The conditional is counterfactual and slightly mournful — the knowledge they lacked was available to them, and its absence is not a mitigating fact but part of the charge. The Qur'an uses the same rebuke repeatedly: the signs are made plain for people of knowledge (10:5), and hearts grow blind in the chests (22:46). Noah's sermon ends its opening movement with that diagnosis. The offer is real, the deadline is fixed, and the only thing standing between this audience and both is a failure to know what it already has every means of knowing.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:5

> He cried, “My Lord! I have surely called my people day and night

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sermon Turns Into a Report**

Verses two through four are Noah speaking to his people. This verse is Noah speaking to God, and the change of addressee changes everything about the sentence. The words *qāla rabbi*, he said "My Lord," mark the moment the mission stops being an argument and becomes a matter of record. He is no longer persuading. He is reporting.

That shift is not a sign of defeat but of procedure. A messenger sent with a commission reports back to the one who sent him. The report has a form: what I did, how long I did it, and what happened. The first element arrives here — I called them day and night — and the rest follows in the next verses, ending with the prayer for destruction in verses twenty-six through twenty-eight. Between the opening report and the final prayer lies the whole shape of a failed mission, told to the only party who can act on it.

The address also fixes the relationship. *Rabb* is the word for the one who sustains, educates, and owns the outcome. Noah does not say "God, judge them." He says "my Lord," the language of a servant inside a household, and he keeps that language even in the harshest verse of the sūrah.

**Day and Night, and the Order of the Two**

The phrase *laylan wa-nahārā* names both halves of the day and puts night first. The reversal has drawn comment for centuries, and the two main explanations are not in conflict. One reads it as emphasis on totality: night and day together mean every hour, every mood of the audience, every occasion — the private and the public, the hours when a man is alone with his thoughts and the hours when he is surrounded by his peers. The other reads it chronologically: Noah began at night, calling people individually and quietly, and only later moved into the open.

Both readings are supported by what follows. The sūrah describes private counsel and public proclamation as distinct methods (71:8–9), so the sequence is real; and the phrase's ordinary function in Arabic is to express uninterrupted continuity, which is what nine and a half centuries of preaching amounts to. What matters most is that the report is quantitative. Noah does not say "I tried hard." He names the units of time. A man accounting for his work to his Lord counts hours, not intentions.

**Nine Hundred and Fifty Years Behind One Sentence**

The brevity of the report is measured against the length of the mission. The Qur'an gives the number elsewhere: *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them for a thousand years, less fifty. Then the Flood overtook them, while they persisted in wrongdoing.”* (29:14). Nine and a half centuries of calling compressed into five words.

That compression is itself a statement about prophetic labor. The visible result of the mission — a handful of believers, one drowned nation, one boat — is tiny compared with the time spent. The Qur'an does not treat this disproportion as a failure of the messenger. It treats it as the normal condition of the office: the work is measured by faithfulness to the commission, not by the size of the crowd. Noah is later honored in the tradition precisely on this ground. In the report of the intercession, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3340 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 194 from Abū Hurayrah, humanity addresses him as the first of the messengers to the people of the earth and reminds him that God named him a grateful servant — the same title given in the Qur'an at 17:3. The man who spent a millennium on a mission that produced almost no converts is the one humanity goes to second on the Day of Resurrection.

**Calling Is Measured by Delivery, Not Response**

The verse states an action completed: I called them. Whether they listened is a separate fact, reported separately, and it does not subtract from the calling. This division of labor is stated as doctrine for the last messenger: *“O Messenger! Convey everything revealed to you from your Lord. If you do not, then you have not delivered His message.”* (5:67), and *“But if they turn away, We have not sent you ˹O Prophet˺ as a keeper over them. Your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺.”* (42:48).

The consequence for the caller is a strange freedom, and it is the only freedom available to anyone who works on other people's convictions. He is not responsible for the result, which means he cannot be broken by it. He is responsible for the delivery, which means he cannot stop. Both halves are required; either one alone destroys the work. Noah's report holds both halves in place: the calling is affirmed absolutely, and the flight is described without being counted against the call. A missionary theology built on outcomes would have ended this mission in the first century.

**A Complaint Without Self-Pity**

The tone deserves notice because of what it omits. There is no request for relief, no complaint about hardship, no mention of his own suffering, and no plea for a different assignment. He reports the labor and its duration, and stops.

Later verses show that the suffering was considerable — the people's plot against him is named without qualification (71:22), and other accounts record the threat of stoning: *“If you do not desist, O Noah, you will surely be stoned ˹to death˺.”* (26:116). None of that enters this sentence. The report is confined to the work and its reception, which is what a servant owes his Lord. The restraint also makes the prayer that ends the sūrah more striking, since it is the one place where Noah asks for something severe, and he asks only after the report is complete and the case is proven.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:6

> but my calls only made them run farther away

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Call Produced Flight, Not Argument**

Noah reports the effect of his work in a single image, and the image is chosen with precision. He does not say they disagreed, or that they answered him with a counter-argument, or that they were unpersuaded. He says the calling made them run. The noun is *firār*, flight — the movement of an animal away from a predator, the movement of a soldier away from a battle he has already lost.

That word choice carries a diagnosis. Argument answers argument; flight answers danger. His people behaved toward forgiveness the way a person behaves toward a threat, which means that somewhere in their understanding, mercy had been recoded as harm. The sermon offered pardon and they experienced it as an attack. Nothing in the verse explains why, but the following verse supplies the mechanism: the moment the invitation to forgiveness was heard, hands went to ears and garments went over heads. The body treated the offer as an assault before the mind produced a reason. The sūrah is describing a real psychological event — the recoil of a person confronted with the requirement to change — and it names it with the crudest available word.

**Increase in the Wrong Direction**

The construction is one the Qur'an returns to whenever it describes the paradox of revelation: the very thing sent to guide increases distance. *“We have surely varied ˹the signs˺ in this Quran so perhaps they may be mindful, but it only drives them farther away.”* (17:41). The same verb of increase appears for those with sickness in the heart — *“it has increased them only in wickedness upon their wickedness”* (9:125) — and for the community that swore it would be better guided than any other if a warner came, then found the warner only *“drove them farther away”* (35:42). When the Quraysh are told to prostrate to the Most Compassionate, the effect is identical: *“And it only drives them farther away.”* (25:60).

The pattern is not a defect in the message but a property of the receiver, and the Qur'an states it as a law rather than as a complaint. Light does not cause blindness; it reveals it. A person who has already decided not to move gains nothing from an additional sign except one more thing to refuse, and the refusal compounds. A sign given to a person who has decided not to move supplies him with one more thing to refuse, and each refusal hardens the next. The Qur'an states the hardening as an act of God — *“We can punish them ˹too˺ for their sins and seal their hearts so they will not hear ˹the truth˺”* (7:100) — and the sealing always follows the choice, never precedes it.

**Flight From Forgiveness Specifically**

The verse that follows makes explicit what the object of the flight was: whenever I invite them *so that You may forgive them*, they block their ears (71:7). The running is away from pardon.

This deserves to be held in view, because it reverses the ordinary account of religious resistance. The standard explanation for unbelief is that people fear punishment and flee toward pleasure. Here the punishment is what is offered as the alternative, and the pardon is what is fled. Noah's call was not primarily a threat; it was an amnesty, and the amnesty was refused. The reason given in the surrounding verses is pride — they persisted and became arrogant (71:7) — and pride explains the direction of the flight accurately. A man can accept a warning from a superior. He cannot accept a pardon without admitting that he needed one, and the admission costs him the position he has built. The elites of Noah's people had wealth, children, and a religious establishment of ancestral statues (71:21, 71:23). Forgiveness would have required dismantling all three.

**The Honest Arithmetic of a Failed Mission**

Noah reports the number without adjustment. Nine and a half centuries of work produced an increase in distance, and he says so to God in the plainest terms available. There is no attempt to convert the flight into a partial success, no claim that seeds were sown, no softening of the result.

This honesty is one of the Qur'an's most distinctive habits. It records its messengers losing. The people of Noah deny him, call him insane, and intimidate him: *“Before them, the people of Noah denied ˹the truth˺ and rejected Our servant, calling ˹him˺ insane. And he was intimidated.”* (54:9). The verse does not resolve the humiliation before reporting the outcome. A book concerned only with winning would have cut that clause. The presence of the loss is what makes the report in this verse credible, and it is what allows the sūrah to be read by anyone whose own work has produced nothing — which is most people, most of the time.

**The Sentence Completes the Report, Not the Argument**

Structurally, verses five and six form one statement split across a line break: I called them day and night, and my calling only increased their flight. The first clause is the labor, the second is the yield. Nothing follows from the yield except the more detailed account in the next verse and the eventual prayer.

That is why the verse carries no appeal. Noah does not ask God to do something about the flight. He records it. The prayer comes later, and it comes only after the record is complete and the evidence is in. A reader who expects the sūrah to move from complaint to intercession here will find instead a long inventory of methods tried and refused — open calling, private counsel, the promise of rain and children, the argument from the seven heavens and the growing of plants — all of it evidence assembled before a verdict is requested. The flight is the first exhibit.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:7

> And whenever I invite them to be forgiven by You, they press their fingers into their ears, cover themselves with their clothes, persist ˹in denial˺, and act very arrogantly

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Movements of Refusal, Recorded in Order**

The verse lists four responses and the order is not accidental. Fingers into the ears: block the hearing. Garments pulled over the head: block the sight and hide the face. Persistence: refuse to move from the position. Arrogance: turn the refusal into a claim of superiority. The sequence moves inward and downward — from the ear to the body to the will to the self-image.

The detail is worth holding because it is behavior, not belief. Noah does not report that they answered him with a theory of the universe or an alternative account of God. He reports gestures. This is the most accurate possible description of a certain kind of resistance: the refusal that cannot be reasoned with because it was never produced by reasoning. A man with his fingers in his ears has not made an argument against sound. He has made a decision not to receive it, and the decision is enacted in muscle.

The word for persistence, *istaṣarrū*, carries the sense of holding fast, concealing oneself, and digging in — the same root that describes a thing that is wrapped or enclosed. They wrapped themselves in their refusal the way they wrapped themselves in their clothes, and the double image in one verse is doing deliberate work: the outer garment and the inner obstinacy are the same act performed twice.

**The Body Refuses Before the Mind Objects**

Something important happens in the space between the invitation and the gesture. There is no deliberation reported, no pause for consideration, no question asked. The hearing of the offer triggers a physical response.

This is exactly the pattern the Qur'an describes elsewhere when it accounts for why signs fail. *“We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears. And when you mention your Lord alone in the Quran, they turn their backs in aversion.”* (17:46). The veils and the deafness are presented as consequences of a prior turning, and the turning is described in bodily language again: backs turned, ears stopped, hearts covered. In al-Fuṣṣilat the audience says the same thing in its own voice: *“Our hearts are veiled against what you are calling us to, there is deafness in our ears, and there is a barrier between us and you.”* (41:5). They are describing themselves with Noah's people's gestures, nine centuries later, in a different city, using the same hands.

The moral point is not that these people were physically incapable of hearing. It is that a settled will can command the senses, and once it does, evidence stops being evidence and becomes noise.

**The Three Faculties, All Closed**

Qur'anic anthropology works with three instruments of perception: hearing, sight, and the heart. They are the organs by which a sign is received, and the text repeatedly reports them open or shut as a pair of states — either the three work together or all three are blocked.

Noah's report names two of the three explicitly and the third implicitly. The ears are stopped. The eyes are covered by the garments. The heart appears in the final word, arrogance, since arrogance in Qur'anic usage is not a feeling about oneself but a disposition of the heart that refuses to accept what it recognizes as true. The catalogue elsewhere is blunt: *“Indeed, We have destined many jinn and humans for Hell. They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with. They are like cattle. In fact, they are even less guided!”* (7:179). And to the destroyed peoples God gave all three instruments and got nothing from them: *“We gave them hearing, sight, and intellect. But neither their hearing, sight, nor intellect were of any benefit to them whatsoever, since they persisted in denying Allah’s signs.”* (46:26).

The parallel between *persisted* in that verse and *persist* in this one is the closest verbal link between Noah's people and the Makkan audience, and it is the link the sūrah wants.

**An Invitation to Forgiveness, Not to Debate**

The content of the call is stated in the middle of the verse and it is easily missed: I invite them *so that You may forgive them*. The purpose clause puts God, not Noah, at the center of the offer. The messenger is the one doing the inviting; the forgiveness belongs to another.

Two consequences follow. First, the invitation is not a demand for loyalty to Noah, which is why the arrogance of the response is so misplaced — they are treating an amnesty offer as a power grab. Second, the second-person address to God inside the report keeps the sūrah's prayer frame intact. Noah is speaking to his Lord about his Lord's own generosity: whenever I call them to be forgiven *by You*. The offense is thus doubled. They refuse the messenger, and they refuse the pardon of the One the messenger serves.

The offer of forgiveness is also the first item in the sermon that follows, where Noah tells them to seek pardon and is answered with the promise of rain, wealth, children, and gardens (71:10–12). The whole middle of the sūrah is an elaboration of this clause: what forgiveness brings, and why it was worth hearing.

**Arrogance as the Last Word**

The verse ends on *istakbarū*, they became arrogant, using the tenth form of the root k-b-r, which denotes taking oneself to be great. It is the summary of everything before it: the stopped ears and the covered heads are the symptoms, and the disease is a settled estimate of the self that cannot accommodate a summons from outside.

Arrogance in the Qur'an is not rudeness. It is a theological position — the claim to be self-sufficient, to owe nothing, to be beyond the reach of an instruction. That is why it is treated as the root sin and why the same word appears at the origin of the whole drama of the book: Iblīs refused to prostrate because he considered himself greater. Noah's people inherit that posture without needing to be told about it, and the sūrah answers it not with a rebuke but with the argument from creation in verses thirteen through twenty — you were made in stages, you were grown from the earth like a plant, the heavens were built above you. A person who has been grown has no standing to be arrogant, and the sermon's strategy is simply to remind him of where he came from.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:8

> Then I certainly called them openly

**Expanded Commentary**

**A New Method After the First One Failed**

The particle *thumma* — then — marks a real interval and a real change. What came before has already been described: continuous calling, day and night, answered by flight and stopped ears. This verse introduces the first of two methods named explicitly, and the naming matters because the sūrah is assembling a case. Noah will be asked, in effect, whether he left anything untried. The answer is a list.

The list is the Qur'an's answer to a question every failing mission provokes: was the failure in the message or in the delivery? By recording the methods one after another — continuous, then open, then public and private together — the sūrah closes that question before it can be asked. Delivery was exhaustive. The failure sits entirely with the receivers, and the receivers will be judged on the basis of a record that includes every approach tried.

The order also has a pastoral logic. A caller who has been refused in private naturally moves to the open square, because the private setting has allowed the refusal to stay informal. An open summons forces a public answer, and a public answer is harder to withdraw. Noah is not escalating pressure out of frustration. He is removing the conditions that made avoidance cheap.

**jihāran — Loud Enough to Be Undeniable**

The adverb *jihāran* derives from a root meaning to be manifest, to appear, to be audible. It describes an action performed in the open, without concealment, in a voice that cannot be mistaken for a whisper. The English "openly" catches the exposure; the Arabic adds the volume.

That combination is what makes the call a formal act. A warning delivered quietly can be denied — he never said it, he said something else, we misunderstood. A warning shouted in a gathering place becomes a matter of public record, and the record is what will be produced on the Day. The Qur'an makes the same move for the last messenger: *“O Messenger! Convey everything revealed to you from your Lord. If you do not, then you have not delivered His message.”* (5:67). Delivery, in that verse and in this one, has an audible dimension. The message must arrive in a form the recipient cannot plausibly claim not to have received.

*jihār* also implies a certain exposure of the speaker. To call openly is to be seen calling, and to be seen calling in a hostile town is to become a target.

**The Prophet's Own Open Summons**

The precedent is recorded in detail for the last messenger, and it is the same movement from the private circle to the public rock. When the command to warn his closest relatives came down — *“And warn ˹all, starting with˺ your closest relatives,”* (26:214) — Ibn ʿAbbās reports that the Prophet ﷺ went out, climbed Mount Ṣafā, and shouted the cry used to summon a town to danger: *yā ṣabāḥāh*. The clans of Quraysh gathered, and he addressed them branch by branch, telling each that he could avail them nothing against God. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3525, and the same event is narrated by Abū Hurayrah in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4771 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 206, where the summons opens with the words: O assembly of Quraysh, purchase your souls from God, for I cannot avail you anything before Him.

Three features of that scene match this verse. It was loud. It was in the open, at a landmark everyone could see. And it named the audience directly, so that no clan could claim the warning was meant for someone else. The Ṣafā episode is the practical commentary on *jihāran*.

**The Price of Going Public**

Open calling removes the excuse of ignorance and simultaneously removes the caller's protection. Once the warning is public, the refusal must be public too, and a public refusal by men of standing turns into a public campaign.

The other accounts of this mission record the escalation that follows. Noah's people tell him plainly: *“If you do not desist, O Noah, you will surely be stoned ˹to death˺.”* (26:116). The chiefs declare that they see him as clearly misguided (7:60), and the argument they use against him is social rather than theological — he is only a man like them, and only the lowest of people follow him (11:27; 26:111). This is the ordinary sequence: a messenger begins quietly, is refused, goes public, and is then treated as a threat to the social order rather than as a man with an argument.

The sūrah reports the plot against him in the second half of the story (71:22), and the plot only makes sense after the public phase. Men do not conspire against a voice they can ignore. They conspire against a voice the whole town has heard.

**Escalation as a Form of Patience**

It would be easy to read the sequence of methods as rising impatience — the man is getting louder because he is losing. The text supports the opposite. Each new method is another attempt, and attempts are what patience looks like from the outside.

The Qur'an's own instruction to the last messenger couples exactly these two things, and the coupling is the definition of the office: *“So endure patiently, as did the Messengers of Firm Resolve. And do not ˹seek to˺ hasten ˹the torment˺ for the deniers.”* (46:35). Endurance is not passivity. It is the refusal to stop working while also refusing to demand an early verdict. Noah's open calling sits inside that definition: he increases his effort rather than his anger, and he does not ask for the punishment to arrive sooner. The prayer for destruction comes at the very end of the sūrah, after the methods are exhausted, the report is filed, and the case is closed.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:9

> then I surely preached to them publicly and privately

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Channels Running at Once**

The verse names both modes in a single breath: I proclaimed to them openly, and I spoke to them in secret. After the general statement of verse eight, this one describes the final and most complete phase of the mission, in which nothing is left to a single setting. The public square and the private conversation are worked simultaneously.

The combination is significant because each mode has a distinct weakness. Public preaching reaches everyone and persuades almost no one individually — a crowd hears a position and takes sides. Private counsel reaches one person and can actually change him, but it cannot reach a nation and it leaves the public record unwritten. Noah runs both, which means the record is complete and the individual is addressed. No member of that society can say he was never spoken to, and none of them was spoken to only as part of a mass.

This is the fullest statement of prophetic method in the sūrah, and it arrives after two other attempts have been recorded. The escalation ends not in volume but in coverage.

**isrārā — The Cognate Accusative and Its Weight**

The Arabic closes the verse with a grammatical intensification: *asrartu lahum isrārā*, I spoke to them secretly, with a secrecy. The noun from the same root as the verb is used to reinforce it, a construction Arabic reserves for actions performed thoroughly and without reservation.

The effect is that the private work is described as an art rather than an afterthought. It is not the leftover time between public appearances. He practiced concealment and discretion as a deliberate technique — choosing the moment, the place, and the person, and keeping the encounter out of public view. The construction tells the reader that a great deal of careful, unrecorded, individual labor went into this mission. None of it survived in any human archive. The only witness to it is the verse itself, which is a point worth noticing in a book that repeatedly insists God records what people do not see.

The same root appears in the Qur'an for the hidden and the open as a matched pair — what is concealed and what is declared both stand before God — and the pairing here is practical: the messenger works in both registers because human beings are moved in both.

**Private Counsel Removes the Cost of Listening**

Why does the private channel matter so much that the verse gives it an intensifying noun? Because of shame.

A man who begins to be persuaded in public has a problem. His peers are watching, and the moment he softens he loses standing with them. The Qur'an records the social mechanism precisely: the chiefs tell the crowd that only the lowest people follow the messenger (26:111), and the followers themselves will say on the Day that they were kept back by their leaders — *“Had it not been for you, we would certainly have been believers.”* (34:31). Persuasion in the open is therefore contested by an audience with a stake in the outcome. Persuasion in private is not.

Away from the crowd, the same person can hear the argument without immediately calculating what accepting it will cost him in front of his friends. He can ask a question without conceding a position. Noah's private work was the only setting in which that was possible, and the sūrah's insistence that he did it — thoroughly — means the eventual destruction cannot be attributed to an audience that was never given a chance to listen without witnesses.

**How the Commentators Order the Three Methods**

The sequence in verses eight and nine has been read in two main ways. One takes it as chronological escalation: he began privately, then called openly, then combined the two. The other takes it as intensification without strict chronology: the open call and the secret call are described as concurrent registers of one continuous effort.

The first reading has the support of the particle *thumma*, which normally indicates sequence with a gap. The second has the support of verse five, where the calling is described as continuous day and night, which suggests all methods were in play across the whole period. The stronger reading is the first, with a qualification: the methods were introduced in stages, but once introduced they ran together. The verse's own grammar points that way — it pairs proclamation and secrecy in a single clause rather than separating them into distinct periods. Nothing important turns on the dispute. Both readings agree that every available channel was used, and that the mission ended with all of them open at once.

**The Same Method in the Makkan Preaching**

The pattern is visible in the sīrah of the last messenger, whose preaching moved through recognizable stages: the earliest years worked through kinship and trusted individuals, then came the open summons from Mount Ṣafā after the revelation of the command to warn his closest relatives (26:214), recorded by Ibn ʿAbbās in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3525, and after that the two modes continued side by side — public recitation at the Kaʿbah and private instruction in houses such as that of al-Arqam.

The pairing of registers is what makes the Qur'anic account of Noah useful to a reader in any century, and it is what the verse reports with an intensifying noun rather than a passing phrase. Preaching is not a single act performed at a single volume. It has a public register that establishes the claim, and a private register that carries it into an individual life. Noah's report insists that he worked both to exhaustion, and the insistence is part of the evidence the sūrah builds before the verdict it will eventually request.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:10

> saying, ‘Seek your Lord’s forgiveness, ˹for˺ He is truly Most Forgiving

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sermon Begins With Repentance, Not With Law**

Verse ten opens the longest continuous speech in the sūrah, and the first thing Noah tells his people to do is ask for pardon. He does not begin with prohibitions, with a code, with a list of what they must stop doing, or with a demonstration of God's power. He begins with *istaghfirū* — seek forgiveness.

That opening is a deliberate choice of sequence and it defines the whole sermon. Repentance is placed before reform because reform without pardon is unbearable: a man who is told to change and is not told that his past can be cancelled will usually decide the change is impossible and stop listening. The offer of forgiveness makes the demand for change survivable. It also places the initiative with the hearer in the smallest possible way — one act of asking, which costs nothing but pride.

The imperative form matters. Seeking forgiveness is commanded, not recommended. In Qur'anic usage it is an act with a direction: a person turns toward God and asks. The sermon's opening move is therefore not information but an instruction to perform something, and everything promised in the verses that follow is attached to that single act.

**ghafūr — The Name Set Beside the Command**

The verse gives the reason for the command in a divine name: *innahu kāna ghaffūrā*, He is truly Most Forgiving. The name is in the intensive form, denoting a quality that is abundant and habitual rather than occasional.

The placement is a lesson in how religious instruction works. The command to seek pardon would be meaningless if the one asked were reluctant; a person does not ask a judge he expects to refuse. So the command is immediately followed by a statement about the character of the one being addressed, and the statement is in the strongest available grammatical form. God is not described as willing to forgive on occasion, or as forgiving when the conditions are exact. He is described as *ghaffūr*, one whose forgiveness is a settled attribute.

The Qur'an pairs this name repeatedly with the act of asking, and the pairing always runs in the same direction — the attribute guarantees the act. The verse also uses the imperfect *kāna*, "He is, He has been," which places the attribute outside time: forgiving is not a mood God enters. It is what He is. A person who understands that has no reason to delay the asking, and delay is precisely what the sermon is fighting, since the whole sūrah is about a people who ran out of time while considering.

**Asking Is an Act, Not a Mood**

*istighfār* in the tradition is not regret. Regret can be entirely private and entirely inert; a man can be sorry for a life he has no intention of leaving. Seeking forgiveness is a speech act with a direction and a request, and the tradition consistently treats it as something done — with the tongue, in the heart, and in a change of course.

The distinction has a practical consequence for Noah's audience. If forgiveness required a feeling they did not have, they could excuse themselves. If it requires asking, the excuse disappears: anyone can ask, including a man who does not yet feel the appropriate sorrow, and asking is the beginning of the sorrow rather than its product. This is why the sermon opens here rather than with the demand for a reformed heart. The reformed heart comes after the request, not before it.

The Qur'an makes the same point structurally when it records God's response to those who sin and then remember Him and ask pardon for what they did — the sequence is sin, remembrance, asking — and the promise attached is forgiveness. The order of operations in this verse is exactly that order, compressed into one imperative and one name.

**The Identical Words in Hūd's Mouth**

Noah's opening is not his alone. Centuries later, in the same region, the prophet Hūd says almost the same sentence to the people of ʿĀd: *“And O my people! Seek your Lord’s forgiveness and turn to Him in repentance. He will shower you with rain in abundance, and add strength to your strength. So do not turn away, persisting in wickedness.”* (11:52).

The parallel is close enough to be a formula: seek forgiveness, and provision follows. Both prophets attach a material consequence to the act of asking, and both name rain as the first item of the consequence. The repetition across two missions tells the reader that this is not Noah improvising a persuasive line but a standing feature of prophetic preaching. The terms on which God deals with a community do not vary by prophet.

There is one difference worth holding. Hūd adds *wa-tūbū ilayhi*, and turn to Him — repentance beside forgiveness. Noah's version, as the sūrah reports it, begins with the asking alone and lets the consequences speak for the turning. The two are not in tension: in Qur'anic usage asking for forgiveness and turning are the same movement described from two ends. But Hūd's audience had already been given strength and stature, and his phrasing addresses men who needed to be told to come back. Noah's audience needed to be told they could be cleaned.

**A Sermon With a Shape**

The verse is the first in a sequence that runs to verse twenty, and the shape of that sequence is worth stating before it unfolds: a command to ask, the consequences of asking, then a question about why the audience has failed to hold God in the weight He deserves, and finally the evidence assembled from their own creation and from the world around them.

The order is argumentative rather than chronological. It moves from what to do, to what will happen if you do it, to the diagnosis of why you have not done it, to the proof that the diagnosis is correct. A listener who follows the sequence is being led through the stages of a real decision. Noah is not delivering a list of pious sentiments. He is constructing a case, and the case ends by returning to the human body and the soil it came from — the last argument available to a preacher whose audience will not listen to anything else.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:11

> He will shower you with abundant rain

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Promise Is Water**

Noah's sermon moves immediately from the command to its consequence, and the first consequence named is rain — the sky sent down in abundance. Not forgiveness repeated, not Paradise, not victory over enemies. Water.

For a pastoral and agricultural society in a dry region, that is the largest conceivable promise, and it is placed first for a reason. Rain determines everything else in such a life: the pasture, the herds, the harvest, the wells, whether a settlement stays where it is or disperses. A promise of abundant rain is a promise that the coming years will be survivable and that the community will not have to break apart to find water. By naming it first, Noah speaks in the currency his audience actually holds. He does not begin with a metaphysical reward that requires a conversion of the imagination; he begins with the thing they already pray for in their own language, and he tells them where it comes from and on what terms.

The move is also a quiet polemic. If rain comes from God in response to asking forgiveness, then the statues they have set up at their gathering places are not in that business, and the ancestral intermediaries they trust have no share in it. The promise contains a theology of providence without ever arguing one.

**midrār — Abundance as Repetition**

The adjective translated *abundant* is *midrār*, from a root whose basic sense is to flow freely and continuously — it is used of an animal giving milk in a steady stream and of clouds that return again and again with rain. The form denotes an action repeated and sustained rather than a single large quantity.

That nuance matters. The promise is not of one dramatic downpour but of a settled pattern: rain that keeps coming, season after season, in the amounts that produce a harvest. A single storm in a desert is a spectacle and occasionally a disaster; repeated, measured rainfall is prosperity. The word therefore describes reliability rather than volume, and reliability is what a farming community means when it says it has been blessed.

The same root and sense appear in Hūd's promise to ʿĀd — He will send rain upon you in abundance (11:52) — which makes the pairing a recognized formula in prophetic preaching. Wherever the Qur'an describes divine provision arriving from the sky, the emphasis falls on recurrence: the sky with its cycles (86:11), the rain that revives a dead land again and again (30:19). Provision in the Qur'anic picture is not an event. It is a rhythm, and repentance is what restores the rhythm.

**The Sky as a Metonym, and the Blessing as Life**

The Arabic says He will send the sky upon you, using the sky for what comes from it. The metonymy is ordinary in the language and it carries an implicit theology: the sky is not itself the giver. It is the direction from which the gift arrives, and the giver is named in the verse before.

That distinction was live for Noah's audience and is live for the Qur'an's first readers, both of whom lived in religious systems that attached agency to celestial objects and to intermediaries. The verse quietly removes the agency from the sky and places it with the One who forgives. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the same move explicitly, listing rain among the signs and refusing to let it be read as anything but a deliberate act: the rain sent down from the skies, reviving the earth after its death, is named among the signs for people of understanding (2:164).

The blessing is also described in terms of life rather than comfort. Reviving a dead land is the Qur'an's standing image for both provision and resurrection — *“There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat.”* (36:33). A promise of abundant rain, in that symbolic world, is a promise that what has died in this community will live again: the soil, the herds, and by extension the people themselves.

**Blessing Presented as Conditional**

The promise is attached to an act. Seek forgiveness, and the sky will be sent with abundance. This conditional structure is a general rule in the Qur'an, stated for other peoples in the same terms: *“Had the people of those societies been faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, We would have overwhelmed them with blessings from heaven and earth. But they disbelieved, so We seized them for what they used to commit.”* (7:96). The same condition is put to the People of the Book: *“And had they observed the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to them from their Lord, they would have been overwhelmed with provisions from above and below.”* (5:66).

Three features of the rule are worth stating plainly. It is conditional, not automatic — the blessing depends on the posture of the community. It is corporate, not only individual — whole towns are described as recipients. And it is reversible — the seizure in the second half of 7:96 is the same event described from the other side. Nothing in the rule guarantees that a prosperous community is an obedient one; that error is what the doctrine of gradual drawing-on exists to correct (68:44). What the rule does guarantee is that obedience opens a channel, and that the channel has been open to every people that ever turned.

**Why Material Provision Comes Before Paradise**

A modern reader may find it odd that a prophet's sermon promises rain before it promises the Hereafter. The oddness is instructive rather than accidental, and it says something about how the Qur'an understands the relation between the two realms.

Material provision is not a lower grade of reward offered to people too coarse for spiritual promises. It is the ordinary medium in which divine care becomes visible, and it is the thing a community actually debates when it asks whether a prophet is worth following. By beginning with water, Noah meets his audience at the point where their skepticism lives. He is also making a claim that the rest of the sermon will support: the One who sends rain is the One who made you in stages, built the seven heavens, and grew you out of the ground. Provision and creation are one argument. A people who accept the second have no reason to doubt the first, and a people who want the first are being led, without noticing, toward the second.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:12

> supply you with wealth and children, and give you gardens as well as rivers

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Promise Widens From Water to Estates**

After rain, the sermon names three further gifts: wealth, sons, and gardens with rivers running through them. The sequence is not random. Water is the condition of everything else in an agrarian society; wealth and offspring are what a settled community accumulates once water is reliable; gardens and rivers are the visible form of that accumulation at its best.

The movement is from survival to flourishing. A promise of rain alone would answer the fear of a bad year. The additions answer the ambitions of a whole generation: property, descendants, and a landscape that looks like prosperity. Noah is not offering austerity. He is telling his people that the things they want are real goods, that they come from a real source, and that the source has set one condition for supplying them.

The three items are also the three things his audience's leaders already possessed and were using as an argument against him. That is the sharp edge of the promise. The same goods are offered to everyone, on terms that require the elite to give up their claim of ownership — which is why, later in the sūrah, the report is that the crowd followed those whose wealth and children increased them only in loss (71:21).

**Wealth and Sons: The Two Measures of a Man**

In the society addressed here, a man's standing rested on two things: what he owned and how many sons stood behind him. The Qur'an names the pairing constantly, usually to unsettle it. *“So let neither their wealth nor children impress you ˹O Prophet˺. Allah only intends to torment them through these things in this worldly life, then their souls will depart while they are disbelievers.”* (9:55, and the near-identical 9:85). The believers are warned directly: *“O believers! Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah. For whoever does so, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.”* (63:9).

The promise in this verse sits inside that teaching rather than against it. Wealth and children are offered as divine gifts, which means they are gifts and not achievements, and not evidence of superiority. The men who already had them treated them as proof that they were right; the sermon tells everyone else that the same goods are available from the same source on different terms. That single reframing dismantles the elite's argument. If property is a gift conditional on repentance, then property is not a verdict, and the rich man is not thereby the correct man.

The Qur'an's own summary of the two is blunt: *“Your wealth and children are only a test, but Allah ˹alone˺ has a great reward.”* (64:15; cf. 8:28). The test and the gift are the same object seen from two ends.

**Gardens and Rivers as the Picture of Sufficiency**

*jannāt wa-anhār* — gardens and rivers — is the Qur'an's standard shorthand for a life with nothing missing. The two words appear together for the provision of this world and for Paradise, which is why the phrase carries both a material and an eschatological resonance.

The garden is the achieved form of the rain promised in the previous verse: water directed, enclosed, productive, and shaded. The river is water that does not have to be carried. For people whose labor was largely the labor of moving water, that image is not decoration. It is the description of a life without the daily struggle that organizes every other life.

The same image serves as the description of the reward in the Hereafter — gardens with rivers running beneath them (2:25) — and the double use is deliberate. A believer who has heard the promise of gardens and rivers in this world has already been given the vocabulary for the next. The Qur'an trains its readers on the visible good so that the invisible good is intelligible, and it uses the same words for both so that the connection is never lost: what God supplies here in measure, He supplies there without limit.

**Desire Redirected Rather Than Denied**

The sermon does not ask this audience to want less. It asks them to want correctly — to look for the goods they already desire in the right direction.

That is a distinctive feature of Qur'anic preaching and it deserves to be stated plainly, because the alternative reading is common and wrong. The message is not that wealth is base and that the pious should be poor. The message is that wealth has an owner, that the owner is generous, and that generosity is conditional. A man who wants a garden is not being rebuked for wanting it. He is being told who makes gardens and what the maker asks in return.

The same structure appears throughout the Book. God describes Himself as the one who provides, and the response required is gratitude rather than renunciation. The danger the Qur'an identifies is not possession but attribution — the man who says, as the owner of two gardens says in al-Kahf, that his property will never perish and that he is superior in wealth and numbers (18:34; cf. 34:35). Noah's offer is aimed precisely at preventing that sentence from being true of his people, by attaching the goods to the giver at the moment they are given.

**A Real Promise, and a Real Hazard**

Two readings of the promise run through the tradition, and both are defensible. One takes it as a straightforward assurance: repent, and the coming years will be better than the last. The other takes it as a warning in the form of an offer: God gives provision to test the recipient, and increase can be the instrument of destruction rather than its opposite — *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise.”* (6:44).

The stronger reading holds both. The promise is genuine, because God does supply provision to those who turn, and the whole sermon depends on that being true. It is also hazardous, because nothing in the human heart reliably survives an increase in wealth. The verse that follows shifts the sermon from provision to the failure of awe, and the shift is the sermon's own acknowledgment of the problem: a people given everything and still without reverence for the giver is a people in danger. The gift does not solve the disease. It changes the conditions of the test.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:13

> What is the matter with you that you are not in awe of the Majesty of Allah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sermon Turns From Offer to Diagnosis**

Up to this point Noah has been speaking about what his people will receive. Now he asks a question about what they are missing, and the question is the pivot of the whole speech. The shift is from provision to perception: the problem with this community is not that God has been stingy but that they have no sense of His weight.

The form of the question — *mā lakum*, what is the matter with you — is diagnostic rather than merely rhetorical. It is the question a physician asks, not the question an accuser asks. Something is wrong with the patient and the physician wants to know what. The Qur'an uses the same opening elsewhere in exactly that register: *“How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life.”* (2:28). In both cases the question is followed by evidence, and in both the evidence is drawn from the person asked.

The diagnosis lands on a specific deficit. Noah does not say they are ignorant of God's existence; his audience plainly believes there is a God, since he invites them to be forgiven by Him. He does not say they are ignorant of His power. He says they do not hold Him in the weight He has. The failure is in the estimation, and an error in estimation explains every other behavior reported in the sūrah — the fingers in the ears, the covered heads, the persistence, the arrogance (71:7).

**The Two Readings of rajāʾ**

The verb in the question is *tarjūna*, from r-j-w, whose ordinary meaning is to hope or to expect. The commentators split over how it functions here, and the split produces two quite different sentences.

The first reading takes it in its normal sense: what is the matter with you that you do not *hope* for majesty from God — that is, why do you not expect Him to honor you, dignify you, and raise your standing, in this life and the next? On this reading the question is an appeal to their ambition, and it fits the sermon's structure well, since the preceding verses have just offered rain, wealth, children, and gardens. You are being offered dignity and you are not interested in it.

The second reading takes *rajāʾ* in the sense attested in some classical usage of expecting with apprehension — why do you not *fear* God's majesty? This reading connects the verse to the command in verse three, fear Him, and to the theme of punishment that opened the sūrah.

The first is stronger. It preserves the ordinary meaning of the verb, it follows naturally from the promises just made, and it explains the word *waqār* as the dignity God confers rather than only as the majesty He possesses. The second is not baseless — Arabic does use *rajāʾ* where fear is meant, and the Qur'an pairs hope and fear as the two attitudes of a believer — but it requires the rarer sense and weakens the link to the surrounding argument. Both readings agree on the substance: something is missing in the way these people stand before God.

**waqār — Weight, Gravity, Dignity**

The noun *waqār* comes from w-q-r, a root meaning heaviness, steadiness, and settled dignity. It describes a thing that has weight and does not shift — the opposite of flightiness. Applied to God it denotes His greatness as something to be reckoned with; applied to a person it denotes the composure of someone who takes things seriously.

The word choice is precise, and it is the sermon's sharpest instrument. Consider the verb used for the people's refusal in verse seven: *istakbarū*, they made themselves great, from k-b-r. Their posture is self-magnification. The question in verse thirteen asks why they do not attribute *waqār* to God. The two words sit inside one argument: a man who inflates himself necessarily deflates everything else, and the specific thing he deflates is the weight of God. Arrogance is not, on this account, an ethical flaw alongside others. It is a distortion of scale — an error about how heavy things are.

The same root produces the Qur'anic description of a heart that carries weight and settles, and it produces the word for a burden carried on a beast. Heaviness in this semantic field is never pejorative. It is the measure of reality. To fail to give God His weight is to live in a world where nothing has its proper mass, and a person in that condition cannot be moved by any argument, because he has already mispriced everything.

**Awe as the Faculty the Whole Sūrah Misses**

If the diagnosis is right, the sūrah's account of failure becomes coherent in a way it otherwise is not. Noah's people were given every kind of evidence: an uninterrupted call over nine and a half centuries, open and private preaching, promises of rain and property, and finally the argument from their own creation and the structure of the heavens. They refused all of it.

The reason supplied here is that the equipment for receiving evidence was misaligned. Awe is not an emotion added to knowledge; it is the posture that makes knowledge count. A man without it can be shown a fact and register it without being changed by it, which is the behavior the Qur'an describes when it says that hearing, sight, and intellect were given to destroyed peoples and benefited them not at all (46:26). The faculties were present. The weighting was wrong.

That is why the sermon's remaining verses do not argue for God's existence. They argue for His magnitude — the seven heavens stacked above you, the moon and sun set in them, your own body grown out of the ground in stages, the earth spread for your feet. Every item is an exhibit in a case about weight.

**Why a Question, and Not an Accusation**

Noah could have stated the diagnosis. He asks it instead, and the difference is a matter of method.

A statement invites denial: you do not fear God. A question invites inspection: what is wrong with you that you do not? The second form leaves the listener holding the problem, and it leaves room for an answer. It is also the form that a caller uses when he still wants the person he is calling. After nine and a half centuries of refusal, Noah is still asking his people to examine themselves, which tells the reader something about what prophetic patience looks like at the end of its tether: not anger, but one more question.

The question is also the last act of the sermon before it turns to evidence. Having asked why they do not hold God in weight, the preacher proceeds to show them the weight, item by item, beginning with the only object they cannot avoid looking at — themselves.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:14

> when He truly created you in stages ˹of development˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The First Exhibit Is the Body of the Listener**

The sermon's argument for the magnitude of God begins with the person being addressed. Not the sky, not the mountains, not history — your own making. The clause is attached to the question of verse thirteen as its ground: how can you fail to hold God in weight, when He created you in stages?

That placement is a rhetorical decision with a purpose. An argument about the cosmos can be met with speculation; an argument about one's own origin cannot be met at all, because the listener is standing inside it. Every person addressed by this verse has already passed through the process described. The evidence is not offered for examination. It is offered as a recollection.

The Qur'an uses this opening move constantly, and it treats it as the strongest available: *“One of His signs is that He created you from dust, then—behold!—you are human beings spreading over ˹the earth˺.”* (30:20). And the appeal is made explicit in al-Dhāriyāt: signs are in the earth and *“as there are within yourselves. Can you not see?”* (51:21). The self is listed as a sign alongside the world, and the question attached to it is the same question Noah asks — not "do you believe?" but "can you not see?"

**aṭwār — Stages, One Condition After Another**

The word *aṭwārā* is the plural of *ṭawr*, which means a state, a condition, a turn, or a phase — something that follows something else and differs from it. The noun also carries the sense of a limit or a measure; the Arabic idiom *ʿadā ṭawrahu*, he exceeded his ṭawr, means he overstepped his proper measure, which shows that a ṭawr is both a stage and a boundary.

That double sense is exactly what the verse needs. Human development is a sequence of bounded states, each with its own limit, each giving way to the next. The Qur'an spells the sequence out at length in al-Muʾminūn: from an extract of clay (23:12), to a sperm-drop in a secure place (23:13), to a clinging clot, to a lump of flesh, to bones, to bones clothed with flesh, to a new creation (23:14). The same sequence appears in al-Ḥajj as the opening of an argument about resurrection: *“We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh —fully formed or unformed—in order to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you.”* (22:5). And in Ghāfir the stages continue past birth: infants, prime, old age, and an appointed end (40:67).

Noah's single word compresses all of it. The plural form is doing the argumentative work: not one act of making, but many, ordered, each dependent on the last.

**Sequence With Direction Requires an Agent**

The force of the argument lies in the word "stages" and not in the word "created." Any observer of the ancient or modern world could agree that a human being develops through phases. What the verse asks the listener to notice is the character of those phases: they are ordered, they are cumulative, and they run in one direction toward a result that none of the earlier stages contains.

A drop does not become a clot by accident and then a lump by accident and then a skeleton by accident. The sequence has an arrow. Each stage prepares for the next, and the final term — a speaking, reasoning person — is nowhere present in the first. The Qur'an's argument from this fact is not a proof in the modern formal sense. It is an invitation to look at an ordered process and ask what kind of cause produces ordered processes. The traditional answer, stated in the same vocabulary, is that a maker who works in measured stages and arrives at a designed end is a maker with knowledge and intent: *“so that you may reach your prime, and become old … reaching an appointed time, so perhaps you may understand ˹Allah’s power˺.”* (40:67).

The purpose clause in that verse is the point. The stages are not merely described; they are described so that the observer will draw the conclusion.

**The Measure in the Womb**

Two features of the staged account deserve emphasis because they carry the theological weight.

The first is that the process happens in concealment. The stages described in al-Muʾminūn take place in a secure lodging (23:13), inside a body, unseen by anyone including the person being made. Nobody witnessed his own formation, and nobody directed it. The Qur'an returns to that fact when it describes the creation of a human being in the wombs of mothers, in threefold darkness, as the work of one Lord (39:6). A process that no one observes and no one controls, yet which produces the same ordered result in every case, is not a process that can be attributed to the person it produces.

The second is that the process includes loss as well as gain. In the Ghāfir account some die young and others are returned to the feebleness of old age, *“so that they may know nothing after having known much”* (40:67). The stages run downhill at the end. A person who is being shown his own development is being shown a curve, not a ladder, and the curve's descent is as much part of the evidence as its ascent. It is the fact that makes the next claim in Noah's sermon — that the same God will return you to the earth and bring you out again — intelligible rather than incredible.

**A Question Addressed to Arrogance**

Read against verse thirteen, this clause is the answer to the diagnostic question. Why do you not hold God in weight? Because you have not looked at how you were made. A person who had followed his own formation through seven or eight dependent stages would not need to be told that he carries no weight of his own.

The arrogance reported in verse seven is thus explained as a failure of attention. It is not a philosophical position defended with reasons; it is a posture adopted by someone who has never examined his own beginning. This is why the Qur'an's creation arguments are usually addressed to the proud rather than to the puzzled. The puzzled man needs information. The proud man needs to be reminded of a fact he already possesses and has stopped consulting.

The argument is also available to every generation on the same terms, which is unusual for a proof. It requires no instrument, no specialized vocabulary, and no historical knowledge. A listener in any century can perform it in the time it takes to consider where he came from, and the result is the same each time: he was assembled, in stages, by someone else.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:15

> Do you not see how Allah created seven heavens, one above the other

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Argument Moves Outward From the Body**

Verse fourteen put the listener inside his own formation. This verse lifts his head. The question *a-lam taraw*, do you not see, is plural now — the whole people are addressed — and the object is the structure overhead.

The movement from the inner sign to the outer one is the standard order of Qur'anic argument, and it is standard because it works. A person who has considered his own staged development is prepared to consider a staged construction; the same maker is being identified by two different objects. The pairing is explicit elsewhere: *“One of His signs is that He created you from dust”* (30:20) sits among signs that include the heavens, the earth, the alternation of night and day, the ships, the rain, the winds (2:164). The self and the cosmos are two pages of one document.

The shift also changes the scale of the audience's problem. A man who refuses to look at his own body can be dismissed as careless. A community that refuses to look at the sky has organized its carelessness, and organized carelessness is what the sūrah has been describing all along: the ears stopped, the heads covered, the persistence, the arrogance (71:7). The question is put to a people who have agreed among themselves not to see.

**ṭibāqā — Layered and Matched**

The description of the seven heavens uses a single word, *ṭibāqā*, which denotes things made in matching layers, one fitted over another — the same word used for a sole stitched in layers, or for the tiers of a structure. The translation renders it *one above the other*, which captures the arrangement; the Arabic adds the fitting.

That addition matters. The heavens are not described as a heap of separate objects but as a construction in which each level corresponds to the next. The same idea appears in the sūrah whose name means Dominion: *“˹He is the One˺ Who created seven heavens, one above the other. You will never see any imperfection in the creation of the Most Compassionate. So look again: do you see any flaws?”* (67:3). The challenge there is identical in form to Noah's question here — look, and report what you find — and the expected report is that the construction is seamless.

The word also carries the sense of exact correspondence between parts. A layered thing held together without cracks implies a single plan executed across levels, and a single plan implies a single planner. The seven heavens are therefore not evidence of size alone. They are evidence of coherence, which is the harder claim and the more useful one: size can be produced by accident in someone's imagination, coherence cannot.

**The Number Seven**

Seven is the Qur'an's fixed number for the heavens, and it is used without variation: God created seven heavens (2:29), He built above you seven mighty heavens (78:12), the seven heavens and everything in them glorify Him. The consistency rules out the reading that the number is merely an idiom for "many," since Arabic had other idioms for that and the Qur'an uses them elsewhere.

Whether the seven are seven concentric levels, seven distinct orders of reality, or something a modern cosmology would describe differently is a question the text does not settle, and the classical commentators did not pretend it did. What the text requires of the number is that it denote a complete, articulated structure — a cosmos with levels rather than a single dome. The lowest of those levels is the one furnished with the visible lights: *“And indeed, We adorned the lowest heaven with ˹stars like˺ lamps”* (67:5), which places the stars inside the first heaven, not above all of them, and implies further structure beyond what the eye reaches.

That is the specific force of Noah's question. He is asking his people to consider an ordered whole whose visible part is only the nearest layer, and to draw from it a conclusion about the One who built the rest.

**Do You Not See — The Demand for Observation**

The interrogative form is not a request for belief. It is a request for looking, and the difference is important. The verb is *ra'ā*, to see, in the negative question with the particle of expectation: have you not seen, is it not the case that you have seen.

The Qur'an addresses people this way when the evidence is already in front of them and the failure is one of attention. It is the form used for the dead earth revived by rain (36:33), for the shade extended and the sun made its guide (25:45), for the ships on the sea (2:164). In each case the object is ordinary and available; the listener is being told that he has been looking at the argument his whole life without registering it.

For Noah's audience this had an added edge. They were farmers and herders whose entire calendar depended on watching the sky — for rain, for seasons, for the movement of the lights. Nobody in that society was inattentive to the heavens as a practical matter. What they had never done was read the same sky as a statement about its maker. The question therefore does not ask them to acquire new data. It asks them to notice what they already track daily, and to accept the conclusion that a layered, seamless, lit structure was built.

**Seamlessness as the Real Evidence**

The challenge in 67:3 — look again, do you see any flaws — states the standard by which the seven heavens are offered as proof. The evidence is not their grandeur. Grandeur overwhelms and then fades. The evidence is the absence of discrepancy.

A structure of that scale, held in matching layers, with lights set in its lowest level and a regular order that produces a usable calendar for creatures on the ground, contains no visible seam and no failed joint. That is what makes it a sign rather than a spectacle. The Qur'an draws the same conclusion from the same object: the lights were set with ordained phases so that people might know the number of years and the reckoning of time, and God did not create all this except for a purpose (10:5).

Noah's sermon is building toward exactly that point. A person who has been made in stages, under heavens built in matching layers, with lights installed for his calendar and ground spread for his feet, is not in a position to claim weight for himself. The two exhibits together — the body and the sky — are the answer to the question of verse thirteen, and they are answered in the listener's own experience rather than in his opponent's rhetoric.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:16

> placing the moon within them as a ˹reflected˺ light, and the sun as a ˹radiant˺ lamp

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Lights, Two Words**

The verse assigns each heavenly body its own term, and the distinction is maintained throughout the Qur'an. The moon is given *nūr*, light; the sun is given *ḍiyāʾ*, radiance or blazing light. The clearest statement of the pairing is in Yūnus: *“He is the One Who made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases, so that you may know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺. Allah did not create all this except for a purpose. He makes the signs clear for people of knowledge.”* (10:5).

The two words are not synonyms being varied for elegance. *Ḍiyāʾ* denotes light that comes with heat and from a source in itself — the word is used for fire and for the blaze that carries warmth with it. *Nūr* denotes light that illuminates without burning, light received and passed on. The Qur'an's usage is consistent: the Torah is *nūr*, the Prophet ﷺ is a *sirāj munīr*, a lamp giving light, and the sun alone is *ḍiyāʾ*.

The bracketed insertions in this translation — *˹reflected˺* and *˹radiant˺* — make explicit what the Arabic terms already distinguish. They are not additions to the sense but statements of it, and they are supported by 10:5, where the same two words are used in the same pairing with the same contrast. A reader who objects to the brackets is objecting to a lexical distinction the Qur'an itself draws elsewhere.

**sirāj — A Lamp Is Made for a User**

The sun is not called a star or a body of fire here. It is called a *sirāj*, a lamp — the ordinary word for the light a household sets up so that people can see to work.

The metaphor carries an argument. A lamp is not a natural object in the way a rock is; it is a device, made to serve a purpose, positioned where it is needed, and lit for someone. To call the sun a lamp is to say that the day is furnished. The Qur'an extends the same word to the Prophet ﷺ: *“and a caller to ˹the Way of˺ Allah by His command, and a beacon of light.”* (33:46). In both cases the term marks something installed for the benefit of others, and in both cases the installation implies an installer.

The lamp image also fixes the scale of the human relation to the sun. Nobody maintains it, nobody lights it, nobody can put it out — and yet it behaves exactly like a thing lit for a purpose. That is the point the verse is making in a sermon whose whole strategy is to establish the weight of the One who arranged these things. A man who has never once considered that his daylight is furnished has not yet begun to answer the question of verse thirteen.

**Lights Set Inside the Structure**

The moon and the sun are placed *within* the heavens — the preposition in the Arabic is *fī*, in — and that placement belongs to the description of the layered structure in the previous verse. The lights are not above the seven heavens; they are installed in them, and the Qur'an identifies the lowest heaven as the one adorned with lamps: *“And indeed, We adorned the lowest heaven with ˹stars like˺ lamps, and made them ˹as missiles˺ for stoning ˹eavesdropping˺ devils.”* (67:5).

Two things follow. The visible sky is the nearest layer of a larger construction, so the evidence offered here is the part of the structure open to inspection by anyone standing outdoors. And the lights are described as adornment as well as function — the heavens are not merely serviceable but beautiful, which is a further argument the sermon leaves unstated: a maker who provides for a creature's needs and also decorates the ceiling has a relation to that creature that goes beyond utility.

The regularity of the arrangement is what makes it usable. Each light travels in its own path: *“And He is the One Who created the day and the night, the sun and the moon—each travelling in an orbit.”* (21:33). Orbits that stay orbits are what allow a farming people to plant, to count months, and to keep a calendar. The reliability is the gift.

**Timekeeping as the Stated Purpose**

In the parallel verse in Yūnus the purpose is given outright: the phases are ordained *so that you may know the number of years and calculation of time* (10:5). The lights were not arranged for their own sake but for the sake of creatures on the ground who need to measure duration.

That purpose clause is the strongest form of the design argument the sermon uses, and it is stated without elaboration because it needs none. Any farmer in Noah's audience already lived inside the calendar. He knew the moon's phases as a clock and the sun's movement as a year. What he had never been asked to consider was that the clock he used daily had been built for him by the One he was refusing to worship.

The verse therefore does something more subtle than prove that God exists. It takes an instrument the audience already trusts, already uses, and already depends upon, and reassigns its origin. A person who accepts that the calendar is given has already accepted a giver. The rest of the sermon simply asks him to behave accordingly — which is the meaning of holding God in weight (71:13).

**A Reflected Light for the Hours Without the Sun**

The pairing of a radiant source with a reflected light is not symmetrical, and the asymmetry is functional. Day has a lamp. Night has something gentler, borrowed, sufficient for travel and for counting but not for heat.

The Qur'an reads this arrangement as deliberate provision rather than as a byproduct. The alternation of night and day is listed among the signs (2:164), the night is made for rest and the day for seeing and for earning, and the moon's measured stations are what turn an undifferentiated dark into a countable period. A people with no artificial light lived by that borrowed illumination; the month itself is named for it.

This is the last item in the sermon's catalogue of the sky, and it completes the case begun in verse thirteen. You were assembled in stages. Above you is a layered structure with no seam in it. Inside that structure two lights were installed, one blazing and one borrowed, timed to give you a calendar. Nothing in this arrangement was necessary for you, and all of it serves you. The question the preacher asked at the start — what is the matter with you that you do not hold God in weight — has now been put to the sky as well as to the body, and the sky's answer is the same as the body's: someone made this for you.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:17

> Allah ˹alone˺ caused you to grow from the earth like a plant

**Expanded Commentary**

**Grown, Not Manufactured**

The verb here is *anbatakum* — He caused you to grow — and it belongs to the vocabulary of agriculture, not of construction. The Qur'an could have said He made you or formed you from the earth; it says He grew you out of it, and it adds the noun for the process: *nabātā*, as a plant.

The difference between making and growing is the difference between a thing assembled from outside and a thing that develops from within by a principle it did not choose. A potter works on clay. A farmer plants a seed and the seed does the rest under conditions he supplies. The verse assigns humanity to the second category, and the assignment is exact: your origin is the soil, your development is a growth, and the growth has a cause that is not you.

This also connects the verse to the one before it about stages. A plant is the classic example of an ordered sequence — seed, shoot, stem, leaf, flower, fruit — in which each stage is produced by the last and none of them is present at the beginning. Human development described as *aṭwār* (71:14) and human origin described as *nabāt* are the same claim made twice in two registers: you are a process with a direction, and the process is not self-directed.

**The Cognate Accusative, Again**

The Arabic reinforces the verb with its own noun — *anbatakum … nabātā*, He grew you a growing — the same intensifying device used in verse nine, where Noah's private counsel was described with a doubled term.

The construction states that the action was performed completely and in the manner the verb implies. It is not that God caused something plant-like to appear in human history; it is that He caused you to grow, with a real growth, in the full sense of the word. The doubling removes any figurative escape route. A listener who wants to say the metaphor is decorative is answered by the grammar: the growth is asserted, not merely suggested.

The device appears at the moments in this sūrah where the argument is most concrete — the thoroughness of the private preaching, and the reality of the vegetable origin. That distribution is probably not accidental. Where the sermon asks for the largest act of attention, it uses the most emphatic form available.

**From Dust: The Material Claim**

The soil is named as the source, and the Qur'an says the same thing in several registers. *“Indeed, the example of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like that of Adam. He created him from dust, then said to him, ‘Be!’ And he was!”* (3:59). Humanity is created from an extract of clay (23:12), from dust and then a sperm-drop (22:5), and the sign is stated in the shortest possible form: *“One of His signs is that He created you from dust, then—behold!—you are human beings spreading over ˹the earth˺.”* (30:20).

The claim is material and it is not softened. Nothing in the human being is exempted from the description; the body is soil that has been organized. The rhetorical power of the statement lies in the gap between its two terms. Dust and a speaking person are as far apart as two things can be while still being the same substance, and the verse asks the listener to hold both ends of that gap in one thought.

That gap is also what defeats the arrogance diagnosed in verse thirteen. A person whose substance is soil has no material basis for self-magnification. The only ground available for dignity is the one the sermon keeps pointing to — that someone chose to grow this particular thing out of this particular ground, and furnished a sky above it and a calendar inside that sky for its use.

**The Metaphor Places Humanity Inside a Cultivated World**

Growing from the earth is not an isolating fact. It puts human beings in the same category as their food, and the Qur'an uses that kinship as an instruction: *“Let people then consider their food:”* (80:24), followed by the account of poured rain, split earth, and grain and plants growing from it. The same water, the same soil, the same process produces the crop and the person eating the crop.

The practical consequence is a picture of the world as provision rather than as scenery. Everything the body needs comes out of the ground by the same mechanism that produced the body, and the mechanism has a single operator. This is the theology of provision stated botanically, and it is the same theology that underlies the promises earlier in the sermon — rain, wealth, children, gardens, rivers (71:11–12). Those gifts are not separate from the creation of the recipient. They are the continuation of the same act by the same agent, in the same medium.

The cattle, the children, the gardens, and the springs that Hūd lists for his people as the known good things provided for them (26:132–134) are all products of one soil under one sky. A person who accepts the provision and denies the provider has accepted the harvest and denied the farmer.

**The Growth That Also Proves the Return**

The vegetable metaphor does its heaviest work in the Qur'an as a proof of resurrection, and this verse is positioned to carry that load. A field that dies in the dry season and greens after rain is the most familiar instance in human experience of a thing returning to life from a state that looked final.

The Qur'an draws the inference explicitly and repeatedly. *“˹as˺ a provision for ˹Our˺ servants. And with this ˹rain˺ We revive a lifeless land. Similar is the emergence ˹from the graves˺.”* (50:11). *“He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And He gives life to the earth after its death. And so will you be brought forth ˹from the grave˺.”* (30:19). The comparison is not poetic decoration in either verse; it is the argument, stated as an analogy with a conclusion attached.

Noah's sermon has now supplied both halves of that analogy in advance. You were grown out of the earth, and the earth grows things that die and return. The next verse completes the sequence by naming the return and the second emergence — which is why the vegetable image here is not merely a description of origin. It is the preparation for the hardest claim the sermon will make.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:18

> Then He will return you to it, and then simply bring you forth ˹again˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**Three Movements in One Sentence**

Growth out of the earth, return into it, extraction from it. The verse completes the circuit begun two verses earlier, and the Qur'an states the same circuit in a single sentence elsewhere, in almost the same words: *“From the earth We created you, and into it We will return you, and from it We will bring you back again.”* (20:55).

The three movements are presented as equally ordinary acts of the same agent. Nothing in the middle term is treated as a catastrophe or an interruption; the return to the earth is a step in a sequence, not a fall out of one. That treatment is the theological point of the verse. A person who sees burial as the end of a story has misread the story's structure: the sequence has three terms, and the listener has only experienced the first.

The order also removes the usual objection to the third movement. If the second movement is accepted — and every human being accepts that the dead go into the ground — then the third is not a new kind of event. It is the first movement repeated. The agent who assembled a person out of soil once is being described as doing it again, from the same material, in the same medium.

**The Symmetry That Makes the Second Emergence Unremarkable**

The force of the verse lies in its refusal to treat resurrection as difficult. The word rendered *simply* in the translation reflects an Arabic construction of emphasis and ease rather than a separate promise: the bringing forth will happen, and it will happen without strain.

The argument is one the Qur'an makes in several places, always in the same direction — the second creation is not harder than the first, it is the same operation with the plan already drawn. *“How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.”* (2:28). The four terms of that sentence are the four terms of Noah's claim, and both are addressed to people who accept the earlier terms and refuse the later ones.

There is an asymmetry worth naming, and it runs in favor of the second act. The first emergence happened without any material prepared in advance; the second happens with the material already gathered in the ground. A person who finds the first credible and the second incredible has reversed the ordinary relation between the two operations. The sermon is not asking for a larger faith at the end than at the beginning. It is asking for a consistent one.

**iḵhrāj — The Verb of Bringing Forth**

The same verb, *akhraja*, is used across the Qur'an for three distinct operations that the language treats as one: bringing a plant out of the soil, bringing a child out of the womb, and bringing the dead out of the ground. *“He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living.”* (30:19). The stages of gestation end in the same verb: then We brought it into being as a new creation (23:14).

That shared vocabulary is not a coincidence of usage. It records an observation: all three are cases of something emerging from a place where it was concealed, by an agency it did not supply. The seed does not open itself. The infant does not deliver itself. The dead do not rise by their own motion. In each case there is a container, a concealment, and an extraction, and in each case the extraction is performed on the contents rather than by them.

The verse therefore uses the most ordinary verb available for the strangest event it describes. Resurrection is spoken of in the language of childbirth and of harvest — two things Noah's audience watched happen every year and every season. The choice of verb makes the unheard-of event legible by placing it in a category the audience already accepts without argument.

**The Objection the Verse Is Answering**

The denial of return is not a modern problem, and the Qur'an records it in the exact form it takes: *“And they argue with Us— forgetting they were created—saying, ‘Who will give life to decayed bones?’”* (36:78). The charge attached to the objection is forgetfulness of one's own creation — precisely the failure this sermon has spent five verses describing.

The structure of the objection is instructive. It treats the bones as a settled object rather than as a stage in a process, and then asks how the object could move. The sermon's answer is to restore the process: you were grown, you will be returned, you will be brought out. Once the terms are arranged in sequence, the question *who will give life to decayed bones* has already been answered by the first term — the one who grew the bones in the first place. The same speaker in al-Yāsīn is told to answer that the One who produced them the first time will produce them again, and that He knows every creation.

Noah's audience had not formulated the objection in those words, but their behavior implies it. A people who run from an invitation to forgiveness (71:6) and refuse to hold God in weight (71:13) have decided, in practice, that no accounting is coming. The verse names the decision by describing the event it denies.

**A Return, Not an End**

The word for the middle movement is *yuʿīdukum*, He will return you — the verb of restoration and repetition, the same root used for the return of a people to their land, of a matter to its beginning, of a season to its time. Returning is what you do to a place you have already been.

That lexical choice quietly affirms a relation between the body and its soil. The earth is not a container that receives waste. It is the point of origin, and going back to it is a homecoming in the most literal sense available: the material returns to where it came from, and stays there until the third movement. The Qur'an describes the burial of a person by God's own arrangement in the story of the first murder, when a bird is sent to show the living how to hide the dead — the first lesson in returning a body to its ground.

The sermon ends its creation argument here, having covered the body, the sky, the lights, the soil, and the return. What remains is the ground underfoot and the roads across it, which is where the next two verses go. The argument has moved from the largest object a person can see to the smallest thing a person can stand on, and at every step the same conclusion has been reached: this was arranged, for you, by someone who intends to ask you about it.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:19

> And Allah ˹alone˺ spread out the earth for you

**Expanded Commentary**

**bisāṭ — A Floor Laid Down**

The earth is described with a word from the vocabulary of furnishing: *bisāṭ*, something spread out, a carpet, a floor covering laid for people to walk and sit on. The same description recurs across the Qur'an: the earth laid out for all of you with pathways set in it (20:53); *“As for the earth, We spread it out and placed upon it firm mountains, and caused everything to grow there in perfect balance.”* (15:19); *“Have We not smoothed out the earth ˹like a bed˺,”* (78:6); *“As for the earth, He spread it out as well,”* (79:30); the earth made a place of settlement with the sky as a canopy (40:64).

The image is domestic rather than architectural. A carpet is not a monument; it is a surface prepared for use by someone who intends to be comfortable on it. To describe the planet that way is to say that the ground beneath a person's feet was arranged with him in mind, and it puts the arrangement on the same level as the two lights installed overhead (71:16) and the body grown out of the soil (71:17).

The consistency of the vocabulary across sūrahs revealed years apart tells the reader that this is a settled description and not a passing metaphor. The Qur'an returns to it whenever it wants to make the point that the world is furnished.

**For You — The Benefactive and Its Claim**

The phrase *lakum*, for you, does most of the theological work in the verse. The spreading is not described as an event that happened to be followed by human arrival. It is described as an act performed with the human being as its beneficiary.

That is the Qur'an's standing position on the material world: *“He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you. Then He turned towards the heaven, forming it into seven heavens.”* (2:29). Everything in the earth is stated to be for the human being before the heavens are formed, and the ordering is deliberate — provision precedes the arrival of the one provided for, and the whole construction is oriented toward him. The same orientation is stated for the sky and the shapes of bodies: He made the earth a settlement for you and the sky a canopy, He shaped you and perfected your form, and He provided you with good things (40:64).

The claim attached to the benefactive is obvious and unavoidable. A thing made for someone obliges that someone. Not with a debt of repayment, since nothing can be repaid to a giver who owns the medium, but with a debt of acknowledgment. The failure named in verse thirteen — the absence of weight given to God — is exactly a failure of acknowledgment, and the verse here supplies the object that should have produced it. You are standing on the evidence.

**A Statement About Habitability, Not About Geometry**

The word *bisāṭ* has been read by some modern readers as a claim about the earth's shape, and the reading should be addressed rather than avoided.

The language of the verse is functional. It describes the earth as it presents itself to a person living on it — extended, traversable, usable — and it uses the ordinary vocabulary of a floor or a spread surface to say so. The same is true of the parallel descriptions: mountains placed as pegs so the ground does not shake (21:31), the earth smoothed like a bed (78:6), the sky raised as a canopy. None of these sentences is a treatise on geodesy, and the classical commentators did not read them as one. They read them as statements about the fitness of the world for human life, which is the subject the sermon is actually working on.

What the verse asserts can be tested by any listener in any century and comes out true every time: the ground you live on is extended enough to travel, stable enough to build on, and productive enough to feed you. That is the claim, and it is the claim the argument needs. A sermon about gratitude does not require a measurement of the planet's curvature, and reading one into the verse imports a question the text is not asking.

**The Sermon Comes Back Down to Earth**

The catalogue that began in verse thirteen has now travelled in a deliberate arc: your own staged formation, the seven layered heavens, the two lights set inside them, your growth out of the soil, your return into it and your extraction from it, and finally the floor itself.

The return to the ground is the last turn of the argument and it is where the sermon lands. Everything above the listener was furnished; everything below him was furnished too. The arc ends with the surface he is standing on while he hears the speech, which means there is no position from which the argument can be observed at a distance. A listener can speculate about the heavens. He cannot speculate about the ground under his feet, because he is on it.

That closing position is also the practical one. The next verse will name the roads across this floor, and roads are where the sermon's demand becomes concrete: a furnished world with pathways in it is a world you can be asked to walk in a particular direction. The provision is not a gift left with the recipient. It is a setting prepared for a journey that has been specified.

**Gratitude as the Only Adequate Response**

The verse supplies the ground for the demand the whole sermon has been making. Seek forgiveness, and the sky will send rain; the earth is already spread for you. The two statements describe one arrangement — a world prepared in advance for people who have not yet arrived in it — and the arrangement calls for a single response.

The prophets put that response in the same terms. Hūd says to his people: *“Fear the One Who has provided you with ˹the good˺ things you know: He provided you with cattle, and children, and gardens, and springs.”* (26:132–134). The structure is identical to Noah's: an inventory of provision followed by a command to fear. The inventory is not an argument for the provider's existence so much as a reminder of an obligation already incurred. A person who lists what he has been given has already answered the question of what he owes.

This is why the sermon's diagnosis and its catalogue fit together so tightly. The failure to hold God in weight is not a speculative error about the size of the divine. It is ingratitude — the state of a person standing on a spread floor, under an installed lamp, in a body grown for him, who has never once acknowledged that any of it was given.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:20

> to walk along its spacious pathways.’”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Roads Wide Enough to Travel**

The last words of the sermon name two things: *subul*, paths, and *fijāj*, the plural of *fajj*, a wide way through terrain — a broad valley, an open pass, a route with room in it. The combination describes not merely the existence of roads but their width: ways that can be travelled by a caravan, that do not force a person through a gap one at a time.

The same pairing appears elsewhere with the same emphasis. *“And We have placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with them, and made in it broad pathways so they may find their way.”* (21:31). The earth is laid out with pathways set in it (20:53), and the One who laid it out *“has laid out the earth for you, and set in it pathways for you so that you may find your way.”* (43:10). In each case the road is presented as a made thing, and the making is attributed to the same agent who spread the floor.

The width matters for the argument. A narrow track through rock is a matter of geology. A wide, usable route across a landscape — with passes positioned where the mountains permit them, and valleys oriented so that travel follows water — is provision. The sermon ends on provision because provision is the claim hardest to argue with: it is experienced daily by the listener's own feet.

**So That You May Find Your Way**

The purpose attached to the roads in the parallel verses is guidance. Pathways were made *so that they may find their way* (21:31); so that you may find your way (43:10). The road exists so that a traveller can arrive.

That is the last turn of the sermon's argument, and it is a quiet one. Everything catalogued so far has been described as made for the human being — the body in stages, the layered heavens, the two lights, the growth from soil, the spread earth. The roads add a further specification: the world is not only furnished but oriented. It has directions in it, and the directions lead somewhere.

Orientation is a gift of a different order from provision. A person can be fed and housed and still be lost. The verse places the making of ways beside the making of the ground and treats both as parts of one arrangement, which implies that the arrangement includes a destination. The sermon never names the destination in this verse — it has already named it, in the opening command to worship, fear, and obey (71:3) — but the image supplies the physical form of the demand. A road built for you is a road you are expected to walk.

**Travel as a Way of Knowing**

The Qur'an consistently treats journeys as instruments of knowledge rather than as interruptions of it. The invitation is issued in the imperative: *“So travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers!”* (16:36); *“Travel throughout the land and see how He originated the creation, then Allah will bring it into being one more time.”* (29:20).

The reason given is that the heart reasons through the eyes and the ears of a traveller: *“Have they not travelled throughout the land so their hearts may reason, and their ears may listen? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind.”* (22:46). Travel is prescribed because a person who stays in one place runs out of evidence and starts to trust his own assumptions. The ruins of destroyed towns are themselves exhibits, and the wide roads of verse twenty are how a person reaches them.

For Noah's audience the point had an immediate edge. The region they lived in was crossed by routes that passed the remains of earlier communities, and the Qur'an's standing argument is that those remains are legible to anyone who walks past them with his eyes open. The sermon's last item is therefore also an instruction: the world you are standing on contains the record of what happens to people who refuse, and the roads are how you get to it.

**The Sermon Ends With a Person in Motion**

The quotation marks close at the end of this verse, and the speech that began with a command to seek forgiveness ends with a road. Between the two, the argument travelled from the listener's own body outward to the heavens, back down through the lights and the soil, and finally to the ground beneath his feet and the ways across it.

That closing position is deliberate. A sermon that ended on the seven heavens would leave the listener a spectator. A sermon that ends on a road leaves him a traveller, and a traveller has to decide on a direction. The whole of the middle section of this sūrah has been assembling reasons for one decision, and the last image supplies the form the decision takes: not a state of mind but a movement, from where you are toward where the road goes.

The report that follows is the answer this audience gave. It is not a state of mind either. It is a movement, in the opposite direction — the flight described in verse six, and the persistence in disobedience described in the verse that opens the second half of the sūrah.

**The Physical Road and the Moral One**

Arabic uses the same vocabulary for both. *Sabīl* is a road and also a course of conduct; *ṭarīq* is a path and also a way of life; *ṣirāṭ* is a highway and also the straight way a person is asked to keep. The overlap is not a metaphor layered onto the language later. It is native to it, and the Qur'an exploits it constantly.

The exploitation has a specific effect in this verse. Roads are described as *made*, which means the moral road is also made rather than invented — a person does not construct the right course of life out of preference, any more than he cuts a pass through a mountain range. He finds it, and he walks in it, and the finding is possible because someone put it there. The wide paths of verse twenty are thus the physical sign of the whole sermon's claim: the way has been prepared in advance, furnished, and opened, and the only question left is whether anyone will use it.

That question is answered in the next movement of the sūrah, and the answer is given by Noah himself, in a report addressed to God rather than to his people.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:21

> ˹Eventually,˺ Noah cried, “My Lord! They have certainly persisted in disobeying me, and followed ˹instead˺ those ˹elite˺ whose ˹abundant˺ wealth and children only increase them in loss

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Report Resumes, and the Word Is Disobedience**

The sermon is over. Noah returns to the address of verse five — *rabbi*, my Lord — and files the second half of his report. The verb he chooses for the outcome is *ʿaṣaw*, they disobeyed, from a root meaning to refuse, to rebel, to act against a stated instruction.

The choice is exact and it is worth holding. He does not say they disbelieved, though they did; he does not say they were mistaken, though they were. He says they disobeyed *me*, which locates the offense in the relation between a messenger and the people he was sent to. Disobedience implies a command, a commander, and an addressee who understood the command and refused it. The word removes every mitigating reading of the story: there was no confusion here, no failure of communication, no honest difference of opinion. There was an instruction and a refusal.

The report is also personal in a way that the earlier complaint was not. Verse five described labor; verse six described flight; this verse names the relation that was broken. A man reporting to his Lord that his people disobeyed *him* is not defending his honor. He is establishing the fact that the commission was received and rejected, which is what the eventual judgment will turn on.

**The Elites: al-Malaʾ**

The second half of the verse identifies who was followed instead. The bracketed *˹elite˺* in the translation renders a term the Qur'an uses as a fixed social category — the chiefs, the notables, the council of men whose standing rests on property and lineage.

The category appears in every prophetic account, and its behavior is uniform. The chiefs of Noah's people declare that they see him as clearly misguided (7:60); the disbelieving chiefs say he is only a human being like them and that only the lowest follow him (11:27); the chiefs tell the masses that he wants to be superior to them and that they never heard of such a thing from their forefathers (23:24); and the argument from social rank is the standard reply — *“How can we believe in you, when you are followed ˹only˺ by the lowest of the low?”* (26:111).

The Qur'an's sociology here is consistent and unsentimental: resistance to a prophet is organized from the top. It is not the poor who defend the ancestral religion, because the ancestral religion is not doing anything for them. It is the men whose authority depends on the existing arrangement, and whose argument against the messenger is always that he is redistributing status rather than telling the truth.

**Wealth and Children That Increase Only Loss**

The description of these men is the sharpest sentence in the sūrah: their property and their offspring increase them in nothing but *khasār*, loss, ruin, the state of a merchant whose trading makes him poorer.

The mechanism is not that wealth harms its possessor directly. It is that wealth, in the hands of a person who has decided against God, supplies him with reasons to keep deciding. Every increase confirms the decision: if this were wrong, would I be prospering? The Qur'an states the rule for the last messenger in the same terms: *“So let neither their wealth nor children impress you ˹O Prophet˺. Allah only intends to torment them through these things in this worldly life, then their souls will depart while they are disbelievers.”* (9:55). The provision is the torment, because it is what holds the person in place.

The counter-statement is just as plain: *“It is not your wealth or children that bring you closer to Us. But those who believe and do good—it is they who will have a multiplied reward for what they did.”* (34:37). Wealth is not nearness. The elite of Noah's people had built a theology out of their own inventory — we are greater in wealth and superior in manpower, as the owner of the two gardens puts it (18:34) — and the sermon had already answered that claim by offering the same goods to everyone on different terms (71:11–12).

**Following Men Rather Than Evidence**

The verse pairs two verbs and the pairing is the diagnosis: they disobeyed me, and they followed those. Obedience was refused where it was owed and given where it was not.

That substitution is what the Qur'an calls following without knowledge, and it treats it as the ordinary engine of collective error. The Day is described as the moment when the arrangement is dissolved: *“˹Consider the Day˺ when those who misled others will disown their followers—when they face the torment—and the bonds that united them will be cut off.”* (2:166). The followers make their case in the same terms: *“Had it not been for you, we would certainly have been believers.”* (34:31). The leaders answer by asking whether they prevented anyone from accepting guidance after it arrived.

Noah's report anticipates that exchange. He names the followers and the followed in a single sentence, which means the sūrah holds both parties accountable in the same breath. The crowd is not exonerated by its leaders, because following is a choice; and the leaders are not excused by their success, because success is what they used as the argument. The verse is describing a closed system: men of property persuade the poor that property proves the truth, and the poor accept the persuasion because the property is visible.

**A Complaint That Identifies a Structure**

The report ends mid-sentence, and the continuation in verse twenty-two names the plot these men devised. Read together, the two verses do something unusual in a prophetic complaint: they describe an organized campaign rather than a scattered unbelief.

This is what makes Noah's prayer at the end of the sūrah intelligible. He is not asking for the destruction of confused individuals. He is reporting a network — leaders with resources, followers in obedience, a devised plan, and a religious establishment of ancestral statues maintained by command (71:23) — that has proven capable of corrupting every generation it touches. The final prayer rests on that finding, and the finding is stated here for the first time: the problem is structural, and structures do not respond to another sermon.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:22

> and who have devised a tremendous plot

**Expanded Commentary**

**kubbārā — The Superlative of Plotting**

The adjective describing the plot is *kubbārā*, and its morphology is the point. Arabic builds intensification from the same root in graded steps: *kabīr*, great; *kubār*, greater, emphatically great; *kubbār*, greatest of all, an extreme form built on the doubled middle radical. The lexical gloss preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī's own chapter heading on this passage states the relation directly — *al-kubbār* is more intense than *al-kibār* — which is the tradition's way of marking that this word sits at the top of its scale.

The Qur'an uses the extreme form rarely, and it reserves it for cases where the quality described has passed beyond ordinary measure. Its appearance here tells the reader that the campaign against Noah was not a disagreement that grew heated. It was a construction — planned, resourced, sustained, and executed at a level of coordination that the language requires a special word for.

The choice of *makr* as the noun matters equally. *Makr* is not open force; it is contrivance, the work of designing an outcome through indirect means. A plot is what people do when they cannot win an argument and have too much standing to lose by fighting openly. The elite of verse twenty-one had wealth, children, and religious authority. What they lacked was an answer, so they built a mechanism instead.

**Plotting Is What Replaces Argument**

The sequence in the sūrah is instructive: the methods of the messenger are exhausted (71:8–9), the sermon is delivered in full (71:10–20), and the next thing reported is a plot. There is no counter-argument anywhere in the account. The people never offer a reason.

That absence is itself evidence. A community with a case makes its case; a community without one changes the subject, and the subject here changes to conspiracy. The Qur'an describes the same pattern in the last messenger's Makkan period: *“And ˹remember, O Prophet,˺ when the disbelievers conspired to capture, kill, or exile you. They planned, but Allah also planned. And Allah is the best of planners.”* (8:30). The three options debated by the Quraysh council are the standard menu of a plot against a preacher — detention, execution, expulsion — and none of the three is an answer to what he said.

The verse in Sūrah Yūnus records the other side of the same exchange. When Noah's people find his presence unbearable, he tells them to assemble their plan with their associate gods, to settle on it without secrecy, and to carry it out against him without delay (10:71). A messenger who invites his opponents to plot openly has already decided that their contrivance cannot touch the message, and the invitation exposes the plot for what it is: not power, but the substitute for it.

**Named, but Not Described**

The sūrah states that the plot was tremendous and then moves directly to its content in the following verse, where the plotters are quoted instructing their followers not to abandon the ancestral gods. The mechanism of the plot — how it was organized, who was paid, what was decided in council — is never narrated.

That economy is characteristic. The Qur'an names conspiracies and rarely stages them, because the staging would turn a report of judgment into a thriller and would give the plot the dignity of detail. What the reader is told is enough to identify the thing: it was planned, it was extreme, and its content was religious conservatism enforced from above.

The same economy applies to the punishment that answers it. The drowning is reported in one clause (71:25) and the ark is never mentioned in this sūrah at all. Neither the conspiracy nor the catastrophe is expanded. The sūrah's interest lies in the preaching, in the refusal, and in the prayer, and the two events that bracket them are given the minimum narration that makes the moral sequence legible.

**The Violence Behind the Plot**

The exegetical tradition preserves a report about what the plot involved in practice: that Noah's people beat him until he lost consciousness, wrapped him in a garment, dragged him to his house, and left him for dead, and that he went back out to preach when he recovered. The report is transmitted in the tafsīr literature on this verse.

[UNVERIFIED: the chain, wording, and grading of the report that Noah's people beat him unconscious and left him wrapped in a blanket could not be confirmed against a primary hadith collection with a numbered reference in the time available; it is recorded here as an exegetical report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās without a verified citation.]

What can be verified from the Qur'an is that the threat of lethal violence was explicit in the parallel account: *“If you do not desist, O Noah, you will surely be stoned ˹to death˺.”* (26:116). Stoning is named as the sanction for continuing to preach, which places the mission in a category the sūrah does not sentimentalize. Noah was not merely unpopular. He was operating under a standing death threat issued by men with the authority to carry it out, and the sūrah's word for their activity is a plot of the extreme kind.

**A Plot Against a Man With Nothing**

The disproportion is what gives the verse its weight. On one side: property, offspring, ancestral religion, tribal authority, and a coordinated plan. On the other: one man, no army, no wealth, a handful of followers drawn from the lowest ranks (11:27), and a message.

The Qur'an returns to this disproportion constantly because it is the ordinary condition of the office. God tells the last messenger that He will protect him from the people (5:67), and that protection is what makes the asymmetry survivable. The plot in verse twenty-two is described in the superlative not to magnify the plotters but to establish the scale of what the messenger endured and continued to do. He called them day and night (71:5), openly and privately (71:8–9), under a plot of the extreme kind.

That is the record Noah files with his Lord, and it is the record the sūrah leaves with its reader: the mission did not fail for lack of effort or of evidence. It failed because a society with everything to lose organized itself against a man who had nothing to offer but the truth.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:23

> urging ˹their followers˺, ‘Do not abandon your idols—especially Wadd, Suwâ’, Yaghûth, Ya’ûq, and Nasr.’

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Plot's Content Is an Instruction to Keep**

The verse quotes the plotters, and what they say is not a call to acquire anything new. It is a command to hold on: do not abandon your gods. The energy of the campaign is conservative in the strictest sense — directed at preventing a departure rather than at promoting an arrival.

The verb form is emphatic. The Arabic uses the doubled imperative with the confirming *nūn*: *lā tadharunna*, you shall certainly not leave. That construction is how Arabic issues an order that admits no negotiation, and it tells the reader how the campaign was conducted. This was not advice offered to the masses by men who preferred the old ways. It was instruction enforced, delivered in a form that treats departure as a punishable act.

The object of the instruction is named first in general terms — *ālihatakum*, your gods — and then item by item. The generality followed by specification is a rhetorical device with a purpose: it establishes the category as a whole before naming its members, which makes the command feel like the defence of an entire order rather than of five objects. What the elite were protecting was not a set of statues. It was the arrangement in which those statues occupied the center and the elite occupied the position of interpreters.

**Five Names**

The idols are listed: Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, and Nasr. Five names, given without explanation in the text, and the explanation comes from the earliest generation of commentators.

Ibn ʿAbbās's account, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4920, does two things at once. It explains the origin of the five, and it traces their survival into pre-Islamic Arabia: the idols of Noah's people ended up among the Arabs afterwards. Wadd belonged to the tribe of Kalb at Dūmat al-Jandal; Suwāʿ to Hudhayl; Yaghūth to Murad, and then to Banū Ghutayf at al-Jurf near Sabaʾ; Yaʿūq to Hamdān; and Nasr to Himyar, to the family of Dhī al-Kalāʿ. The report converts a list of ancient names into a map of the Arabia the first audience lived in, and it explains why the sūrah bothers to name them at all.

The mapping has a polemical function that would not have been lost on a Makkan listener. The religious establishment his tribe defended as ancestral was not merely old. It was traceable, by name and by tribe, to the specific idols that a prophet had spent nine and a half centuries trying to remove, and whose removal ended in the drowning of the people who kept them.

**How the Statues Began**

The second half of Ibn ʿAbbās's report is the more important half, because it describes a mechanism rather than a list. The names belonged to pious men from among Noah's people. When those men died, Satan inspired their community to set up statues at the gathering places where they used to sit, and to name the statues after them. They did so — and the statues were not worshipped. Not then. The report continues: they were not worshipped until that generation had died and knowledge was lost, and then they were worshipped.

That sequence is the whole lesson of the verse. Idolatry did not begin as idolatry. It began as commemoration — an act of respect, arguably an act of piety, undertaken by people who understood perfectly well that the stone was a memorial and not a god. The failure came one generation later, when the people who knew were gone and the objects remained. The Arabic phrase in the report is *tanasakh al-ʿilm*, the knowledge was effaced or transferred away; the statues stayed and the explanation did not.

No moral error was made by the men who erected them. What was made was an unforced error of design: they created an object whose meaning depended entirely on living memory, and memory does not survive a century without transmission. The report is a permanent warning about religious practices whose justification is not written down and not taught.

**Do Not Abandon — The Logic of Sunk Cost**

The command is phrased negatively, and the phrasing reveals what the elite understood about their own congregation. Nobody needed to be told to keep worshipping; they were already doing it. What they needed to be told was not to stop.

The instruction therefore addresses the one real danger facing an inherited religion: the moment a person considers leaving. Every institutional system that depends on inheritance rather than conviction has to police that moment, and the policing takes the form the verse records — a prohibition on departure rather than an argument for staying. No reason is given in the quotation. There is no claim that the gods are powerful, no evidence offered, no answer to anything Noah said. The command stands on its own authority.

The Qur'an identifies this posture elsewhere with precision: *“These ˹idols˺ are mere names that you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized. They follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions and whatever ˹their˺ souls desire, although ˹true˺ guidance has already come to them from their Lord.”* (53:23). Inherited assumption plus personal desire, with guidance already present and refused, is the diagnosis — and it fits the command in verse twenty-three exactly. An instruction with no reason attached is an instruction resting on inheritance and appetite.

**The Same Three Names in Makkah**

The sūrah's first audience had its own list. The Qur'an addresses it directly: *“Now, have you considered ˹the idols of˺ Lât and ’Uzza,”* and *“and the third one, Manât, as well?”* (53:19–20). Three named objects, defended by the same mechanism, in the same city where the sūrah was first recited.

The parallel is not merely formal. It supplies the reason the five names of verse twenty-three are recorded at all. A Makkan listener hearing Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, and Nasr was hearing the ancestry of his own practice, and Ibn ʿAbbās's report closes the gap by naming the tribes that kept them into historical times. The sermon of Noah and the argument of the Qur'an are addressed to the same problem in two centuries: a society whose religion rests on commemorative objects whose origin has been forgotten, defended by men whose standing depends on the forgetting.

The verse ends with the dash of an unfinished instruction, and the sūrah's next words are Noah's own verdict on the result of that instruction — a result he had watched accumulate for nine and a half centuries.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:24

> Those ˹elite˺ have already led many astray. So ˹O Lord˺, only allow the wrongdoers to stray farther away.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Statement and a Prayer Welded Into One Verse**

The verse has two halves and they are different kinds of speech. The first is a report: they have led many astray. The second is a request: my Lord, increase the wrongdoers in nothing but straying. A finding of fact followed by a petition, in one breath, with the petition resting on the finding.

That structure explains the verse's difficulty. Noah is not asking for a punishment he wants. He is drawing a conclusion from evidence he has spent centuries collecting, and then asking God to complete a process that is already underway. The report is in the perfect tense — the misleading has happened, it is finished, its scale is known — and the prayer is built on that completed fact rather than on a hope about the future.

The transition also marks the end of the mission in narrative terms. From verse two to verse twenty Noah speaks to his people. From verse twenty-one he speaks only to God. There is no further sermon, no further offer, no further method. The final four verses are a report, a verdict, and three prayers, and this verse is where the preacher becomes a plaintiff.

**"Many" — The Scale Recorded**

The word *kathīran* is doing quiet work. Not all, but many; and the many are counted on the side of the elite, which means the campaign described in verses twenty-two and twenty-three succeeded at scale.

The figure is consistent with everything else the sūrah reports. Noah's followers were few and drawn from the lowest ranks — the chiefs say so openly, and they say it as an argument: only the lowliest follow you (11:27; 26:111). The elite's success was not marginal. It absorbed the majority of a society over nine and a half centuries, generation after generation, and the mechanism was the one described in the previous verse: a prohibition on leaving, enforced by men whose standing required that nobody leave.

Naming the number matters for the prayer that follows. A misleading that had touched a few people could be answered with more preaching. A misleading that has absorbed a society, sustained over generations, with the religious apparatus organized to reproduce itself, is a different problem, and Noah's petition recognizes the difference. He is not asking God to punish individuals for private errors. He is reporting a system that has proven capable of manufacturing error indefinitely.

**The Hard Prayer: Asking That People Stray Further**

No reader can miss the difficulty. A prophet asks God to increase wrongdoers in straying. The question the tradition worked on is how such a request can be made by a man whose whole life was an attempt to prevent it.

Three answers are given, and they are not mutually exclusive. The first: the petition is a request that a decree already fixed be carried out — Noah knows the case is closed, and he asks that what is settled be settled visibly. The second: the imperative form is being used for what is in fact a prediction, a common feature of Arabic supplicatory speech, where the petitioner states as a request what God has already determined. The third and strongest: increase in straying is itself the punishment. This is the doctrine the Qur'an names *istidrāj*, the drawing-on of a person stage by stage while he believes he is being favored — *“As for those who deny Our signs, We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend.”* (7:182), with the seal: *“I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless.”* (7:183).

On the third reading the prayer asks for no new harm. It asks that the harm the wrongdoers have chosen for themselves be allowed to run its course without interruption. That is what the Qur'an describes happening to a society that refuses its signs: the call increases them only in aversion (17:41), and the sickness in the heart increases wickedness upon wickedness (9:125). Noah is asking God to stop restraining a process his people have already started.

**ḍalāl — Wandering, Loss, and Error at Once**

The word used for what the elite caused and what Noah asks to be increased is *ḍalāl*, from a root meaning to lose the way, to perish, to be absent from what is right. It covers three things that English separates: being wrong, being lost, and being ruined.

The range is not accidental, and it explains why the petition is coherent. To stray is not merely to hold a false belief; it is to be off the road, and to be off the road in a desert is to be in the process of dying. The sermon's final image was the wide path made so that people could find their way (71:20); the prayer's vocabulary is the same vocabulary turned around. The wrongdoers are not people with a different opinion. They are people off the road, and the increase Noah speaks of is the distance growing.

The same root produces the word for what the guided are saved from, and the Qur'an's closing sūrah names it as the thing to seek refuge from. Straying in that language is a condition with a direction and an outcome, not a state of mind. Asking that it be increased is asking that the outcome arrive, and the outcome in this sūrah is stated in the very next verse.

**The Moment Intercession Stops**

There is a rule in the Qur'anic accounts about when a prophet may plead for his people, and this verse sits at the boundary. Once the decree is fixed, intercession for the condemned is forbidden: *“And build the Ark under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes and directions, and do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong, for they will surely be drowned.”* (11:37). The same prohibition is given to the believers in general: *“It is not ˹proper˺ for the Prophet and the believers to seek forgiveness for the polytheists, even if they were close relatives, after it has become clear to the believers that they are bound for the Hellfire.”* (9:113).

Noah's prayer in verse twenty-four is the speech of a man who has crossed that boundary and knows it. The mercy he spent centuries offering has been refused at every level — by the crowd, by the elite, by the institutions the elite maintained. What remains is not his to withhold or grant. He asks for the completion of the case, and the sūrah records the completion in the following verse without commentary.

The order of the two verses is itself the argument: the prayer for straying, then the drowning and the Fire. The reader is shown the petition and the answer in sequence, and is left to draw the only conclusion the sequence permits.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:25

> So because of their sins, they were drowned, then admitted into the Fire. And they found none to help them against Allah

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Thousand Years of Story in One Sentence**

The whole catastrophe occupies a single verse. They were drowned because of their sins, then they were made to enter a fire, and they found no helpers besides Allah. There is no ark, no rising water, no mountain, no rain, no oven bursting, and no narrative of anyone's last hours.

The economy is a decision. The other accounts of this event supply the scenery in detail: the gates of the sky opened with pouring rain, the vessel of planks and nails, the sailing under watchful eyes, the punishment on behalf of the one they denied (54:11–14); the ark built under divine direction with a pair of every species and the oven bursting as the sign (23:27, 11:37–44); the water overflowing and the carrying in the floating ark (69:11). This sūrah gives none of it. It gives the cause, the two punishments, and the absence of help.

The result is the most severe version of the story in the Qur'an. A reader who came to this sūrah first would never learn that there was a boat. What he would learn is that a people were warned for nine and a half centuries, refused, plotted, and were destroyed — and that the destruction had two stages, one in water and one in fire. The omission of the rescue is what makes this account a warning rather than an adventure.

**Because of Their Sins**

The causal clause comes first, and the word for sins is *khaṭīʾātihim*, a plural built on the noun for a fault or a wrong act. The punishment is attached to a cause, and the cause is theirs.

That attachment is the theological center of the verse. The flood is not presented as a divine mood, as a test with no stated reason, or as a catastrophe that happened to be survived by one righteous family. It is presented as a consequence with an antecedent, and the antecedent is named in the first word of the sentence. The whole sūrah has been building the antecedent: the flight from the call (71:6), the stopped ears and covered heads (71:7), the disobedience and the following of the elite (71:21), the tremendous plot (71:22), the enforced idolatry (71:23), the many led astray (71:24). Verse twenty-five collects all of it under one noun and states the result.

The other accounts use the same formula. Those who rejected Our signs were drowned (7:64; 10:73). The flood overtook them *while they persisted in wrongdoing* (29:14). In every version the water is described as an answer rather than an event, and in this version the answer is given before the water is even mentioned — the sin first, the drowning second.

**Then — The Order of the Two Punishments**

The particle *thumma* separates the drowning from the fire, and it indicates sequence with an interval. They drowned, and then they were admitted to fire. The two are not simultaneous, and they are not the same order of reality.

The first punishment belongs to this world and to the body. The second belongs to what follows and does not end when the body does. The Qur'an describes the doubling explicitly for another group: *“We will punish them twice ˹in this world˺, then they will be brought back ˹to their Lord˺ for a tremendous punishment.”* (9:101). The structure is the same — a punishment inside history, a pause, and a punishment outside it.

The interval matters, and the tradition's account of it is that the punished are not simply stored. The fire in this verse is described as something they were *entered into*, using the verb of admission and introduction, and the tense of the narration is past even though the Day has not come — a feature of Qur'anic style that treats the certain future as already accomplished. What the verse requires the reader to accept is the sequence and the doubling: drowning was not the end of the matter for these people, and the sūrah does not allow a reading in which death closed the case.

**No Helpers Found**

The final clause is short and it answers verse twenty-one directly. They followed men of wealth and children; when the water came, they found nobody to help them against Allah.

The word for helpers is *anṣār*, the plural of *nāṣir* — one who gives aid, the same word used for supporters and allies in a military sense. The verse reports a search: they looked, and the looking produced nothing. This is the standard Qur'anic description of the end of every patronage system. The idols themselves turn on their worshippers: *“But no! Those ˹gods˺ will deny their worship and turn against them.”* (19:82). The leaders disown the followers and the bonds between them are cut (2:166). Even the closest relation is of no use: the wife of Noah and the wife of Lōt, married to two righteous servants, are told to enter the Fire because their husbands avail them nothing (66:10).

The clause also completes the sūrah's argument about the elite. Their wealth and children increased them only in loss (71:21), and the loss is now itemized: at the moment it mattered, the network they had built and enforced and killed for produced no helper. The verse is not gloating. It is recording the failure of an institution, and the failure is total enough that the sūrah can state it in five words.

**What the Verse Leaves Out**

One omission deserves notice, because it is the omission the rest of the Qur'an fills. This verse does not mention that anyone was saved. The ark is absent, the family is absent, and the believers who entered Noah's house in faith — whom the final verse prays for — are not narrated here as survivors.

The effect is that the sūrah's account of the flood is unrelieved. A reader encounters the drowning, the fire, and the absence of help before he encounters any mention of rescue, and the rescue arrives only obliquely, in the closing prayer for the believers. This ordering is what makes Sūrah Nūḥ the hardest of the Noah accounts to read and the most useful for its original audience. A Makkan listener hearing it in the years before Badr was hearing a society's end described without a single mitigating scene — and was being told, implicitly, that his own society was in the same position, with the same offer still open.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:26

> Noah had prayed, “My Lord! Do not leave a single disbeliever on earth

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Effect Reported Before the Cause**

Verse twenty-five described the drowning. This verse goes back and reports the prayer that preceded it, using the past tense — *qāla*, he said, or in the narration's logic, he had said. The sūrah has already told the reader what happened; now it tells him what was asked for.

The inversion is deliberate and it changes how the prayer is heard. A reader who met the petition first would judge it in advance, with the outcome unknown. A reader who meets it after the drowning judges it with the whole case in front of him: nine and a half centuries of continuous calling, every method tried, the flight, the stopped ears, the organized plot, the enforced idolatry, the many led astray, and then the water. The prayer arrives as the last document in a completed file rather than as a request made in the dark.

That is also why the verse is introduced with the plain verb of speech rather than with a formula of supplication. It is presented as testimony. The reader is being told what the messenger said when the evidence was complete, and the sūrah offers no comment on whether it was appropriate, because the whole of the preceding twenty-five verses is the comment.

**dayyārā — Not One Who Stirs**

The noun at the end of the verse is *dayyār*, and it is one of the strangest words in the Qur'an. It comes from a root connected with movement and with dwelling, and classical Arabic uses it almost exclusively in negative constructions, where it means anyone at all — not one dweller, not one moving thing, not a soul.

The word is emphatic by its very form. Noah does not ask that the disbelievers be reduced, or punished, or made to submit. He asks that no single one of them be left upon the earth. The doubled construction *min aḥadin* with *dayyār* stacked together — the first meaning "anyone," the second intensifying it to "anyone who stirs or dwells" — produces an absolute negation. Nothing survives.

That absoluteness has an obvious cost, and it is what makes this the harshest verse in the Noah accounts. A reader is entitled to ask why a prophet asks for extinction rather than correction. The verse itself supplies the answer in the one that follows, and it supplies a condition in its own wording: the word it uses is *kāfir*, the one who covers over and denies — a description of a settled posture rather than of a momentary lapse. The petition is about people whose denial has become their nature, and the sūrah has spent twenty-five verses demonstrating that it has.

**The One Verse That Implies a Universal Flood**

Every other Qur'anic account of the flood is limited in its phrasing. Those who rejected Our signs were drowned (7:64; 10:73); the ones drowned were those who did wrong (11:37; 23:27); an evil people were delivered from (21:77). In each case the group is defined by its behavior, and no statement is made about anyone outside it.

This verse is different. It asks that no disbeliever be left *upon the earth*, without restriction, and it is the only verse in the Qur'an's Noah material that implies a global scope. It is also the reason later Muslim scholars could hold either position about the extent of the flood — universal, as the plain sense of this petition suggests, or local to Noah's people, as the other accounts imply.

The stronger reading, on the evidence of the Qur'an alone, is that the text does not settle the question and does not need to. The petition states what Noah asked for; it does not state what the geographical range of the judgment was, and the Qur'an nowhere reports the range. What is reported everywhere is the moral range: the people who were warned and refused. A reader who insists on a hydrological map is asking the text a question it was not composed to answer, and the answer he wants is not needed for anything the sūrah is doing.

**Why a Prophet Asks for Extinction**

The petition has a rule attached to it elsewhere in the Qur'an, and the rule explains both why Noah could ask and why he asked only now. Once the decree against a people is fixed, pleading for them is forbidden: *“do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong, for they will surely be drowned”* (11:37). The same rule is generalized for the Prophet ﷺ and the believers: seeking forgiveness for the polytheists is not proper even for close relatives, once it is clear they are bound for the Fire (9:113).

Noah's prayer belongs to the moment after that boundary. He is not interceding; intercession has been closed. He is asking that the closure be complete. The distinction is what makes the petition prophetic rather than vindictive: a man seeking revenge would ask for suffering, and Noah asks for absence. What he wants is that the mechanism described in verses twenty-one through twenty-four — an elite producing error at scale, generation after generation — be given no further material to work on.

That motive is stated in the next verse, and it is stated as a reason rather than as a feeling. The sūrah is careful to give the petition a ground, because a petition without a ground would be indistinguishable from hatred.

**Upon the Earth**

The phrase *ʿalā al-arḍ* closes the verse, and its placement is significant. The earth is the same earth the sermon described in verses seventeen through twenty — the soil people are grown from, returned to, and brought out of; the floor spread for them with wide roads across it.

Asking that no denier be left upon it is asking that the furnished world be cleared of the people who refused the furnisher. The irony is exact: the sermon spent its last eight verses showing that the earth was made for human beings, that the roads in it were made so they could find their way, and that the lights above were installed to give them a calendar. The petition asks that the people who treated all of it as theirs by right, and its giver as negligible, be removed from it.

The removal is not described as the earth's loss. In the sūrah's economy, a world with the refusers gone is a world with the plot ended — the tremendous contrivance of verse twenty-two finally without operators.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:27

> For if You spare ˹any of˺ them, they will certainly mislead Your servants, and give birth only to ˹wicked˺ sinners, staunch disbelievers

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Petition Comes With Its Ground**

Verse twenty-six asked; this verse argues. The structure is conditional and predictive — if You leave them, they will mislead Your servants, and they will beget only a transgressor, a denier — and the condition is offered as the reason the request should be granted.

That a prophet should justify his petition at all is worth noting. Noah does not need to persuade God of anything; the Qur'an is explicit that God knows what is in the hearts and what a people will do. The justification is for the reader. The sūrah records the ground of the harshest prayer in the prophetic accounts so that nobody can read it as an outburst. A man who spent nine and a half centuries calling, who tried every method, who delivered a sermon of provision and creation and was answered with fingers in ears and an organized plot, states his reason in two clauses and asks for the case to be closed.

The reason is empirical. It is not a claim about the nature of these particular individuals as such. It is a claim about what a surviving remnant of them would do to the next generation, based on nine and a half centuries of observed behavior. The verse is the report of a man who has watched a mechanism operate for a millennium and has concluded that it will not stop operating if it is left intact.

**Your Servants, Not My People**

The object of the predicted misleading has changed, and the change is the verse's quiet center. In the sermon and in the earlier report, Noah speaks of *my people* (71:1–2, 71:21). Here he speaks of *Your servants*.

The shift marks the end of the claim of kinship. For nine and a half centuries he addressed them as his own, and warned them as a man warns his clan. The petition says that if they are left, the people they will damage are God's, not his. The allegiance has been transferred, and the transfer is not spiteful — it is the recognition that the community he was sent to has ceased to be a community he belongs to, and that any future believers in that region will be God's servants rather than Noah's people.

The same transfer appears in the closing prayer, where the objects of his petition for mercy are described as whoever enters my house in faith, and the believing men and women (71:28) — a category defined by faith rather than by blood. Between the two verses the sūrah has redrawn the boundary of Noah's concern. It now runs through belief, not lineage, and it runs through every believer on earth rather than through one town.

**fājiran kaffārā — Two Intensives in a Row**

The description of what these people will beget uses two nouns of the same grammatical weight, and the pairing is the most severe in the sūrah. *Fājir* comes from a root meaning to cleave, to burst open — the same root that gives the word for dawn, the thing that splits the dark. A *fājir* is one who breaks out of bounds, who ruptures the limit set for him. *Kaffār* is the intensive form of the one who covers over, who denies by burying what he knows.

The two words describe one condition from two ends. The *fājir* acts without limit; the *kaffār* refuses to see the limit. Together they name a person whose behavior is unbounded and whose perception is obstructed, which is exactly the combination the sūrah has been documenting: the plot, the enforced idolatry, the arrogance, the stopped ears, the covered heads.

The intensive forms matter. The Qur'an could have said a sinner and a disbeliever. It says a *fājir* and a *kaffār*, both at the extreme end of their scales, in a verse describing what a surviving generation would produce. The escalation is not rhetorical padding; it is the finding of the report. This is not a community that errs occasionally. It is a community whose errors have reached the intensive form and that has built institutions to transmit them.

**Not a Claim About Blood**

The prediction that they will beget only transgressors and deniers has been read by some as a statement about descent, and the reading should be refused rather than softened.

The Qur'an's rule on inheritance of guilt is stated without exception: *“No one will reap except what they sow. No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another.”* (6:164; cf. 53:38). No person is condemned for a parent's posture, and Noah's own son provides the sharpest illustration in the whole story — a prophet's child, drowned, with God telling the prophet that he is not of his family because his conduct was unrighteous (11:46). Lineage did not save the prophet's son, and lineage is not what condemns anyone else.

What the verse describes is an environment, not a genome. A child born into a society where the elite enforce idolatry by command, where leaving is prohibited, and where the only prophet available has been beaten out of the public square will be formed by that society. The prediction is about formation. It is the statement of a man who has watched the mechanism produce the same output for a thousand years and who has no reason to expect a different output from the same machine.

The Qur'an itself confirms the description of the input rather than the blood: the Flood overtook them *while they persisted in wrongdoing* (29:14). Persistence is a chosen posture, and it is the posture that gets transmitted.

**A Prophet's Knowledge and Its Limits**

The confidence of the prediction raises a question the verse answers implicitly: how does Noah know? He is not told the future in this passage, and the Qur'an is emphatic that the unseen belongs to God alone.

The answer is that the prediction is drawn from experience of the most extreme kind. Nine and a half centuries of the same behavior, escalating through every method of appeal, produces knowledge of a sort that no shorter observation can — not certainty about individuals, but certainty about a settled pattern. The sūrah frames the prayer as the conclusion of that observation, and God's response confirms it by acting.

The confirmation is important because it separates this petition from the ordinary human desire to see an enemy end. A man who wants an enemy destroyed says whatever he believes will justify it. A prophet whose petition is answered has reported something true, and the truth he reported is about the future conduct of a group rather than about its current guilt. The verse is thus a document about moral prediction: what a settled society will do next, known by a man who has watched it long enough to see the pattern complete itself many times over.

## Sūrah Nūḥ 71:28

> My Lord! Forgive me, my parents, and whoever enters my house in faith, and ˹all˺ believing men and women. And increase the wrongdoers only in destruction.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Requests for Mercy and One for Ruin**

The sūrah's last verse is a prayer in five parts, and the proportions are the point. Noah asks forgiveness for himself, for his parents, for whoever enters his house in faith, and for the believing men and believing women. Only then, in the final clause, does he ask that the wrongdoers be increased in destruction. Four petitions for mercy, one for ruin, and the ruin comes last.

That order is the answer to any reader who has been unsettled by verse twenty-six. The harshest prayer in the prophetic accounts is not the shape of Noah's inner life. It is one clause in a prayer dominated by requests for pardon, and the requests for pardon are ordered from the nearest to the widest: himself, his parents, the people of his house, then every believing man and woman who will ever live.

The expansion is what gives the verse its lasting use. A prayer that moved from the self outward through four concentric circles until it reached the whole community of believers is a prayer that any believer can say, and the tradition has treated it exactly that way — these words are among the supplications recited for the forgiveness of others. The final clause is not addressed to a person the supplicant hates. It is addressed to a category, and the category is defined by conduct rather than by name.

**baytī — My House**

The word for the third circle is *bayt*, house, and its reference has been discussed since the earliest generations. Three readings are given. It is Noah's dwelling, the physical home into which a person enters. It is his place of worship, since *bayt* is the ordinary word for a house of God. It is the ark, the enclosed space in which the believers were carried.

Each reading has support and each has a difficulty. The dwelling is the plainest sense and fits the phrase *whoever enters* naturally, but it narrows the prayer to visitors. The place of worship fits the religious use of *bayt* and explains why entry would be connected with faith, but it imports a sense the word does not usually carry without a qualifier. The ark explains the survival of the believers and matches the sūrah's silence about it — a silence that would then be broken only here, obliquely.

The first reading is the strongest, and it is strengthened rather than weakened by its plainness. Noah prays for whoever enters his house as a believer, which in the context of a man whose family includes a drowned son and whose society is under a plot is a prayer defined by faith alone. Blood does not qualify a person for it. The son of the prophet did not enter that house in faith and is therefore not included, which is why the prayer needs no exception clause.

**My Parents**

The prayer includes Noah's father and mother, and the inclusion is reported in the tradition as evidence that both were believers. The point is not genealogical but doctrinal, and it sits beside two other cases in the Qur'an that make the same argument from the opposite direction.

Abraham asked forgiveness for his father and then stopped, because it had become clear that his father was an enemy of God: seeking forgiveness for the polytheists is not proper for a prophet or for believers even when the person is a close relative (9:113). Noah's own wife is named among the examples set for the disbelievers — married to a righteous servant, betraying him, availed nothing by the marriage, and told to enter the Fire (66:10). Two of the closest relations a prophet can have are recorded as lost.

Against that background the inclusion of Noah's parents is significant. It shows that the boundary of the prayer is belief and not kinship, in both directions: parents who believed are inside it, and a son who did not is outside it. The sūrah thereby answers in advance any suspicion that its closing petition is tribal. The categories are not family and enemy. They are faith and its refusal.

**The Widest Circle**

The fourth request is for the believing men and the believing women, with no restriction of time, place, or relation. A man whose mission produced almost no converts in nine and a half centuries prays for every believer who will exist after him.

The scope is remarkable and it is not incidental. Noah is described in the tradition as the first messenger sent to the people of the earth, and humanity after the flood descends from those carried with him — a title the Qur'an gives him directly: *“˹O˺ descendants of those We carried with Noah ˹in the Ark˺! He was indeed a grateful servant.”* (17:3). In the report of the intercession on the Day of Resurrection, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3340 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 194, the assembled humanity goes to Adam, is turned away, and then goes to Noah with those words: you are the first of the messengers to the people of the earth.

A prayer from that man for all believing men and women is a prayer that reaches every believer who has ever lived after the flood. The sūrah ends by placing its prophet in the position of an ancestor interceding for his whole descendants, defined not by blood but by faith. The narrowest verse in the story — a man alone with a hostile town — closes into the widest one.

**tabārā — The Last Word Is Ruin**

The final clause asks that the wrongdoers be increased in nothing but *tabār*, destruction, perdition, the state of a thing ruined beyond use. The root b-w-r denotes land left fallow and wasted, a thing that has come to nothing, and it is used in the Qur'an for the ruin of a people and of their works.

The word is chosen for its finality. Noah does not ask for their punishment in a general term, and he does not ask for their humiliation or their suffering. He asks for *tabār*, the exhaustion of a thing's substance — and the sūrah has already shown what that looks like: drowning, then fire, then no helpers found (71:25).

The closing symmetry is exact. The sūrah began with a warning about a painful punishment to come (71:1), and it ends with a prayer that the punishment be complete. Between the two, everything that could have prevented it was tried and refused. The last word is therefore not cruel. It is accurate: the file is closed, the verdict has been given, and the man who spent a millennium arguing for mercy asks that the case against the refusers be finished. The reader is left with the two circles side by side — forgiveness for every believer who enters, ruin for those who would not — and with the knowledge that the door was open for nine hundred and fifty years.
