# Sūrah Nūḥ (Chapter 71) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**A Story Told in Pieces, and Why**

The Qur'an returns to Noah in at least nine other places, and no two accounts are the same: 7:59–64, 10:71–73, 11:25–48, 21:76–77, 23:23–30, 26:105–121, 29:14–15, 37:75–82, and 54:9–15. Some give the dialogue with the chiefs, some the building of the vessel, some the drowning of the son, some the rain and the oven. Together they do not add up to a continuous chronicle, and they are not meant to. The Qur'an states its own reason for telling prophet stories at all: *“And We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ the stories of the messengers to reassure your heart. And there has come to you in this ˹sûrah˺ the truth, a warning ˹to the disbelievers˺, and a reminder to the believers.”* (11:120). The function is guidance, not history, and each account selects the phase of the story that serves the audience hearing it.

This sūrah selects the phase of exhaustion. Noah has called day and night, openly and privately, for a thousand years less fifty (29:14). Every method has been tried. The audience has answered with stopped ears, covered heads, an organized plot, and enforced idolatry. What remains is a report filed with God and a petition for the case to be closed. A Makkan listener in the years of the boycott and the persecution heard his own situation described in the oldest voice available.

**Its Place Between Two Messengers**

Sūrah Nūḥ sits immediately before Sūrah al-Jinn, and the pairing is not accidental. The tradition reports Noah as the first messenger sent to the people of the earth — the title given to him by humanity itself in the intercession narration recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3340 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 194 — while al-Jinn reports a delegation of non-human listeners arriving at the last messenger's recitation. The first chapter of the pair describes a mission that ends in total refusal and a destroyed town; the second describes a mission whose audience includes beings the preacher never saw, who believed on hearing a single recitation.

Both chapters also carry the same promise in the same image: abundant water given to a people on condition that they hold to the way (71:11; 72:16). Read together, the two sūrahs set out the two possible outcomes of one message — the society that refuses for a millennium and is removed from the earth, and the listeners who hear once and turn. The warning of the first is made intelligible by the hope of the second, and the sequence places the Prophet ﷺ, who was being told both stories in Makkah, between the first messenger and a mission that had not yet reached its audience.

## Verse 71:1

> Indeed, We sent Noah to his people ˹saying to him˺, “Warn your people before a painful punishment comes to them.”

**The Identical Charge Given to the Last Messenger**

Noah is not commissioned alone in this way, and the sūrah's first audience knew it. The same command to warn, before a punishment arrives, is issued to Muḥammad ﷺ throughout the Makkan revelation: *“˹This is˺ a Book sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺— do not let anxiety into your heart regarding it—so with it you may warn ˹the disbelievers˺. As a reminder to the believers.”* (7:2); *“And warn the people of the Day when the punishment will overtake ˹the wicked among˺ them”* (14:44). *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘I warn you only by revelation.’ But the deaf cannot hear the call when they are warned!”* (21:45); *“Warn them ˹O Prophet˺ of the approaching Day when the hearts will jump into the throats, suppressing distress.”* (40:18).

The parallel is the point of telling the story at all. The messengers are one line of work, and the work is warning. Stories of the prophets are not antiquarian material in the Qur'an; they are the résumé of the office. *“And We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ the stories of the messengers to reassure your heart.”* (11:120). A man being called a madman in Makkah hears that a man was called the same thing for nine and a half centuries, and that the second man was right.

**Painful, but Not Yet Described**

The punishment is named by its quality — *alīm*, painful — and by nothing else. No water, no sky opening, no oven boiling over. The reader who knows the story from other sūrahs supplies the scene; this one withholds it. When the drowning finally appears it is half a clause: they were drowned because of their sins (71:25).

That economy is a choice, not an omission. Compare the accounts that do stage the event: *“So We opened the gates of the sky with pouring rain,”* (54:11) followed by the vessel of planks and nails (54:13), or the detailed instructions of 11:37–44 where the ark is built under divine direction and the oven bursts. Those passages make the catastrophe visible. This one keeps it abstract on purpose, so that attention stays where the sūrah wants it: on the warning, on the response to it, and on the interior of a man who keeps talking to people who keep leaving. The adjective *alīm* also does quiet work. It describes the punishment as felt — pain located in a body and a heart — which raises the stakes of the warning without staging a spectacle. The reader is told enough to fear and nothing at all to be entertained.

**The First Messenger, and a Title He Shares**

Islamic tradition places Noah at the head of the prophetic line sent to the earth. In the long report of the intercession on the Day of Resurrection, recorded in both Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3340 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 194 from Abū Hurayrah, humanity goes to Adam, is turned away. Then goes to Noah with these words: you are the first of the messengers to the people of the earth, and God named you a grateful servant. The name is scriptural: *“˹O˺ descendants of those We carried with Noah ˹in the Ark˺! He was indeed a grateful servant.”* (17:3).

Two things follow for this sūrah. First, Noah belongs to the group the Qur'an calls the messengers of firm resolve: *“So endure patiently, as did the Messengers of Firm Resolve.”* (46:35). Second, the whole human population after the flood descends from him, so the address in verse one — *his people* — is, in a sense, everyone. The sūrah that opens with a warning to one town is the oldest warning on record for the species that survived it.

## Verse 71:2

> Noah proclaimed, “O my people! I am truly sent to you with a clear warning

**The Warner Introduces Himself Before He Warns**

Noah's first public act is not the warning itself but a statement about who is speaking: I am a warner sent to you, and the warning is clear. He establishes the office before the content, because the content will be unbearable unless the authority behind it is settled. A stranger shouting about punishment is a nuisance; an envoy with credentials is a matter of state.

The self-introduction is a standing feature of prophetic speech in the Qur'an. Noah says the same thing in Hūd: *“Indeed, I am sent to you with a clear warning”* (11:25), and again in al-Shuʿarāʾ: *“I am only sent with a clear warning.”* (26:115). The formula does two jobs at once. It claims a commission from God, and it disclaims everything else: not a miracle worker on demand, not a ruler, not a fortune teller. Only a clear warner.

**"O My People" — Kinship Retained While Delivering a Threat**

This is why the address keeps returning across the prophetic accounts, and always at the hardest moments. Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, Shuʿayb and Mūsā all open with it; Noah uses it here and again in the complaint of verse twenty-one and in the sermon of verse ten. The Qur'an elsewhere draws out the psychology of the audience who hear it: his people's answer is that he is only a human like them, and that only the lowliest follow him (11:27), and the chiefs tell the crowd he wants to be superior to them (23:24). Kinship is precisely what makes the warning hard to swallow. A stranger's threat can be dismissed; a brother's threat has to be answered. By holding on to the word *qawm*, Noah refuses to let the conversation become a debate between opponents. He keeps it a family matter, and he keeps himself accountable to it — his grief in the verses that follow is the grief of a man whose own people are walking away.

**Every Community Has Had Its Warner**

The claim Noah makes about himself is not a personal privilege but an instance of a general law. The Qur'an states that law without exception: a warner has come to every people. *“We have never destroyed a society without warners”* (26:208); *“We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, ‘Worship Allah and shun false gods.’”* (16:36); *“You ˹O Prophet˺ are only a warner. And every people had a guide.”* (13:7).

Muḥammad ﷺ is told to say it in his own voice over and over — *“What is revealed to me is that I am only sent with a clear warning.”* (38:70); *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ ‘O humanity! I am only sent to you with a clear warning.’”* (22:49); *“Signs are only with Allah. And I am only sent with a clear warning.”* (29:50); *“That knowledge is with Allah alone, and I am only sent with a clear warning.”* (67:26).

That is why the same adjective is attached to the Book itself, to signs, and to the messengers: *“We have surely sent you with the truth as a deliverer of good news and a warner. There is no community that has not had a warner.”* (35:24).

## Verse 71:3

> worship Allah ˹alone˺, fear Him, and obey me

**ʿIbādah — Worship as Exclusive Service**

The verb is *uʿbudū*, from ʿ-b-d, the same root as *ʿabd*, servant. Worship in Qur'anic Arabic is not mainly an emotion or a ritual performance; it is the condition of being owned and of acting accordingly. The bracketed *˹alone˺* in the translation is doing real work, since the sentence is addressed to a people who already worshipped, and worshipped a great deal — they simply did not restrict it.

That is the standard shape of the prophetic quarrel. Noah's people were not atheists, and neither were the Quraysh. The dispute is about exclusivity, which is why the same call is reported in the other accounts with the second half spelled out: *“O my people! Worship Allah ˹alone˺. You have no god other than Him.”* (23:23), and *“O my people! Worship Allah—you have no other god except Him. I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous Day.”* (7:59). Service divided between several masters is not partial service; it is no service at all, because each master is obeyed only when convenient. The demand for exclusive ʿibādah is therefore a demand for a life with a single ordering principle — one authority that cannot be traded against another when the two disagree.

**Taqwā — Fear That Functions as Care**

*Ittaqūhu* is the imperative of the same root as *taqwā*, usually rendered as fear, God-consciousness, or mindfulness. The literal sense of the root w-q-y is to shield oneself, to put something between you and harm. A man with taqwā has taken cover.

This is why "fear" is not quite the right English word and yet survives translation. The fear in question is not terror at a tyrant; it is the alertness of someone who knows where the edge is and does not want to fall over it. It is the disposition of a person who expects consequences. Al-Rāzī's triad needs this middle term because worship can be performed externally by a man whose heart is elsewhere, and obedience can be imitated by a man who has not accepted that anything is at stake. Taqwā is what makes both real. It is also the faculty Noah's people lacked, and the sūrah names that lack later with a rhetorical question about majesty and awe (71:13). The connection between the two verses is direct: he tells them to fear God, they respond by refusing to hold Him in weight, and the diagnosis of the whole story is that the missing organ was never knowledge. It was reverence.

**Obey Me — The Messenger as the Practical Form of the Message**

The third command is the one that provokes. It asks for obedience to a human being, and the Qur'an anticipates the objection by recording it: his people say he is only a man like them who wants to be superior (23:24). The chiefs insist they will not believe in a man followed only by the lowest of the low (26:111).

Put the third item first and you have a cult of the leader, which the Qur'an explicitly refuses: *“It is not appropriate for someone who Allah has blessed with the Scripture, wisdom. Prophethood to say to people, ‘Worship me instead of Allah.’ Rather, he would say, ‘Be devoted to the worship of your Lord ˹alone˺’”* (3:79).

## Verse 71:4

> He will forgive your sins, and delay your end until the appointed time. Indeed, when the time set by Allah comes, it cannot be delayed, if only you knew!”

**Delay Until a Term Appointed**

*Yu'akhkhirkum ilā ajalin musammā* — He will postpone you to a named term. *Ajal* is the fixed span allotted to a person or a people; *musammā* adds that it is specified, written, and known to the One who wrote it. The blessing is therefore not longevity in the abstract but the completion of a span.

This is a distinctively Qur'anic idea, and it appears wherever the subject of time and judgment comes up. Nations have terms as individuals do: *“For each community there is an appointed term. When their time arrives, they can neither delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it.”* (7:34); the same sentence is repeated in 10:49. God's forbearance is described in the same vocabulary: *“If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for what they have committed, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term.”* (16:61; cf. 35:45). The commentators differ on what the term means in Noah's offer — an individual's natural death, the arrival of a punishment in this world, the end of the age, or the Day of Judgment — and the disagreement is not resolvable from the text. What all the readings share is more important than what separates them: the span is fixed, it is named, and it can be reached rather than truncated.

**The Clock That Cannot Be Moved**

The structure is deliberate. Postponement is available now, on a condition; postponement will not be available then, at any price. The two halves of the verse describe one clock with two settings. While the offer stands, time is the mercy — it is the room in which a decision can still be made. Once the term arrives, time is the sentence. This is the doctrine the Qur'an presses against every audience that assumes it has more years coming: *“Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment.”* (3:178). Delay is neutral. What it produces depends completely on the person holding it, and the sūrah's own ending shows the alternative outcome in the starkest form — drowning, then Fire (71:25). The difference between the two outcomes was not the amount of time. Both parties had time.

**If Only You Knew**

That placement is consistent with everything the sūrah says about this people. They are not described as weak, oppressed, deceived by a stronger power, or born into an impossible situation. They are described as refusing: fingers in ears, garments pulled over the head, persistence, arrogance (71:7). The conditional is counterfactual and slightly mournful — the knowledge they lacked was available to them, and its absence is not a mitigating fact but part of the charge. The Qur'an uses the same rebuke repeatedly: the signs are made plain for people of knowledge (10:5), and hearts grow blind in the chests (22:46). Noah's sermon ends its opening movement with that diagnosis. The offer is real, the deadline is fixed, and the only thing standing between this audience and both is a failure to know what it already has every means of knowing.

## Verse 71:5

> He cried, “My Lord! I have surely called my people day and night

**The Sermon Turns Into a Report**

Verses two through four are Noah speaking to his people. This verse is Noah speaking to God, and the change of addressee changes everything about the sentence. The words *qāla rabbi*, he said "My Lord," mark the moment the mission stops being an argument and becomes a matter of record. He is no longer persuading. He is reporting.

That shift is not a sign of defeat but of procedure. A messenger sent with a commission reports back to the one who sent him. The report has a form: what I did, how long I did it, and what happened. The first element arrives here — I called them day and night — and the rest follows in the next verses, ending with the prayer for destruction in verses twenty-six through twenty-eight. Between the opening report and the final prayer lies the whole shape of a failed mission, told to the only party who can act on it.

The address also fixes the relationship. *Rabb* is the word for the one who sustains, educates, and owns the outcome. Noah does not say "God, judge them." He says "my Lord," the language of a servant inside a household, and he keeps that language even in the harshest verse of the sūrah.

**Nine Hundred and Fifty Years Behind One Sentence**

The brevity of the report is measured against the length of the mission. The Qur'an gives the number elsewhere: *“Indeed, We sent Noah to his people, and he remained among them for a thousand years, less fifty. Then the Flood overtook them, while they persisted in wrongdoing.”* (29:14). Nine and a half centuries of calling compressed into five words.

That compression is itself a statement about prophetic labor. The visible result of the mission — a handful of believers, one drowned nation, one boat — is tiny compared with the time spent. The Qur'an does not treat this disproportion as a failure of the messenger. It treats it as the normal condition of the office: the work is measured by faithfulness to the commission, not by the size of the crowd. Noah is later honored in the tradition precisely on this ground. In the report of the intercession, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3340 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 194 from Abū Hurayrah, humanity addresses him as the first of the messengers to the people of the earth and reminds him that God named him a grateful servant — the same title given in the Qur'an at 17:3. The man who spent a millennium on a mission that produced almost no converts is the one humanity goes to second on the Day of Resurrection.

**Calling Is Measured by Delivery, Not Response**

The verse states an action completed: I called them. Whether they listened is a separate fact, reported separately, and it does not subtract from the calling. This division of labor is stated as doctrine for the last messenger: *“O Messenger! Convey everything revealed to you from your Lord. If you do not, then you have not delivered His message.”* (5:67), and *“But if they turn away, We have not sent you ˹O Prophet˺ as a keeper over them. Your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺.”* (42:48).

## Verse 71:6

> but my calls only made them run farther away

**Increase in the Wrong Direction**

The construction is one the Qur'an returns to whenever it describes the paradox of revelation: the very thing sent to guide increases distance. *“We have surely varied ˹the signs˺ in this Quran so perhaps they may be mindful, but it only drives them farther away.”* (17:41). The same verb of increase appears for those with sickness in the heart — *“it has increased them only in wickedness upon their wickedness”* (9:125) — and for the community that swore it would be better guided than any other if a warner came, then found the warner only *“drove them farther away”* (35:42). When the Quraysh are told to prostrate to the Most Compassionate, the effect is identical: *“And it only drives them farther away.”* (25:60).

The pattern is not a defect in the message but a property of the receiver, and the Qur'an states it as a law rather than as a complaint. Light does not cause blindness; it reveals it. A person who has already decided not to move gains nothing from an additional sign except one more thing to refuse, and the refusal compounds. A sign given to a person who has decided not to move supplies him with one more thing to refuse, and each refusal hardens the next. The Qur'an states the hardening as an act of God — *“We can punish them ˹too˺ for their sins and seal their hearts so they will not hear ˹the truth˺”* (7:100) — and the sealing always follows the choice, never precedes it.

**Flight From Forgiveness Specifically**

The verse that follows makes explicit what the object of the flight was: whenever I invite them *so that You may forgive them*, they block their ears (71:7). The running is away from pardon.

This deserves to be held in view, because it reverses the ordinary account of religious resistance. The standard explanation for unbelief is that people fear punishment and flee toward pleasure. Here the punishment is what is offered as the alternative, and the pardon is what is fled. Noah's call was not mainly a threat; it was an amnesty, and the amnesty was refused. The reason given in the surrounding verses is pride — they persisted and became arrogant (71:7) — and pride explains the direction of the flight accurately. A man can accept a warning from a superior. He cannot accept a pardon without admitting that he needed one, and the admission costs him the position he has built. The elites of Noah's people had wealth, children, and a religious establishment of ancestral statues (71:21, 71:23). Forgiveness would have required dismantling all three.

**The Honest Arithmetic of a Failed Mission**

This honesty is one of the Qur'an's most distinctive habits. It records its messengers losing. The people of Noah deny him, call him insane, and intimidate him: *“Before them, the people of Noah denied ˹the truth˺ and rejected Our servant, calling ˹him˺ insane. And he was intimidated.”* (54:9). The verse does not resolve the humiliation before reporting the outcome. A book concerned only with winning would have cut that clause. The presence of the loss is what makes the report in this verse credible, and it is what allows the sūrah to be read by anyone whose own work has produced nothing — which is most people, most of the time.

## Verse 71:7

> And whenever I invite them to be forgiven by You, they press their fingers into their ears, cover themselves with their clothes, persist ˹in denial˺, and act very arrogantly

**The Body Refuses Before the Mind Objects**

Something important happens in the space between the invitation and the gesture. There is no deliberation reported, no pause for consideration, no question asked. The hearing of the offer triggers a physical response.

This is exactly the pattern the Qur'an describes elsewhere when it accounts for why signs fail. *“We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to understand it—and deafness in their ears. And when you mention your Lord alone in the Quran, they turn their backs in aversion.”* (17:46). The veils and the deafness are presented as consequences of a prior turning, and the turning is described in bodily language again: backs turned, ears stopped, hearts covered. In al-Fuṣṣilat the audience says the same thing in its own voice: *“Our hearts are veiled against what you are calling us to, there is deafness in our ears, and there is a barrier between us and you.”* (41:5). They are describing themselves with Noah's people's gestures, nine centuries later, in a different city, using the same hands.

**The Three Faculties, All Closed**

Noah's report names two of the three explicitly and the third implicitly. The ears are stopped. The eyes are covered by the garments. The heart appears in the final word, arrogance, since arrogance in Qur'anic usage is not a feeling about oneself but a disposition of the heart that refuses to accept what it recognizes as true. The catalogue elsewhere is blunt: *“Indeed, We have destined many jinn and humans for Hell. They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with. They are like cattle. In fact, they are even less guided!”* (7:179). And to the destroyed peoples God gave all three instruments and got nothing from them: *“We gave them hearing, sight, and intellect. But neither their hearing, sight, nor intellect were of any benefit to them whatsoever, since they persisted in denying Allah’s signs.”* (46:26).

The parallel between *persisted* in that verse and *persist* in this one is the closest verbal link between Noah's people and the Makkan audience, and it is the link the sūrah wants.

**An Invitation to Forgiveness, Not to Debate**

The offer of forgiveness is also the first item in the sermon that follows, where Noah tells them to seek pardon and is answered with the promise of rain, wealth, children, and gardens (71:10–12). The whole middle of the sūrah is an elaboration of this clause: what forgiveness brings, and why it was worth hearing.

## Verse 71:8

> Then I certainly called them openly

**jihāran — Loud Enough to Be Undeniable**

The adverb *jihāran* derives from a root meaning to be manifest, to appear, to be audible. It describes an action performed in the open, without concealment, in a voice that cannot be mistaken for a whisper. The English "openly" catches the exposure; the Arabic adds the volume.

That combination is what makes the call a formal act. A warning delivered quietly can be denied — he never said it, he said something else, we misunderstood. A warning shouted in a gathering place becomes a matter of public record, and the record is what will be produced on the Day. The Qur'an makes the same move for the last messenger: *“O Messenger! Convey everything revealed to you from your Lord. If you do not, then you have not delivered His message.”* (5:67). Delivery, in that verse and in this one, has an audible dimension. The message must arrive in a form the recipient cannot plausibly claim not to have received.

*jihār* also implies a certain exposure of the speaker. To call openly is to be seen calling, and to be seen calling in a hostile town is to become a target.

**The Prophet's Own Open Summons**

The precedent is recorded in detail for the last messenger, and it is the same movement from the private circle to the public rock. When the command to warn his closest relatives came down — *“And warn ˹all, starting with˺ your closest relatives,”* (26:214) — Ibn ʿAbbās reports that the Prophet ﷺ went out, climbed Mount Ṣafā, and shouted the cry used to summon a town to danger: *yā ṣabāḥāh*. The clans of Quraysh gathered, and he addressed them branch by branch, telling each that he could avail them nothing against God. The report is in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3525, and the same event is narrated by Abū Hurayrah in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4771 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 206, where the summons opens with the words: O assembly of Quraysh, purchase your souls from God, for I cannot avail you anything before Him.

**The Price of Going Public**

Open calling removes the excuse of ignorance and simultaneously removes the caller's protection. Once the warning is public, the refusal must be public too, and a public refusal by men of standing turns into a public campaign.

The other accounts of this mission record the escalation that follows. Noah's people tell him plainly: *“If you do not desist, O Noah, you will surely be stoned ˹to death˺.”* (26:116). The chiefs declare that they see him as clearly misguided (7:60), and the argument they use against him is social rather than religious — he is only a man like them, and only the lowest of people follow him (11:27; 26:111). This is the ordinary sequence: a messenger begins quietly, is refused, goes public, and is then treated as a threat to the social order rather than as a man with an argument.

The sūrah reports the plot against him in the second half of the story (71:22), and the plot only makes sense after the public phase. Men do not conspire against a voice they can ignore. They conspire against a voice the whole town has heard.

## Verse 71:9

> then I surely preached to them publicly and privately

**isrārā — The Cognate Accusative and Its Weight**

The Arabic closes the verse with a grammatical intensification: *asrartu lahum isrārā*, I spoke to them secretly, with a secrecy. The noun from the same root as the verb is used to reinforce it, a construction Arabic reserves for actions performed thoroughly and without reservation.

**Private Counsel Removes the Cost of Listening**

Why does the private channel matter so much that the verse gives it an intensifying noun? Because of shame.

A man who begins to be persuaded in public has a problem. His peers are watching, and the moment he softens he loses standing with them. The Qur'an records the social mechanism precisely: the chiefs tell the crowd that only the lowest people follow the messenger (26:111), and the followers themselves will say on the Day that they were kept back by their leaders — *“Had it not been for you, we would certainly have been believers.”* (34:31). Persuasion in the open is therefore contested by an audience with a stake in the outcome. Persuasion in private is not.

Away from the crowd, the same person can hear the argument without immediately calculating what accepting it will cost him in front of his friends. He can ask a question without conceding a position. Noah's private work was the only setting in which that was possible, and the sūrah's insistence that he did it — thoroughly — means the eventual destruction cannot be attributed to an audience that was never given a chance to listen without witnesses.

**The Same Method in the Makkan Preaching**

The pattern is visible in the sīrah of the last messenger, whose preaching moved through recognizable stages: the earliest years worked through kinship and trusted individuals, then came the open summons from Mount Ṣafā after the revelation of the command to warn his closest relatives (26:214), recorded by Ibn ʿAbbās in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3525. After that the two modes continued side by side — public recitation at the Kaʿbah and private instruction in houses such as that of al-Arqam.

The pairing of registers is what makes the Qur'anic account of Noah useful to a reader in any century, and it is what the verse reports with an intensifying noun rather than a passing phrase. Preaching is not a single act performed at a single volume. It has a public register that establishes the claim, and a private register that carries it into an individual life. Noah's report insists that he worked both to exhaustion, and the insistence is part of the evidence the sūrah builds before the verdict it will eventually request.

## Verse 71:10

> saying, ‘Seek your Lord’s forgiveness, ˹for˺ He is truly Most Forgiving

**ghafūr — The Name Set Beside the Command**

The verse gives the reason for the command in a divine name: *innahu kāna ghaffūrā*, He is truly Most Forgiving. The name is in the intensive form, meaning a quality that is abundant and habitual rather than occasional.

**The Identical Words in Hūd's Mouth**

Noah's opening is not his alone. Centuries later, in the same region, the prophet Hūd says almost the same sentence to the people of ʿĀd: *“And O my people! Seek your Lord’s forgiveness and turn to Him in repentance. He will shower you with rain in abundance, and add strength to your strength. So do not turn away, persisting in wickedness.”* (11:52).

There is one difference worth holding. Hūd adds *wa-tūbū ilayhi*, and turn to Him — repentance beside forgiveness. Noah's version, as the sūrah reports it, begins with the asking alone and lets the consequences speak for the turning. The two are not in tension: in Qur'anic usage asking for forgiveness and turning are the same movement described from two ends. But Hūd's audience had already been given strength and stature, and his phrasing addresses men who needed to be told to come back. Noah's audience needed to be told they could be cleaned.

---

## Verse 71:11

> He will shower you with abundant rain

**midrār — Abundance as Repetition**

The adjective translated *abundant* is *midrār*, from a root whose basic sense is to flow freely and continuously — it is used of an animal giving milk in a steady stream and of clouds that return again and again with rain. The form means an action repeated and sustained rather than a single large quantity.

The same root and sense appear in Hūd's promise to ʿĀd — He will send rain upon you in abundance (11:52) — which makes the pairing a recognized formula in prophetic preaching. Wherever the Qur'an describes divine provision arriving from the sky, the emphasis falls on recurrence: the sky with its cycles (86:11), the rain that revives a dead land again and again (30:19). Provision in the Qur'anic picture is not an event. It is a rhythm, and repentance is what restores the rhythm.

**The Sky as a Metonym, and the Blessing as Life**

That distinction was live for Noah's audience and is live for the Qur'an's first readers, both of whom lived in religious systems that attached agency to celestial objects and to intermediaries. The verse quietly removes the agency from the sky and places it with the One who forgives. The Qur'an elsewhere makes the same move explicitly, listing rain among the signs and refusing to let it be read as anything but a deliberate act: the rain sent down from the skies, reviving the earth after its death, is named among the signs for people of understanding (2:164).

The blessing is also described in life rather than comfort. Reviving a dead land is the Qur'an's standing image for both provision and resurrection — *“There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat.”* (36:33). A promise of abundant rain, in that symbolic world, is a promise that what has died in this community will live again: the soil, the herds, and by extension the people themselves.

**Blessing Presented as Conditional**

The promise is attached to an act. Seek forgiveness, and the sky will be sent with abundance. This conditional structure is a general rule in the Qur'an, stated for other peoples in the same terms: *“Had the people of those societies been faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, We would have overwhelmed them with blessings from heaven and earth. But they disbelieved, so We seized them for what they used to commit.”* (7:96). The same condition is put to the People of the Book: *“And had they observed the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to them from their Lord, they would have been overwhelmed with provisions from above and below.”* (5:66).

Three features of the rule are worth stating plainly. It is conditional, not automatic — the blessing depends on the posture of the community. It is corporate, not only individual — whole towns are described as recipients. And it is reversible — the seizure in the second half of 7:96 is the same event described from the other side. Nothing in the rule guarantees that a prosperous community is an obedient one; that error is what the teaching about gradual drawing-on exists to correct (68:44). What the rule does guarantee is that obedience opens a channel, and that the channel has been open to every people that ever turned.

## Verse 71:12

> supply you with wealth and children, and give you gardens as well as rivers

**Wealth and Sons: The Two Measures of a Man**

In the society addressed here, a man's standing rested on two things: what he owned and how many sons stood behind him. The Qur'an names the pairing constantly, usually to unsettle it. *“So let neither their wealth nor children impress you ˹O Prophet˺. Allah only intends to torment them through these things in this worldly life, then their souls will depart while they are disbelievers.”* (9:55, and the near-identical 9:85). The believers are warned directly: *“O believers! Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah. For whoever does so, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers.”* (63:9).

The Qur'an's own summary of the two is blunt: *“Your wealth and children are only a test, but Allah ˹alone˺ has a great reward.”* (64:15; cf. 8:28). The test and the gift are the same object seen from two ends.

**Desire Redirected Rather Than Denied**

The sermon does not ask this audience to want less. It asks them to want correctly — to look for the goods they already desire in the right direction.

That is a distinctive feature of Qur'anic preaching and it deserves to be stated plainly, because the alternative reading is common and wrong. The message is not that wealth is base and that the pious should be poor. The message is that wealth has an owner, that the owner is generous, and that generosity is conditional. A man who wants a garden is not being rebuked for wanting it. He is being told who makes gardens and what the maker asks in return.

The same structure appears throughout the Book. God describes Himself as the one who provides, and the response required is gratitude rather than renunciation. The danger the Qur'an identifies is not possession but attribution — the man who says, as the owner of two gardens says in al-Kahf, that his property will never perish and that he is superior in wealth and numbers (18:34; cf. 34:35). Noah's offer is aimed precisely at preventing that sentence from being true of his people, by attaching the goods to the giver at the moment they are given.

**A Real Promise, and a Real Hazard**

Two readings of the promise run through the tradition, and both are defensible. One takes it as a straightforward assurance: repent, and the coming years will be better than the last. The other takes it as a warning in the form of an offer: God gives provision to test the recipient, and increase can be the instrument of destruction rather than its opposite — *“When they became oblivious to warnings, We showered them with everything they desired. But just as they became prideful of what they were given, We seized them by surprise.”* (6:44).

The same image serves as the description of the reward in the Hereafter — gardens with rivers running beneath them (2:25) — and the double use is deliberate.

## Verse 71:13

> What is the matter with you that you are not in awe of the Majesty of Allah

**The Sermon Turns From Offer to Diagnosis**

Up to this point Noah has been speaking about what his people will receive. Now he asks a question about what they are missing, and the question is the pivot of the whole speech. The shift is from provision to perception: the problem with this community is not that God has been stingy but that they have no sense of His weight.

The form of the question — *mā lakum*, what is the matter with you — is diagnostic rather than merely rhetorical. It is the question a physician asks, not the question an accuser asks. Something is wrong with the patient and the physician wants to know what. The Qur'an uses the same opening elsewhere in exactly that register: *“How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life.”* (2:28). In both cases the question is followed by evidence, and in both the evidence is drawn from the person asked.

The diagnosis lands on a specific deficit. Noah does not say they are ignorant of God's existence; his audience plainly believes there is a God, since he invites them to be forgiven by Him. He does not say they are ignorant of His power. He says they do not hold Him in the weight He has. The failure is in the estimation, and an error in estimation explains every other behavior reported in the sūrah — the fingers in the ears, the covered heads, the persistence, the arrogance (71:7).

The verb in the question is *tarjūna*, from r-j-w, whose ordinary meaning is to hope or to expect. The commentators split over how it functions here, and the split produces two quite different sentences.

**Awe as the Faculty the Whole Sūrah Misses**

The reason supplied here is that the equipment for receiving evidence was misaligned. Awe is not an emotion added to knowledge; it is the posture that makes knowledge count. A man without it can be shown a fact and register it without being changed by it, which is the behavior the Qur'an describes when it says that hearing, sight, and intellect were given to destroyed peoples and benefited them not at all (46:26). The faculties were present. The weighting was wrong.

---

## Verse 71:14

> when He truly created you in stages ˹of development˺

**The First Exhibit Is the Body of the Listener**

The sermon's argument for the magnitude of God begins with the person being addressed. Not the sky, not the mountains, not history — your own making. The clause is attached to the question of verse thirteen as its ground: how can you fail to hold God in weight, when He created you in stages?

The Qur'an uses this opening move constantly, and it treats it as the strongest available: *“One of His signs is that He created you from dust, then—behold!—you are human beings spreading over ˹the earth˺.”* (30:20). And the appeal is made explicit in al-Dhāriyāt: signs are in the earth and *“as there are within yourselves. Can you not see?”* (51:21). The self is listed as a sign alongside the world, and the question attached to it is the same question Noah asks — not "do you believe?" but "can you not see?".

**aṭwār — Stages, One Condition After Another**

That double sense is exactly what the verse needs. Human development is a sequence of bounded states, each with its own limit, each giving way to the next. The Qur'an spells the sequence out at length in al-Muʾminūn: from an extract of clay (23:12), to a sperm-drop in a secure place (23:13), to a clinging clot, to a lump of flesh, to bones, to bones clothed with flesh, to a new creation (23:14). The same sequence appears in al-Ḥajj as the opening of an argument about resurrection: *“We did create you from dust, then from a sperm-drop, then ˹developed you into˺ a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then a lump of flesh —fully formed or unformed—to demonstrate ˹Our power˺ to you.”* (22:5). And in Ghāfir the stages continue past birth: infants, prime, old age, and an appointed end (40:67).

Noah's single word compresses all of it. The plural form is doing the argumentative work: not one act of making, but many, ordered, each dependent on the last.

**The Measure in the Womb**

Two features of the staged account deserve emphasis because they carry the religious weight.

The first is that the process happens in concealment. The stages described in al-Muʾminūn take place in a secure lodging (23:13), inside a body, unseen by anyone including the person being made. Nobody witnessed his own formation, and nobody directed it. The Qur'an returns to that fact when it describes the creation of a human being in the wombs of mothers, in threefold darkness, as the work of one Lord (39:6). A process that no one observes and no one controls, yet which produces the same ordered result in every case, is not a process that can be attributed to the person it produces.

The second is that the process includes loss as well as gain. In the Ghāfir account some die young and others are returned to the feebleness of old age, *“so that they may know nothing after having known much”* (40:67). The stages run downhill at the end. A person who is being shown his own development is being shown a curve, not a ladder, and the curve's descent is as much part of the evidence as its ascent. It is the fact that makes the next claim in Noah's sermon — that the same God will return you to the earth and bring you out again — intelligible rather than incredible.

## Verse 71:15

> Do you not see how Allah created seven heavens, one above the other

**The Argument Moves Outward From the Body**

Verse fourteen put the listener inside his own formation. This verse lifts his head. The question *a-lam taraw*, do you not see, is plural now — the whole people are addressed — and the object is the structure overhead.

The movement from the inner sign to the outer one is the standard order of Qur'anic argument, and it is standard because it works. A person who has considered his own staged development is prepared to consider a staged construction; the same maker is being identified by two different objects. The pairing is explicit elsewhere: *“One of His signs is that He created you from dust”* (30:20) sits among signs that include the heavens, the earth, the alternation of night and day, the ships, the rain, the winds (2:164). The self and the cosmos are two pages of one document.

The shift also changes the scale of the audience's problem. A man who refuses to look at his own body can be dismissed as careless. A community that refuses to look at the sky has organized its carelessness, and organized carelessness is what the sūrah has been describing all along: the ears stopped, the heads covered, the persistence, the arrogance (71:7). The question is put to a people who have agreed among themselves not to see.

**The Number Seven**

Seven is the Qur'an's fixed number for the heavens, and it is used without variation: God created seven heavens (2:29), He built above you seven mighty heavens (78:12), the seven heavens and everything in them glorify Him. The consistency rules out the reading that the number is merely an phrase for "many," since Arabic had other idioms for that and the Qur'an uses them elsewhere.

Whether the seven are seven concentric levels, seven distinct orders of reality, or something a modern astronomer would describe differently is a question the text does not settle, and the classical commentators did not pretend it did. What the text requires of the number is that it denote a complete, expressed structure — a cosmos with levels rather than a single dome. The lowest of those levels is the one furnished with the visible lights: *“And indeed, We adorned the lowest heaven with ˹stars like˺ lamps”* (67:5), which places the stars inside the first heaven, not above all of them, and implies further structure beyond what the eye reaches.

**Do You Not See — The Demand for Observation**

The Qur'an addresses people this way when the evidence is already in front of them and the failure is one of attention. It is the form used for the dead earth revived by rain (36:33), for the shade extended and the sun made its guide (25:45), for the ships on the sea (2:164). In each case the object is ordinary and available; the listener is being told that he has been looking at the argument his whole life without registering it.

The same idea appears in the sūrah whose name means Dominion: *“˹He is the One˺ Who created seven heavens, one above the other. You will never see any imperfection in the creation of the Most Compassionate. So look again: do you see any flaws?”* (67:3).

## Verse 71:16

> placing the moon within them as a ˹reflected˺ light, and the sun as a ˹radiant˺ lamp

**Two Lights, Two Words**

The verse assigns each heavenly body its own term, and the distinction is maintained throughout the Qur'an. The moon is given *nūr*, light; the sun is given *ḍiyāʾ*, radiance or blazing light. The clearest statement of the pairing is in Yūnus: *“He is the One Who made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases, so that you may know the number of years and calculation ˹of time˺. Allah did not create all this except for a purpose. He makes the signs clear for people of knowledge.”* (10:5).

The two words are not synonyms being varied for elegance. *Ḍiyāʾ* means light that comes with heat and from a source in itself — the word is used for fire and for the blaze that carries warmth with it. *Nūr* means light that illuminates without burning, light received and passed on. The Qur'an's usage is consistent: the Torah is *nūr*, the Prophet ﷺ is a *sirāj munīr*, a lamp giving light, and the sun alone is *ḍiyāʾ*.

The bracketed insertions in this translation — *˹reflected˺* and *˹radiant˺* — make explicit what the Arabic terms already distinguish. They are not additions to the sense but statements of it, and they are supported by 10:5, where the same two words are used in the same pairing with the same contrast. A reader who objects to the brackets is objecting to a lexical distinction the Qur'an itself draws elsewhere.

**Lights Set Inside the Structure**

The moon and the sun are placed *within* the heavens — the preposition in the Arabic is *fī*, in — and that placement belongs to the description of the layered structure in the previous verse. The lights are not above the seven heavens; they are installed in them, and the Qur'an identifies the lowest heaven as the one adorned with lamps: *“And indeed, We adorned the lowest heaven with ˹stars like˺ lamps, and made them ˹as missiles˺ for stoning ˹eavesdropping˺ devils.”* (67:5).

The regularity of the arrangement is what makes it usable. Each light travels in its own path: *“And He is the One Who created the day and the night, the sun and the moon—each travelling in an orbit.”* (21:33). Orbits that stay orbits are what allow a farming people to plant, to count months, and to keep a calendar. The reliability is the gift.

**Timekeeping as the Stated Purpose**

In the parallel verse in Yūnus the purpose is given outright: the phases are ordained *so that you may know the number of years and calculation of time* (10:5). The lights were not arranged for their own sake but for the sake of creatures on the ground who need to measure duration.

The verse therefore does something more subtle than prove that God exists. It takes an instrument the audience already trusts, already uses, and already depends upon, and reassigns its origin. A person who accepts that the calendar is given has already accepted a giver. The rest of the sermon simply asks him to behave accordingly — which is the meaning of holding God in weight (71:13).

The Qur'an extends the same word to the Prophet ﷺ: *“and a caller to ˹the Way of˺ Allah by His command, and a beacon of light.”* (33:46).

## Verse 71:17

> Allah ˹alone˺ caused you to grow from the earth like a plant

**From Dust: The Material Claim**

The soil is named as the source, and the Qur'an says the same thing in several registers. *“Indeed, the example of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like that of Adam. He created him from dust, then said to him, ‘Be!’ And he was!”* (3:59). Humanity is created from an extract of clay (23:12), from dust and then a sperm-drop (22:5), and the sign is stated in the shortest possible form: *“One of His signs is that He created you from dust, then—behold!—you are human beings spreading over ˹the earth˺.”* (30:20).

That gap is also what defeats the arrogance diagnosed in verse thirteen. A person whose substance is soil has no material basis for self-magnification. The only ground available for dignity is the one the sermon keeps pointing to — that someone chose to grow this particular thing out of this particular ground, and furnished a sky above it and a calendar inside that sky for its use.

**The Metaphor Places Humanity Inside a Cultivated World**

Growing from the earth is not an isolating fact. It puts human beings in the same category as their food, and the Qur'an uses that kinship as an instruction: *“Let people then consider their food:”* (80:24), followed by the account of poured rain, split earth, and grain and plants growing from it. The same water, the same soil, the same process produces the crop and the person eating the crop.

The practical consequence is a picture of the world as provision rather than as scenery. Everything the body needs comes out of the ground by the same mechanism that produced the body, and the mechanism has a single operator. This is the theology of provision stated botanically, and it is the same theology that underlies the promises earlier in the sermon — rain, wealth, children, gardens, rivers (71:11–12). Those gifts are not separate from the creation of the recipient. They are the continuation of the same act by the same agent, in the same medium.

The cattle, the children, the gardens, and the springs that Hūd lists for his people as the known good things provided for them (26:132–134) are all products of one soil under one sky. A person who accepts the provision and denies the provider has accepted the harvest and denied the farmer.

**The Growth That Also Proves the Return**

The Qur'an draws the inference explicitly and repeatedly. *“˹as˺ a provision for ˹Our˺ servants. And with this ˹rain˺ We revive a lifeless land. Similar is the emergence ˹from the graves˺.”* (50:11). *“He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And He gives life to the earth after its death. And so will you be brought forth ˹from the grave˺.”* (30:19). The comparison is not poetic decoration in either verse; it is the argument, stated as an analogy with a conclusion attached.

Noah's sermon has now supplied both halves of that analogy in advance. You were grown out of the earth, and the earth grows things that die and return. The next verse completes the sequence by naming the return and the second emergence — which is why the vegetable image here is not only a description of origin. It is the preparation for the hardest claim the sermon will make.

## Verse 71:18

> Then He will return you to it, and then simply bring you forth ˹again˺

**The Symmetry That Makes the Second Emergence Unremarkable**

The force of the verse lies in its refusal to treat resurrection as difficult. The word rendered *simply* in the translation reflects an Arabic construction of emphasis and ease rather than a separate promise: the bringing forth will happen, and it will happen without strain.

The argument is one the Qur'an makes in several places, always in the same direction — the second creation is not harder than the first, it is the same operation with the plan already drawn. *“How can you deny Allah? You were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned.”* (2:28). The four terms of that sentence are the four terms of Noah's claim, and both are addressed to people who accept the earlier terms and refuse the later ones.

**iḵhrāj — The Verb of Bringing Forth**

The same verb, *akhraja*, is used across the Qur'an for three distinct operations that the language treats as one: bringing a plant out of the soil, bringing a child out of the womb, and bringing the dead out of the ground. *“He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living.”* (30:19). The stages of gestation end in the same verb: then We brought it into being as a new creation (23:14).

**The Objection the Verse Is Answering**

The denial of return is not a modern problem, and the Qur'an records it in the exact form it takes: *“And they argue with Us— forgetting they were created—saying, ‘Who will give life to decayed bones?’”* (36:78). The charge attached to the objection is forgetfulness of one's own creation — precisely the failure this sermon has spent five verses describing.

The structure of the objection is instructive. It treats the bones as a settled object rather than as a stage in a process, and then asks how the object could move. The sermon's answer is to restore the process: you were grown, you will be returned, you will be brought out. Once the terms are arranged in sequence, the question *who will give life to decayed bones* has already been answered by the first term — the one who grew the bones in the first place. The same speaker in al-Yāsīn is told to answer that the One who produced them the first time will produce them again, and that He knows every creation.

Noah's audience had not formulated the objection in those words, but their behavior implies it. A people who run from an invitation to forgiveness (71:6) and refuse to hold God in weight (71:13) have decided, in practice, that no accounting is coming. The verse names the decision by describing the event it denies.

The verse completes the circuit begun two verses earlier, and the Qur'an states the same circuit in a single sentence elsewhere, in almost the same words: *“From the earth We created you, and into it We will return you, and from it We will bring you back again.”* (20:55).

## Verse 71:19

> And Allah ˹alone˺ spread out the earth for you

**bisāṭ — A Floor Laid Down**

The earth is described with a word from the vocabulary of furnishing: *bisāṭ*, something spread out, a carpet, a floor covering laid for people to walk and sit on. The same description recurs across the Qur'an: the earth laid out for all of you with pathways set in it (20:53); *“As for the earth, We spread it out and placed upon it firm mountains. Caused everything to grow there in perfect balance.”* (15:19); *“Have We not smoothed out the earth ˹like a bed˺,”* (78:6); *“As for the earth, He spread it out as well,”* (79:30); the earth made a place of settlement with the sky as a canopy (40:64).

The image is domestic rather than architectural. A carpet is not a monument; it is a surface prepared for use by someone who intends to be comfortable on it. To describe the planet that way is to say that the ground beneath a person's feet was arranged with him in mind, and it puts the arrangement on the same level as the two lights installed overhead (71:16) and the body grown out of the soil (71:17).

The consistency of the vocabulary across sūrahs revealed years apart tells the reader that this is a settled description and not a passing metaphor. The Qur'an returns to it whenever it wants to make the point that the world is furnished.

**For You — The Benefactive and Its Claim**

The phrase *lakum*, for you, does most of the religious work in the verse. The spreading is not described as an event that happened to be followed by human arrival. It is described as an act performed with the human being as its beneficiary.

That is the Qur'an's standing position on the material world: *“He is the One Who created everything in the earth for you. Then He turned toward the heaven, forming it into seven heavens.”* (2:29). Everything in the earth is stated to be for the human being before the heavens are formed, and the ordering is deliberate — provision precedes the arrival of the one provided for, and the whole construction is oriented toward him. The same orientation is stated for the sky and the shapes of bodies: He made the earth a settlement for you and the sky a canopy, He shaped you and perfected your form, and He provided you with good things (40:64).

**A Statement About Habitability, Not About Geometry**

The word *bisāṭ* has been read by some modern readers as a claim about the earth's shape, and the reading should be addressed rather than avoided.

The language of the verse is functional. It describes the earth as it presents itself to a person living on it — extended, traversable, usable — and it uses the ordinary vocabulary of a floor or a spread surface to say so. The same is true of the parallel descriptions: mountains placed as pegs so the ground does not shake (21:31), the earth smoothed like a bed (78:6), the sky raised as a canopy. None of these sentences is a treatise on geodesy, and the classical commentators did not read them as one. They read them as statements about the fitness of the world for human life, which is the subject the sermon is actually working on.

## Verse 71:20

> to walk along its spacious pathways.’”

**Roads Wide Enough to Travel**

The last words of the sermon name two things: *subul*, paths, and *fijāj*, the plural of *fajj*, a wide way through terrain — a broad valley, an open pass, a route with room in it. The combination describes not merely the existence of roads but their width: ways that can be travelled by a caravan, that do not force a person through a gap one at a time.

The same pairing appears elsewhere with the same emphasis. *“And We have placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with them, and made in it broad pathways so they may find their way.”* (21:31). The earth is laid out with pathways set in it (20:53), and the One who laid it out *“has laid out the earth for you, and set in it pathways for you so that you may find your way.”* (43:10). In each case the road is presented as a made thing, and the making is attributed to the same agent who spread the floor.

**So That You May Find Your Way**

The purpose attached to the roads in the parallel verses is guidance. Pathways were made *so that they may find their way* (21:31); so that you may find your way (43:10). The road exists so that a traveller can arrive.

That is the last turn of the sermon's argument, and it is a quiet one. Everything catalogued so far has been described as made for the human being — the body in stages, the layered heavens, the two lights, the growth from soil, the spread earth. The roads add a further specification: the world is not only furnished but oriented. It has directions in it, and the directions lead somewhere.

Orientation is a gift of a different order from provision. A person can be fed and housed and still be lost. The verse places the making of ways beside the making of the ground and treats both as parts of one arrangement, which implies that the arrangement includes a destination. The sermon never names the destination in this verse — it has already named it, in the opening command to worship, fear, and obey (71:3) — but the image supplies the physical form of the demand. A road built for you is a road you are expected to walk.

**Travel as a Way of Knowing**

The Qur'an consistently treats journeys as instruments of knowledge rather than as interruptions of it. The invitation is issued in the imperative: *“So travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers!”* (16:36); *“Travel throughout the land and see how He originated the creation, then Allah will bring it into being one more time.”* (29:20).

The reason given is that the heart reasons through the eyes and the ears of a traveller: *“Have they not travelled throughout the land so their hearts may reason, and their ears may listen? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind.”* (22:46). Travel is prescribed because a person who stays in one place runs out of evidence and starts to trust his own assumptions. The ruins of destroyed towns are themselves exhibits, and the wide roads of verse twenty are how a person reaches them.

## Verse 71:21

> ˹Eventually,˺ Noah cried, “My Lord! They have certainly persisted in disobeying me, and followed ˹instead˺ those ˹elite˺ whose ˹abundant˺ wealth and children only increase them in loss

**The Elites: al-Malaʾ**

The second half of the verse identifies who was followed instead. The bracketed *˹elite˺* in the translation renders a term the Qur'an uses as a fixed social category — the chiefs, the notables, the council of men whose standing rests on property and lineage.

The category appears in every prophetic account, and its behavior is uniform. The chiefs of Noah's people declare that they see him as clearly misguided (7:60). The disbelieving chiefs say he is only a human being like them and that only the lowest follow him (11:27). The chiefs tell the masses that he wants to be superior to them and that they never heard of such a thing from their forefathers (23:24). And the argument from social rank is the standard reply — *“How can we believe in you, when you are followed ˹only˺ by the lowest of the low?”* (26:111).

**Wealth and Children That Increase Only Loss**

The description of these men is the sharpest sentence in the sūrah: their property and their offspring increase them in nothing but *khasār*, loss, ruin, the state of a merchant whose trading makes him poorer.

The mechanism is not that wealth harms its possessor directly. It is that wealth, in the hands of a person who has decided against God, supplies him with reasons to keep deciding. Every increase confirms the decision: if this were wrong, would I be prospering? The Qur'an states the rule for the last messenger in the same terms: *“So let neither their wealth nor children impress you ˹O Prophet˺. Allah only intends to torment them through these things in this worldly life, then their souls will depart while they are disbelievers.”* (9:55). The provision is the torment, because it is what holds the person in place.

The counter-statement is just as plain: *“It is not your wealth or children that bring you closer to Us. But those who believe and do good—it is they who will have a multiplied reward for what they did.”* (34:37). Wealth is not nearness. The elite of Noah's people had built a theology out of their own inventory — we are greater in wealth and superior in manpower, as the owner of the two gardens puts it (18:34) — and the sermon had already answered that claim by offering the same goods to everyone on different terms (71:11–12).

**Following Men Rather Than Evidence**

The verse pairs two verbs and the pairing is the diagnosis: they disobeyed me, and they followed those. Obedience was refused where it was owed and given where it was not.

That substitution is what the Qur'an calls following without knowledge, and it treats it as the ordinary engine of collective error. The Day is described as the moment when the arrangement is dissolved: *“˹Consider the Day˺ when those who misled others will disown their followers—when they face the torment—and the bonds that united them will be cut off.”* (2:166). The followers make their case in the same terms: *“Had it not been for you, we would certainly have been believers.”* (34:31). The leaders answer by asking whether they prevented anyone from accepting guidance after it arrived.

## Verse 71:22

> and who have devised a tremendous plot

**Plotting Is What Replaces Argument**

The sequence in the sūrah is instructive: the methods of the messenger are exhausted (71:8–9), the sermon is delivered in full (71:10–20), and the next thing reported is a plot. There is no counter-argument anywhere in the account. The people never offer a reason.

That absence is itself evidence. A community with a case makes its case; a community without one changes the subject, and the subject here changes to conspiracy. The Qur'an describes the same pattern in the last messenger's Makkan period: *“And ˹remember, O Prophet,˺ when the disbelievers conspired to capture, kill, or exile you. They planned, but Allah also planned. And Allah is the best of planners.”* (8:30). The three options debated by the Quraysh council are the standard menu of a plot against a preacher — detention, execution, expulsion — and none of the three is an answer to what he said.

The verse in Sūrah Yūnus records the other side of the same exchange. When Noah's people find his presence unbearable, he tells them to assemble their plan with their associate gods, to settle on it without secrecy, and to carry it out against him without delay (10:71). A messenger who invites his opponents to plot openly has already decided that their contrivance cannot touch the message, and the invitation exposes the plot for what it is: not power, but the substitute for it.

**The Violence Behind the Plot**

The interpretive tradition preserves a report about what the plot involved in practice: that Noah's people beat him until he lost consciousness, wrapped him in a garment, dragged him to his house, and left him for dead, and that he went back out to preach when he recovered. The report is transmitted in the tafsīr literature on this verse.

What can be verified from the Qur'an is that the threat of lethal violence was explicit in the parallel account: *“If you do not desist, O Noah, you will surely be stoned ˹to death˺.”* (26:116). Stoning is named as the sanction for continuing to preach, which places the mission in a category the sūrah does not sentimentalize. Noah was not merely unpopular. He was operating under a standing death threat issued by men with the authority to carry it out, and the sūrah's word for their activity is a plot of the extreme kind.

**A Plot Against a Man With Nothing**

The disproportion is what gives the verse its weight. On one side: property, offspring, ancestral religion, tribal authority, and a coordinated plan. On the other: one man, no army, no wealth, a handful of followers drawn from the lowest ranks (11:27), and a message.

The Qur'an returns to this disproportion constantly because it is the ordinary condition of the office. God tells the last messenger that He will protect him from the people (5:67), and that protection is what makes the asymmetry survivable. The plot in verse twenty-two is described in the superlative not to magnify the plotters but to establish the scale of what the messenger endured and continued to do. He called them day and night (71:5), openly and privately (71:8–9), under a plot of the extreme kind.

## Verse 71:23

> urging ˹their followers˺, ‘Do not abandon your idols—especially Wadd, Suwâ’, Yaghûth, Ya’ûq, and Nasr.’

**Five Names**

The idols are listed: Wadd, Suwāʿ, Yaghūth, Yaʿūq, and Nasr. Five names, given without explanation in the text, and the explanation comes from the earliest generation of commentators.

Ibn ʿAbbās's account, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4920, does two things at once. It explains the origin of the five, and it traces their survival into pre-Islamic Arabia: the idols of Noah's people ended up among the Arabs afterwards. Wadd belonged to the tribe of Kalb at Dūmat al-Jandal; Suwāʿ to Hudhayl; Yaghūth to Murad, and then to Banū Ghutayf at al-Jurf near Sabaʾ; Yaʿūq to Hamdān; and Nasr to Himyar, to the family of Dhī al-Kalāʿ. The report converts a list of ancient names into a map of the Arabia the first audience lived in, and it explains why the sūrah bothers to name them at all.

The mapping has an argumentative function that would not have been lost on a Makkan listener. The religious establishment his tribe defended as ancestral was not merely old. It was traceable, by name and by tribe, to the specific idols that a prophet had spent nine and a half centuries trying to remove, and whose removal ended in the drowning of the people who kept them.

**Do Not Abandon — The Logic of Sunk Cost**

The Qur'an identifies this posture elsewhere with precision: *“These ˹idols˺ are mere names that you and your forefathers have made up—a practice Allah has never authorized. They follow nothing but ˹inherited˺ assumptions and whatever ˹their˺ souls desire, although ˹true˺ guidance has already come to them from their Lord.”* (53:23). Inherited assumption plus personal desire, with guidance already present and refused, is the diagnosis — and it fits the command in verse twenty-three exactly. An instruction with no reason attached is an instruction resting on inheritance and appetite.

**The Same Three Names in Makkah**

The sūrah's first audience had its own list. The Qur'an addresses it directly: *“Now, have you considered ˹the idols of˺ Lât and ’Uzza,”* and *“and the third one, Manât, as well?”* (53:19–20). Three named objects, defended by the same mechanism, in the same city where the sūrah was first recited.

## Verse 71:24

> Those ˹elite˺ have already led many astray. So ˹O Lord˺, only allow the wrongdoers to stray farther away.”

**"Many" — The Scale Recorded**

The word *kathīran* is doing quiet work. Not all, but many; and the many are counted on the side of the elite, which means the campaign described in verses twenty-two and twenty-three succeeded at scale.

The figure is consistent with everything else the sūrah reports. Noah's followers were few and drawn from the lowest ranks — the chiefs say so openly, and they say it as an argument: only the lowliest follow you (11:27; 26:111). The elite's success was not marginal. It absorbed the majority of a society over nine and a half centuries, generation after generation, and the mechanism was the one described in the previous verse: a prohibition on leaving, enforced by men whose standing required that nobody leave.

**The Hard Prayer: Asking That People Stray Further**

Three answers are given, and they are not mutually exclusive. The first: the petition is a request that a decree already fixed be carried out — Noah knows the case is closed, and he asks that what is settled be settled visibly. The second: the imperative form is being used for what is in fact a prediction, a common feature of Arabic supplicatory speech, where the petitioner states as a request what God has already determined. The third and strongest: increase in straying is itself the punishment. This is the doctrine the Qur'an names *istidrāj*, the drawing-on of a person stage by stage while he believes he is being favored — *“As for those who deny Our signs, We will gradually draw them to destruction in ways they cannot comprehend.”* (7:182), with the seal: *“I ˹only˺ delay their end for a while, but My planning is flawless.”* (7:183).

On the third reading the prayer asks for no new harm. It asks that the harm the wrongdoers have chosen for themselves be allowed to run its course without interruption. That is what the Qur'an describes happening to a society that refuses its signs: the call increases them only in aversion (17:41), and the sickness in the heart increases wickedness upon wickedness (9:125). Noah is asking God to stop restraining a process his people have already started.

**The Moment Intercession Stops**

There is a rule in the Qur'anic accounts about when a prophet may plead for his people, and this verse sits at the boundary. Once the decree is fixed, intercession for the condemned is forbidden: *“And build the Ark under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes and directions, and do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong, for they will surely be drowned.”* (11:37). The same prohibition is given to the believers in general: *“It is not ˹proper˺ for the Prophet and the believers to seek forgiveness for the polytheists, even if they were close relatives, after it has become clear to the believers that they are bound for the Hellfire.”* (9:113).

Noah's prayer in verse twenty-four is the speech of a man who has crossed that boundary and knows it. The mercy he spent centuries offering has been refused at every level — by the crowd, by the elite, by the institutions the elite maintained. What remains is not his to withhold or grant. He asks for the completion of the case, and the sūrah records the completion in the following verse without commentary.

## Verse 71:25

> So because of their sins, they were drowned, then admitted into the Fire. And they found none to help them against Allah

**A Thousand Years of Story in One Sentence**

The whole catastrophe occupies a single verse. They were drowned because of their sins, then they were made to enter a fire, and they found no helpers besides Allah. There is no ark, no rising water, no mountain, no rain, no oven bursting, and no narrative of anyone's last hours.

The economy is a decision. The other accounts of this event supply the scenery in detail: the gates of the sky opened with pouring rain, the vessel of planks and nails, the sailing under watchful eyes, the punishment on behalf of the one they denied (54:11–14). The ark built under divine direction with a pair of every species and the oven bursting as the sign (23:27, 11:37–44); the water overflowing and the carrying in the floating ark (69:11). This sūrah gives none of it. It gives the cause, the two punishments, and the absence of help.

**Because of Their Sins**

The causal clause comes first, and the word for sins is *khaṭīʾātihim*, a plural built on the noun for a fault or a wrong act. The punishment is attached to a cause, and the cause is theirs.

That attachment is the religious center of the verse. The flood is not presented as a divine mood, as a test with no stated reason, or as a catastrophe that happened to be survived by one righteous family. It is presented as a consequence with an antecedent, and the antecedent is named in the first word of the sentence. The whole sūrah has been building the antecedent: the flight from the call (71:6), the stopped ears and covered heads (71:7), the disobedience and the following of the elite (71:21), the tremendous plot (71:22), the enforced idolatry (71:23), the many led astray (71:24). Verse twenty-five collects all of it under one noun and states the result.

The other accounts use the same formula. Those who rejected Our signs were drowned (7:64; 10:73). The flood overtook them *while they persisted in wrongdoing* (29:14). In every version the water is described as an answer rather than an event, and in this version the answer is given before the water is even mentioned — the sin first, the drowning second.

**No Helpers Found**

The final clause is short and it answers verse twenty-one directly. They followed men of wealth and children; when the water came, they found nobody to help them against Allah.

The word for helpers is *anṣār*, the plural of *nāṣir* — one who gives aid, the same word used for supporters and allies in a military sense. The verse reports a search: they looked, and the looking produced nothing. This is the standard Qur'anic description of the end of every patronage system. The idols themselves turn on their worshippers: *“But no! Those ˹gods˺ will deny their worship and turn against them.”* (19:82). The leaders disown the followers and the bonds between them are cut (2:166). Even the closest relation is of no use: the wife of Noah and the wife of Lōt, married to two righteous servants, are told to enter the Fire because their husbands avail them nothing (66:10).

The Qur'an describes the doubling explicitly for another group: *“We will punish them twice ˹in this world˺, then they will be brought back ˹to their Lord˺ for a tremendous punishment.”* (9:101).

## Verse 71:26

> Noah had prayed, “My Lord! Do not leave a single disbeliever on earth

**dayyārā — Not One Who Stirs**

The noun at the end of the verse is *dayyār*, and it is one of the strangest words in the Qur'an. It comes from a root connected with movement and with dwelling, and classical Arabic uses it almost exclusively in negative constructions, where it means anyone at all — not one dweller, not one moving thing, not a soul.

The word is emphatic by its very form. Noah does not ask that the disbelievers be reduced, or punished, or made to submit. He asks that no single one of them be left upon the earth. The doubled construction *min aḥadin* with *dayyār* stacked together — the first meaning "anyone," the second intensifying it to "anyone who stirs or dwells" — produces an absolute negation. Nothing survives.

**The One Verse That Implies a Universal Flood**

Every other Qur'anic account of the flood is limited in its phrasing. Those who rejected Our signs were drowned (7:64; 10:73); the ones drowned were those who did wrong (11:37; 23:27); an evil people were delivered from (21:77). In each case the group is defined by its behavior, and no statement is made about anyone outside it.

This verse is different. It asks that no disbeliever be left *upon the earth*, without restriction, and it is the only verse in the Qur'an's Noah material that implies a global scope. It is also the reason later Muslim scholars could hold either position about the extent of the flood — universal, as the plain sense of this petition suggests, or local to Noah's people, as the other accounts imply.

The stronger reading, on the evidence of the Qur'an alone, is that the text does not settle the question and does not need to. The petition states what Noah asked for; it does not state what the geographical range of the judgment was, and the Qur'an nowhere reports the range. What is reported everywhere is the moral range: the people who were warned and refused. A reader who insists on a hydrological map is asking the text a question it was not composed to answer, and the answer he wants is not needed for anything the sūrah is doing.

**Why a Prophet Asks for Extinction**

The petition has a rule attached to it elsewhere in the Qur'an, and the rule explains both why Noah could ask and why he asked only now. Once the decree against a people is fixed, pleading for them is forbidden: *“do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong, for they will surely be drowned”* (11:37). The same rule is generalized for the Prophet ﷺ and the believers: seeking forgiveness for the polytheists is not proper even for close relatives, once it is clear they are bound for the Fire (9:113).

Noah's prayer belongs to the moment after that boundary. He is not interceding; intercession has been closed. He is asking that the closure be complete. The distinction is what makes the petition prophetic rather than vindictive: a man seeking revenge would ask for suffering, and Noah asks for absence. What he wants is that the mechanism described in verses twenty-one through twenty-four — an elite producing error at scale, generation after generation — be given no further material to work on.

## Verse 71:27

> For if You spare ˹any of˺ them, they will certainly mislead Your servants, and give birth only to ˹wicked˺ sinners, staunch disbelievers

**Your Servants, Not My People**

The object of the predicted misleading has changed, and the change is the verse's quiet center. In the sermon and in the earlier report, Noah speaks of *my people* (71:1–2, 71:21). Here he speaks of *Your servants*.

The same transfer appears in the closing prayer, where the objects of his petition for mercy are described as whoever enters my house in faith, and the believing men and women (71:28) — a category defined by faith rather than by blood. Between the two verses the sūrah has redrawn the boundary of Noah's concern. It now runs through belief, not lineage, and it runs through every believer on earth rather than through one town.

**fājiran kaffārā — Two Intensives in a Row**

The description of what these people will beget uses two nouns of the same grammatical weight, and the pairing is the most severe in the sūrah. *Fājir* comes from a root meaning to cleave, to burst open — the same root that gives the word for dawn, the thing that splits the dark. A *fājir* is one who breaks out of bounds, who ruptures the limit set for him. *Kaffār* is the intensive form of the one who covers over, who denies by burying what he knows.

The two words describe one condition from two ends. The *fājir* acts without limit; the *kaffār* refuses to see the limit. Together they name a person whose behavior is unbounded and whose perception is obstructed, which is exactly the combination the sūrah has been documenting: the plot, the enforced idolatry, the arrogance, the stopped ears, the covered heads.

The intensive forms matter. The Qur'an could have said a sinner and a disbeliever. It says a *fājir* and a *kaffār*, both at the extreme end of their scales, in a verse describing what a surviving generation would produce. The escalation is not rhetorical padding; it is the finding of the report. This is not a community that errs sometimes. It is a community whose errors have reached the intensive form and that has built institutions to transmit them.

**Not a Claim About Blood**

The prediction that they will beget only transgressors and deniers has been read by some as a statement about descent, and the reading should be refused rather than softened.

The Qur'an's rule on inheritance of guilt is stated without exception: *“No one will reap except what they sow. No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another.”* (6:164; cf. 53:38). No person is condemned for a parent's posture, and Noah's own son provides the sharpest illustration in the whole story — a prophet's child, drowned, with God telling the prophet that he is not of his family because his conduct was unrighteous (11:46). Lineage did not save the prophet's son, and lineage is not what condemns anyone else.

The Qur'an itself confirms the description of the input rather than the blood: the Flood overtook them *while they persisted in wrongdoing* (29:14). Persistence is a chosen posture, and it is the posture that gets transmitted.

## Verse 71:28

> My Lord! Forgive me, my parents, and whoever enters my house in faith, and ˹all˺ believing men and women. And increase the wrongdoers only in destruction.”

**The Widest Circle**

The fourth request is for the believing men and the believing women, with no restriction of time, place, or relation. A man whose mission produced almost no converts in nine and a half centuries prays for every believer who will exist after him.

**My Parents**

The prayer includes Noah's father and mother, and the inclusion is reported in the tradition as evidence that both were believers. The point is not genealogical but doctrinal, and it sits beside two other cases in the Qur'an that make the same argument from the opposite direction.

Abraham asked forgiveness for his father and then stopped, because it had become clear that his father was an enemy of God: seeking forgiveness for the polytheists is not proper for a prophet or for believers even when the person is a close relative (9:113). Noah's own wife is named among the examples set for the disbelievers — married to a righteous servant, betraying him, availed nothing by the marriage, and told to enter the Fire (66:10). Two of the closest relations a prophet can have are recorded as lost.

The scope is remarkable and it is not incidental. Noah is described in the tradition as the first messenger sent to the people of the earth, and humanity after the flood descends from those carried with him — a title the Qur'an gives him directly: *“˹O˺ descendants of those We carried with Noah ˹in the Ark˺! He was indeed a grateful servant.”* (17:3). In the report of the intercession on the Day of Resurrection, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3340 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 194, the assembled humanity goes to Adam, is turned away, and then goes to Noah with those words: you are the first of the messengers to the people of the earth.

**tabārā — The Last Word Is Ruin**

The final clause asks that the wrongdoers be increased in nothing but *tabār*, destruction, perdition, the state of a thing ruined beyond use. The root b-w-r means land left fallow and wasted, a thing that has come to nothing, and it is used in the Qur'an for the ruin of a people and of their works.

The word is chosen for its finality. Noah does not ask for their punishment in a general term, and he does not ask for their humiliation or their suffering. He asks for *tabār*, the exhaustion of a thing's substance — and the sūrah has already shown what that looks like: drowning, then fire, then no helpers found (71:25).

The closing symmetry is exact. The sūrah began with a warning about a painful punishment to come (71:1), and it ends with a prayer that the punishment be complete. Between the two, everything that could have prevented it was tried and refused. The last word is therefore not cruel. It is accurate: the file is closed, the verdict has been given, and the man who spent a millennium arguing for mercy asks that the case against the refusers be finished. The reader is left with the two circles side by side — forgiveness for every believer who enters, ruin for those who would not — and with the knowledge that the door was open for nine hundred and fifty years.
